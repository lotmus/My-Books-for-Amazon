# How to Read This Book

## Who this book is for

This book is for first-time and early independent authors who have written, or are writing, a serious ebook and want to publish it on Kindle without wasting money. You will learn to work out what one sale leaves you, price inside the right royalty band, ask for honest reviews the way Amazon allows, run a small capped ad test, and plan the second book that turns one title into a catalog.

It is not for everyone, and it says so now so you can decide before you spend an evening on it.

- It is not an advanced Amazon Ads manual. Chapter 26 stops at the first profitable campaign.
- It is not a writing-craft book. It will not teach plot, structure, or style.
- It is not a manual for publishing wide on every store. Chapter 28 helps you choose between KDP Select and going wide; the store-by-store work is left to those stores’ own guides.
- It is not a system for publishing many books quickly, with or without AI.
- It does not promise income. No method can, and this one does not try.

## Why the arithmetic is always shown

Kevin Drew Peters is a graduate of Munich University of Applied Sciences. He spent many years in the semiconductor industry, most of them in the United States, supporting several start-ups. That work teaches a habit: measure before you decide, write the arithmetic down, change one variable at a time, and put a cap on the cost of every experiment.

This book applies that habit to Kindle publishing. What you will get: the platform rules with their sources and dates, the arithmetic behind every price and every ad, and small bounded tests you can run on your own book. What you will not get: the author’s own sales figures held up as proof, stories of overnight success, or a claim to know how Amazon ranks books. Amazon does not publish its ranking algorithm, and nobody outside Amazon has it.

## How the book is built

Part I is the short road: fifteen short chapters, one action each, and a one-page plan for your first week. You follow one first-time author and her cozy mystery through one month. She is a teaching example, not a real person. If you read only Part I, you can publish honestly and know what each sale pays you.

Parts II to VIII are the full guide. They do not repeat Part I. Each chapter points back to the place where a rule is taught and adds what Part I left out: the marketplace detail, the edge cases, the reasons, and the tests. Part VII takes the first-review problem in depth, and Part VIII covers AI. The appendices hold a launch checklist, a royalty quick reference, thirty-two ways a book can pay you, a glossary, and the numbered notes.

## The five labels

Every claim that could cost you money carries one of five labels.

**Platform rule [n].** Something Amazon or another authority publishes, with a numbered note giving the page and the date it was checked. Rules change; the note tells you where to look.

**Worked example.** Hypothetical arithmetic with stated assumptions, usually a 2 MB file and no VAT. It shows how the sum works. It is not anyone’s result.

**Field practice.** What many authors report doing. It is common, not guaranteed, and not published by Amazon.

**Recommendation.** What this book advises, and why. You may disagree.

**Test it yourself.** A question that only your own book can answer, with a small, capped way to answer it.

Nothing in this book is a promise that you will earn a stated amount.
