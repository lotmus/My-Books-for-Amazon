TAGLINE: Varying the action in four dimensions, the general field equation, and its application to the scalar, Dirac, and electromagnetic fields

# How to use this lesson

Lesson 26 wrote down the Lagrangian densities of the scalar, Dirac, and electromagnetic fields and stated the equations they lead to. This lesson derives those equations. The derivation is the field-theoretic version of the argument of Lesson 5, Section 2.2, and uses the same three ingredients: vary the action, integrate by parts to move the derivative off the variation, and invoke the fundamental lemma that a function that integrates to zero against every test function must vanish. The only new feature is that the integration is over four-dimensional spacetime and the derivative is a four-gradient.

Once the general equation is in hand it is applied in turn to each field. The scalar equation is the Klein–Gordon equation of Lesson 18, the Dirac field gives the Dirac equation and its adjoint, and the electromagnetic field gives the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations. That a single Lagrangian density per field reproduces every equation of Parts II and III, and the Maxwell equations of Lesson 7, is the justification for the whole Lagrangian approach. The lesson closes with a solution of the sourced scalar equation that shows how a massive field mediates a force of finite range, an idea that returns in Lesson 46. Expect seven to nine focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Derive the Euler–Lagrange equation for a field by varying the action and integrating by parts in four dimensions.

- State the equation for a system of several fields and for complex fields treated as independent variables.

- Compute the derivatives ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) correctly, keeping track of index positions.

- Derive the Klein–Gordon equation, including the nonlinear case with a φ⁴ interaction.

- Derive the Dirac equation and its adjoint from the Dirac Lagrangian density.

- Derive the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations ∂_μF^(μν) = j^ν from the electromagnetic Lagrangian density and verify that current conservation follows.

- Write Hamilton's equations for a field using functional derivatives.

- Solve the static sourced Klein–Gordon equation and relate the range of the resulting Yukawa potential to the mass of the field.

# 1 The general equation

## 1.1 Varying the action

Let S[φ] = ∫_Ω d⁴x ℒ(φ, ∂_μφ) be the action for a single field over a spacetime region Ω. Replace φ by φ + δφ, where δφ is an arbitrary small function that vanishes on the boundary of Ω, in the same way that the variation of a particle path vanished at its endpoints (Lesson 5, Section 2.2). Since the derivative of a variation is the variation of the derivative, δ(∂_μφ) = ∂_μ(δφ), the first-order change of ℒ is

$$ δℒ = (∂ℒ/∂φ) δφ + [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] ∂_μ(δφ)

A sum over the repeated index μ is understood. Here ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) means the derivative of ℒ with respect to the four quantities ∂_μφ, regarded as independent variables, and it carries an upper index because it is contracted with the lower index of ∂_μδφ.

## 1.2 Integrating by parts

Apply the product rule to the second term, as in the derivation of Lesson 2, Section 8, but with a four-dimensional derivative.

$$ [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] ∂_μ(δφ) = ∂_μ{ [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] δφ } − ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] δφ

The first term on the right is a total derivative. By the four-dimensional divergence theorem, its integral over Ω is a surface integral over the boundary of Ω, where δφ vanishes, so it contributes nothing. What remains is

$$ δS = ∫_Ω d⁴x { ∂ℒ/∂φ − ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] } δφ

## 1.3 The field equation

Because δφ is arbitrary, the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations, used in Lesson 2, Section 8, requires the bracket to vanish at every point.

$$ ∂ℒ/∂φ − ∂_μ [ ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) ] = 0

This is the Euler–Lagrange equation for a field. When there are several fields φ_a, each can be varied independently, and there is one equation of this form for each of them, with ℒ depending on all the fields and their gradients. The equation is a covariant statement: if ℒ is a Lorentz scalar, both terms are scalars, so the field equation has the same form in every frame.

### Worked example 1

Show that the equation reduces to the particle equation of Lesson 5. Suppose the fields depend only on time, so that a single coordinate q(t) replaces φ(t, x) and ℒ becomes the ordinary Lagrangian L(q, q̇). Then the only nonzero derivative is ∂_0q = q̇, and the equation is ∂L/∂q − d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) = 0, the Euler–Lagrange equation of Lesson 5, Section 2.2. A particle is a field in zero space dimensions, and the general equation includes the mechanics of Part I as its simplest case.

## 1.4 Index conventions

The derivative with respect to a lowered-index quantity produces a raised index, and vice versa. For μ = 0 the object ∂ℒ/∂(∂_0φ) = ∂ℒ/∂φ̇ is the conjugate momentum density π of Lesson 26, Section 3.2. For the spatial components, ∂ℒ/∂(∂ᵢφ) is the derivative with respect to the ordinary spatial gradient ∂φ/∂xⁱ. A convenient check is to write the equation out in components: ∂ℒ/∂φ − ∂_t π − ∂ᵢ[∂ℒ/∂(∂ᵢφ)] = 0, in which the time derivative of the momentum density appears explicitly, exactly as ṗ = ∂L/∂q does for a particle.

# 2 The scalar field

## 2.1 The Klein–Gordon equation

For ℒ = (1/2)∂_αφ∂^αφ − (1/2)m²φ², the derivative with respect to φ is ∂ℒ/∂φ = −m²φ. To differentiate the kinetic term with respect to ∂_μφ, write it as (1/2)g^(αβ)∂_αφ∂_βφ. The derivative acts on each of the two factors, and by the symmetry of g^(αβ) the two contributions are equal.

$$ ∂[ (1/2) g^(αβ) ∂_αφ ∂_βφ ] / ∂(∂_μφ) = (1/2)(g^(μβ)∂_βφ + g^(αμ)∂_αφ) = ∂^μφ

Then ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] = ∂_μ∂^μφ = □φ, and the Euler–Lagrange equation reads −m²φ − □φ = 0, that is

$$ (□ + m²) φ = 0

the Klein–Gordon equation. In components, ∂ℒ/∂φ̇ = φ̇ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂ᵢφ) = −∂ᵢφ, so the equation is −m²φ − φ̈ + ∇²φ = 0, the same equation, confirming the index bookkeeping. The equation that in Lesson 18 was postulated by the operator substitution E → i∂_t, p → −i∇ now follows from a variational principle.

## 2.2 The complex field

For ℒ = ∂_μφ*∂^μφ − m²φ*φ, write φ = a + ib with a and b real, so that varying the real and imaginary parts independently is equivalent to varying φ and φ* independently (the combinations δφ = δa + iδb and δφ* = δa − iδb span the same space). Varying φ* gives ∂ℒ/∂φ* = −m²φ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ*) = ∂^μφ, so the Euler–Lagrange equation is (□ + m²)φ = 0. Varying φ gives ∂ℒ/∂φ = −m²φ* and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ*, so (□ + m²)φ* = 0. Both the field and its conjugate obey the Klein–Gordon equation, consistent with the treatment of Lesson 18. The trick of treating φ and φ* as independent is the field-theoretic counterpart of treating q and p as independent in Lesson 6.

## 2.3 An interaction makes the equation nonlinear

Add a quartic interaction, ℒ = (1/2)∂_μφ∂^μφ − (1/2)m²φ² − (λ/4!)φ⁴ (Lesson 26, Section 3.3). Then ∂ℒ/∂φ = −m²φ − (λ/6)φ³ and the equation of motion becomes

$$ (□ + m²) φ = −(λ/6) φ³

The right side is nonlinear in φ, so, unlike the free equation, the sum of two solutions is not a solution. Waves no longer pass through one another without change: they scatter. This is the classical origin of particle interactions, and the reason that quantum field theories with interaction terms produce scattering amplitudes (Lesson 51).

### Worked example 2

Show that the sum of two solutions of the φ⁴ equation is not a solution. Let φ₁ and φ₂ each satisfy (□ + m²)φ = −(λ/6)φ³. Then (□ + m²)(φ₁ + φ₂) = −(λ/6)(φ₁³ + φ₂³), while the right side of the equation for φ₁ + φ₂ would be −(λ/6)(φ₁ + φ₂)³ = −(λ/6)(φ₁³ + 3φ₁²φ₂ + 3φ₁φ₂² + φ₂³). The mismatch is −(λ/2)(φ₁²φ₂ + φ₁φ₂²), nonzero wherever both waves are present: the two waves interact through the cross terms, and superposition fails for λ ≠ 0.

# 3 The Dirac field

## 3.1 Varying ψ̄

Treat ψ and ψ̄ as independent fields, as for φ and φ* above. The Dirac Lagrangian density ℒ = ψ̄(iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ contains no derivative of ψ̄, so ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μψ̄) = 0 and ∂ℒ/∂ψ̄ = (iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ. The Euler–Lagrange equation for ψ̄ is therefore

$$ (iγ^μ∂_μ − m) ψ = 0

the Dirac equation of Lesson 20, obtained here without any of the algebra of Lesson 19: it is the equation of motion of the Lagrangian density built from the simplest Lorentz scalars.

## 3.2 Varying ψ

Writing the Lagrangian density as ℒ = iψ̄γ^μ∂_μψ − mψ̄ψ, the derivatives with respect to ψ are ∂ℒ/∂ψ = −mψ̄ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μψ) = iψ̄γ^μ, with the matrix index of γ^μ contracted with the row index of ψ̄. The Euler–Lagrange equation for ψ is −mψ̄ − ∂_μ(iψ̄γ^μ) = 0, that is

$$ −i (∂_μψ̄) γ^μ − m ψ̄ = 0

exactly the adjoint Dirac equation derived in Lesson 24, Section 1.1, by a different route. The two equations obtained by varying ψ̄ and ψ are Hermitian conjugates of each other, so they are consistent, and either can be used to derive the conserved current ψ̄γ^μψ of Lesson 24.

### Worked example 3

Add a Yukawa interaction with a scalar field, ℒ = (1/2)∂_μφ∂^μφ − (1/2)M²φ² + ψ̄(iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ − gφψ̄ψ, and find the equations of motion. Varying ψ̄ gives (iγ^μ∂_μ − m − gφ)ψ = 0: the fermion has an effective mass m + gφ(x) that depends on the scalar field. Varying φ gives −M²φ − gψ̄ψ − □φ = 0, that is (□ + M²)φ = −gψ̄ψ: the scalar is sourced by the fermion bilinear ψ̄ψ. The pair of equations shows in miniature the structure of every interacting theory in this course, including QED: each field's equation of motion contains the other field as a source.

# 4 The electromagnetic field

## 4.1 Differentiating the field tensor

For ℒ = −(1/4)F_αβF^(αβ) − j^αA_α, the fields are the four components A_ν. The derivative of the interaction term is ∂ℒ/∂A_ν = −j^ν. The kinetic term depends on the derivatives ∂_μA_ν only through F_αβ, and the required derivative is (Exercise 8)

$$ ∂(F_αβ F^(αβ)) / ∂(∂_μA_ν) = 4 F^(μν)

which follows from expanding F_αβF^(αβ) = 2∂_αA_β∂^αA^β − 2∂_αA_β∂^βA^α and differentiating each factor. Therefore ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μA_ν) = −F^(μν).

## 4.2 The Maxwell equations

The Euler–Lagrange equation for A_ν is ∂ℒ/∂A_ν − ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μA_ν)] = 0, that is −j^ν + ∂_μF^(μν) = 0.

$$ ∂_μ F^(μν) = j^ν

These are the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations in the covariant form previewed in Lesson 4, Section 4.3, and Lesson 8, Section 7.2. The homogeneous pair, ∇·B = 0 and ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, are not equations of motion; they are the Bianchi identity ∂_[αF_βγ] = 0, satisfied automatically because F_μν = ∂_μA_ν − ∂_νA_μ (Lesson 8, Section 1). The Lagrangian density therefore produces all four Maxwell equations, two as equations of motion and two as identities.

### Worked example 4

Verify that the ν = 0 component of ∂_μF^(μν) = j^ν is Gauss's law. With F₀ᵢ = Eᵢ (Lesson 8, Section 7.2) and F^(0i) = −Eᵢ, F^(i0) = +Eᵢ. Since F^(00) = 0, ∂_μF^(μ0) = ∂ᵢF^(i0) = ∂ᵢEᵢ = ∇·E, and the equation reads ∇·E = j⁰ = ρ, Gauss's law in Heaviside–Lorentz units (Lesson 7, Section 7.1).

The spatial components ν = i give the Ampère Maxwell law. With the convention Fᵢⱼ = −εᵢⱼₖBₖ (which follows from Aᵢ = −Aⁱ) one has F^(ji) = Fⱼᵢ = εᵢⱼₖBₖ, so ∂_μF^(μi) = ∂_tF^(0i) + ∂ⱼF^(ji) = −∂_tEᵢ + (∇×B)ᵢ = jⁱ, that is ∇×B = j + ∂E/∂t (Exercise 9).

## 4.3 Current conservation follows

Take ∂_ν of the Maxwell equation. Since ∂_ν∂_μ is symmetric in ν and μ while F^(μν) is antisymmetric, ∂_ν∂_μF^(μν) = 0 identically (Lesson 4, Exercise 7), so

$$ ∂_ν j^ν = 0

The field equations can be solved only if the source is conserved, the result found from a different direction in Lesson 8, Exercise 16, and in Lesson 26, Section 6.3, where gauge invariance of the interaction required it. This is the third appearance of the same fact, and it is not a coincidence: gauge invariance, current conservation, and the consistency of the equations of motion are one requirement seen from three sides. In the Lorenz gauge, ∂_μF^(μν) = □A^ν − ∂^ν(∂_μA^μ) = □A^ν, so the equation reduces to □A^ν = j^ν, the form of Lesson 8, Section 4.1 (Exercise 11).

### Worked example 5

Confirm the derivative of the kinetic term for one component. Take the case in which the only nonzero field derivative in the relevant sector is ∂₁A₂ with all other ∂_μA_ν held fixed. Then F₁₂ = ∂₁A₂ − ∂₂A₁ = ∂₁A₂, and F_αβF^(αβ) contains F₁₂F^(12) + F₂₁F^(21) = 2F₁₂F^(12) = 2(∂₁A₂)², using F^(12) = F₁₂. Its derivative with respect to ∂₁A₂ is 4∂₁A₂ = 4F₁₂ = 4F^(12), in agreement with the general result 4F^(μν) for μ = 1, ν = 2.

# 5 Hamilton's equations for a field

## 5.1 Functional derivatives

The Hamiltonian formulation of Lesson 6 extends to fields by replacing the sum over coordinates with an integral over space. The Hamiltonian is H = ∫d³x ℋ, with ℋ = πφ̇ − ℒ the Hamiltonian density of Lesson 26, Section 3.2, regarded as a function of φ and π. The functional derivative of H with respect to the field is defined by the change of H under a small change of the field.

$$ δH/δφ(x) = ∂ℋ/∂φ − ∂ᵢ[∂ℋ/∂(∂ᵢφ)],    δH/δπ(x) = ∂ℋ/∂π

Hamilton's equations for a field then take the form of Lesson 6, Section 2.1, with the functional derivative in place of the partial derivative.

$$ φ̇ = δH/δπ,    π̇ = −δH/δφ

## 5.2 The scalar field

For ℋ = (1/2)π² + (1/2)(∇φ)² + (1/2)m²φ², δH/δπ = π and δH/δφ = m²φ − ∇²φ, so Hamilton's equations are φ̇ = π and π̇ = ∇²φ − m²φ. Differentiating the first and substituting the second gives φ̈ = ∇²φ − m²φ, that is (□ + m²)φ = 0, the Klein–Gordon equation once more, this time from the Hamiltonian formulation (Exercise 12). The pair of equations, each first order in time, is the field version of q̇ = p/m, ṗ = −mω²q for the oscillator of Lesson 6, and it is the starting point for the canonical quantization of Lesson 33, where φ and π become operators with a commutator playing the role of the Poisson bracket.

# 6 A sourced field and the range of a force

## 6.1 The static equation

Add a source to the scalar Lagrangian density, ℒ = (1/2)∂_μφ∂^μφ − (1/2)m²φ² + Jφ. The Euler–Lagrange equation is (□ + m²)φ = J. For a time-independent source and a static solution, □ = −∇², so

$$ (−∇² + m²) φ(x) = J(x)

For a point source J = gδ³(x) at the origin, the solution is the Yukawa function.

$$ φ(r) = g e^(−mr)/(4πr)

Away from the origin this satisfies the homogeneous equation: with f = e^(−mr)/r and the spherical Laplacian ∇²f = (1/r)d²(rf)/dr², rf = e^(−mr) gives ∇²f = m²e^(−mr)/r = m²f, so (−∇² + m²)f = 0. Near the origin the solution behaves as g/(4πr), and since ∇²(1/4πr) = −δ³(x) (the Coulomb potential of Lesson 7, Section 7.1), the source term is reproduced with the correct strength, so (−∇² + m²)φ = gδ³ holds everywhere.

## 6.2 Range of the force

The factor e^(−mr) cuts the potential off at distances larger than 1/m. A massless field, m = 0, gives φ = g/(4πr), the Coulomb form of infinite range: this is the electrostatic potential of a point charge, since the photon field is massless. A massive field gives a force of finite range 1/m, in natural units, or ℏ/(mc) in laboratory units. Yukawa used this argument to predict a particle of mass of order 100 MeV to mediate the nuclear force, whose range is about 1.4 fm; the particle was later identified with the pion, of mass 139.6 MeV (Exercise 13). The relationship between the mass of the exchanged particle and the range of the force is one of the most useful pieces of intuition in particle physics, and Lesson 46 will show that the same Yukawa function is the Fourier transform of the propagator 1/(k² + m²) appearing in every Feynman diagram.

### Worked example 6

Estimate the range of the force mediated by a field of mass 100 GeV. In natural units the range is 1/m = 1/(100 GeV). Converting with ℏc = 0.1973 GeV fm (Lesson 3, Section 6.2), the range is 0.1973/100 fm ≈ 2 × 10⁻³ fm, a few thousandths of the size of a proton. A force mediated by so heavy a field is effectively a contact interaction at ordinary energies, which is why the weak interaction, mediated by particles of mass about 80 to 90 GeV, appears weak and short ranged.

The Euler–Lagrange equations of this lesson are classical field equations. In QED they hold as operator equations for the quantized fields, which is why the classical analysis of Parts III and IV is not merely preparation but is the content of the free theory. The nonlinear terms, the products of different fields at one point, are what remain to be treated by perturbation theory in Parts VII and IX.

# 8 Summary

1. Varying the action S = ∫d⁴x ℒ with fixed boundary values and integrating by parts in four dimensions gives ∂ℒ/∂φ − ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] = 0 for each field.

2. For a field depending only on time the equation reduces to the particle Euler–Lagrange equation of Lesson 5.

3. The scalar Lagrangian density gives the Klein–Gordon equation; a φ⁴ term makes the equation nonlinear and superposition fails.

4. A complex field and its conjugate are varied independently and each obeys the Klein–Gordon equation.

5. The Dirac Lagrangian density gives the Dirac equation on varying ψ̄ and the adjoint Dirac equation on varying ψ.

6. The electromagnetic Lagrangian density gives ∂_μF^(μν) = j^ν; the homogeneous equations are an identity, and current conservation follows from the antisymmetry of F^(μν).

7. Hamilton's equations for a field, φ̇ = δH/δπ and π̇ = −δH/δφ, reproduce the Klein–Gordon equation.

8. A static point source of a massive scalar field produces the Yukawa potential ge^(−mr)/(4πr), with a range 1/m set by the mass of the field.

# Solutions

*Attempt each exercise before reading its solution. If you are stuck, read only the first sentence of the solution, then try again.*

**1.** δS = ∫d⁴x{(∂ℒ/∂φ)δφ + [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)]∂_μδφ}. Writing [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)]∂_μδφ = ∂_μ{[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)]δφ} − ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)]δφ, the first term is a total derivative whose integral is a surface integral of [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)]δφ over the boundary of Ω. It vanishes if δφ = 0 on the boundary. What remains is δS = ∫d⁴x{∂ℒ/∂φ − ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)]}δφ, and since δφ is arbitrary in the interior the bracket must vanish.

**2.** For φ₁: ∂ℒ/∂φ₁ = −m₁²φ₁ − (g/2)φ₂² and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ₁) = ∂^μφ₁, so −m₁²φ₁ − (g/2)φ₂² − □φ₁ = 0, that is (□ + m₁²)φ₁ = −(g/2)φ₂². For φ₂: ∂ℒ/∂φ₂ = −m₂²φ₂ − gφ₁φ₂ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ₂) = ∂^μφ₂, so (□ + m₂²)φ₂ = −gφ₁φ₂. Each field is sourced by a product involving the other.

**3.** ∂ℒ/∂φ̇ = φ̇ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂ᵢφ) = −∂ᵢφ for each spatial i, while ∂ℒ/∂φ = −m²φ. The component form of the equation is ∂ℒ/∂φ − ∂_t(∂ℒ/∂φ̇) − ∂ᵢ[∂ℒ/∂(∂ᵢφ)] = −m²φ − φ̈ + ∂ᵢ∂ᵢφ = −m²φ − φ̈ + ∇²φ = 0, that is φ̈ − ∇²φ + m²φ = (□ + m²)φ = 0.

**4.** With −(λ/4!)φ⁴, ∂ℒ/∂φ = −m²φ − (λ/6)φ³, and ∂_μ[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)] = □φ, so −m²φ − (λ/6)φ³ − □φ = 0, that is (□ + m²)φ = −(λ/6)φ³. A constant φ has □φ = 0, so the equation becomes m²φ = −(λ/6)φ³, that is m²φ + (λ/6)φ³ = 0, which is V′(φ) = 0 for the potential of Lesson 26, Exercise 13.

**5.** Write φ = a + ib. An arbitrary variation is δφ = δa + iδb with δa and δb independent real functions, and δφ* = δa − iδb. Requiring δS = 0 for independent δa and δb is equivalent to requiring the coefficients of δφ and δφ* to vanish separately. Varying φ*: ∂ℒ/∂φ* = −m²φ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ*) = ∂^μφ, so (□ + m²)φ = 0. Varying φ: ∂ℒ/∂φ = −m²φ* and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ*, so (□ + m²)φ* = 0.

**6.** With ℒ = iψ̄γ^μ∂_μψ − mψ̄ψ, ∂ℒ/∂ψ = −mψ̄ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μψ) = iψ̄γ^μ. The equation −mψ̄ − ∂_μ(iψ̄γ^μ) = 0 becomes −i(∂_μψ̄)γ^μ − mψ̄ = 0, the adjoint Dirac equation of Lesson 24.

**7.** Varying ψ̄: ∂ℒ/∂ψ̄ = (iγ^μ∂_μ − m − gφ)ψ, so (iγ^μ∂_μ − m − gφ)ψ = 0, with the scalar field acting as a position-dependent addition to the mass. Varying φ: ∂ℒ/∂φ = −M²φ − gψ̄ψ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ, so (□ + M²)φ = −gψ̄ψ. The source of φ is the fermion bilinear −gψ̄ψ, and the source of ψ is the term gφψ.

**8.** Write F_αβF^(αβ) = (∂_αA_β − ∂_βA_α)(∂^αA^β − ∂^βA^α) = 2∂_αA_β∂^αA^β − 2∂_αA_β∂^βA^α. Differentiating 2∂_αA_β∂^αA^β with respect to ∂_μA_ν, each of the two identical factors contributes ∂^μA^ν, giving 4∂^μA^ν. For −2∂_αA_β∂^βA^α, the factor ∂_αA_β with α = μ, β = ν leaves ∂^νA^μ, and the factor ∂^βA^α with the same identification also leaves ∂^νA^μ, so the derivative is −4∂^νA^μ. The total is 4(∂^μA^ν − ∂^νA^μ) = 4F^(μν).

**9.** For ν = i, ∂_μF^(μi) = ∂_0F^(0i) + ∂ⱼF^(ji). With F^(0i) = −F₀ᵢ = −Eᵢ, the first term is −∂_tEᵢ. With F^(ji) = Fⱼᵢ = −εⱼᵢₖBₖ = εᵢⱼₖBₖ, the second term is εᵢⱼₖ∂ⱼBₖ = (∇×B)ᵢ. The equation is −∂_tEᵢ + (∇×B)ᵢ = jⁱ, that is ∇×B − ∂_tE = j, the Ampère Maxwell law.

**10.** The operator ∂_ν∂_μ is symmetric in ν and μ, since partial derivatives commute, and F^(μν) is antisymmetric. Contracting a symmetric with an antisymmetric tensor gives zero: ∂_ν∂_μF^(μν) = ∂_μ∂_νF^(μν) = −∂_μ∂_νF^(νμ) = −∂_ν∂_μF^(μν) after renaming the dummy indices, so it equals its own negative and vanishes. Taking ∂_ν of ∂_μF^(μν) = j^ν then gives ∂_νj^ν = 0.

**11.** ∂_μF^(μν) = ∂_μ(∂^μA^ν − ∂^νA^μ) = □A^ν − ∂^ν(∂_μA^μ), using the equality of mixed partial derivatives. If ∂_μA^μ = 0, the second term vanishes and the equation is □A^ν = j^ν.

**12.** δH/δπ = ∂ℋ/∂π = π. δH/δφ = ∂ℋ/∂φ − ∂ᵢ[∂ℋ/∂(∂ᵢφ)] = m²φ − ∂ᵢ(∂ᵢφ) = m²φ − ∇²φ. Hamilton's equations are φ̇ = π and π̇ = −(m²φ − ∇²φ) = ∇²φ − m²φ. Then φ̈ = π̇ = ∇²φ − m²φ, that is (∂_t² − ∇² + m²)φ = (□ + m²)φ = 0.

**13.** For f = e^(−mr)/r, rf = e^(−mr), so d²(rf)/dr² = m²e^(−mr), and ∇²f = (1/r)d²(rf)/dr² = m²e^(−mr)/r = m²f for r > 0. Hence (−∇² + m²)f = 0 for r > 0. The range is ℏc/(mc²) = 197.3 MeV fm/139.6 MeV = 1.41 fm, about the range of the nuclear force.

**14.** For a static source, ∂_t²φ = 0 and □φ = −∇²φ, so (□ + m²)φ = J becomes (−∇² + m²)φ = J. The operator is linear, so the solution for two sources is the sum of the solutions for each: φ(x) = g₁e^(−m|x−x₁|)/(4π|x−x₁|) + g₂e^(−m|x−x₂|)/(4π|x−x₂|).

**15.** If δφ did not vanish on the boundary, the total-derivative term would leave a surface contribution ∮[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ)]δφ dΣ_μ in δS. The field equation is the condition that the interior contribution vanishes for every δφ, including those that vanish on the boundary, and that condition is unchanged. The surface term matters only for the choice of boundary conditions and for deriving conserved charges (Lesson 28), where a symmetry variation δφ is generally nonzero at the boundary.

**16.** Using (−∇² + m²)φ = J, integrating by parts, and discarding surface terms, ∫[(1/2)(∇φ)² + (1/2)m²φ²]d³x = (1/2)∫φ(−∇² + m²)φ d³x = (1/2)∫Jφ d³x. Then H = (1/2)∫Jφ − ∫Jφ = −(1/2)∫Jφ. With J as in Exercise 14 and the Yukawa function Y(r) = e^(−mr)/(4πr), φ = g₁Y(|x−x₁|) + g₂Y(|x−x₂|), and ∫Jφ d³x = g₁[g₁Y(0) + g₂Y(r)] + g₂[g₁Y(r) + g₂Y(0)] = (g₁² + g₂²)Y(0) + 2g₁g₂Y(r). Hence H = −(1/2)(g₁² + g₂²)Y(0) − g₁g₂Y(r). The first term is a divergent self-energy independent of the separation, and the interaction energy is −g₁g₂e^(−mr)/(4πr), which is negative and decreasing in magnitude with r for g₁g₂ > 0. Energy is lowered when like sources approach one another, so a scalar field attracts like sources, in contrast with the electromagnetic potential, which repels like charges (a difference traced to the spin of the exchanged particle in Lesson 48).
