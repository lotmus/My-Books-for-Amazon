# PART IV — The Missing Most

## 17. The Extra Pull

Dark matter is a terrible name. It sounds like a feeling, or a brand of chocolate, or the opposite of enlightenment. It is none of those. It is a pull we can weigh and cannot photograph in ordinary light.

Barrow, in the short origin book this one uses as a model, had a habit of refusing a pretty noun until a measurement had earned it. Dark matter has not earned a pretty noun. It has earned a scale. The scale is the extra weight in galaxies and in clusters: more pull than the shining stars and gas can account for, by a factor of several. The name is a confession. We weighed it. We have not bagged it.

In the kitchen you do not weigh a truck by climbing into the trailer and counting crates. You weigh it by how the bridge leans when the truck rolls on — by the strain gauges, the sag, the way the other traffic feels the extra. Dark matter is the trailer you cannot open. The bridge still leans. That is the whole method of this chapter, and it is why the extra pull is a hot fact even while the particle is a warm rumor.

In the 1930s, Fritz Zwicky timed the galaxies in the Coma Cluster and found them moving too fast. The visible stars did not supply enough gravity to hold the swarm. He said so, rudely, and was mostly ignored. In the 1970s, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford timed stars in the disks of spiral galaxies and found the same insult at a friendlier scale: stars out in the suburbs orbit as fast as stars closer in, as if most of the mass were in a big, dark halo, not in the shining bulge. The light is a liar about the weight.

The Coma paper is 1933, and the cluster is a city of cities: thousands of galaxies in a well so deep that, if you time them as Zwicky did, the virial theorem becomes a speed trap. If the members are this fast, the well has to be this deep, or the swarm would have flown apart long ago. The well was too deep for the starlight. The rudeness was not the problem. The problem was that nobody wanted an extra ingredient that did not shine. Extra ingredients are expensive. Astronomy in the 1930s was still finishing the argument about whether spiral nebulae were even other galaxies. An invisible majority was a second insult.

Rubin and Ford brought the second instrument down to a single disk. A rotation curve is speed versus distance from the center, a plot that Newtonian common sense said should fall once you leave the shining bulge. Their curves stayed flat. Flat is not a mood. Flat is *v* staying large while *r* grows, which, if gravity is still gravity, means *M* is still growing in the dark. They did this galaxy after galaxy, with a spectrograph and a stubbornness that later textbooks still under-thank. The suburban stars were clocks. The clocks did not slow.

What dark matter is *not*, while we are cleaning the noun:

It is not dark energy. Dark energy shoves. Dark matter pulls. They share a marketing department and nothing else.

Keep the divorce or the rest of this part will lie to you. The extra pull clumps. It makes halos. The extra shove does none of those things. Chapter 18 is the other spouse. Do not let a shared adjective fuse two ingredients.

It is not a black hole in the everyday sense, though a zoo of old, small black holes is a cold candidate people still kick. Ordinary black holes made from dead stars are not numerous enough, and we have looked.

Microlensing surveys have walked the Magellanic Clouds looking for the wink of a compact object passing in front of a star. They did not find a halo made of dead stars. A halo made of primordial pebbles of collapsed spacetime is still allowed by some papers and disliked by others. It is not the working fluid.

It is not the failure of our eyes. We have looked in radio, infrared, X-ray. The missing weight does not glow in those either, not as atoms would.

Atoms, given a chance, find a wavelength. The extra pull has had thirteen billion years of chances and has not found one we can tune a dish to. Hidden steam would have shown up as heat, as lines, as a fog in some band.

It is not a mood in the equations. The leftover glow’s freckles have a pattern that, when grown by gravity, matches a universe in which most of the pulling-stuff does not collide with light. Atoms slam into photons in the early plasma and leave a different signature. The extra pull did not slam. It is *non-baryonic* in the jargon: not the protons-and-neutrons club. Appendix A17 says that more carefully. Here: it is a different ingredient, not hidden steam.

The leftover helium of Chapter 10 already counted the baryons. There are not enough of them. You cannot hide the extra weight in more hydrogen that forgot to shine. The nuclear minutes already took inventory. The leftover glow’s odd and even acoustic peaks take the same inventory a second way. Two fossils, two methods, one minority of atoms. The extra pull is the difference between that minority and the weight the clocks and the arcs demand.

Candidates have names that sound like card games. WIMPs: heavy, shy particles that would leave a rare cough in an underground tank. Axions: wisps from an old fix to nuclear physics, convertible into photons in a magnetic field if you listen with a sufficiently obsessive radio. Sterile neutrinos: a shy cousin of the neutrinos we already know. None of them has been caught in the act. The tanks have gotten very quiet. XENON, a vat of noble liquid under a mountain in Italy, listens for WIMP coughs and hears the Earth’s radioactivity and the Sun’s neutrinos and not much else. LZ, a larger vat in a South Dakota mine, does the same with a stiller ear. The radios have gotten very proud. The sky has not changed its mind about the pull.

WIMPs were the easy hope: weakly interacting massive particles, heavy enough to sit still on galaxy scales, shy enough to pass through a mountain, common enough that a ton of liquid xenon should, over a year, record a few recoils. The year keeps ending without the recoils. Axions were a patch Peccei and Quinn wrote for a problem in the strong force; if they exist at the right mass they can be the halo, and a magnetic cavity can try to turn them into a microwave whisper. The whisper has not arrived. Sterile neutrinos are a shy extra flavor that would not join the usual weak interactions; some recipes make them cold enough to scaffold galaxies, some make them warm enough to erase dwarf galaxies we can still see. None of the three has been caught in the act. That silence is a scientific result, not a failure of nerve. A WIMP at the mass and interaction strength that would have made the tanks cough by now is, to a first approximation, not there. The window has not slammed shut on every mass. It has slammed shut on the masses that were supposed to be easy.

Modified gravity — change the law instead of adding an ingredient — still has clever friends. It has a harder time with clusters, with the leftover glow, and with the Bullet, where two kinds of stuff have been caught in the act of *not staying together*. The working theory is an extra ingredient. The working theory has a hole in it the size of a noun. That is allowed. Chemistry once had a hole named “what is fire.” The hole was not a reason to deny the flame.

MOND, and the relativistic clothes it has tried on, can mimic a flat rotation curve in a lonely disk with a tweak to Newton at low accelerations. A lonely disk is not a colliding pair of clusters. If gravity itself were simply stronger on large scales, the slammed gas and the extra weight would still sit on the same map, because they would be the same map. They do not. Some papers try to dress modified gravity so the offset still happens. The try is allowed. The try has not become the working theory. Barrow would have walked you to the photograph, named the extra ingredient, and then said the noun is still an idea for a laboratory, not a trophy.

How do you weigh something that refuses to shine?

You watch what it does to things that do. Galaxies lean. Clusters lean. The leftover glow rang like a bell, and bells remember what was pulling on them.

Rotation curves are the suburban clocks: stars and gas, timed in their orbits, going too fast. Gravitational lensing is the more theatrical trick. A cluster’s weight bends spacetime, and the bend makes multiple, stretched images of galaxies behind it — arcs, crosses, rings. Count the arcs, measure the stretch, and you have a scale. The scale disagrees with the starlight by a factor of several. The disagreement is the extra pull.

Weak lensing, which does not make a full arc, still shears the background into a statistical lean. Stack enough galaxies and the lean is a map. Strong lensing is the theater. Weak lensing is the survey. Both weigh the trailer by the bridge. Neither needs the crates to glow.

The leftover glow offers a third scale. The freckles are a sound. The sound has harmonics, like a bell. The position and height of those harmonics depend on how much ordinary matter was slamming into light, and how much quiet matter was only pulling. The bell says: a little slamming stuff, a lot of quiet stuff. The numbers line up with the clocks and the arcs. Three instruments, one extra weight.

Before atoms formed, the plasma could ring: photons and baryons slamming, compressing, bouncing, while the extra pull sat in the wells and did not slam. Odd peaks versus even peaks are a baryon census. Peak positions are a total-matter census. A single method can lie. Three methods, of different families, lying in the same direction, is a courtroom. That is why this chapter’s first sentence is hot even though the last noun is missing.

Then there is the Bullet.

The catalog name is 1E 0657-56. In 2006, Clowe and colleagues published the map that made the argument visual even for people who dislike maps.

Two clusters have passed through each other. The gas — atoms, shining in X-rays — slammed, shocked, and lagged in the middle, the way two crowds trying to walk through each other get stuck at the doors. The extra pull, mapped by lensing, did not slam. It went on through, like two ghosts politely declining to notice they had overlapped. In the famous color posters, gas is pink and mass is blue. On a Kindle those posters lie. Figure 17 is one composite: the slammed gas labeled where it piled up, the mass labeled where lensing put it, same scene. The contours do not sit on the gas. That is the photograph of “not atoms.”

If you wanted modified gravity to swallow the extra pull, this is the exhibit it has to explain without an extra ingredient that can leave the gas behind. The gas is most of the shining baryons in the cluster. After the crossing, the gas is in the middle. The lensing mass is not. Collisionless stuff goes on through. Collisional stuff sticks.

![Figure 17. The Bullet Cluster as one composite: hot gas piled in the middle; mass, mapped by lensing, on either side. The pull did not stay with the gas.](Figures/figs/fig17.jpg)

Hot claim: there is more pulling-stuff than shining-stuff, and the extra stuff does not collide the way gas does. Warm claim: it is a particle we have not bagged, or a small zoo of them. Cold claim: we know its name, its mass, and which underground tank will cough first. We do not.

The extra pull is why galaxies are not just pretty. It is the scaffolding they grew on. Without the quiet halos, the twitches of Chapter 14 would have had a harder time becoming spirals before the stretch pulled the scaffolding out of reach. You live in a suburb of a ghost.

The baryons, left to themselves, would have been a thinner foam. The quiet wells were already there, grown from the same freckles, and the gas fell in. The scaffolding is not a purpose. It is a head start. Gravity ran on noise in the dark wells first; the shining disks are late tenants. The ghost is an ingredient, not a destiny, and not a synonym for the shove. It owes you a weight. The weight is on the bridge. The trailer is still locked.

## 18. The Shove

In 1998 two teams of astronomers, racing each other with dying stars, found that the stretch is not sulking. It is speeding up.

Saul Perlmutter’s Supernova Cosmology Project, and the High-Z team of Adam Riess and Brian Schmidt, were doing the same rude job: hunting Type Ia supernovae far enough away that their light had been in transit for billions of years, and asking whether the stretch had been tiring the way gravity said it should. Two teams, two pipelines, one sky. That is how you keep a shared mistake from dressing up as a discovery. In 2011 the three of them shared a Nobel Prize for the answer. The answer was no. The stretch had been getting a second wind.

The dying stars were Type Ia supernovae, a kind of standard bomb: a white dwarf that reaches a bad weight and detonates with a brightness you can almost trust. Trust the brightness, measure how faint it looks, and you have distance. Measure the stretch of its light and you have the universe’s size when it exploded. Plot size against time for bombs going off over the last several billion years and you expected, if you were educated before 1998, a slowdown. Gravity pulls. Stretching should tire.

The bombs were too faint. They were farther than the tired-stretch story allowed. The stretch had *accelerated*. Something was shoving.

Acceleration, in the kitchen, is not a galaxy putting its foot down. A raisin does not gun an engine. The dough between raisins changes its mind about how fast to rise. The plot of distance against stretch, once it is corrected for the way candles look faint, tips the wrong way for a tired universe. Gravity is still pulling inside the raisins. Between them, the budget has a term that does not thin, and that term has started to win.

A Type Ia is not a random firework. It is a white dwarf — a dead star’s leftover ember, about Earth’s size and the Sun’s mass — stealing matter from a companion, or merging with another dwarf, until it crosses a mass where electron degeneracy can no longer hold it. The explosion is nuclear and brief and, after you calibrate the way the light fades, nearly the same candle each time. Nearly is doing a lot of work. The teams spent years arguing about dust, about evolution, about whether a candle at redshift one is still the candle you calibrated nearby. The argument did not eat the result. Later candles, better calibrated, still sit too far away. That is how a hot claim is born: not from a press conference, from a result that survives the people who wanted it to be dust.

The simplest shove is a cosmological constant: an energy that comes with empty space itself, a tension Einstein once put in to freeze the universe and then regretted. The vacuum, in quantum field theory, is allowed to have a cost. The cost it *should* have, if you naively add up the jitters of every field down to the Planck scale, is off by a number so large it has become a joke: 10¹²⁰, give or take a few zeros. The measured shove is tiny. The predicted vacuum is a monster. This is the worst prediction in physics if you are in the mood to be rude, and one of the deepest problems if you are not.

Einstein wanted a static loaf. The constant was a fudge to hold the raisins still. When Hubble’s stretch showed the raisins were not still, he called the fudge his blunder. The sky, seventy years later, hired the blunder back — not to freeze the loaf, but to speed the later rise. Barrow liked numbers that make a kitchen feel small. This one makes the kitchen feel like a rounding error that somehow landed on a habitable decimal. If the vacuum energy were even a few orders of magnitude larger, galaxies would not have had time to assemble before the shove tore the wells apart. Weinberg said that out loud in 1987, as an anthropic upper bound, before the bombs had voted. The bombs voted near the bound. That is either a clue about an ensemble of vacuums — Chapter 15’s rumor — or a coincidence we have not earned the right to spend. Warm either way. Not a photograph.

Dark energy is the placeholder name for the shove. It might be Einstein’s constant. It might be a field that changes slowly, a “quintessence,” which is a word that sounds like a perfume and behaves like a maybe. It might be a mistake in how we read distances. The last option has been losing for twenty-five years. The leftover glow, the baryon acoustic oscillations — a leftover sound-scale printed in the way galaxies cluster — and the bombs now tell the same story. About two-thirds of the budget is the shove.

Quintessence would have an equation of state *w* that can wander a little. Einstein’s constant sits at *w = −1* and does not wander. The data, as of the kitchen date of this book, sit at *w ≈ −1*. That is a measured shrug in the direction of the constant, not a proof that the perfume is empty. A *w* that later turns around and becomes a crunch is still a cold loophole. It is not the working fit. The working fit is a shove that looks, to the present instruments, like empty space with a tiny, stubborn cost.

The sound-scale is worth a kitchen minute. In the plasma of Chapter 9, baryons and photons rang together. When the fog lifted, the ringing froze. The frozen wavelength is a ruler about 150 megaparsecs long, comoving — a stick you can still lay across the clustering of galaxies today. That is BAO: baryon acoustic oscillations. It is not a bomb. It is a leftover chime. Lay the ruler at different redshifts and you measure how the stretch has been behaving, *H(z)*, the Hubble rate as a function of how much the universe has grown. Appendix A18 writes the Friedmann line for the case *w = −1*:

*H²(z)/H₀² = Ω_m (1+z)³ + Ω_r (1+z)⁴ + Ω_Λ + Ω_k (1+z)²*

Matter thins as the cube of the stretch. Radiation thins as the fourth power. A cosmological constant does not thin. That is the whole plot twist in one line: at late times the thing that does not thin takes over, and the stretch accelerates. Acceleration, in the data, begins around redshift 0.6 — a few billion years ago. The last part of the bake is the fast part.

It does not clump. It does not make halos. It does not sit in the Bullet. It is not dark matter in a hat. If you take nothing else from this chapter, take the divorce: pull and shove are not a couple.

The extra pull is scaffolding. The extra shove is a later wind between the scaffolds. You can live in a suburb of a ghost and still be carried apart from the next suburb by a dough that has started to hurry. The marketing department will keep printing “dark” on both bottles. The kitchen will keep two spoons.

Once the shove is in the budget, the future changes personality.

Without it, you still had a fight between pull and stretch. Enough atoms-and-ghosts and the universe might someday stop expanding and fall back. Too little and it would stretch forever, tiring, thinning, cooling. The knife-edge of Chapter 12 was, in part, a knife-edge of fate.

With the shove, the fight is already over, unless the shove itself is a liar that will someday switch sides. The stretch accelerates. Isolated lumps — galaxies, bound clusters — remain bound. Between them, space wins and keeps winning. The cosmic web freezes. New filaments stop forming. The voids get ruder.

A second-digit fight has been making the newspapers, and it should not be allowed to steal the first digit. Local candles and nearby geometry — Cepheids, nearby Type Ia, a geometric trick with water masers — want a Hubble constant a little larger than the leftover glow prefers, once the glow is grown forward with the working model. Planck’s sky, read through the shove and the extra pull, likes a slope near 67 kilometers per second per megaparsec. Some local ladders like 73. That is the Hubble tension. It is a real argument among careful people. It is an argument about the slope *now*, about whether we have misread a candle or missed a late-time wrinkle. It is not an argument that the stretch is not accelerating. Both camps still need a shove. The bombs are still too faint. The 150-megaparsec ruler still says the late bake sped up. Do not let a fight about the second digit become a rumor that 1998 did not happen.

![Figure 18. The Crab Nebula, a supernova remnant with a bright rim. Explosions of a related kind, seen much farther off, taught us the stretch is speeding up.](Figures/figs/fig18.jpg)

Appendix A18 writes *H(z)* and the distance-redshift relation. Here is the kitchen version. Imagine a raisin bread that not only rises but starts rising faster in the last part of the bake. The raisins that are already touching stay a family. The raisins that were only neighbors become, in time, rumors. The early bake was the pull’s era: matter thinning, stretch tiring. The last bake is the shove’s. You can feel that as a change in the dough without ever naming a field. *H(z)* is only the baker’s clock for how fast the pan is growing at each stage.

That is bound versus unbound in a loaf whose yeast has found a second wind. You, in a galaxy, are inside a raisin. The next cluster over may still be family. The next supercluster is already practicing being a rumor. The kitchen mistake is to think the raisins themselves are swelling. Bound things hold. The dough between the unbound is what hurries.

This is why a required Omega Point — a final crunch in which life steers the universe and computes forever — fails as physics, not as poetry. Poetry can crunch whatever it wants. The bombs, the leftover glow, and the frozen sound-scale say we are not on a crunch track. The next pages put shoes on that sentence.

A detail for later travel: the accelerating stretch makes a *cosmic event horizon*. There are galaxies whose light, emitted today, will never reach us, and there are galaxies we now see whose future light never will. Most of the universe has already left, not by walking away, but by the dough rising too well. Chapter 26 is the map. Chapter 18 is why the map is not optional.

A horizon of this kind is not a black hole. It is a fact about which events can still be connected by light in a loaf that keeps hurrying. The kitchen clock cannot hold it. The kitchen clock never could. If the shove stays near *w ≈ −1*, the map gets ruder with time: more of the web leaves the set of events that can still answer a signal. That is not despair. It is a reason to keep the word *now* local, and a reason Chapter 26 has a job.

Hot claim: the expansion is accelerating. Warm claim: a cosmological constant, or something that looks like one, is the shove. Cold claim: we know why the shove is the size it is. We do not. The worst prediction in physics is still on the board.

## 19. Why Forever-Mind Does Not Follow

Frank Tipler, working from a book he wrote with Barrow, described a final state in which the universe recollapses, intelligent information-processing takes the controls, the amount of computation diverges, and this last, infinitely clever moment is — he was not shy — God. He called it the Omega Point. He said the known laws require it. He said life must never die out.

The book was *The Anthropic Cosmological Principle*, 1986, a thick, serious, sometimes dazzling inventory of how the constants look arranged, followed by a last act that stopped being an inventory. Barrow’s name is on the cover. Barrow’s later habit, in the short origin book, was the one this chapter will keep: walk you up to a speculation and then call it an idea for ideas. Tipler kept walking. He later wrote a thicker popularization in which the last computation wore the noun more openly. The Omega Point is what you get if you let an idea for ideas put on a lab coat and then a mitre.

The known laws do not require it. The data have been voting no since 1998.

A crunch needs the pull to win. The shove is winning. For the Omega Point to be a destiny rather than a story, the shove would have to fade, reverse, or be a mirage. None of those is the working theory. A story can still imagine a different bubble, a different branch, a later surprise in the vacuum. A destiny cannot hide in a surprise. Destiny is supposed to be what the laws demand. The laws, plus the bombs — the Type Ia supernovae of Chapter 18, not weapons — demand a long, thinning, accelerating future, not a steered collapse.

Here is the kitchen version, and it is ruder than a diagram. Imagine a will that names destinies the bank account cannot pay. The will is eloquent. It leaves the house to a dynasty, the orchard to a school, the future to a mind that never sleeps. Then you open the ledger. The shove is two-thirds of the budget. Recollapse is not in the data. The will is still a document. The bank is still empty of that particular future. You do not get to call the will a law of physics because the prose was confident.

Perlmutter, Riess, and Schmidt did not set out to probate a metaphysical estate. They set out to weigh the stretch. The estate failed anyway. A required forever-mind needed the loaf to turn over. The loaf tipped up. Twenty-five years of leftover glow, of the 150-megaparsec ruler, of later candles, have not put recollapse back on the working track. Warm loopholes remain — a *w* that turns around, a vacuum that decays, a different bubble. Spend those as destinies and you have left the data for a story. Chapter 18 already said the story is allowed. Destiny is not the word for a loophole.

Barrow himself, in the short origin book this one uses as a formal model, had a better habit: he would walk you up to a speculation and then call it an idea for ideas. The Final Anthropic Principle — intelligent processing must arise and must never die out — is an idea for ideas that put on a lab coat. Martin Gardner made a joke of the acronym. The joke was unkind. The data have been unkinder.

In the 1986 book the Final Anthropic Principle is stated as a requirement, not a hope: observers come into existence, and once they do, they persist. That is FAP. Gardner, a working skeptic with a magazine column and no patience for cosmic last acts dressed as theorems, suggested that a principle which *demands* eternal observers deserved a cruder expansion of the letters. He did not need the 1998 bombs. He needed only the difference between a condition we observe from — we are here, therefore the universe is the kind of place that can have a here — and a commandment that we must remain. The first is the weak anthropic cut, a likelihood filter, useful once you have an ensemble. The second is a wish with a Latin name. Chapter 41 will give the filter its honest job. Here the job is simpler: FAP is not selected by the leftover glow, the leftover helium, the extra pull, or the extra shove. It is selected by a desire that the story not end without a speaker.

![Figure 19. Three curves in three line styles: recollapse, coast, accelerate. The one marked data is the accelerating curve.](Figures/figs/fig19.png)

Look at the figure until it is boring. Recollapse is a curve that turns over. Coast is a curve that tires. Accelerate is a curve that tips up. The data sit on the one that tips up. Appendix A19 writes the same sentence with density parameters: observed matter about 0.3, observed shove about 0.7, deceleration parameter negative. Closed, matter-only recollapse needed a universe heavier than the critical density and no lasting shove. We are not that universe. The working fit is the accelerating curve.

Barrow would have made you sit with the figure before he let you talk about God. Three line styles, one of them marked data. The temptation is to treat the other two as equally live options, the way a menu treats three dinners. They are not equally live. Recollapse was the dinner the 1986 last act needed. The kitchen is not serving it. An idea for ideas may still draw the turning-over curve. A destiny may not.

Chaos, from Chapter 16, is the second objection, even in a universe that *did* crunch. Near a final singularity — the same wall Chapter 16 named and Chapter 24 properly explains — the mixmaster scrambles. Steering requires control. Control requires that nearby futures stay nearby. They do not. Infinite computation in a dying crunch is a wish that the geometry be a computer. Geometry is not that polite.

This is the objection that would survive a miracle. Suppose, against the bombs, the shove later faded and the loaf turned over. Suppose a civilization of perfect machines was ready with a plan. The plan still has to steer a geometry that, near the end, swaps its axes the way a chaotic kitchen fire swaps which pan is hottest. You cannot cook a required last thought on a stove that will not hold a setting. FAP said life must never die out. BKL says that even a dying crunch is not a stove you can borrow. The first objection is the data. The second is the shape of Einstein’s ending, crunch or no crunch.

Belinski, Khalatnikov, and Lifshitz — BKL, in the jargon — showed that a generic spacelike crunch in Einstein’s theory does not approach a polite point. It approaches a sequence of Kasner eras: squash in two directions, stretch in one, then swap, then swap again, the axes changing in a way that is chaotic. Barrow proved things in that vicinity about how little control you get. Say that again without the names, because the names can sound like a priestly veto. If the loaf recollapses, the last slice is not a quiet room in which a civilization of perfect machines can store more and more bits in less and less space. The last slice is a scramble. Tiny differences become different universes of shape. A steered crunch to a single, infinitely processed point is a measure-zero demand unless you add extra structure by hand. Extra structure by hand is not “the known laws require it.” Extra structure by hand is a story.

Tipler needed a recollapse, a cosmology that could be forced toward a point, a way to dump unlimited energy into unlimited computation, and a reading of God as that last computation. Each of those is a cold brick. Stacked, they are a chapel. This book will not bless the chapel. It will also not sneer at the hunger that built it. People want the story to have a speaker at the end. The leftover glow does not hire speakers. The Bekenstein count of Chapter 23 will later make the bit-budget ruder still: shelf space likes area, not a vanishing volume asked to hold an infinity. The first objection does not need that count. The first objection is the shove.

None of this says mind is small. It says mind is not a clause in the gravitational field equation. If we want mind to last — in machines that do not sleep, in libraries of Earth sent to dark seas under ice, in a greenhouse on a dead world — that is a project. Projects can fail. Destinies, if they are real, cannot. The distinction is the moral of this book’s second half. Meaning, if you want it, is local, expensive, and optional. The leftover helium does not care. The leftover glow does not care. The shove does not care. That is why a greenhouse is interesting. It is a care that the universe did not budget for.

The greenhouse is a thought with dirt on it: someone, somewhere, keeping a volume of air at a temperature a plant can use, on a world the shove and the pull did not arrange for gardening. The hours are still hours. The failure modes are still failure modes. The universe did not set aside a line item. The line item, if it exists, is us, locally, for a while. That is not a blessing of forever-mind. It is the opposite. Forever-mind was the claim that the field equation would staff the garden. The field equation staffs a stretch. The garden, if it happens, is overtime.

Meaning, in this book, is that overtime. It is not a clause that appears when the scale factor is large. It is a woman checking a valve, a library that can fail in a fire, a melt-probe that can miss the sea. The leftover helium will still be a quarter by mass if the valve sticks. The shove will still be two-thirds of the budget if the library burns. That is not a scolding. It is why the care is interesting. Care that the cosmos already budgeted for would be weather. Care it did not budget for is a project.

A will that names a forever-mind is the same document as FAP in a more theatrical font. The bank account is the energy budget and the stretch. The bank will pay galaxies that stay bound, stars that finish their fuel, holes that leak on clocks we will not live to read, a leftover glow that thins, a web that freezes. It will not pay a required last computation. You may still write libraries. You may still staff a greenhouse. You may still send a melt-probe into a dark sea. Those are checks you write on a local account. They clear, or they bounce, in kitchens. They do not clear because the cosmos owes you a dynasty.

Hot claim: the working cosmology is not a recollapse. The bombs, the leftover glow, and the frozen ruler say the stretch is accelerating. Warm claim: the shove will keep behaving like a cosmological constant, *w ≈ −1*, long enough that the web freezes and most worldlines never meet again. Cold claim: mind is scheduled to take the controls and compute without end. That claim is not selected by the data. It is a will the ledger cannot honor.

Forever-mind does not follow. What follows, if you want a moral from a budget, is smaller and ruder and better: meaning is a local project, or it is not a project at all. The universe did not budget for it. That is the permission. It is also the bill.
