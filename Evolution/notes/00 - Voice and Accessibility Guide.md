# Voice and Accessibility Guide — "Life: Evolution"

_Written 2026-09-25, per Lothar's instruction to make the book gentle for a total beginner, applied from now on to every chapter._

This book assumes the reader has never taken a biology class and may be nervous about "science books." Every chapter must be readable by that person start to finish, without ever making them feel stupid or lost. This is a standing rule, not a one-time pass: apply it to every chapter you touch, including the ten that existed before this rule was written.

## The core test

Before finishing any section, ask: **could someone who barely remembers high school biology, or never took it, follow this without rereading?** If a sentence needs a second read, cut it in half. If a term needs a dictionary, define it on the spot, in the same breath, in words a curious twelve-year-old would already have.

## Concrete rules

1. **Define every technical term the instant it appears, in the same sentence, using an everyday comparison.** Not "a protocell is a much simpler compartment" (still abstract) but "a protocell is basically a soap bubble that happens to hold some useful chemistry." Never assume a word survives from a science class the reader may not have taken.
2. **One idea per sentence. Average sentence length in the 9-15 word range.** Long sentences are the single biggest way a book quietly loses a nervous reader. Break any sentence over about 22 words into two or three.
3. **Never assume the reader remembers an earlier chapter's term without a one-line reminder.** Instead of "LUCA's fingerprint" cold, write "LUCA (Chapter 4's name for the ancestor all life shares) left a fingerprint." The reminder costs one clause and saves the reader a flip-back.
4. **Translate every big number into something felt, not just stated.** "3.5 billion years" means nothing on its own. "If Earth's whole history were one calendar year, humans show up in the last few minutes of December 31st" means something. Do this for every large number, tiny probability, or vast timescale.
5. **Name-check people gently.** Anyone named (Darwin, Mendel, Dawkins, a living scientist) gets a four-to-eight-word tag the first time, in the same sentence: "the monk who counted pea plants," "the biologist who coined the word meme." Never assume the name alone means something.
6. **Keep the humor, but make sure every joke lands without outside knowledge.** A joke that only works if you already know who Minsky was is not gentle. Either give the setup inline or pick a joke that needs nothing but what the chapter just explained.
7. **Cross-references stay light and are never load-bearing.** At most a couple of "Chapter N" mentions per chapter, and each one must work as a pure bonus — a reader skipping straight to this chapter should never feel they missed something required.
8. **Prefer questions and second person where they help a beginner feel guided, not lectured.** "Why would evolution bother with that?" invites the reader in; "It is worth noting that selection favors this trait" pushes them out. Use "you" occasionally, especially to plant a concrete image ("Picture holding a single grain of sand...").
9. **Do not simplify the science into something wrong.** Gentle means clearer, not dumber. Keep every hedge the facts require ("scientists still argue about this"); just say the hedge in plain words instead of academic ones.
10. **Trim throat-clearing.** Delete "it is worth noting," "genuinely," "considerably," "in other words," "this chapter will examine." They read as padding to a confident reader and as extra friction to a nervous one.

## What does NOT change

- The book's voice: short declarative lines, dry understated wit, one sentence per line in the manuscript's paragraph convention.
- The facts, structure, anchors, and closing "Punchline" section of each chapter.
- The chapter's title, number, and section headings (unless a heading itself is jargon that needs softening).
- Length stays roughly the same, 1,700-2,400 body words.

## How to apply this to a chapter

Read the chapter's current text. Rewrite it sentence by sentence against the ten rules above, not by adding a beginner-friendly intro paragraph and leaving the rest untouched. The whole chapter should read gently, not just the opening.
