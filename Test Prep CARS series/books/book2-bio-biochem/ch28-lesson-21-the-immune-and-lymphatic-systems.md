# Lesson 21: The Immune and Lymphatic Systems

**What you will be able to do**

- Compare innate and adaptive immunity, and name the cells, organs and molecules that belong to each.
- Explain how antigens are presented on MHC class I and class II molecules, and predict which T cell responds.
- Describe the structure of an antibody, the five classes and their jobs, and the logic of clonal selection and immunologic memory.
- Explain why vaccines work, and distinguish active from passive immunity.
- Describe the complement system and the steps of acute inflammation.
- Classify autoimmune disease, immunodeficiency and the four types of hypersensitivity, and apply ABO and Rh blood group logic.

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson covers category 3B, the structure and function of the major organ systems. Immunology is a favorite source of passages because it combines molecular recognition (the protein structure of [Lesson 1](chapter:4)), gene rearrangement and gene regulation ([Lesson 8](chapter:12)), cell signaling ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), infection ([Lesson 14](chapter:19)) and genetics ([Lesson 10](chapter:14)). Passages often describe an experiment in which a cell type is removed, an antibody is blocked or a mouse is immunized twice, and then ask what you would expect to see. The lymphatic system also returns fluid to the blood, which links to [Lesson 17](chapter:23).

Praxis Tip: For any immunology passage, ask four questions in order: is the response innate (fast, general) or adaptive (slower, specific, remembered), which cell recognized the threat, what did that cell present or secrete, and what is the outcome (kill, neutralize, tag, remember)?

**Concept Review**

**Innate and adaptive immunity.** The immune system has two cooperating arms.

| Feature | Innate | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Minutes to hours | Days for a first exposure |
| Specificity | Broad patterns | A single antigen |
| Memory | Generally none | Yes |
| Main cells | Phagocytes, NK cells | B and T lymphocytes |

Innate defenses start with barriers: skin, mucus, cilia, acidic stomach, lysozyme in tears and saliva, and the normal microbiota that compete with invaders. Cells that pass the barrier meet **phagocytes**, mainly **neutrophils** (the most abundant white cell, short-lived, first to arrive) and **macrophages** (long-lived tissue cells derived from monocytes). Phagocytes recognize **pathogen-associated molecular patterns**, such as bacterial cell wall components and viral RNA, with **pattern recognition receptors** including the Toll-like receptors. Binding triggers phagocytosis and the release of **cytokines**, which are small signaling proteins. **Dendritic cells** are phagocytes whose main job is to carry antigen to lymph nodes and start the adaptive response. **Natural killer (NK) cells** kill virus-infected and tumor cells without prior exposure. **Eosinophils** attack parasites, and **basophils** and tissue **mast cells** release histamine. Virus-infected cells also secrete **interferons**, which prompt neighboring cells to make proteins that interfere with viral replication.

Adaptive immunity has four hallmark features: **specificity** (each lymphocyte carries one receptor type), **diversity** (an enormous repertoire of receptors), **memory** and **self-tolerance** (generally not attacking the body's own molecules). It is carried out by **lymphocytes**: B cells (humoral immunity, via antibodies) and T cells (cell-mediated immunity).

Praxis Tip: Innate means "same response every time, within hours"; adaptive means "tailored response, remembered, better the second time". If a passage shows a faster and larger response on the second exposure, it is adaptive.

**Cells and organs.** All blood cells derive from hematopoietic stem cells in the **bone marrow**. The **myeloid** lineage gives neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, monocytes (which become macrophages), dendritic cells, red cells and platelets. The **lymphoid** lineage gives B cells, T cells and NK cells.

| Organ | Role |
|---|---|
| Bone marrow | Makes blood cells; B cells mature here |
| Thymus | T cells mature here; shrinks with age |
| Lymph nodes | Filter lymph; where antigen meets lymphocytes |
| Spleen | Filters blood; removes old red cells |

B is for bone marrow and T is for thymus. **Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT)**, including the tonsils and Peyer's patches of the small intestine, guards the surfaces that meet the outside world. The **lymphatic system** is a one-way drainage network. Fluid that leaks from capillaries and is not reabsorbed ([Lesson 17](chapter:23)) enters lymphatic capillaries as **lymph**, passes through lymph nodes, and returns to the blood near the heart through the large veins at the base of the neck. Lymphatics also absorb dietary fat as chylomicrons from the intestine ([Lesson 19](chapter:26)). Blocked lymph flow causes edema, and lymph nodes swell during infection because lymphocytes multiply inside them.

**Antigens.** An **antigen** is any molecule that can be recognized by a lymphocyte receptor or an antibody. The specific small part recognized is the **epitope**, and one antigen usually has several. A **hapten** is a small molecule that provokes a response only when attached to a larger carrier protein (the basis of some drug allergies). Immune cells are shaped so that they generally ignore self antigens: **central tolerance** means that developing lymphocytes that strongly react to self are eliminated or inactivated in the thymus or bone marrow (negative selection), and **regulatory T cells** suppress self-reactive cells that escape.

**MHC molecules and antigen presentation.** T cells cannot see free antigen. Their receptor (the **T cell receptor, TCR**) recognizes a short peptide held in the groove of a **major histocompatibility complex (MHC)** molecule on another cell. MHC genes are among the most variable in the population, which is why transplants are rejected unless tissue types are matched. In humans, MHC proteins are also called HLA.

| Property | MHC class I | MHC class II |
|---|---|---|
| Found on | Nearly all nucleated cells | Antigen-presenting cells |
| Peptides from | Inside the cell (viral, tumor) | Taken up from outside |
| Recognized by | CD8 T cells | CD4 T cells |

**Antigen-presenting cells (APCs)** are dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells. A virus-infected cell makes viral proteins in the cytosol, cuts some into peptides, and displays them on MHC I. A **CD8 cytotoxic T cell** whose receptor fits that complex kills the infected cell. An APC that has engulfed a bacterium digests it in a vesicle and displays the peptides on MHC II, and a **CD4 helper T cell** with a matching receptor is activated. The coreceptor **CD8** binds MHC I and **CD4** binds MHC II, which explains the pairing. A cell that has lost its MHC I (some viruses and tumors do this to hide) is attacked by NK cells: the "missing self" rule, because MHC I normally delivers an inhibitory signal to NK cells.

Praxis Tip: Use the rule of eight: MHC I times CD8 (1 x 8) and MHC II times CD4 (2 x 4) both make eight. Class I shows the inside of the cell to a killer; class II shows what was swallowed to a helper.

**T cells.** T cell precursors travel from the bone marrow to the thymus. There, **positive selection** retains cells whose receptor can bind the body's own MHC at all, and **negative selection** removes cells that bind self peptide too strongly. Most developing T cells die in the thymus. Mature T cell types:

- **Helper T cells (CD4)** secrete cytokines that activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells and macrophages. They are the coordinators of the adaptive response, which is why their loss in HIV infection is so damaging.
- **Cytotoxic T cells (CD8)** kill infected and abnormal cells by releasing **perforin**, which forms pores in the target membrane, and **granzymes**, proteases that trigger apoptosis ([Lesson 13](chapter:18)).
- **Regulatory T cells** dampen responses and maintain tolerance.

Full activation of a naive T cell needs two signals: the TCR binding a peptide-MHC complex, and a costimulatory signal from the APC. Receiving signal one without signal two tends to make the cell unresponsive (anergy), which is a safeguard against self-reactivity.

**B cells and antibodies.** Each B cell displays membrane-bound antibody, the **B cell receptor (BCR)**, which binds a native antigen directly (no MHC is needed). For most protein antigens, full activation also needs help from a CD4 T cell that recognizes the same antigen presented by the B cell on MHC II. The activated B cell divides, and many daughters become **plasma cells**, antibody factories with abundant rough ER ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)); others become **memory B cells**.

An antibody (immunoglobulin, Ig) is a Y-shaped protein built from **two identical heavy chains** and **two identical light chains**, held together by disulfide bonds and noncovalent forces ([Lesson 1](chapter:4) on quaternary structure). Each chain has a **variable region** at its tip and a **constant region**. The variable regions of a heavy and a light chain together form one **antigen-binding site**, so a typical antibody has two sites and is bivalent. The two arms are the **Fab** fragments (antigen-binding) and the stem is the **Fc** region, which determines the class and engages Fc receptors on phagocytes and mast cells and activates complement. Within the variable region, three **hypervariable** loops on each chain make the contact with the epitope.

Diversity comes from several mechanisms. In developing B and T cells, gene segments (V, D and J for the heavy chain) are cut and joined in random combinations, called **V(D)J recombination**, and imprecise joining adds further variation. Each lymphocyte thus ends up with a unique receptor gene ([Lesson 7](chapter:11) on DNA breakage and joining). After activation, B cells undergo **somatic hypermutation** in the variable region and selection for tighter binding (**affinity maturation**), and they can switch the constant region from one class to another (**class switching**) while keeping the same variable region and so the same specificity.

| Class | Form | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| IgM | Pentamer | First antibody made; strong complement activation |
| IgG | Monomer | Most abundant in blood; crosses the placenta |
| IgA | Dimer | Secretions: tears, saliva, milk, gut |
| IgE | Monomer | Allergy; binds mast cells; parasites |

A fifth class, **IgD**, is mainly a B cell surface receptor. The roles of antibodies are **neutralization** (blocking a toxin or the viral binding site), **opsonization** (coating a microbe so that phagocytes bind it through Fc receptors), **agglutination** (cross-linking particles into clumps), and **complement activation**.

Praxis Tip: First, M. Most, G. Mucosa, A. Allergy, E. M arrives first and is a big pentamer; G is the long-lived workhorse and the one a mother passes to her fetus; A is for secretions, including milk; E is for allergy.

**Clonal selection and memory.** The repertoire of receptors exists before any infection. When an antigen enters, only the lymphocytes whose receptor fits it are selected: they are activated, proliferate (**clonal expansion**) and differentiate into effector cells and memory cells. This is **clonal selection**.

The first exposure produces a **primary response**: a lag of about a week or so before antibody appears, a modest rise (mostly IgM first, then IgG) and a decline. A second exposure produces a **secondary response**: a shorter lag, a much higher antibody level, mostly IgG of higher affinity, and persistence for longer. This is **immunologic memory**, carried by long-lived memory B and T cells. Antibody levels are often reported as a **titer**, the highest dilution of serum that still gives a detectable reaction.

**Vaccination and types of immunity.** **Active immunity** means the person makes their own response, either after infection or after vaccination, and it is long-lasting because memory cells form. **Passive immunity** means that antibodies are transferred ready-made: IgG across the placenta, IgA in breast milk, or an injected antibody preparation. It is immediate but temporary, because the person has no memory cells and the antibody is gradually cleared.

A **vaccine** presents antigen without causing the disease, so that clonal selection and memory happen safely. Types include **live attenuated** vaccines (a weakened microbe; a strong response, but generally not suitable for people with weak immunity), **inactivated** (killed) vaccines, **subunit and toxoid** vaccines (purified proteins or an inactivated toxin), and **mRNA** vaccines (an mRNA that causes host cells to make an antigen protein, which is then presented; [Lesson 8](chapter:12)). When a large fraction of a population is immune, an outbreak cannot sustain itself (**herd immunity**). Pathogens that mutate quickly, such as influenza, escape existing memory by **antigenic drift** ([Lesson 14](chapter:19)).

**The complement system.** Complement is a set of about 30 plasma and surface proteins [VERIFY], mostly made in the liver, that circulate as inactive precursors and are activated in a cascade, in the same way that blood clotting is. It can start by three routes: the **classical pathway** (antibody bound to a microbe), the **alternative pathway** (spontaneous activation on microbial surfaces) and the **lectin pathway** (a protein that binds microbial sugars). All routes converge on cleaving the central protein **C3**. The products have three jobs: **opsonization** (the fragment C3b coats the microbe), **inflammation and chemotaxis** (small fragments such as C5a recruit phagocytes and cause mast cells to release histamine) and **lysis** (the late components assemble the **membrane attack complex, MAC**, a pore that causes the microbe to swell and burst). Host cells carry regulatory proteins that block complement on their own surfaces.

**Inflammation.** Acute inflammation is the innate response to injury or infection. Resident macrophages and mast cells detect the problem and release histamine and cytokines, and these cause local **vasodilation** (heat, redness) and increased **capillary permeability** (swelling, as fluid and plasma proteins leak out), while chemical signals attract phagocytes. Neutrophils first roll along, then adhere to, and finally squeeze out of the vessel wall (**diapedesis**) and follow a chemical gradient (**chemotaxis**) to the site. The four classic signs are redness, heat, swelling and pain. Cytokines such as interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor reach the hypothalamus and raise the temperature set point, causing **fever**, which can slow some microbes and speed up immune cell activity ([Lesson 20](chapter:27) on thermoregulation). Glucocorticoids suppress many immune genes ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)), which is why they are used as anti-inflammatory drugs.

**Autoimmunity.** Autoimmune disease results from a failure of tolerance, in which the adaptive response attacks self. Mechanisms include self-reactive lymphocytes that escape selection, and **molecular mimicry**, where a pathogen epitope resembles a self epitope so that the response to the pathogen cross-reacts. Examples: type 1 diabetes (T cells destroy pancreatic beta cells), multiple sclerosis (attack on myelin in the central nervous system), rheumatoid arthritis (joints), systemic lupus erythematosus (antibodies to nuclear components, with immune complexes deposited in many tissues), Graves disease (antibodies that stimulate the TSH receptor) and myasthenia gravis (antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction; [Lesson 15](chapter:21)).

**Immunodeficiency.** A primary immunodeficiency is inherited; for example, severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) involves defects in both B and T cell function, and a thymus that fails to form (DiGeorge syndrome) leads to a lack of T cells. A secondary (acquired) deficiency has an outside cause, such as malnutrition, immunosuppressive drugs, or **HIV**, a retrovirus ([Lesson 14](chapter:19)) that infects CD4 T cells, macrophages and dendritic cells and gradually depletes helper T cells, leaving the person vulnerable to opportunistic infections and some cancers.

**Hypersensitivity.** An exaggerated or inappropriate response to a harmless antigen, or to self, is a hypersensitivity reaction. The classic scheme has four types.

| Type | Effector | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I (immediate) | IgE, mast cells | Hay fever, anaphylaxis |
| II | IgG or IgM on cell surfaces | Transfusion reaction |
| III | Immune complexes | Lupus kidney damage |
| IV (delayed) | T cells | Poison ivy, tuberculin test |

In type I, a first exposure (**sensitization**) produces IgE that binds mast cells. On re-exposure, the allergen cross-links the IgE, and the mast cells release histamine, causing vasodilation, bronchoconstriction and swelling within minutes; in **anaphylaxis** this can drop blood pressure severely, and epinephrine is the emergency treatment because it counteracts these effects. Type II reactions occur when antibody binds a cell-surface antigen, causing destruction by complement or phagocytes. Type III reactions occur when soluble antigen-antibody complexes deposit in tissues and activate complement. Type IV is not antibody-mediated; it takes a day or two to develop because T cells and macrophages must arrive.

**Blood groups.** ABO type is set by carbohydrate antigens on red cells. The **I gene** has alleles **Iᴬ**, **Iᴮ** and **i**; Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant, and both are dominant over i ([Lesson 10](chapter:14)). Genotype Iᴬ– gives type A, Iᴮ– gives type B, IᴬIᴮ gives type AB, and ii gives type O (no A or B antigen). People generally have **antibodies (mostly IgM) against the A or B antigens they lack**, formed after exposure to similar sugars on gut bacteria, without any transfusion. A person with type A has anti-B; type B has anti-A; type AB has neither; type O has both. A transfusion must therefore avoid giving red cells that carry an antigen the recipient has antibody against, or the recipient's antibodies will agglutinate and lyse them (a type II reaction). Type O red cells lack A and B and are the "universal donor" for red cells, and AB recipients are "universal recipients" for red cells. For plasma, the logic reverses.

The **Rh factor** (the D antigen) is a separate protein antigen. People who have it are Rh-positive. Anti-Rh antibodies are not present naturally; an Rh-negative person makes them only after exposure to Rh-positive blood. If an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus, fetal red cells can enter her circulation at delivery and sensitize her. In a later Rh-positive pregnancy, her IgG crosses the placenta and attacks fetal red cells (**hemolytic disease of the newborn**). Prevention uses an injection of anti-Rh antibody (passive immunity) so that she clears fetal cells before making her own response.

Praxis Tip: For a transfusion problem, write the recipient's antibodies and the donor's antigens side by side. A reaction needs an antigen on the donor cells that matches an antibody in the recipient, and nothing else.

**Worked Example 1: Clonal expansion (skill: quantitative reasoning)**

A single naive T cell with a matching receptor is activated, and its progeny divide once every 12 hours for 7 days. Assuming no cell death, how many cells result, and what does this show about the response lag?

Seven days is 168 hours, or 168 / 12 = 14 divisions, giving 2¹⁴ = 16,384 cells. The number grows by doubling, so most of the cells appear in the last few divisions, and the first few days produce few cells. This is why a first response takes about a week to become effective, and why memory cells that already exist in large numbers shorten the lag and speed the second response.

**Worked Example 2: Presentation pathways (skill: reasoning from cell biology)**

A virus infects an epithelial cell. (a) Which MHC class presents the viral peptides, and which T cell responds? (b) A dendritic cell engulfs debris from the dead infected cell. Which MHC class presents the peptides from the debris, and which cell does it activate? (c) The virus blocks the infected cell from displaying MHC I. Which cell may still kill it?

(a) The viral proteins are made inside the cell, so the peptides appear on MHC I and are recognized by a CD8 cytotoxic T cell. (b) The dendritic cell took the material up from outside, so it uses MHC II and activates a CD4 helper T cell (dendritic cells can also cross-present some antigens on MHC I, but the default rule is the one stated). (c) An NK cell, which is triggered by the absence of the inhibitory MHC I signal.

**Worked Example 3: Antibody valency (skill: structure)**

Count the antigen-binding sites and polypeptide chains in (a) an IgG monomer, (b) a secretory IgA dimer, and (c) an IgM pentamer. Use two heavy and two light chains for each monomer unit, and ignore accessory chains.

Each monomer unit has 4 chains and 2 binding sites. (a) IgG: 4 chains, 2 sites. (b) IgA dimer: 2 units, so 8 chains and 4 sites. (c) IgM pentamer: 5 units, so 20 chains and 10 sites. IgM, with its ten sites, is well suited to agglutinate and activate complement, even though each individual site binds with modest affinity early in the response.

**Worked Example 4: Reading a titer (skill: data)**

In a vaccine study, a serum antibody titer was 1:16 before the booster and 1:512 afterward. How many fold, and how many doublings? What does this say about the type of response?

512 / 16 = 32-fold, which is 2⁵, so five doublings of the dilution. A much larger and faster rise after the second exposure is the signature of a secondary (memory) response, with mostly IgG. 

**Worked Example 5: Blood groups and genetics (skill: probability)**

A mother is type A (genotype Iᴬi) and Rh-positive (Dd), and the father is type B (Iᴮi) and Rh-positive (Dd). Rh-positive is dominant (D), and Rh-negative is dd. What is the probability that their child is type O and Rh-negative?

For ABO: Iᴬi × Iᴮi gives IᴬIᴮ, Iᴬi, Iᴮi and ii in equal fractions, so P(type O) = 1/4. For Rh: Dd × Dd gives dd with probability 1/4. The genes are on different chromosomes and assort independently, so P = 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: When a passage removes a cell type, ask what that cell's job was. No thymus: no mature T cells, so poor cell-mediated and antibody help. No B cells: no antibody, so recurrent bacterial infections but a fairly normal response to many viruses.

Praxis Tip: A vaccine trains memory; it is not a treatment for an infection already under way. Contrast with passive antibody, which helps immediately and is gone within weeks to months.

Praxis Tip: Treat the hypersensitivity types as a lookup table, but remember the reasoning: fast and IgE is type I, antibody on a cell is type II, complexes are type III, and slow with T cells is type IV.

Praxis Tip: Neutrophils and macrophages eat, dendritic cells present, NK cells and cytotoxic T cells kill, helper T cells direct, B cells make antibody. If a question mentions only one verb, you probably have the answer.

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying that the antigen "teaches" a lymphocyte what antibody to make, instead of selecting a cell that already has the receptor.
- Mixing up MHC I (all nucleated cells, CD8, internal peptides) and MHC II (APCs, CD4, external peptides).
- Assuming that a second exposure produces an IgM response again; the secondary response is generally dominated by IgG.
- Treating passive immunity as long-lasting, or thinking that it produces memory.
- Applying the "universal donor" idea to plasma as well as red cells without reversing the logic.
- Forgetting that an Rh-negative mother usually causes no harm in her first Rh-positive pregnancy; sensitization happens mainly at delivery.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. A virus replicates in the cytosol of a liver cell. Peptides from a viral protein appear on the cell surface and are recognized by a T cell, which then destroys the liver cell. Which pair correctly identifies the presenting molecule and the T cell?
   A. MHC class II and a CD4 helper T cell
   B. MHC class II and a CD8 cytotoxic T cell
   C. MHC class I and a CD8 cytotoxic T cell
   D. MHC class I and a CD4 helper T cell

2. A newborn is protected against many infections for the first few months by antibodies that came from the mother, but the infant makes no memory cells against those pathogens. Which statement best describes this protection?
   A. Active immunity, because the infant's own B cells made the antibody
   B. Passive immunity, because ready-made antibody is transferred
   C. Innate immunity, because it requires no antibodies
   D. Herd immunity, because the infant is vaccinated

3. A mouse is injected twice with antigen X, several weeks apart. Compared with the response 7 days after the first injection, the response 7 days after the second injection will most likely show
   A. a higher antibody titer, composed mostly of IgG
   B. a lower titer, because memory cells suppress the response
   C. the same titer, mostly of IgM, because each exposure starts from nothing
   D. no antibody, because the antigen is already cleared

4. A person has a genetic defect in the final components of the complement cascade, but the early components and C3 work normally. Which function is most directly lost?
   A. Opsonization of microbes by C3b
   B. Recruitment of phagocytes by C5a
   C. Production of IgE by B cells
   D. Formation of the membrane attack complex that lyses microbes

5. An Rh-negative woman has her first child, who is Rh-positive, and has no complications. During a second pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus, the fetus develops severe hemolysis. Which explanation is best?
   A. She has naturally occurring IgM against the Rh antigen that crosses the placenta
   B. She was sensitized at the first delivery and now has IgG that crosses the placenta
   C. The fetus made antibodies against its own red cells
   D. The father's blood group antigens were transferred to the mother's cells

6. A person with a known allergy to pollen develops hives, wheezing and a fall in blood pressure within minutes of a bee sting. Cross-linking of which molecule on which cell most directly triggered the release of histamine?
   A. IgE bound to mast cells
   B. IgG bound to neutrophils
   C. The T cell receptor on cytotoxic T cells
   D. IgM bound to red blood cells

7. A mouse is engineered so that its thymus does not develop. Compared with a normal mouse, which finding is most likely?
   A. Normal T cell numbers, because T cells mature in the bone marrow
   B. No B cells, because B cells depend on the thymus
   C. A stronger response, because self-tolerance is lost
   D. Few mature T cells, and a weak antibody response to most protein antigens

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** Peptides made inside a cell are displayed on MHC I, and CD8 cytotoxic T cells recognize them and kill the cell with perforin and granzymes. (A MHC II presents peptides from outside material on antigen-presenting cells, and helper cells do not kill targets directly; B CD8 binds MHC I, not MHC II; D CD4 binds MHC II, and helper cells coordinate rather than kill.)
2. **B.** Maternal IgG crosses the placenta, and IgA is delivered in milk. The infant gets immediate but temporary protection without memory. (A the antibody was made by the mother's B cells, not the infant's; C the protection is antibody-based, and innate immunity does not rely on antibody; D herd immunity describes protection of a population when many are immune, and no vaccination is described.)
3. **A.** Memory B cells that remain from the first exposure produce a faster, larger, higher-affinity secondary response dominated by IgG. (B memory cells speed and enlarge the response rather than suppressing it; C existing memory cells make the response faster and larger, and class switching favors IgG; D clearance of antigen does not erase memory cells.)
4. **D.** The late components assemble the pore-like membrane attack complex, so direct lysis is impaired while opsonization and inflammation can continue. (A depends on cleavage of C3, which is normal; B C5a is a small fragment released when C5 is cleaved, which is generally not blocked by loss of the later pore-forming proteins; C complement is not required to make an antibody class.)
5. **B.** Exposure to fetal Rh-positive red cells at the first delivery primes maternal B cells, and in the next pregnancy her IgG enters the fetal circulation and attacks Rh-positive red cells. (A anti-Rh antibodies are not generally present before exposure, and IgM is too large to cross the placenta well; C the damage is caused by maternal antibody, not by the fetus' immune system; D the problem is the mother's response to fetal red cell antigen, not transfer to her own cells.)
6. **A.** Allergen cross-linking of IgE on mast cells causes degranulation and histamine release, which is the mechanism of type I hypersensitivity. (B the immediate allergic reaction is mediated by IgE on mast cells, and neutrophils are not the main source of histamine; C a reaction within minutes does not involve T cell killing; D this describes a type II reaction, and does not release histamine from mast cells.)
7. **D.** T cells mature in the thymus. Without them, there is no CD4 help for B cells, so antibody production to most protein antigens falls, although B cells are present. (A T cells mature in the thymus, not in the bone marrow; B B cells develop in the bone marrow, so they are present; C the loss of T cells weakens, not strengthens, the adaptive response.)
