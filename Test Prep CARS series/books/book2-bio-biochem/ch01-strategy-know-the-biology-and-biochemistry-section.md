# Strategy: Know the Biology and Biochemistry Section

**What you will be able to do**

- State the format, timing and scoring of the Biological and Biochemical Foundations section, and flag each fact for checking against the exam owner's current pages.
- Read the section's content outline as a map, and see where each lesson in this book sits on categories 1A to 3B.
- Turn the time limit into a pacing plan and the published shares into a study budget.
- Explain how this book relates to Book 1 (chemistry and physics) and to the Core Test Skills book, and use an ebook without writing in it.

**Why it matters**

This section is where most of the biology you have met in your life gets examined, in a form that surprises people. The questions are not mostly "name the organelle." They are mostly "here is an experiment, here is a result, what follows?" A student who has memorized every pathway and never practiced reading a figure will feel the section is unfair. A student who has done both will find it merely long. This chapter gives you the map so that you know which one you are training to be.

Praxis Tip: Do this chapter with the exam owner's official pages open beside you. Where this book and the official pages disagree, the official pages win. They are more current than any book, including this one, and they do not charge by the page.

**A note on using this ebook.** You cannot write in or mark an ebook, so keep a notebook (paper or digital) beside you. Use it for answer sheets, confidence ratings, your error log, hand-drawn pathway maps, flashcard ideas and your study plan. Every practice set tells you to write answers on scratch paper and to read the explanations only afterward. The tables in this book appear as pictures, so copy the ones you want into your notes. Drawing is not optional in biology: the strategy chapters that follow ask you to redraw pathways and sketch gels, and a notebook is where that happens.

This book is independent. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by the owner of the exam, and it contains no actual exam questions.

**How the content is organized**

The section rests on three foundational concepts, subdivided into nine content categories. The shares below are approximate and are understood to be as published by the exam owner. [VERIFY the category weightings against the current outline]

*Foundational Concept 1 (about 55 percent of questions [VERIFY]).* Biomolecules have properties that determine how they build cells and carry out the processes that maintain life.

- 1A: structure and function of proteins and their constituent amino acids (including enzymes)
- 1B: transmission of genetic information from the gene to the protein
- 1C: transmission of heritable information from generation to generation, and the processes that increase genetic diversity (including evolution)
- 1D: principles of bioenergetics and fuel molecule metabolism

*Foundational Concept 2 (about 20 percent [VERIFY]).* Highly organized assemblies of molecules, cells and organs interact to carry out the functions of life.

- 2A: assemblies of molecules, cells and groups of cells within single cellular and multicellular organisms
- 2B: the structure, growth, physiology and genetics of prokaryotes and viruses
- 2C: processes of cell division, differentiation and specialization

*Foundational Concept 3 (about 25 percent [VERIFY]).* Complex systems of tissues and organs sense the internal and external environments of multicellular organisms and, through integrated functioning, maintain a stable internal environment.

- 3A: structure and functions of the nervous and endocrine systems and ways in which they coordinate the organ systems
- 3B: structure and integrative functions of the main organ systems

The exam owner also gives an approximate split by discipline. For this section it is understood to be biology about 65 percent, biochemistry about 25 percent, general chemistry about 5 percent and organic chemistry about 5 percent. [VERIFY] Note how different this is from Book 1's section, where biology was a sliver. Here, chemistry is a sliver and biology is the main body.

**Worked Example 1: From shares to question counts**

Convert the discipline split into rough counts for a 59-question section.

| Discipline | Share | Questions | Rounded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biology | 65% | 38.35 | about 38 |
| Biochemistry | 25% | 14.75 | about 15 |
| General chemistry | 5% | 2.95 | about 3 |
| Organic chemistry | 5% | 2.95 | about 3 |

The rounded counts add to 59. Now the foundational concepts: 55 percent is 59 × 0.55 = 32.45, about 32 questions; 20 percent is 11.8, about 12; 25 percent is 14.75, about 15. Those also add to 59.

*Reading the table.* Roughly 32 questions come from Foundational Concept 1, which is molecules, genes and metabolism. That is more than the other two combined. A study plan that spends equal time on every organ system and every pathway is therefore a plan that under-weights the most examined material.

*Skill used:* converting published percentages into a study-time budget.

Praxis Tip: Shares set the budget, but weakness sets the overdraft. Allocate by share first, then add hours to your weakest categories. A table on the wall that says "3A is 10 percent of your section" is a polite way to stop a three-day excursion into one brain region.

**The four reasoning skills**

Every question sits at the intersection of a content category and a skill. The skills, with the approximate shares used in Book 1 [VERIFY for this section]:

| Skill | Share | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Concepts and principles | 35% | Know the idea and its neighbors |
| 2. Reasoning and problem solving | 45% | Apply and predict |
| 3. Research design | 10% | Controls, variables, ethics |
| 4. Data and statistics | 10% | Read figures and tables |

For 59 questions that is about 21, 27, 6 and 6. So about two in three questions ask you to apply, design or interpret, not recall. In biology, skills 3 and 4 show up wrapped inside nearly every experiment passage, and Skill 2 often hides inside a question that looks like pure recall: "if this enzyme were missing, what accumulates?" is a reasoning question about a fact.

Praxis Tip: When you review a miss, tag it with its category (1A to 3B) and its skill (1 to 4). If your misses cluster in Skill 2 within 1D, you do not need more flashcards. You need to practice predicting what happens when a metabolic step is blocked.

**What a passage looks like**

Biology passages usually fall into a few types, and it helps to know them before you meet them.

- *Experiment passages* describe a question, a method and some results, often in a table or figure. These are the most common.
- *Mechanism or system passages* describe a pathway or organ system and ask you to apply it to a new condition.
- *Research-summary passages* describe two or three linked experiments and ask what the next one should be, or what a conclusion rests on.
- *Independent questions* are short. They test whether you know a fact or can use it once.

Praxis Tip: Do not try to memorize the passage. Read for the experimental logic (what was changed, what was measured, what happened) and mark where the specific details live. You can look them up again in four seconds. You cannot recover time spent memorizing them.

**Worked Example 2: A pacing plan**

Use the figures above: 59 questions, 95 minutes. Reserve 5 minutes at the end for flagged questions.

- Working time: 95 minus 5 = 90 minutes.
- Time per question: 90 divided by 59 is about 1.525 minutes, or 1 minute 31 seconds.
- Checkpoints (elapsed minutes at the end of question n): n = 15 gives 22.9; n = 30 gives 45.8; n = 45 gives 68.6; n = 59 gives 90.

Rounded for your scratch paper: 23, 46, 69, 90. Passage sets carry a reading cost before the first question, so a dense experiment passage with five questions uses more than 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 minutes, while an independent question may take 30 seconds. The checkpoints are averages, not quotas. If you are three minutes behind at question 30, you have the reserve and the rule to flag and move on ([Core Test Skills, Chapter 11]).

*Skill used:* converting a published time limit into four clock times you can glance at.

Praxis Tip: Biology has a particular pacing trap: the unfamiliar-topic passage. If a passage describes a protein you have never heard of, you have lost nothing. The passage must tell you what you need. Set a two-minute reading limit and begin the questions.

- Studying from a friend's account of the test instead of the official outline.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** 59 × 0.55 = 32.45, which rounds to 32. (A corresponds to about 41 percent, not 55; C is the biology share (65 percent), not the Foundational Concept 1 share; D is the 25 percent share.)
2. **D.** Shares set the budget and weakness adds to it; interest alone is not a reason. (A ignores the published weightings; B leaves whole categories unprepared; C an account from memory is less reliable than the official outline.)
3. **A.** 90 divided by 59 is about 1.525 minutes. (B would use only 59 of the 90 working minutes; C uses all 95 minutes with no reserve (95/59); D would need 118 minutes.)
4. **C.** Predicting accumulation from a block uses reasoning about the pathway. (A the enzyme is unfamiliar, so name recall cannot be the task; B no data figure is mentioned; D nothing in the stem concerns conduct of research.)
5. **B.** Prokaryotes and viruses are category 2B. (A concerns the flow of genetic information from gene to protein; C concerns cell division and differentiation; D concerns organ systems.)
6. **D.** A notebook supplies the answer sheet, error log and drawing space that the ebook cannot. (A gives no record of errors to review; B removes the retrieval practice that makes questions useful; C tables being images does not remove the need to draw.)
7. **A.** Techniques produce the gels, blots and plots that experiment passages report, so understanding them serves research-design and data reasoning everywhere. (B no such restriction is claimed; C skill 4 is data-based by definition; D Book 1 is not replaced; it is linked.)
