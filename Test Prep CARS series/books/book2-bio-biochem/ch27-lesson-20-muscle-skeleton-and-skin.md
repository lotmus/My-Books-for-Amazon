# Lesson 20: Muscle, Skeleton and Skin

**What you will be able to do**

- Compare skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle in structure, control and calcium handling.
- Describe the sarcomere, the sliding-filament mechanism and the cross-bridge cycle, and say where ATP and calcium act.
- Trace excitation-contraction coupling from the neuromuscular junction to relaxation.
- Explain how muscle makes ATP over different time scales and how fiber types differ.
- Describe bone structure, the cells of bone, calcium regulation and remodeling, and the roles of cartilage, tendon and ligament.
- Describe the layers of the skin and the physical and neural mechanisms of thermoregulation.

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson covers category 3B, organ systems, and combines three themes you have already met. Contraction is a motor protein cycle driven by ATP hydrolysis ([Lesson 12](chapter:17) on motors and the cytoskeleton, [Lesson 5](chapter:8) on ATP), calcium is a second messenger ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), and the neuromuscular junction is a synapse ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). Bones are a calcium reservoir regulated by the hormones of [Lesson 16](chapter:22). Passages often give a toxin, a mutation or an experiment on isolated muscle, and ask which step fails and what the muscle does. Limb mechanics and heat transfer reuse Book 1, Lessons 2 and 14.

Praxis Tip: In muscle questions, find the missing ingredient. Contraction needs a signal (acetylcholine, action potential), calcium, and ATP. Remove any one, and you can predict what happens: no signal, no calcium release; no calcium, no sliding; no ATP, no detachment.

**Concept Review**

**The three muscle types.**

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Striated? | Yes | Yes | No |
| Control | Voluntary (somatic) | Involuntary | Involuntary (autonomic) |
| Nuclei per cell | Many | Usually one | One |
| Calcium sensor | Troponin | Troponin | Calmodulin |

Skeletal muscle fibers are long multinucleate cells formed by the fusion of precursor cells ([Lesson 13](chapter:18) on differentiation). Cardiac cells are branched, joined end to end by **intercalated discs**, which contain desmosomes (strength) and gap junctions (electrical coupling), so that the heart behaves as a coordinated unit ([Lesson 17](chapter:23)). Some cardiac cells are autorhythmic and set the pace. Smooth muscle lines the walls of the gut, vessels, airways, bladder and uterus. It is slow, can sustain a contraction at low energy cost, and in many organs its cells are coupled by gap junctions.

**The sarcomere.** A skeletal muscle fiber contains many **myofibrils**, each a chain of **sarcomeres** joined end to end. The sarcomere is the contractile unit, bounded by **Z lines**. It contains two interdigitating filament systems:

- **Thick filaments**, made of the motor protein **myosin**, which is anchored at the **M line** in the center.
- **Thin filaments**, made of **actin** with the regulatory proteins **tropomyosin** and **troponin**, anchored at the Z lines.

The banding pattern follows from the overlap. The **A band** is the full length of the thick filament (it does not change in length). The **I band** contains only thin filaments, and spans the Z line between two adjacent A bands. The **H zone** is the middle of the A band where there is no overlap with thin filaments. During contraction, the thin filaments slide inward over the thick ones: the sarcomere shortens, and the **I band and H zone shrink**, while the **A band stays the same** and the filaments themselves do not change length. This is the **sliding-filament model**.

Praxis Tip: To remember the bands, think "A stays, I and H vanish". Anything that is the length of a filament stays constant, and anything that is a gap shrinks.

**The cross-bridge cycle.** Myosin heads bind actin and pull, using ATP. A single cycle has four steps:

1. **ATP binds** the myosin head, which releases from actin.
2. **ATP is hydrolyzed** to ADP and Pi, which re-cocks the head into a high-energy position.
3. The head binds actin (a **cross-bridge**) and releases Pi, triggering the **power stroke**, in which the head swivels and pulls the thin filament toward the center of the sarcomere.
4. **ADP is released**, leaving the head bound tightly to actin until a new ATP binds.

The important consequences: ATP is needed for **detachment** (not only for the power stroke). Without ATP, the heads remain locked to actin, which is the basis of **rigor mortis**, when ATP supplies run out after death. Muscle relaxation also needs ATP, partly for the calcium pump that removes calcium.

**Calcium control in skeletal muscle (excitation-contraction coupling).**

1. A motor neuron action potential releases **acetylcholine** at the neuromuscular junction, binding nicotinic receptors on the muscle end plate ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). The receptor is a ligand-gated cation channel, and the resulting depolarization triggers a muscle action potential.
2. The action potential travels along the **sarcolemma** (the muscle cell membrane) and into the cell through the **T tubules**.
3. In the T tubule, a voltage-sensing channel (the dihydropyridine receptor) is coupled to the **ryanodine receptor** in the **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)**, which opens and releases stored Ca²⁺ into the cytosol.
4. Calcium binds **troponin**, which shifts **tropomyosin** off the myosin-binding sites on actin. Cross-bridges form and the muscle shortens.
5. Relaxation: the action potential ends, the channels close, and the SR Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) pumps calcium back into the SR. Calcium falls, tropomyosin covers the sites again, and the muscle relaxes. Acetylcholine is cleared by acetylcholinesterase.

The pattern of control is therefore "tropomyosin blocks, calcium unblocks". A drug that blocks acetylcholinesterase prolongs stimulation of the end plate; a drug that blocks the nicotinic receptor (as in curare) paralyzes the muscle, because no action potential arises, even though the nerve fires normally. Botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release.

**Cardiac and smooth muscle compared.** In **cardiac muscle**, the action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in the membrane, and the entering calcium triggers more release from the SR (calcium-induced calcium release). Cardiac action potentials have a long plateau, which prevents tetanus and makes the heart relax between beats ([Lesson 17](chapter:23)). Cardiac contraction depends on extracellular calcium, and skeletal muscle much less so. In **smooth muscle**, there are no sarcomeres or troponin. Calcium binds **calmodulin**, and the complex activates **myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK)**, which phosphorylates myosin and allows cross-bridge cycling. Relaxation requires dephosphorylation by a phosphatase. Smooth muscle can be controlled by hormones, local chemical factors and stretch as well as nerves: for example, NO relaxes vascular smooth muscle, and epinephrine acting at some receptors relaxes airway smooth muscle ([Lesson 18](chapter:24)).

**Mechanics of contraction.** A single action potential causes a brief **twitch**. If stimuli arrive faster than the muscle can relax, twitches add (**summation**), and at high frequency the muscle reaches a smooth, sustained maximal contraction called **tetanus**, because calcium stays high. A muscle increases force by raising the stimulation frequency and by **recruiting** more motor units (a motor unit is one neuron and the fibers it supplies). Small motor units are recruited first, which allows fine control. There is also a **length-tension relationship**: the force is greatest at an intermediate sarcomere length, where overlap of thick and thin filaments is optimal, and falls if the muscle is stretched so that overlap is poor.

**Energy for muscle.** The ATP stored in muscle lasts only seconds, so it must be regenerated ([Lesson 4](chapter:7), [Lesson 5](chapter:8)).

| Source | Speed | Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| Creatine phosphate | Immediate | A few seconds of effort |
| Anaerobic glycolysis | Fast | Tens of seconds; makes lactate |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | Slower | Long; uses glucose, fatty acids |

**Creatine phosphate** donates its phosphate to ADP (catalyzed by creatine kinase) to restore ATP quickly. Glycolysis supplies ATP faster than oxidative metabolism but yields little per glucose, and when oxygen is limited it ends in lactate, regenerating NAD⁺ (see [Lesson 4](chapter:7)). Lactate travels to the liver, which can convert it back to glucose (the Cori cycle, [Lesson 6](chapter:9)). Muscle stores glycogen, and the muscle enzymes cannot release glucose into the blood, because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase. Muscle also contains **myoglobin**, an oxygen store with a higher affinity than hemoglobin ([Lesson 18](chapter:24)).

Skeletal muscle fibers differ in type. **Slow-twitch (type I)** fibers are rich in mitochondria and myoglobin (red), are oxidative and resist fatigue, and suit posture and endurance. **Fast-twitch (type II)** fibers contract rapidly, rely more on glycolysis, have fewer mitochondria and fatigue sooner, and suit short bursts of power. Training can change the properties of the fibers, to a degree.

Praxis Tip: Match the fuel to the time. Seconds means creatine phosphate, a minute means glycolysis, and minutes to hours means oxidative metabolism. The longer the task, the more the muscle relies on mitochondria.

**Muscles and levers.** Muscles pull across joints, and bones act as levers. With the physics of torque (Book 1, Lesson 2), the biceps force is much larger than the load it holds, because it acts close to the joint. The balance of torques about the elbow gives (F muscle) × (d muscle) = (W load) × (d load). Muscles can only pull, so they work in antagonistic pairs, such as biceps and triceps.

**Bone.** Bone is a living tissue made of cells and a mineralized matrix: roughly one third organic (mostly **type I collagen**, which gives tensile strength) and two thirds mineral (**hydroxyapatite**, a crystalline calcium phosphate, which gives compressive strength) [VERIFY]. The combination is strong and slightly flexible, much as reinforced concrete is. **Compact bone** forms the dense outer shell, built from cylindrical units called **osteons**, each with a central **Haversian canal** carrying vessels and nerves. **Spongy (cancellous) bone** is a lattice of **trabeculae** with marrow in between, found at the ends of long bones. Red marrow makes blood cells (hematopoiesis, [Lesson 17](chapter:23)), and yellow marrow stores fat.

| Cell | Origin / role |
|---|---|
| Osteoblast | Builds bone; secretes collagen and promotes mineralization |
| Osteocyte | Mature, embedded cell; senses mechanical load |
| Osteoclast | Resorbs bone; secretes acid and enzymes |

*Remodeling and calcium.* Bone is constantly remodeled, with osteoclasts resorbing and osteoblasts rebuilding. Remodeling responds to mechanical load (bone strengthens when used and weakens when unloaded) and to hormones. Plasma calcium is tightly regulated, because calcium affects neurons, muscle and clotting ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). When plasma calcium falls, the parathyroid glands release **parathyroid hormone (PTH)**, which stimulates osteoclast activity (releasing calcium from bone, indirectly through osteoblasts), increases calcium reabsorption in the kidney and stimulates formation of active **vitamin D** (calcitriol), which increases intestinal calcium absorption. In humans, **calcitonin** from the thyroid can lower calcium by reducing osteoclast activity, but its normal role is minor [VERIFY]. Low calcium makes nerves and muscle hyperexcitable (tetany), because calcium normally stabilizes sodium channels. Vitamin D deficiency causes poor mineralization (rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults). **Osteoporosis** is loss of bone mass; estrogen restrains osteoclast activity, so the risk rises after menopause ([Lesson 22](chapter:29)).

**Cartilage, tendon, ligament and joints.** **Cartilage** is a flexible tissue of chondrocytes in a matrix rich in collagen and proteoglycans. It has no blood supply, which is why it heals slowly. It forms joint surfaces, the template for many embryonic bones and the structure of the ear and nose. **Tendons** connect muscle to bone, and **ligaments** connect bone to bone; both are dense connective tissue with parallel collagen fibers, and both heal slowly because of their poor blood supply. Collagen requires vitamin C for its synthesis (the hydroxylation of proline, [Lesson 1](chapter:4)), and deficiency causes scurvy. In general, connective tissues consist of cells in an extracellular matrix ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)): collagen provides tensile strength and elastin provides recoil.

**The skin.** The skin is the largest organ. It provides a barrier against water loss and microbes, protects against ultraviolet radiation, senses touch and temperature, and regulates body temperature.

| Layer | Contents | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Epidermis | Keratinocytes, melanocytes, Langerhans cells | Barrier, pigment, immune surveillance |
| Dermis | Collagen, elastin, vessels, nerves, glands, follicles | Strength, blood supply, sensation |
| Hypodermis | Fat and loose connective tissue | Insulation, energy store, cushion |

The **epidermis** is a stratified epithelium with no blood vessels. Cells divide in the deepest layer, mature as they move up, fill with keratin and die; the outermost layer, the **stratum corneum**, is a waterproof layer of dead cells that is shed continuously. **Melanocytes** make the pigment melanin, which absorbs ultraviolet light and shields the DNA of keratinocytes ([Lesson 7](chapter:11) on DNA damage and repair). Skin also begins the synthesis of vitamin D when exposed to UV light. The **dermis** contains sweat glands, sebaceous glands, hair follicles, sensory receptors and the blood vessels that regulate heat. The skin is also the first barrier of the immune system ([Lesson 21](chapter:28)).

**Thermoregulation.** Humans are endotherms that keep a core temperature near 37 °C. Heat is produced by metabolism ([Lesson 5](chapter:8)) and lost by radiation, conduction, convection and evaporation (Book 1, Lesson 14). The control center is the **hypothalamus**, which compares input from temperature sensors to a set point, and uses negative feedback ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)).

| Condition | Responses |
|---|---|
| Too warm | Skin vasodilation, sweating, behavior |
| Too cold | Skin vasoconstriction, shivering, piloerection, more thyroid hormone |

When overheated, **vasodilation** of vessels in the dermis increases blood flow to the skin, which brings heat to the surface, and **sweating** cools by evaporation, because water absorbs a large amount of heat as it vaporizes. When cold, **vasoconstriction** reduces heat loss, **shivering** (rapid involuntary skeletal muscle contraction) makes heat from ATP hydrolysis and the inefficiency of metabolism, and hormones such as thyroid hormone and epinephrine raise the metabolic rate. Infants have **brown fat**, whose mitochondria use an uncoupling protein to release the proton gradient energy as heat instead of ATP ([Lesson 5](chapter:8)). In a **fever**, pyrogens from immune cells reset the hypothalamic set point upward, so the body acts cold at 37 °C and shivers until it reaches the new set point. Evaporation does not work well in humid air, so high humidity makes heat stress more dangerous.

Praxis Tip: Sweat cools only if it evaporates. A question about humidity, a wet cloth or a fan is a heat of vaporization question in disguise.

**Worked Example 1: Sarcomere bands (skill: sliding filament model)**

A resting sarcomere is 2.4 μm long, and the thick filaments are 1.6 μm long. During a contraction the sarcomere shortens to 1.9 μm. What is the A band length and the I band length in each state?

The A band equals the thick filament length and is 1.6 μm in both states. The I band is the remaining length of the sarcomere, which is the length of the sarcomere minus the A band: 2.4 − 1.6 = 0.8 μm at rest, and 1.9 − 1.6 = 0.3 μm in the contracted state. The I band shrank by 0.5 μm while the A band was constant, which is the signature of filaments sliding and not shortening. (In this simplified model, the I band stretches across a full Z line, so it is the sum of the two half-bands on either side.)

**Worked Example 2: A muscle and a lever (skill: torque)**

The biceps attaches 4.0 cm from the elbow joint. A person holds a 5.0 kg mass in the hand, 30 cm from the elbow, with the forearm horizontal. Ignoring the weight of the forearm, find the force in the biceps. Use g = 9.8 m/s².

Balance torques about the elbow: F × 0.040 m = (5.0 × 9.8 N) × 0.30 m. F = 14.7 / 0.040 = about 370 N, which is about 7.5 times the weight of the load (49 N). The muscle has a short lever arm and gives up force in exchange for speed and range of motion at the hand. This is a class 3 lever.

**Worked Example 3: Predicting a toxin (skill: step identification)**

A toxin blocks the SERCA pump of skeletal muscle. A muscle exposed to this toxin is stimulated once. Predict what happens to the contraction and the relaxation, and compare with a toxin that blocks the ryanodine receptor.

With SERCA blocked, calcium is released normally and the contraction begins, but calcium is not returned to the SR, so it stays high in the cytosol, troponin stays occupied, and the muscle fails to relax (it stays contracted, as in a sustained cramp). With the ryanodine receptor blocked, the action potential arrives normally but calcium is not released, so there is no contraction at all, despite normal electrical activity. The two toxins affect different steps: the first affects relaxation, and the second affects activation.

**Worked Example 4: Heat loss by sweating (skill: thermoregulation and energy)**

During exercise, a 70 kg person evaporates 100 g of sweat. Assume the heat of vaporization of sweat is 2.4 kJ/g, and the specific heat of the body is 3.5 kJ/(kg·°C). How much heat is removed, and by how many degrees would this lower the temperature of the body if there were no other heat input?

Heat removed = 100 g × 2.4 kJ/g = 240 kJ. The body's heat capacity is 70 kg × 3.5 kJ/(kg·°C) = 245 kJ/°C. The change is 240 / 245, about 1 °C. Exercising muscle can release this much heat in a few minutes, so evaporation is a necessary part of keeping the temperature steady. Sweat that drips off without evaporating removes much less heat.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Three muscle types, three calcium stories. Skeletal: troponin, with calcium from the SR. Cardiac: troponin, with calcium from the SR and from outside. Smooth: calmodulin and MLCK. Know which protein senses calcium, and the rest follows.

Praxis Tip: ATP does two jobs in the cross-bridge cycle, and students remember only one. It detaches the head from actin (binding), and its hydrolysis re-cocks the head. Without ATP, the muscle is stiff, not limp.

Praxis Tip: When a question asks what a paralyzed muscle is doing, work backward from the muscle: is it receiving a signal, releasing calcium, or able to cycle? A drug at the junction and a drug on the SR give different clues.

Praxis Tip: Bone is a dynamic calcium bank. High PTH takes calcium out; low PTH leaves it in. If a patient has a parathyroid tumor, expect high blood calcium and weak bones together.

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying that the thick or thin filaments shorten during contraction, or that the A band shortens.
- Thinking that ATP is needed only for the power stroke, and not for release.
- Putting troponin in smooth muscle, which uses calmodulin and MLCK.
- Saying that calcium comes mainly from outside in skeletal muscle (it comes from the SR).
- Assuming muscle glycogen can raise blood glucose. Muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.
- Confusing tendons (muscle to bone) with ligaments (bone to bone).
- Treating bone as inert. It is remodeled throughout life, and it responds to load and hormones.
- Forgetting that a fever is a change in the set point, not a failure of thermoregulation.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** The A band is the length of the thick filaments, which do not change in length during sliding. (B the I band shortens as thin filaments slide inward; C the H zone narrows as thin filaments approach the center; D the Z lines move closer together as the sarcomere shortens.)
2. **C.** ATP binding releases myosin from actin. Without it, the cross-bridges stay locked, as in rigor mortis. (A detachment itself requires ATP; B tropomyosin is not the ATP-dependent step; D calcium removal needs the ATP-dependent SR pump.)
3. **D.** The receptor must open to depolarize the end plate. Without it, the muscle membrane does not fire, and calcium is not released. (A release is normal, and contraction depends on the receptor; B curare acts on the receptor, not the esterase; C the SR is released only after a muscle action potential.)
4. **B.** Smooth muscle has no troponin, and calcium acts through calmodulin and MLCK, which phosphorylates myosin. (A those are features of striated muscle; C troponin is found in striated muscle; D smooth muscle is regulated by the autonomic system and hormones.)
5. **D.** PTH raises plasma calcium by activating osteoclasts, increasing renal reabsorption and increasing vitamin D activation. (A low calcium raises PTH rather than lowering it; B PTH raises calcium by stimulating bone resorption; C PTH stimulates the activation of vitamin D.)
6. **C.** Vasodilation brings blood to the skin, and sweat that evaporates removes heat. (A these are responses to cold; B these conserve and produce heat; D the set point is not lowered, and sweating increases.)
7. **A.** Slow-twitch fibers are oxidative, and they support prolonged, low-intensity activity. (B this describes fast-twitch fibers; C these suit short bursts, not endurance; D slow fibers are red because of myoglobin.)
