# Strategy: Reviewing a Practice Exam

**What you will be able to do**

- Turn a full-length score into a diagnosis, not just a number.
- Sort every miss by cause, using the general causes (content gap, misread, rushed, trap choice, calculation slip) and four biology tags (pathway gap, technique gap, regulation logic, data misread).
- Spot false confidence and slow spots using your confidence notes and your checkpoint times.
- Build a one-week repair plan, and take Exam 2 only after the repairs are made.

**Why it matters for the exam**

A practice exam is worth about as much as the review you do afterward. Two students can take the same exam and score the same; one learns three things from it and the other learns nothing. This chapter is the review routine, tuned to the Biological and Biochemical section. It builds on Core Test Skills, "Review a Practice Test", and it assumes you kept an error log as described in [Strategy: Reading Biology Experiments](chapter:3) and the earlier strategy chapters. Book 1 had a version of this routine for its own section (Book 1, Chapter 41). This version keeps the five passes and adds what biology needs: many misses here are not about a fact you forgot but about a map you cannot draw or a result you read the wrong way round.

You should have finished **Full-Length Exam 1** (the two parts, [Part A](chapter:32) and [Part B](chapter:33)) before reading this. Do not open the answers for Exam 2 ([Part A](chapter:35), [Part B](chapter:36)) yet. Exam 2 is the comparison; you want a clean one.

Praxis Tip: Review within 24 hours of the exam, while your memory of what you were thinking is fresh. After a week you will be reviewing the answer, not your reasoning.

**The review in five passes**

**Pass 1: Score and feelings (10 minutes).** Count correct answers for each part (out of 30) and for the whole (out of 60). Write down how you felt: confident, rushed, bored, shaky. This is data. Do not convert the number into a predicted real score. The practice exams in this book are original and are not calibrated to any official scale; treat the number as a measure of progress against yourself.

**Pass 2: Every wrong or guessed answer.** For each one, write in your notebook or error log:

- the question number and the content tag (for example, 1D, "bioenergetics and metabolism");
- the lesson number it belongs to;
- the cause, from the lists below (one general cause, plus a biology tag if one applies);
- the fix, in one sentence;
- the date you will re-attempt it.

The general causes are the same as in Book 1:

| Cause | Meaning | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Content gap | You did not know or misremembered a fact | Re-read the section; make flashcards |
| Misread | You answered a different question | Underline the verb in the stem |
| Rushed | You knew it, but skipped a step | Slow the setup, not the reading |
| Trap choice | You chose a plausible distractor | Name the trap; find why it fails |
| Calc slip | Right method, wrong arithmetic or units | Estimate first; write units |

*(The cause tables are images in the ebook, so you cannot edit them. Copy the causes into your notebook as column headings.)*

Four biology tags sit beside these. They are more specific than "content gap", and they point to a different kind of repair.

| Biology tag | Meaning | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pathway gap | You could not place a step, product or location on the map | Redraw the pathway from memory |
| Technique gap | You misjudged what a method shows or needs | Rewrite what the method detects and controls |
| Regulation logic | You missed what turns a process on or off, or why | Write the "on by, off by, because" line |
| Data misread | The table, graph or text said one thing; you saw another | Say the pattern in words before answering |

*(This is also an image in the ebook; copy the four tags into your notebook.)*

Tagging examples. You chose the wrong enzyme for a step in the TCA cycle: **pathway gap** (and perhaps a content gap on the enzyme's name). You said that a Western blot shows how much RNA is present: **technique gap**. You said that a high-energy-charge cell speeds up glycolysis: **regulation logic**. You swapped the control and treated lanes in a described gel: **data misread**. One miss can carry both a general cause and a biology tag, such as "rushed, data misread". Record both; the pair tells you what to do.

Praxis Tip: Never review by only reading the answer explanation. Cover it, retry the question, and then compare. The effort of retrieving is what makes it stick.

**Pass 3: Timing.** Compare your actual checkpoints with the plan from [Strategy: Pacing and Test Day for Passage-Heavy Biology](chapter:31). Where did you gain time? Where did you lose it? Identify the single slowest passage and ask why: an unfamiliar protein, a pathway diagram, a dense table, or poor triage. If you followed the passage-level checkpoints, compare your reading times with the 2 to 3 minute goal. A pattern such as "reading takes four minutes on pathway passages" is a training target.

**Pass 4: Confidence.** Place each question in one of four groups using your confidence ratings:

- *High confidence, correct:* fine.
- *Low confidence, correct:* lucky or fragile. Review these as if they were wrong.
- *Low confidence, wrong:* you knew you were unsure. This is usually a content gap or pathway gap; the lesson tells you what to do.
- *High confidence, wrong:* the most valuable group. You believed something false. Rewrite the idea correctly, in your own words.

Biology produces a lot of high-confidence errors because so many ideas are nearly true. "Enzymes make reactions happen that otherwise could not" is nearly true and wrong in a way that matters. Those are the errors worth pinning to a wall.

**Pass 5: Patterns and a plan.** Count misses by topic and by cause. Pick the top two issues. Write one concrete action for each: a lesson to re-read, a set of 10 problems to redo, or a habit to practice.

**Worked Example: Reading an error summary**

Suppose after Exam 1 your counts are as below. The question counts and misses are invented for illustration and do not describe the exams in this book.

| Topic group | Questions | Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Biomolecules, metabolism (L1-6) | 16 | 7 |
| Genes, techniques, evolution (L7-11) | 14 | 3 |
| Cells, microbes (L12-14) | 10 | 3 |
| Organ systems (L15-22) | 16 | 5 |
| Chemistry from Book 1 | 4 | 1 |

The totals are 16 + 14 + 10 + 16 + 4 = 60 questions, and 7 + 3 + 3 + 5 + 1 = 19 misses, so 41 correct. The miss rates are 7/16 = 44%, 3/14 = 21%, 3/10 = 30%, 5/16 = 31% and 1/4 = 25%. Biomolecules and metabolism is the weakest area both in the count (7 of 19 misses, about 37%) and in the rate.

Now add the causes for those 7 misses: two pathway gaps (both in the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation), two regulation-logic misses (both about what inhibits a rate-limiting step), one content gap, one trap choice and one calculation slip in an energy problem. Two actions follow: (1) redraw glycolysis, the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation from memory with locations and carriers ([Lesson 4](chapter:7) and [Lesson 5](chapter:8)), then write the "on by, off by, because" line for each rate-limiting step ([Lesson 6](chapter:9)), and redo the [Biomolecules and Metabolism Set](chapter:10) cold; (2) before every calculation, write units and a rough estimate.

Notice what you do *not* conclude. You do not conclude "I am bad at metabolism." You conclude "two maps, one kind of reasoning, and one habit."

*Skill used:* separating count, rate and cause.

Praxis Tip: Rates beat counts, but counts matter too. Weight a topic by how often it appears. Sixteen questions with seven misses is a bigger problem than four questions with two misses, though the second is a higher rate.

**Using the biology tags to choose a repair**

The tag tells you which tool to use. A content gap is repaired with flashcards. The biology tags need other tools, because a flashcard for "step 3 of the TCA cycle" does not teach you to place steps on a map.

- **Pathway gap.** Draw the pathway on scratch paper from memory, without a source: inputs, outputs, location, carriers and the direction of flow. Check it against the lesson. Redraw the parts you got wrong. Do this three times over two weeks. See [Strategy: Learning Pathways and Systems Without Rote Memory](chapter:2).
- **Technique gap.** For the method in question, write three lines: what it detects, what it needs (a primer, an antibody, a probe), and what a control would be. Then write one result it cannot show. See [Lesson 9](chapter:13).
- **Regulation logic.** Write one sentence of the form "this is turned on by ___ because ___, and off by ___ because ___". If you cannot finish "because", reread the lesson section. The reason is what the exam reuses.
- **Data misread.** Redo the question, this time writing the pattern in words before reading the choices: "the treated group is higher than the control at 4 hours and equal at 8". Then check the axis labels and units. If a tag keeps recurring, the fix is a habit, not content. See [Strategy: Reading Biology Experiments](chapter:3).

Praxis Tip: If the same biology tag appears three or more times, that tag is the finding, whatever the topics. Three technique gaps across three lessons is one problem, not three.

**Worked Example: Tagging a miss.**

A question showed lysate from knockout cells and wild-type cells run on a gel and probed with an antibody for protein X, with a second probe for a protein expected to be constant. The knockout lane showed no band for X, and the constant protein was present in both lanes. You chose "the knockout cells did not load enough sample". Cause: this is a **technique gap** (you did not use the loading control, which was present at equal intensity) and also a **trap choice** (a plausible but unsupported explanation). The fix is one sentence: "A loading control present at equal intensity means equal loading, so a missing band is real absence of the protein." The re-attempt date is three days out.

*Skill used:* tagging a miss with a general cause and a biology cause.

**Re-attempt rules**

1. Re-attempt each miss after three days, without looking at the explanation.
2. If you get it right, schedule one more attempt a week later.
3. If you get it wrong again, it is no longer a mistake; it is a topic. Reread the lesson section and, for a pathway gap, redraw the map; for any other cause, make three flashcards or write the "on by, off by, because" line.
4. After two successful re-attempts, retire the question.

**A one-week repair plan**

- **Day 1:** Passes 1 to 3 (score, misses, timing).
- **Day 2:** Passes 4 and 5. Write the two priorities and the plan.
- **Days 3 and 4:** Re-read the targeted lesson sections, redraw the pathway maps, and redo the targeted practice set cold.
- **Day 5:** Re-attempt Day-1 misses.
- **Day 6:** Light review: pathway cards, error log, the wrong-answer wall. Rest.
- **Day 7:** Take Exam 2 (Parts A and B) as a full-length simulation, with the clock.

If you are short on time, compress the plan, but keep the order: diagnose, repair, retest. Do not skip the cold redo; an unrepaired weak area simply reappears in Exam 2 with a fresh passage around it.

Praxis Tip: Do not take Exam 2 as soon as you finish this chapter. The point of two exams is a before-and-after. Repair first; then measure.

After Exam 2, repeat the five passes, then compare the two summaries side by side. Look for the *tags* that fell, not only the score. An area that stays flat after a week of repair needs another method, not just more time with the old one.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Keep a "wrong-answer wall" in your notebook: one line per false belief you caught ("I thought enzymes shift the equilibrium"), corrected. Reread it before the exam.

Praxis Tip: For every calculation slip, check whether the final answer was even the right size. An estimate would have caught most of them.

Praxis Tip: For every trap choice, write one sentence naming the trap ("this is true biology, but it answers a different question"). Named traps are easier to avoid.

Praxis Tip: Compare the first half and the second half of each part. If the second half is much worse, you have a stamina or pacing issue, not a knowledge issue.

Praxis Tip: Put a star by every miss you tagged "rushed" and ask which of your triage rules you broke. A rushed error is often a pacing error wearing a content costume.

**Common mistakes**

- Reviewing only the wrong answers, not the lucky ones.
- Reading explanations instead of retrying the question.
- Treating the practice score as a prediction.
- Tagging every miss "content gap" and so repairing nothing.
- Fixing every problem at once instead of the top two.
- Taking the next exam before repairs are made.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. Name the five general causes of a miss and the four biology tags used in this chapter.
2. Why is a high-confidence wrong answer more valuable to study than a low-confidence wrong answer?
3. A student says a PCR needs an antibody to find its target. Which tag fits, and what is the repair?
4. In the worked example, which topic group is the top priority, and why?
5. What do you do with a correct answer you marked low confidence?
6. When should you re-attempt a missed question, and what makes it "a topic" rather than "a mistake"?
7. What is the purpose of splitting the practice exams in two, with a repair week between?

**Stop here.** Answer each question in your notebook before reading the answers.

**Answers and reasons**

1. Content gap, misread, rushed, trap choice, calculation slip; and pathway gap, technique gap, regulation logic, data misread.
2. It reveals a false belief held with certainty, which will repeat on test day. A low-confidence miss is a known weakness and is already on your list.
3. Technique gap. Write what PCR detects and needs: it amplifies a target DNA sequence and needs primers that match it, not an antibody. Then add a control and a limit.
4. Biomolecules and metabolism, because it has both the highest count of misses (7 of 19) and the highest rate (44%) among groups of meaningful size.
5. Review it as if it were wrong: it was probably luck or a fragile guess.
6. After about three days, without looking at the explanation, then again a week later if correct. It becomes a topic when you miss it again on a re-attempt.
7. To measure the effect of your repairs: Exam 1 is the baseline, Exam 2 is the comparison.

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

Explain the five passes to an imaginary friend in two minutes, then give one example of each biology tag. After two days, one week and three weeks, write the five general causes and the four biology tags from memory.
