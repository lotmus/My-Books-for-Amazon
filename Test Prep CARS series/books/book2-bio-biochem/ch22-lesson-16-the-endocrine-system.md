# Lesson 16: The Endocrine System

**What you will be able to do**

- Classify hormones as peptide, steroid or amine, and predict their receptor location, transport, half-life and mechanism from the class.
- Describe how receptors and second messengers (cAMP, IP₃/DAG/Ca²⁺, tyrosine kinases, nuclear receptors) produce a hormone's effect.
- Distinguish negative from positive feedback, and use hormone levels to locate a defect in a hormonal axis.
- Describe the hypothalamus-pituitary connection and the hormones of the anterior and posterior pituitary.
- Summarize the actions of thyroid, adrenal, pancreatic and gonadal hormones, and the hormonal control of calcium.
- Explain the acute and chronic stress responses and link hormones to the fuel metabolism of [Lesson 4](chapter:7) and [Lesson 6](chapter:9).

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson covers category 3A, the endocrine side of the nervous and endocrine systems. Passages in this area typically give a set of hormone levels, a drug or a mutation and ask where the defect is, or which pathway has been switched on or off. That is mostly a feedback question and a signaling question, and the signaling material reuses what you learned in [Lesson 12](chapter:17) on GPCRs, second messengers and receptor tyrosine kinases. The metabolic consequences, such as what insulin or cortisol does to glycolysis, glycogen and gluconeogenesis, rest on [Lesson 4](chapter:7) and [Lesson 6](chapter:9). The nervous system ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)) and the endocrine system together coordinate the body: the nervous system is fast and local, and the endocrine system is slower, widespread and longer lasting.

Praxis Tip: Every endocrine question has the same skeleton: which gland, which hormone, which receptor, which target, and what stops the signal. Fill in those five blanks and the answer is usually visible.

**Concept Review**

**Hormones and how they are classified.** A **hormone** is a chemical messenger released into the blood by endocrine glands or cells, acting on target cells that carry the right receptor. Chemical class predicts almost everything else.

| Class | Examples | Made from |
|---|---|---|
| Peptide/protein | Insulin, glucagon, ADH, GH | Amino acid chains |
| Steroid | Cortisol, aldosterone, estrogen | Cholesterol |
| Amine | Epinephrine, thyroid hormones | Tyrosine |

**Peptide and protein hormones** are made like other secreted proteins ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)): as a precursor on the rough ER, processed in the Golgi and stored in secretory vesicles until a signal triggers exocytosis. They are water-soluble, so they travel free in the blood, have short half-lives (minutes), and cannot cross the plasma membrane. They bind **cell-surface receptors** and act through second messengers or kinases, giving rapid effects that generally change the activity of existing proteins (by phosphorylation). **Steroid hormones** are lipid-soluble derivatives of cholesterol ([Lesson 2](chapter:5)), made on demand rather than stored. They are carried in the blood bound to **transport proteins**, which makes their effective half-lives longer (hours). They cross the membrane and bind **intracellular receptors** in the cytoplasm or nucleus, and the hormone-receptor complex acts as a **transcription factor** at hormone response elements in DNA ([Lesson 8](chapter:12)). The effects are therefore slower (hours) and longer lasting, and involve new protein synthesis. **Amine hormones** divide into two groups. The catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine) are water-soluble, are stored in vesicles, and bind cell-surface GPCRs. The **thyroid hormones**, made from tyrosine and iodine, behave like steroids: they are lipid-soluble, are carried bound to proteins, have long half-lives (days for T4 [VERIFY]), and bind nuclear receptors. This is a classic exception to the statement "amines act at the surface".

Praxis Tip: Solubility is the master key. Water-soluble hormones travel free, act fast and use surface receptors. Lipid-soluble hormones ride on carrier proteins, act slowly and use intracellular receptors. Thyroid hormone is the amine that is lipid-soluble, and it is the exception the exam likes.

**Receptors and second messengers.** The pathways below were introduced in [Lesson 12](chapter:17) and recur throughout this lesson.

- **Gs-coupled receptors** raise cAMP through adenylyl cyclase, which activates protein kinase A. Examples: glucagon and epinephrine (β receptors) in the liver, ACTH on the adrenal cortex, TSH on the thyroid, and LH and FSH on the gonads. **Gi-coupled receptors** lower cAMP.
- **Gq-coupled receptors** activate phospholipase C, making IP₃ (which releases Ca²⁺ from the ER) and DAG (which activates protein kinase C). Examples: epinephrine at α₁ receptors, and TRH on the pituitary.
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases** are used by insulin and growth factors. After insulin binds, the receptor autophosphorylates and phosphorylates docking proteins, leading to the PI3K-Akt pathway [VERIFY] that moves glucose transporters (GLUT4) to the membrane in muscle and fat, and to the Ras-MAPK pathway that affects growth.
- **Cytokine receptors** work through associated kinases (JAKs [VERIFY]); growth hormone and prolactin use this route.
- **Nuclear receptors** bind steroid and thyroid hormones, as above.

The same hormone can have different effects in different tissues, because the receptor subtype and the downstream proteins differ. Epinephrine, for example, relaxes smooth muscle in the airways through β₂ receptors (Gs) but contracts it in many blood vessels through α₁ receptors (Gq). Responses can be modified by the number of receptors: **down-regulation** (fewer receptors after a prolonged high hormone level) and **up-regulation** reduce or increase sensitivity. A target cell's response to a hormone depends on the fraction of receptors occupied, which follows the same binding relationship as ligand binding in Book 1, Lesson 24: fractional occupancy = [H] / ([H] + Kd).

**Feedback.** Most hormone systems use **negative feedback**: the product (or its effect) inhibits further secretion, which keeps a variable near a set point. The classic form is a **hierarchy** or **axis** in which a chain of hormones signals down from the brain, and the final hormone feeds back to shut off the upper ones:

- **Long-loop feedback:** the end hormone (for example cortisol) inhibits the hypothalamus and the pituitary.
- **Short-loop feedback:** the pituitary hormone inhibits the hypothalamus.
- **Direct feedback with no axis:** the regulated variable itself controls secretion, for example high blood glucose triggers insulin, and low blood calcium triggers parathyroid hormone.

**Positive feedback** amplifies a change until an endpoint is reached, and is less common. Examples: oxytocin during labor (stretch of the cervix triggers more oxytocin, which strengthens contractions, which stretches the cervix further), and the surge of LH before ovulation, which is triggered by rising estrogen after a threshold is passed. Positive feedback loops end when the event itself ends.

To locate a defect in an axis, compare the hormone levels at each level. In **primary** disease, the final gland is the problem. In **secondary** disease the pituitary is the problem, and in **tertiary** disease the hypothalamus is. In primary failure of the final gland, the end hormone is low and the pituitary hormone is high (it is no longer being inhibited); in pituitary failure, both are low.

Praxis Tip: Loss of an end hormone releases the brakes, so the upstream hormones rise. If the whole chain is low, the lesion is at the top of the chain. If the end hormone is high and the upstream hormones are low, the end gland is overproducing on its own.

**The hypothalamus and pituitary.** The **hypothalamus** is the brain's link to the endocrine system ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). The pituitary (hypophysis) hangs below it and has two parts that are developmentally and functionally different.

The **posterior pituitary** (neurohypophysis) is neural tissue. Its hormones are made by neurons whose cell bodies are in the hypothalamus; the axons run down into the posterior pituitary, where the hormones are stored and released on nerve impulses. The two hormones are **antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin)**, which makes the kidney collecting duct more permeable to water (by inserting aquaporins [VERIFY]) and raises blood pressure by constricting vessels, and **oxytocin**, which stimulates uterine contraction and milk ejection. ADH release is triggered by high blood osmolarity or low blood volume. Both are peptides.

The **anterior pituitary** (adenohypophysis) is glandular tissue. The hypothalamus controls it with **releasing and inhibiting hormones**, which travel in a small **portal system** of blood vessels directly to the anterior pituitary (so a tiny amount of hormone reaches the target without being diluted in the general circulation). The anterior pituitary then secretes its own **tropic hormones**, which act on other glands, and some direct hormones.

| Hypothalamic | Anterior pituitary | Target and effect |
|---|---|---|
| TRH | TSH | Thyroid releases T3 and T4 |
| CRH | ACTH | Adrenal cortex makes cortisol |
| GnRH | LH and FSH | Gonads: sex hormones, gametes |
| GHRH (somatostatin inhibits) | GH | Growth; liver makes IGF-1 |
| Dopamine inhibits | Prolactin | Milk production |

Memory aid by acronym: the anterior pituitary hormones are TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, GH and prolactin. **Growth hormone** (GH) promotes growth through IGF-1 made in the liver, and also has direct metabolic effects: it raises blood glucose and promotes the breakdown of fat (an anti-insulin effect). Too much GH before the growth plates close causes gigantism, and after, acromegaly. **Prolactin** is controlled mainly by inhibition: the hypothalamus tonically releases dopamine, which suppresses prolactin, so damage to the connection raises prolactin. This is unusual among anterior pituitary hormones.

**The thyroid.** The thyroid gland is made of follicles, and the follicular cells make **thyroxine (T4)** and **triiodothyronine (T3)**. Thyroid hormone synthesis needs iodide, which the gland concentrates from the blood. Iodine is attached to tyrosine residues on the protein thyroglobulin, and the iodinated tyrosines are coupled and then released by proteolysis. T4 is the main product, and T3, which is made in part from T4 in the tissues, is the more active form. **TSH** stimulates the thyroid, and T3/T4 inhibit TSH and TRH by negative feedback. Thyroid hormones raise the **basal metabolic rate** by increasing the number of Na⁺/K⁺ pumps and mitochondria, increase heat production, increase heart rate and sensitivity to catecholamines, and are required for normal development of the nervous system.

- **Hypothyroidism** (low T3/T4): fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, slow heart rate. In primary hypothyroidism TSH is high. Dietary iodine deficiency causes a goiter (an enlarged thyroid) because the low T4 raises TSH, which stimulates growth of the gland. Hypothyroidism in infancy causes impaired development.
- **Hyperthyroidism** (high T3/T4): weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, rapid heart rate; TSH is low in primary hyperthyroidism. Graves disease is an autoimmune cause in which antibodies mimic TSH and stimulate the TSH receptor, so T4 is high and TSH is low (the antibodies are not subject to feedback).

The parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid make **calcitonin**, which lowers blood calcium (by inhibiting osteoclasts), but its role in adult humans is minor.

**The adrenal glands.** Each adrenal gland has two tissues with different origins. The **adrenal cortex** (outer) makes steroid hormones in three layers [VERIFY layer order is glomerulosa, fasciculata, reticularis from outside in]:

- **Mineralocorticoids** (mainly **aldosterone**) act on the distal nephron to increase Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ secretion, and water follows sodium, which raises blood volume and pressure. Aldosterone is controlled mainly by the **renin-angiotensin system** (low blood pressure or volume triggers renin release from the kidney, which leads to angiotensin II, which stimulates aldosterone) and by high plasma K⁺, rather than by ACTH ([Lesson 19](chapter:26) covers the kidney).
- **Glucocorticoids** (mainly **cortisol**) are controlled by the CRH-ACTH axis. Cortisol raises blood glucose by promoting gluconeogenesis in the liver and by breaking down muscle protein to supply amino acids, mobilizes fat, and suppresses inflammation and the immune system. In terms of [Lesson 6](chapter:9), cortisol upregulates the gluconeogenic enzymes (a transcriptional effect, consistent with a nuclear receptor).
- **Adrenal androgens** are weak sex steroids made in the inner layer.

The **adrenal medulla** (inner) is neural-crest tissue that works as a modified sympathetic ganglion ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). Preganglionic sympathetic neurons release acetylcholine onto its cells, which release **epinephrine** (mostly) and norepinephrine into the blood. Too little cortisol (adrenal insufficiency) causes low blood sugar, low blood pressure and fatigue; too much (Cushing syndrome) causes high blood sugar, central weight gain, and muscle weakness. Because ACTH-secreting pituitary tumors and adrenal tumors both raise cortisol, the ACTH level tells you which: in an adrenal tumor, cortisol is high and ACTH is low (feedback suppresses the pituitary); in a pituitary tumor, both are high.

**The pancreas and glucose homeostasis.** The pancreas has an exocrine part (digestive enzymes, [Lesson 19](chapter:26)) and an endocrine part, the **islets of Langerhans**, with three main cell types: **α cells** release **glucagon**, **β cells** release **insulin**, and **δ cells** release **somatostatin**, which inhibits both.

**Insulin** (the "fed" hormone) is released when blood glucose rises. Glucose enters the β cell, and its metabolism raises ATP, which closes ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels, depolarizes the cell, opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels and triggers insulin exocytosis (the same sequence as a synapse, [Lesson 15](chapter:21)). Insulin binds its receptor tyrosine kinase and lowers blood glucose by: inserting GLUT4 transporters in muscle and adipose tissue, stimulating glycogen synthesis, glycolysis and fat synthesis, and inhibiting gluconeogenesis and glycogen breakdown in the liver ([Lesson 4](chapter:7), [Lesson 6](chapter:9)). **Glucagon** (the "fasting" hormone) is released when blood glucose falls. It acts mainly on the liver through Gs and cAMP, promoting glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis, and ketone body formation, and it raises blood glucose. Epinephrine, cortisol and growth hormone also raise blood glucose, so insulin is the only hormone that lowers it, a point examiners enjoy.

In **type 1 diabetes**, autoimmune destruction of β cells leads to little or no insulin. In **type 2 diabetes**, target tissues respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance), often with a later decline in insulin production. In both, blood glucose stays high, glucose spills into the urine and pulls water with it (increased urination and thirst), and tissues that cannot take up glucose break down fat and protein. The liver, with low insulin and high glucagon, makes ketone bodies, and in uncontrolled type 1 diabetes this can cause **ketoacidosis** (the ketone bodies are acids).

**Calcium homeostasis.** Plasma Ca²⁺ is held within a narrow range, because it is essential for neuronal excitability, muscle contraction and the clotting cascade. Low plasma calcium increases the excitability of nerves and muscles (cramps, tetany), and high calcium decreases it. The main regulators are:

- **Parathyroid hormone (PTH)**, a peptide made by the four parathyroid glands in response to **low** plasma Ca²⁺. PTH raises plasma calcium by stimulating bone resorption (through osteoblasts that activate osteoclasts), by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidney, and by increasing the kidney's activation of vitamin D. It also increases phosphate excretion in the urine.
- **Vitamin D** (calcitriol is its active form), a steroid-like hormone made from cholesterol-derived precursors in skin using ultraviolet light and activated in the liver and kidney. It increases calcium and phosphate absorption in the intestine.
- **Calcitonin**, from the thyroid C cells, lowers plasma calcium by inhibiting bone resorption, with a minor role in adults.

Removal of the parathyroid glands, as can happen in thyroid surgery, causes low calcium and tetany. Chronic kidney disease reduces vitamin D activation, which lowers calcium.

**The gonads.** The gonads are both endocrine glands and gamete producers. Hypothalamic **GnRH**, released in pulses, stimulates the anterior pituitary to release **LH** and **FSH**. In males, **LH** acts on the Leydig cells of the testis to make **testosterone**, and **FSH** acts on the Sertoli cells to support sperm production. Testosterone feeds back negatively on the hypothalamus and the pituitary, and **inhibin** (from Sertoli cells) specifically inhibits FSH. Testosterone drives male development and secondary sex characteristics, increases muscle mass and bone density, and acts through an intracellular receptor. In females, FSH stimulates ovarian follicles to grow and make **estrogen**; the LH surge triggers ovulation, and the remnant of the follicle (the corpus luteum) makes **progesterone** and some estrogen, preparing and maintaining the uterine lining. Estrogen generally has negative feedback on LH and FSH, but a high, sustained level in the middle of the cycle switches to positive feedback and produces the LH surge. Details of the cycle, fertilization and development belong to the reproduction and development lessons; here you need the hormone logic. Sex steroids are made from cholesterol ([Lesson 2](chapter:5)), and all use nuclear receptors.

**The stress response.** The body responds to a stressor with two overlapping systems.

- The **acute (fast) response** uses the sympathetic nervous system and the adrenal medulla: epinephrine and norepinephrine raise heart rate, blood pressure and airway diameter, redirect blood to muscle, and mobilize glucose (glycogen breakdown) and fatty acids. This is the "fight or flight" response ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)) and acts within seconds.
- The **chronic (slower) response** uses the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis: CRH → ACTH → cortisol over minutes to hours. Cortisol maintains blood glucose by gluconeogenesis, mobilizes amino acids and fat, and (at high levels) suppresses immune and inflammatory activity. Cortisol feeds back to inhibit CRH and ACTH. Chronic stress leaves cortisol elevated and has consequences: high blood glucose, muscle breakdown, weakened immunity, high blood pressure, and mood effects, which Book 3 discusses.

Other hormones also contribute to energy balance: growth hormone, thyroid hormones, and, for fat, the hormone **leptin** from adipose tissue (which signals fullness to the hypothalamus [VERIFY]).

**Worked Example 1: Locating a defect in an axis (skill: feedback reasoning)**

Patient A has low T4 and high TSH. Patient B has low T4 and low TSH. Patient C has high T4 and low TSH. Identify the most likely location of each defect.

Patient A: the thyroid is failing (primary hypothyroidism): T4 is low, so the brakes are released and TSH rises. Patient B: both are low, so the pituitary (or hypothalamus) is not driving the thyroid (secondary or tertiary hypothyroidism). A TRH test could separate the pituitary from the hypothalamus; the pituitary would not respond to TRH if it is the site. Patient C: T4 is high and TSH is suppressed by the feedback. The thyroid itself (or an outside source of T4, or stimulating antibodies as in Graves disease) is the cause.

**Worked Example 2: Receptor occupancy (skill: quantitative)**

A hormone binds its receptor with Kd = 5 nM. What fraction of the receptors is occupied at (a) 5 nM hormone and (b) 20 nM hormone? (c) A second hormone has the same Kd but its receptor number is halved by down-regulation. What happens to the response at 20 nM?

Fraction occupied = [H] / ([H] + Kd). (a) 5 / (5 + 5) = 0.50. (b) 20 / (20 + 5) = 0.80. (c) The fraction occupied is still 0.80, but there are half as many receptors, so the number of occupied receptors, and generally the response, falls to about half of what the original number would give (0.80 × 0.5 = 0.40 of the original total receptor count). The cell has become less sensitive without any change in the hormone.

**Worked Example 3: Glucose after a meal and after a fast (skill: integrating hormones and metabolism)**

Describe the hormonal signals and the liver's behavior (a) 1 hour after a carbohydrate-rich meal, and (b) 12 hours after the last meal.

(a) Blood glucose is high, so insulin is high and glucagon is low. The liver takes up glucose, stores it as glycogen, and runs glycolysis and fat synthesis, while gluconeogenesis is inhibited. Muscle and fat cells insert GLUT4 and take up glucose. (b) Blood glucose is falling, so insulin is low and glucagon is high. The liver breaks down glycogen, then (as the stores deplete) switches to gluconeogenesis, and begins making ketone bodies ([Lesson 6](chapter:9)). The ratio of insulin to glucagon, not either hormone alone, sets the direction.

**Worked Example 4: Half-life (skill: kinetics)**

A peptide hormone has a plasma half-life of 4 minutes. Secretion stops. What fraction remains after 12 minutes? A steroid hormone with a half-life of 90 minutes is given the same test: what fraction remains after 12 minutes?

For 12 minutes with a 4-minute half-life, there are 3 half-lives: (1/2)³ = 1/8 = 12.5%. For the steroid, 12/90 = 0.133 half-lives, so the fraction is (1/2)^0.133 = about 0.91 (91%). The peptide signal can be switched off in minutes, and the steroid signal persists for hours, which is why peptide hormones suit rapid, tightly timed responses and steroids suit long-term changes. (This is the same first-order decay as radioactive decay, Book 1, Lesson 8.)

**Worked Example 5: Calcium disturbance (skill: reasoning from a case)**

After thyroid surgery, a patient has numbness around the mouth and muscle spasms, and the plasma calcium is low. Which hormone is likely deficient, and what do you expect for the phosphate concentration and for intestinal calcium absorption?

The parathyroid glands, close to the thyroid, were probably damaged or removed, so PTH is low. Without PTH, bone resorption and renal calcium reabsorption fall and vitamin D activation falls, so plasma calcium falls and intestinal absorption is reduced. PTH normally promotes phosphate loss in the urine, so with low PTH the plasma phosphate tends to be high. Low calcium makes nerves more excitable (they fire more easily), which explains the tingling and spasm. Treatment replaces calcium and active vitamin D.

**Worked Example 6: Signaling routes (skill: second messengers)**

Three cell types are exposed to glucagon, cortisol and insulin. For each, state the receptor location and the type of response.

Glucagon binds a surface GPCR (Gs) on liver cells, raising cAMP and activating PKA within seconds to minutes, which changes the phosphorylation of existing enzymes (glycogen breakdown). Cortisol diffuses through the membrane, binds an intracellular receptor, and changes transcription over hours (more gluconeogenic enzymes). Insulin binds a receptor tyrosine kinase at the surface; the response begins within minutes (GLUT4 insertion) and also includes longer-term changes in gene expression.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Hormone names often tell you the function: antidiuretic (against urine), gluco-corticoid (glucose, cortex), mineralo-corticoid (minerals, cortex), tropic (turns on another gland). Read the name before you start recalling.

Praxis Tip: Only the posterior pituitary releases hormones that were made in the brain (ADH and oxytocin). The anterior pituitary makes its own, under the control of releasing hormones delivered through the portal vessels.

Praxis Tip: Insulin is the only major hormone that lowers blood glucose. Glucagon, epinephrine, cortisol and growth hormone all raise it. If a question asks which hormone lowers glucose, the list is very short.

Praxis Tip: For a Cushing or thyroid case, write the axis as a chain on scratch paper, then mark each level high or low. The pattern shows where the break is, and it takes ten seconds.

Praxis Tip: Calcium and phosphate often move in opposite directions under PTH. PTH raises plasma calcium and lowers plasma phosphate, and this is a good check on any answer.

Praxis Tip: Steroid and thyroid hormone effects need hours, because they change transcription. A passage showing an effect within seconds suggests a surface receptor or a nervous signal instead.

Praxis Tip: A hormone "excess" with a low upstream hormone means the gland is producing independently. Think tumor or stimulating antibody, and remember that the pituitary is shut off by the excess end hormone. Nothing in endocrinology is quite as polite as a feedback loop; when one is broken, the symptoms are immediate and rude.

**Common Mistakes**

- Assuming all amine hormones act at the cell surface (thyroid hormone uses a nuclear receptor).
- Saying the posterior pituitary makes ADH and oxytocin. It stores and releases them; the hypothalamic neurons make them.
- Forgetting that loss of negative feedback raises the upstream hormone.
- Reading "tropic" as "tropical": a tropic hormone acts on another endocrine gland.
- Saying that steroid hormones are stored in vesicles; they are made on demand from cholesterol and diffuse out.
- Assuming that insulin deficiency means low blood glucose; the opposite is true.
- Confusing the adrenal cortex (steroids, ACTH control for cortisol) with the medulla (catecholamines, neural control).
- Treating calcitonin as the main calcium regulator in adults; PTH and vitamin D matter most.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. A hormone is lipid-soluble, circulates bound to a carrier protein, binds a receptor in the cytoplasm, and acts over hours. Which hormone class does it most likely belong to?
   A. A peptide hormone that signals through cAMP
   B. A steroid hormone that alters gene transcription
   C. A catecholamine acting on a GPCR
   D. A peptide hormone that signals through a tyrosine kinase

2. A patient has high levels of cortisol and low levels of ACTH. Which of the following is the most likely cause?
   A. A pituitary tumor that secretes ACTH
   B. Failure of the adrenal cortex
   C. A hypothalamic tumor that secretes CRH
   D. A cortisol-secreting tumor of the adrenal cortex

3. Which of the following is released from the posterior pituitary and increases the water permeability of the collecting duct?
   A. Antidiuretic hormone
   B. Aldosterone
   C. Oxytocin
   D. Thyrotropin-releasing hormone

4. Glucagon binds a Gs-coupled receptor on liver cells. Which sequence of events is correct?
   A. Phospholipase C activation, IP₃ release, glycogen synthesis
   B. Nuclear receptor binding, new transcription, glycogen synthesis
   C. Adenylyl cyclase activation, cAMP increase, PKA activation, glycogen breakdown
   D. Receptor autophosphorylation, Ras activation, glycogen breakdown

5. A patient has low plasma calcium, high plasma phosphate, and a low PTH level. Which of the following is the most likely explanation?
   A. Hypoparathyroidism
   B. Hyperparathyroidism
   C. Excess vitamin D
   D. Excess calcitonin

6. Which change is expected within seconds of a sudden frightening event?
   A. A rise in plasma cortisol caused by increased ACTH, which reaches its peak within seconds
   B. A rise in thyroid hormone, which changes gene expression
   C. Release of epinephrine from the adrenal medulla, increasing heart rate and mobilizing glucose
   D. A rise in insulin, to lower the blood glucose

7. A drug blocks the dopamine receptors on the cells of the anterior pituitary that make prolactin. What is the most likely effect?
   A. Decreased prolactin, because dopamine stimulates its release
   B. No change, because prolactin is controlled by TRH only
   C. Decreased prolactin, because the receptors are blocked
   D. Increased prolactin, because dopamine normally inhibits its release

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** Lipid solubility, carrier proteins, an intracellular receptor and a slow time course are the signature of a steroid hormone acting as a transcription factor. (A and D describe peptides, which are water-soluble and use surface receptors; C catecholamines are water-soluble and act quickly on surface GPCRs.)
2. **D.** High cortisol with low ACTH means the adrenal cortex is producing cortisol independently, and the excess cortisol suppresses the pituitary by negative feedback. (A a pituitary ACTH-secreting tumor would give high ACTH; B adrenal failure would give low cortisol and high ACTH; C CRH excess would give high ACTH.)
3. **A.** ADH is made in the hypothalamus, released from the posterior pituitary, and makes the collecting duct more permeable to water. (B aldosterone comes from the adrenal cortex; C oxytocin acts on the uterus and breast; D TRH is a hypothalamic hormone acting on the anterior pituitary.)
4. **C.** Gs activates adenylyl cyclase, cAMP activates PKA, and PKA promotes glycogen breakdown in the liver. (A describes a Gq pathway and glycogen synthesis would not be the response to glucagon; B is the route of steroids, not of a surface peptide receptor; D is the receptor tyrosine kinase route.)
5. **A.** Low PTH explains low calcium, and without PTH's phosphate-excreting effect the phosphate is high. (B would give high calcium and low phosphate; C would give high calcium; D calcitonin would lower calcium but would not give a high phosphate and a low PTH. Its role in adults is minor, and low calcium would raise PTH, not lower it.)
6. **C.** The adrenal medulla responds to sympathetic input within seconds, releasing epinephrine. (A the ACTH-cortisol axis acts over minutes to hours; B thyroid hormones change gene expression slowly and are not acute stress hormones; D insulin does not rise in the acute stress response; epinephrine tends to suppress insulin release.)
7. **D.** Dopamine from the hypothalamus tonically inhibits prolactin, so blocking its receptors releases the inhibition and prolactin rises. (A dopamine inhibits rather than stimulates; B prolactin is controlled mainly by dopamine inhibition; C blocking an inhibitory signal increases, not decreases, secretion.)
