**Timing and conditions**

Fourteen original questions on [Lessons 7 to 11](chapter:11): DNA replication and repair, transcription and translation, laboratory techniques, classical and population genetics, and evolution and diversity (categories 1B and 1C). Three passage sets are followed by two independent questions, and the emphasis is on reasoning from data: gels, crosses, pedigrees, Hardy-Weinberg numbers and phylogenies. Allow about 22 minutes, in one sitting, with scratch paper and no notes or calculator. Write your answer letters, with a confidence level (high, medium or low) for each, on scratch paper or in your notebook; an ebook cannot be written in. Check only when you have finished.

Praxis Tip: In every data passage, spend the first thirty seconds on the table or description alone. Write down on scratch paper what was measured, what was varied, and what the units are. Questions that follow are almost always a rearrangement of those three facts.

**Questions**

**Passage 1 (questions 1-4)**

A circular plasmid 6.0 kb in size was digested separately with two restriction enzymes, EcoRI and BamHI, and with both together. The products were run on an agarose gel and the fragment sizes were read from a size ladder. Each enzyme cuts only at its own recognition sequence, and every digest was allowed to run for the same time with the same amount of enzyme.

| Digest | Fragment sizes (kb) |
|---|---|
| EcoRI alone | 6.0 |
| BamHI alone | 4.0 and 2.0 |
| EcoRI + BamHI | 3.0, 2.0 and 1.0 |

In a later experiment, a mutant version of the plasmid was digested with EcoRI + BamHI and gave only two bands, at 4.0 kb and 2.0 kb. In the EcoRI-alone lane of the original plasmid, a faint extra band also appeared just ahead of the main 6.0 kb band.

1. How many EcoRI recognition sites does the original plasmid have?
   A. None
   B. One
   C. Two
   D. Three

2. Which change in the mutant plasmid best explains the two-band pattern after the double digest?
   A. One of the BamHI sites was destroyed
   B. The EcoRI site was destroyed
   C. A second EcoRI site was created
   D. Both enzymes lost their activity

3. In the lane for the original EcoRI + BamHI digest, which order lists the bands from nearest the well to farthest from the well?
   A. 1.0 kb, then 2.0 kb, then 3.0 kb
   B. 2.0 kb, then 3.0 kb, then 1.0 kb
   C. 3.0 kb, then 2.0 kb, then 1.0 kb
   D. All three bands at the same position

4. Which explanation best accounts for the faint extra band just ahead of the main 6.0 kb band in the EcoRI-alone lane?
   A. A second EcoRI site shortened the main band
   B. Some plasmid remained uncut and supercoiled, which generally travels faster than linear DNA of the same length
   C. Contaminating BamHI cut part of the sample
   D. A band that travels farther must be a larger fragment

**Passage 2 (questions 5-8)**

A rare disorder appears in a family. In generation I, a father and a mother are both unaffected. They have three children (generation II): an affected daughter (II-1), an unaffected son (II-2) and an unaffected daughter (II-3). II-3 is planning to have children with an unaffected man who is unrelated to the family and was drawn from a large, randomly mating population in which the disorder affects 1 person in 10,000. Treat the population as being in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium for this gene, and assume that there is no new mutation and that the partner has no family history of the disorder.

Separately, a genetics class sampled 200 people from another population at a different locus with two codominant alleles, M and N, and counted these genotypes:

| Genotype | MM | MN | NN |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number observed | 110 | 60 | 30 |

5. Which mode of inheritance does the family best fit?
   A. Autosomal dominant
   B. X-linked recessive
   C. Autosomal recessive
   D. X-linked dominant

6. What is the probability that II-3 is a carrier?
   A. 2/3
   B. 1/2
   C. 1/4
   D. 1

7. About what is the probability that the first child of II-3 and her unrelated partner is affected?
   A. 1/300
   B. 1/75
   C. 1/600
   D. 1/60,000

8. Based on the counts for the M and N locus, which interpretation is best supported?
   A. The population is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, because the three genotype counts total 200
   B. There is an excess of heterozygotes, which suggests outbreeding
   C. Selection against allele N has lowered its frequency in this generation
   D. The M allele has frequency 0.70, and there are fewer heterozygotes (60) than the 84 expected, which suggests nonrandom mating such as inbreeding

**Passage 3 (questions 9-12)**

Researchers compared a neutral DNA region among four species, A, B, C and D, and counted the differences per 1,000 sites for each pair. Fossil evidence places the split between D and the lineage leading to A, B and C at 50 million years ago.

| Species | vs B | vs C | vs D |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 20 | 60 | 100 |
| B | - | 60 | 100 |
| C | - | - | 100 |

The sequences were aligned without gaps, and all four species have similar generation times. Assume that neutral differences accumulate at a constant rate in every lineage. The researchers also scored a trait that is present in species A, B and D and absent in species C.

9. Which relationship among the species do the sequence data support?
   A. A and C are sister species, and B is the most distant
   B. C and D are sister species
   C. All four species diverged at the same time
   D. A and B are sister species, C is the next closest relative to them, and D is the most distant

10. Using the fossil calibration, when did species A and B diverge?
   A. About 20 million years ago
   B. About 30 million years ago
   C. About 10 million years ago
   D. About 50 million years ago

11. Which explanation of the trait is the most parsimonious?
   A. The trait was present in the common ancestor of all four species and was lost once, in the lineage leading to C
   B. The trait evolved independently in D and in the ancestor of A and B
   C. The trait evolved independently in A, in B and in D
   D. The trait shows that D is more closely related to A and B than C is

12. Another team uses a gene under strong purifying selection (most changes are removed) and applies the same rate, 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ per site per year, to estimate the A and B split. Compared with the neutral-region estimate, their estimate would be expected to be
   A. too young, because fewer differences accumulate in a constrained gene than the neutral rate predicts
   B. too old, because constrained genes accumulate more differences
   C. the same, because the rate is the same
   D. impossible to calculate, because two lineages are compared

**Questions 13-14**

These two questions are independent of the passages.

13. E. coli grown for many generations in medium containing heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) are moved to medium with light nitrogen (¹⁴N) and allowed to replicate twice. DNA is then separated by density in a centrifuge. If replication is semiconservative, what fractions of the DNA duplexes are expected after the second round?
   A. All at the intermediate density
   B. Half heavy and half light
   C. One quarter heavy and three quarters light
   D. Half at the intermediate density and half light

14. A patient's gene is analyzed. The mRNA from the gene is normal in size on a Northern blot. A Western blot with an antibody against the protein's amino-terminal end shows a single band at 30 kDa; the normal protein is 50 kDa. Which mutation type is most consistent with these results?
   A. A missense mutation that changes one amino acid
   B. A nonsense mutation that creates a premature stop codon
   C. A silent mutation
   D. A mutation that deletes the promoter

**Answers and explanations**

1. **B.** Cutting a circle once gives one linear fragment of the full length, and the number of fragments from a circular plasmid equals the number of cuts. EcoRI alone gives a single 6.0 kb fragment, so there is one site. BamHI gives two fragments, so it has two sites, and the double digest (three fragments) shows that EcoRI cuts inside the 4.0 kb BamHI fragment. [1B · Skill 4] (A a plasmid with no EcoRI site would not be cut and would not give a linear 6.0 kb fragment; C two cuts in a circular plasmid would give two fragments; D three cuts in a circular plasmid would give three fragments.)
2. **B.** The 4.0 and 2.0 kb bands are exactly the BamHI-alone pattern. If the EcoRI site is gone, only BamHI cuts in the double digest, and the 4.0 kb fragment is no longer split into 3.0 and 1.0 kb. [1B · Skill 4] (A losing either BamHI site would leave one BamHI cut plus the EcoRI cut, giving a pair of fragments (1.0 and 5.0 kb, or 3.0 and 3.0 kb) different from 4.0 and 2.0 kb; C an extra site would add a fragment and give more than three bands, not fewer; D if neither enzyme cut, the plasmid would remain circular and the 4.0 and 2.0 kb bands would not appear.)
3. **C.** DNA is negatively charged and moves toward the positive electrode. Because charge per unit length is generally similar for all fragments, separation depends on size: small fragments move through the gel pores most easily and travel farthest. The largest fragment (3.0 kb) stays nearest the well and the smallest (1.0 kb) is farthest. [1B · Skill 4] (A reverses the order: larger fragments move more slowly through the gel; B does not follow fragment size; D DNA has a roughly constant charge-to-mass ratio, so fragments are separated by size and do not travel together.)
4. **B.** A supercoiled plasmid is compact and generally migrates faster than the linear form of the same size. A faint band just ahead of the main one therefore suggests incomplete digestion, a point to confirm with a longer digestion or more enzyme as a control. [1B · Skill 3] (A a second site would have made the main band smaller than 6.0 kb, not left it at 6.0 kb; C BamHI would produce 4.0 and 2.0 kb bands, not a band just ahead of 6.0 kb; D larger fragments travel less far, not farther.)
5. **C.** Unaffected parents with an affected child point to a recessive allele. An affected daughter with an unaffected father rules out X-linked recessive inheritance, so the allele is on an autosome and both parents must be carriers. [1C · Skill 2] (A two unaffected parents cannot have an affected child if the allele is dominant, since each affected person would need an affected parent; B an affected daughter would need an affected father, but the father is unaffected; D a dominant X-linked allele would make at least one parent affected.)
6. **A.** Both parents are Aa, so offspring are 1/4 AA, 1/2 Aa and 1/4 aa. II-3 is unaffected, so aa is excluded and the remaining outcomes are 1/4 AA and 1/2 Aa, which gives a carrier probability of (1/2) / (3/4) = 2/3. [1C · Skill 2] (B ignores the fact that she is known to be unaffected, which removes the aa possibility; C is the probability of an affected child from two carriers, not the carrier probability of an unaffected child; D not every unaffected child of two carriers is a carrier, since one in three is AA.)
7. **A.** The disease frequency is q² = 1/10,000, so q = 0.01, p = 0.99, and the carrier frequency among people is 2pq = 0.0198, about 1/50. The chain is (2/3 that II-3 is a carrier) × (1/50 that the partner is a carrier) × (1/4 for an affected child) = 2/600, which is 1/300. [1C · Skill 2] (B omits the 1/4 chance that two carriers have an affected child; C uses 1/3 instead of 2/3 for the carrier probability of II-3; D uses q² (the disease frequency) for the partner's carrier probability instead of 2pq.)
8. **D.** Allele M appears 2 × 110 + 60 = 280 times among 400 alleles, so p = 0.70 and q = 0.30. Hardy-Weinberg expects MM = 0.49 × 200 = 98, MN = 0.42 × 200 = 84 and NN = 0.09 × 200 = 18. The observed excess of homozygotes and deficit of heterozygotes is the typical signature of inbreeding or other nonrandom mating, which changes genotype frequencies without necessarily changing allele frequencies. [1C · Skill 4] (A the genotype counts must match 98, 84 and 18 for the observed allele frequencies; a total of 200 is true of every sample; B the observed number of heterozygotes (60) is lower than the expected 84, a deficit, not an excess; C a single snapshot of genotype counts does not show a change in allele frequency over time.)
9. **D.** The smallest distance (20) is between A and B, so they share the most recent common ancestor. A and B are each 60 from C, so C branches next, and all three are 100 from D, so D branches first. [1C · Skill 4] (A A and C differ by 60 per 1,000 sites, three times the 20 separating A and B; B C and D differ by 100 per 1,000 sites, the largest distance in the table; C the distances are not all equal; A and B are much closer than any other pair.)
10. **C.** For the D split, d = 100 per 1,000 sites = 0.10, so r = 0.10 / (2 × 50 million) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ per site per year. For A and B, d = 0.02, so t = 0.02 / (2 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁹) = 10 million years. [1C · Skill 4] (A uses d = rt and forgets that both lineages accumulate differences; B is the time at which C split from the lineage leading to A and B; D is the fossil calibration for the deepest split, not the A and B split.)
11. **A.** On this tree, the pattern (present in A, B, D; absent in C) can be explained by one change, a loss in the C lineage, if the ancestor had the trait. Any gain-based explanation requires at least two changes. Parsimony prefers the explanation requiring the fewest evolutionary changes, though it is a guide and not proof. [1C · Skill 2] (B this needs two gains, while a single loss in C accounts for the same pattern; C this needs three separate gains; D a trait inherited from a distant common ancestor does not show recent relationship, and the sequence data place D as the most distant.)
12. **A.** Purifying selection removes harmful changes, so a constrained gene shows fewer differences than the neutral rate predicts. Fewer observed differences, put through the same neutral rate, give a smaller t and so a date that is too young. This is why clocks are generally calibrated on neutral or weakly constrained sites. [1C · Skill 3] (B strong purifying selection removes changes, so it reduces rather than raises the number of differences; C the rate applies to neutral sites; a constrained gene has a lower observed rate; D two lineages are exactly why the factor of 2 appears in d = 2rt.)
13. **D.** After one round, every duplex has one old (¹⁵N) strand and one new (¹⁴N) strand. In the second round each of those strands is copied, producing two intermediate duplexes (one old strand each) and two fully light duplexes: half intermediate, half light. No heavy duplexes remain, because the original heavy strands are always paired with new light strands. [1B · Skill 3] (A this is the result after one round, not two; B a heavy duplex would need two ¹⁵N strands, but ¹⁵N strands are always paired with a new ¹⁴N strand; C this is the conservative model's prediction after two rounds, not the semiconservative one.)
14. **B.** A normal-size mRNA but a shorter protein that still carries the amino-terminal epitope means translation ended early: a premature stop codon (nonsense mutation). The normal mRNA rules out a loss of transcription, and a point change that only swaps one residue would not shorten the chain. (A frameshift might also truncate a protein, but it is not offered here.) [1B · Skill 4] (A this generally leaves the protein at nearly the same size, not shortened by 20 kDa; C a silent change keeps the same amino acid and the full-length protein; D this would be expected to reduce or abolish mRNA, but the mRNA size and presence are normal.)

Praxis Tip: Passage 1 was one idea in four disguises: the number of fragments equals the number of cuts in a circle, and size sets the distance traveled. If you missed these, sketch the plasmid as a circle on scratch paper and place the cuts on it. It takes twenty seconds and removes most of the errors.

Praxis Tip: In the pedigree, the order of the steps was the whole question: mode of inheritance, then the sibling's carrier probability (conditioned on being unaffected), then the partner's carrier frequency (2pq), then the Mendelian 1/4. If you dropped a factor, write the chain as a product before you compute next time.

Praxis Tip: For phylogeny questions, build the tree from the smallest distance outward, then place the trait on the tree. A trait shared by distant relatives is a hint of ancestry or convergence, never a reason to redraw the tree on its own.

Praxis Tip: When a question asks for the "most likely" explanation, list all candidates and count what each assumes. The best answer is usually the one that needs the fewest unsupported events and explains every observation, including the odd faint band.
