# Lesson 22: Reproduction and Development

**What you will be able to do**

- Trace the path of sperm and the structure and function of the male and female reproductive organs.
- Compare spermatogenesis and oogenesis, including the timing of the meiotic arrests, and count chromosomes and chromatids at each stage.
- Explain the hormonal control of the male system and of the ovarian and uterine cycles, including the feedback switches.
- Describe fertilization, cleavage, blastocyst formation, gastrulation and the fates of the three germ layers.
- Explain implantation, the placenta, and the hormones of pregnancy, birth and lactation.
- Describe how differentiation is controlled by gene expression, cytoplasmic determinants, induction and morphogen gradients.

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson covers category 3B (the reproductive system) and category 2C (development and differentiation). It rests on [Lesson 13](chapter:18), where you learned mitosis and meiosis, and on [Lesson 8](chapter:12), because development is gene regulation in action. The hormone logic of [Lesson 16](chapter:22), especially negative and positive feedback, is the key to the menstrual cycle. Passages commonly give a graph of hormone levels across a cycle, a drug that mimics or blocks a hormone, or an experiment that moves or removes cells from an embryo, and ask what follows.

Praxis Tip: For any reproductive hormone question, draw the axis first: hypothalamus (GnRH), anterior pituitary (FSH and LH), then gonad (sex steroids and inhibin), and mark each arrow as stimulating or inhibiting.

**Concept Review**

**Male anatomy.** Sperm are made in the **seminiferous tubules** of the **testes**. The testes hang in the **scrotum**, where the temperature is a few degrees below core body temperature, which sperm production requires. Within the testis there are two key cell types. **Sertoli cells**, inside the tubules, nourish developing sperm, form the blood-testis barrier, and are stimulated by FSH; they also secrete **inhibin**. **Leydig cells**, in the tissue between the tubules, make **testosterone** when stimulated by LH. Sperm leave the testis and mature and are stored in the **epididymis**, travel through the **vas deferens**, merge with the duct of the seminal vesicle in the ejaculatory duct, and exit through the **urethra** (the urethra also carries urine, but not at the same time). The accessory glands add fluid to the semen: the **seminal vesicles** (fructose as fuel, and other components), the **prostate** (an alkaline fluid) and the **bulbourethral glands** (a lubricating fluid before ejaculation). A mature sperm cell is streamlined: a head containing the haploid nucleus capped by the **acrosome** (a vesicle of digestive enzymes derived from the Golgi), a midpiece packed with mitochondria to power movement ([Lesson 5](chapter:8)), and a flagellum with the 9+2 microtubule arrangement ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)).

**Female anatomy.** The paired **ovaries** hold the **follicles**, each of which contains an oocyte. At ovulation the oocyte is released near the opening of an **oviduct** (fallopian tube), which is lined with cilia that sweep it toward the uterus; fertilization typically occurs in the upper part of the oviduct. The **uterus** has a muscular wall, the **myometrium**, and an inner lining, the **endometrium**, which thickens each cycle and is the site of implantation. The **cervix** is the narrow lower end of the uterus, and the **vagina** is the canal that receives sperm and is the birth canal. Breast tissue contains milk-producing glands.

**Gametogenesis.** Gametes are made by meiosis ([Lesson 13](chapter:18)). Humans are diploid (2n = 46), and the gametes are haploid (n = 23).

*Spermatogenesis* is continuous from puberty. A diploid **spermatogonium** divides by mitosis (some daughters stay as stem cells). One grows into a **primary spermatocyte** (2n, with replicated chromosomes), which completes meiosis I to give two **secondary spermatocytes** (n), and meiosis II gives four haploid **spermatids**, which differentiate into mature sperm. Each primary spermatocyte therefore yields four functional gametes, and the whole process takes a couple of months [VERIFY].

*Oogenesis* is different in timing and in the division of cytoplasm.

- Before birth, oogonia enter meiosis and become **primary oocytes**, which stop in **prophase I**. They stay arrested for years, until puberty. A female is born with all the primary oocytes she will have.
- In each cycle, under the influence of the LH surge, one primary oocyte completes meiosis I, giving a large **secondary oocyte** and a tiny **first polar body**. The cytoplasm divides unequally, so that the oocyte keeps nearly all of the nutrients. The secondary oocyte then arrests at **metaphase II** and is ovulated in this state.
- Meiosis II is completed only if a sperm penetrates the oocyte, giving a mature **ovum** and a **second polar body**.

One primary oocyte therefore gives one ovum and (usually) two or three polar bodies, which degenerate. Because the oocyte is arrested for decades, errors in chromosome separation, such as nondisjunction, become more likely with maternal age ([Lesson 13](chapter:18)).

| Feature | Spermatogenesis | Oogenesis |
|---|---|---|
| Starts | Puberty | Fetal life |
| Products per meiosis | 4 sperm | 1 ovum + polar bodies |
| Arrest | None | Prophase I; metaphase II |
| Duration | Continuous | Until menopause |

Praxis Tip: Count the arrests for oogenesis in order: prophase I until puberty, metaphase II until fertilization. Meiosis finishes only when a sperm arrives. That one fact answers a whole category of questions.

**Hormonal control in males.** The hypothalamus releases **gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)** in pulses, which prompts the anterior pituitary to release **FSH** and **LH** ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)). LH acts on Leydig cells to make testosterone. FSH acts on Sertoli cells to support spermatogenesis, and testosterone also contributes locally to sperm production. Testosterone drives the male reproductive tract and secondary sex characteristics, and has anabolic effects on muscle and bone. The system is controlled by **negative feedback**: testosterone inhibits GnRH and LH release, and inhibin from Sertoli cells inhibits FSH. A man who takes anabolic steroids or testosterone from outside will have a low LH and FSH, and as a result his testes make less testosterone and fewer sperm, and may shrink.

**The ovarian and uterine cycles.** The human cycle is about 28 days on average [VERIFY], counted from the first day of menstrual bleeding. It has two linked cycles: the ovarian cycle (in the ovary) and the uterine cycle (in the endometrium).

*Follicular phase (days 1 to about 14).* Rising FSH recruits a group of follicles to grow, and one becomes dominant. The follicle cells make **estrogen**. The theca cells make androgens in response to LH, and the granulosa cells convert them into estrogen in response to FSH. At low to moderate levels, estrogen exerts **negative feedback** on the pituitary, as does inhibin. Estrogen also makes the endometrium thicken (**proliferative phase**). Menstruation, the shedding of the endometrium, occurs in the first days.

*Ovulation (about day 14).* When the dominant follicle has made enough estrogen for long enough, the feedback switches to **positive**: high, sustained estrogen causes a surge of LH (and a smaller surge of FSH). The **LH surge** triggers the completion of meiosis I and the rupture of the follicle. Ovulation follows about a day later.

*Luteal phase (days 15 to 28).* The ruptured follicle becomes the **corpus luteum**, which makes **progesterone** (and some estrogen) under the influence of LH. Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation, turning it into a secretory, nutrient-rich lining (**secretory phase**), and exerts negative feedback on GnRH, FSH and LH. If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum degenerates after about 10 to 14 days [VERIFY], progesterone and estrogen fall, the endometrium is no longer supported, and it is shed as menstruation, while the loss of negative feedback allows FSH to rise and start the next cycle. If an embryo implants, it makes a signal that rescues the corpus luteum (see below).

| Phase | Ovary | Dominant hormone |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1-14 | Follicle grows | FSH, then estrogen |
| About day 14 | Ovulation | LH surge |
| Days 15-28 | Corpus luteum | Progesterone |
| End of cycle | Corpus luteum fails | Hormones fall |

**Hormonal contraception and menopause.** Combined oral contraceptives provide a steady dose of an estrogen and a progestin. This keeps the negative feedback on the pituitary constant, so that the FSH rise and the LH surge do not occur and ovulation is suppressed. At **menopause**, the supply of follicles is exhausted, so ovarian estrogen and progesterone fall; with the loss of negative feedback, FSH and LH levels become high.

Praxis Tip: Estrogen is the only hormone in the cycle that flips from negative to positive feedback. Progesterone, inhibin and testosterone always inhibit. If a graph shows an LH spike, look for the estrogen peak before it.

**Fertilization.** Sperm deposited in the vagina travel through the uterus to the oviduct and undergo **capacitation**, a final maturation that makes them fully motile and able to fertilize. The sperm must pass through the cells surrounding the oocyte and bind to the **zona pellucida**, a glycoprotein layer around the oocyte. Binding triggers the **acrosomal reaction**, in which the acrosome releases its enzymes to digest a path through the zona. A sperm then fuses with the oocyte membrane. Fusion triggers the **cortical reaction**: granules just under the oocyte surface release their contents, and the zona hardens so that other sperm cannot bind (the block to **polyspermy**, which would produce a lethal chromosome number). Fusion also triggers a rise in calcium and the completion of meiosis II. The male and female **pronuclei** then come together, and the diploid **zygote** begins development. Only the sperm nucleus generally enters in a functional way, so mitochondrial DNA is inherited maternally ([Lesson 10](chapter:14)). Chromosomal sex is set by the sperm: X-bearing sperm give XX (female) and Y-bearing sperm give XY (male). **Twins** arise either from two eggs fertilized by two sperm (fraternal, not genetically identical) or from one zygote or early embryo that splits (identical, genetically the same).

**Early embryonic development.**

*Cleavage.* The zygote divides by rapid mitotic divisions that do not involve growth between them. There is little time spent in the gap phases, so the embryo stays about the same size while the cells (**blastomeres**) become smaller and more numerous. After several divisions the embryo is a solid ball, the **morula**.

*Blastocyst.* The morula develops a fluid-filled cavity, becoming the **blastocyst**, which has two parts: the **inner cell mass**, which makes the embryo proper, and the outer layer, the **trophoblast**, which makes the embryonic part of the placenta. Cells of the inner cell mass are **pluripotent** (able to make any cell type of the body, but not the placenta), and are the source of embryonic stem cells. The blastocyst reaches the uterus several days after fertilization and hatches from the zona.

*Gastrulation.* Cells of the embryo move and rearrange to form three **germ layers**, which make all later tissues. This is a defining event of animal development.

| Germ layer | Examples of derivatives |
|---|---|
| Ectoderm | Epidermis, nervous system, neural crest |
| Mesoderm | Muscle, bone, blood, heart, kidneys |
| Endoderm | Lining of gut and lungs, liver, pancreas |

Praxis Tip: Learn the germ layers by the outside, middle and inside. Ectoderm is the outside (skin and nerves, which sense the outside), endoderm is the inside (the tube of the gut and its glands), and mesoderm is everything in between (muscle, bone, blood, kidney).

*Neurulation.* A rod of mesoderm, the **notochord**, signals to the overlying ectoderm, which thickens into the neural plate and folds into the **neural tube**, which becomes the brain and spinal cord. Neural crest cells leave the tube and migrate to form many structures, including peripheral neurons and pigment cells. Failure of the neural tube to close causes neural tube defects such as spina bifida, and adequate folic acid before and during early pregnancy reduces the risk.

**Implantation and the placenta.** About a week after fertilization, the blastocyst attaches to the endometrium and the trophoblast invades it (**implantation**). The trophoblast and the maternal endometrium together form the **placenta**, the organ of exchange. Fetal and maternal blood flow close to each other, and oxygen, nutrients, hormones, antibodies (IgG) and wastes cross by diffusion, facilitated diffusion and active transport ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), but they do not generally mix. Fetal hemoglobin binds oxygen more tightly than adult hemoglobin, so oxygen moves from mother to fetus ([Lesson 18](chapter:24)). The **umbilical cord** has two arteries (carrying deoxygenated blood from the fetus to the placenta) and one vein (carrying oxygenated blood to the fetus). Alcohol, some drugs and some infections cross the placenta and can harm the fetus. Four **extraembryonic membranes** support the embryo: the **amnion** (the fluid-filled sac that cushions the fetus), the **chorion** (which forms the fetal side of the placenta), the **yolk sac** (an early site of blood cell formation) and the **allantois** (which contributes to the umbilical blood vessels).

**Hormones of pregnancy.** If the embryo implants, the trophoblast secretes **human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)**, which resembles LH and keeps the corpus luteum alive, so that progesterone and estrogen continue to be made and the endometrium is maintained. The hormone hCG appears in blood and urine early, and is the basis of pregnancy tests. After the first trimester, the **placenta** itself makes large amounts of progesterone and estrogen, the corpus luteum regresses, and hCG falls. High progesterone and estrogen levels keep the uterus quiet, and also suppress the GnRH axis, so no new cycles occur during pregnancy. Estrogen increases the sensitivity of the uterus to oxytocin late in pregnancy. **Relaxin** loosens the pelvic ligaments.

**Birth and lactation.** Labor is driven by a **positive feedback** loop: stretching of the cervix by the fetal head signals the hypothalamus, and the posterior pituitary releases **oxytocin**, which strengthens uterine contractions, which push the fetus harder against the cervix, which releases more oxytocin ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)). The loop ends with delivery of the baby. Prostaglandins also promote contraction. After birth, the fall of progesterone allows **prolactin** from the anterior pituitary to stimulate milk synthesis, and suckling triggers oxytocin release, causing milk **let-down** (ejection) by contracting cells around the milk ducts. Suckling also suppresses GnRH and so delays ovulation, although it is not a reliable contraceptive.

**Control of differentiation and development.** All cells of the body generally carry the same genome, and cell types differ because they express different genes ([Lesson 8](chapter:12)). The classic experiments in which a nucleus from a differentiated cell was placed in an enucleated egg showed that differentiation usually does not involve permanent loss of genes (the lymphocyte gene rearrangements of [Lesson 21](chapter:28) are an exception). Several mechanisms set cells on different paths.

- **Cytoplasmic determinants** are mRNAs and proteins unequally distributed in the egg. When the cells divide, daughter cells receive different amounts and so follow different fates. In *Drosophila*, mRNA for the protein Bicoid is anchored at one end of the egg, and its protein forms a gradient that specifies the head-to-tail axis.
- **Induction** means that one group of cells signals another and changes its fate, using secreted proteins, receptors and signal transduction ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)). The notochord inducing the neural plate is an example. Signals that act over distance and give different responses at different concentrations are **morphogens**.
- **Homeotic (Hox) genes** are transcription factors arranged along the chromosome in the same order as the body regions they pattern. Mutations can transform one body part into another (for example, legs growing where antennae should).
- **Epigenetic changes**, such as DNA methylation and histone modification, lock in a pattern of gene expression so that a cell remembers its identity through cell division ([Lesson 8](chapter:12)).
- **Apoptosis** is programmed cell death ([Lesson 13](chapter:18)), and sculpts structures, such as separating the fingers by removing the cells between them.

Cells lose potency as development proceeds: the zygote and the earliest blastomeres are **totipotent** (able to make a whole organism including the placenta), the inner cell mass is **pluripotent**, and adult stem cells are **multipotent** (limited to a family of cell types, as hematopoietic stem cells are). Stem cells can divide to renew themselves and also to produce differentiating daughters. Differentiated cells can be reprogrammed into **induced pluripotent stem cells** by forcing the expression of a small set of transcription factors. Sex determination also relies on gene expression: in humans the *SRY* gene on the Y chromosome encodes a transcription factor that directs the gonad to become a testis. The testis then makes testosterone and a factor that causes regression of the female duct system, and without these signals the default pathway is female.

**Worked Example 1: Counting chromosomes and DNA (skill: meiosis)**

A human primary spermatocyte has 46 chromosomes. How many chromosomes and how many chromatids are present in (a) the primary spermatocyte, (b) a secondary spermatocyte, and (c) a spermatid? How many sperm result if 100 primary spermatocytes complete meiosis, and how many ova if 100 primary oocytes complete meiosis (including the second division)?

(a) The DNA has replicated, so there are 46 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids: 92 chromatids. (b) Meiosis I separates homologs, so the secondary spermatocyte has 23 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids: 46 chromatids. (c) Meiosis II separates the sister chromatids, so a spermatid has 23 chromosomes of 1 chromatid each. 100 primary spermatocytes give 100 × 4 = 400 sperm, while 100 primary oocytes give 100 ova (each with 2 or 3 polar bodies, which are not functional gametes). The asymmetry is the point of oogenesis: all the nutrients are concentrated in a single large cell.

**Worked Example 2: Reading a cycle graph (skill: data and feedback)**

A graph shows hormone levels across 28 days. On day 6, FSH is moderate and estrogen is low and rising. At day 13, estrogen reaches its peak, and on day 14 there is a sharp spike in LH. On days 20 to 22, progesterone is at its peak. Identify what is happening on each of these days, and predict what a drug that blocks estrogen receptors in the pituitary would do.

Day 6 is the follicular phase: FSH is driving follicle growth, and the follicle is beginning to make estrogen. The estrogen peak on day 13 produces the positive-feedback LH surge on day 14, which triggers ovulation. Days 20 to 22 are the middle of the luteal phase, when the corpus luteum is making progesterone and the endometrium is secretory. A drug that blocks estrogen receptors in the pituitary would prevent estrogen from exerting its feedback, so there would be no positive-feedback LH surge and ovulation would fail. Because the negative feedback is also blocked, baseline FSH and LH would tend to rise.

**Worked Example 3: Cleavage and cell size (skill: scaling)**

A zygote of diameter 100 μm undergoes 5 rounds of cleavage with no growth. How many cells are present, and what is the volume of each cell as a fraction of the zygote volume? How does the total surface area change?

After 5 divisions there are 2⁵ = 32 cells. The total volume is unchanged, so each cell has 1/32 of the volume of the zygote. Each cell is smaller, so the ratio of surface area to volume of each cell is larger, and the total surface area of all cells combined is larger than that of the original zygote (each cell's linear dimension is about 32^(1/3), roughly 3.2 times, smaller, and the total surface area increases by roughly 3.2 times). Cleavage thus restores a nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio typical of ordinary somatic cells, and increases the cell contacts that allow signaling, without any need for new synthesis of cytoplasm.

**Worked Example 4: Interpreting an induction experiment (skill: research design)**

In a model embryo, a researcher cuts a small piece of tissue from region X and grafts it under ectoderm in a different region. The ectoderm there forms neural tissue. Control grafts of tissue from region Y do nothing. When region X tissue is placed in a dish separated from the ectoderm by a fine filter that lets proteins pass but not cells, neural tissue still forms. What do the results suggest?

Region X contains an **inducer**: it changes the fate of the ectoderm, so the ectoderm is not already committed. Region Y serves as a control for the effect of the grafting itself. Neural tissue forms even across the filter, so the signal is a diffusible molecule (a secreted protein or small molecule) and does not need direct cell contact. A further experiment would identify the molecule, for example by adding the purified candidate protein or blocking it with an antibody.

**Worked Example 5: Pregnancy hormones (skill: reasoning from feedback)**

A woman has a hormone profile with hCG rising from about two weeks after her last ovulation. (a) What is the source of the hCG? (b) What would happen to the pregnancy if the corpus luteum were removed at 6 weeks, and at 14 weeks?

(a) The trophoblast of the implanted embryo. hCG acts like LH and rescues the corpus luteum from degeneration. (b) At 6 weeks the placenta has not yet taken over, so the pregnancy depends on the corpus luteum for progesterone, and removal would be expected to cause loss of the pregnancy unless progesterone were given. At 14 weeks, the placenta makes enough progesterone and estrogen itself, so the pregnancy would be expected to continue. The timing of the switch is the reason why the first trimester is the vulnerable one.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: In a feedback question, ask which hormone is high before the event. An LH surge follows an estrogen peak, menstruation follows a progesterone fall, and hCG protects the corpus luteum.

Praxis Tip: The phrase "same genome, different expression" is the answer to many development questions. A transplant, a mutant or a drug changes what is expressed, not what is present, unless the question describes a mutation or a rearrangement.

Praxis Tip: Pluripotent cells cannot make a placenta, and totipotent cells can. Multipotent means "family of cells". The words are tested more often than the details of the examples.

Praxis Tip: For a graft or transplant experiment, ask whether the tissue acted as a source of a signal (induction) or as a source of a determinant that was already present (autonomous fate). A filter experiment separates the two.

Praxis Tip: Meiosis in females has arrests, and male meiosis does not. If a question asks what stage a cell is in at ovulation, the answer is metaphase II (a secondary oocyte).

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying that an oocyte completes meiosis at ovulation; it finishes meiosis II only after sperm entry.
- Placing the LH surge after ovulation rather than before it, or forgetting that estrogen causes it.
- Mixing up Sertoli cells (FSH, support, inhibin) with Leydig cells (LH, testosterone).
- Calling the corpus luteum a hormone-secreting structure of the pituitary; it is the remnant of the ovarian follicle.
- Thinking that fetal and maternal blood mix in the placenta.
- Assuming that differentiated cells have lost genes; they have generally lost the ability to express them, not the genes themselves.
- Reversing the germ layers, for example placing the nervous system in the mesoderm.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** The LH surge allows meiosis I to finish, and the secondary oocyte is ovulated arrested in metaphase II. Sperm entry triggers the completion of meiosis II. (B this arrest is before the LH surge, and ovulation occurs after meiosis I is completed; C meiosis II is not completed until a sperm enters; D a zygote exists only after fertilization.)
2. **B.** In the late follicular phase, high estrogen levels switch the feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary from negative to positive, causing the LH surge. (A this occurs early in the cycle, when estrogen is low and feedback is negative; C this occurs after ovulation, and is supported by LH from the pituitary; D this follows the fall in progesterone and estrogen, not the high estrogen level.)
3. **D.** Testosterone inhibits GnRH and LH release (and so FSH support), reducing the testicular stimulation that supports sperm production. (A exogenous testosterone inhibits LH through negative feedback, which lowers Leydig cell stimulation; B low LH and FSH reduce testicular stimulation, and the Sertoli cells are not driven harder; C with FSH and intratesticular testosterone low, sperm production generally falls.)
4. **C.** hCG is secreted by the trophoblast after implantation. It acts like LH and keeps the corpus luteum making progesterone until the placenta takes over. (A it is made by the embryonic trophoblast, and the corpus luteum makes progesterone; B this describes the LH surge and not the hormone that is detected in pregnancy; D it is made from implantation onward and declines after the first trimester.)
5. **B.** The endoderm forms the lining of the gut and respiratory tract and associated organs such as the liver and pancreas. (A this layer makes the epidermis and the nervous system; C this layer makes muscle, bone, blood and kidneys; D the trophoblast is part of the blastocyst that forms the placenta, and it is not a germ layer.)
6. **D.** Cleavage divides the large zygote into smaller cells by rapid mitosis, with little gap phase and no increase in overall size. (A cleavage divisions are mitotic and increase the cell number; B cleavage divides existing cytoplasm without net growth of the embryo; C each daughter receives a full diploid set.)
7. **A.** Because the nucleus was reprogrammed by the egg cytoplasm and directed normal development, the differentiated cell had retained its full set of genes. (B a normal tadpole could not develop from a nucleus that had lost genes; C egg cytoplasm provides determinants and factors that reprogram the transplanted nucleus; D the result concerns the genome that the nucleus carries and does not show that the adult cell itself was totipotent.)
