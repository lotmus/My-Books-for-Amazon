# Lesson 2: Carbohydrates and Lipids

**What you will be able to do**

- Use glycosidic linkage type to explain why starch and glycogen store energy while cellulose and chitin build structure.
- Describe how sugars are attached to proteins and lipids, and what glycoproteins do on the cell surface.
- Relate fatty acid structure to melting point, storage and signaling, and explain why triacylglycerols are the body's main energy reserve.
- Predict how fatty acid composition, temperature and cholesterol change membrane fluidity.
- Trace how lipids travel in blood as lipoproteins, and match fat-soluble vitamins and steroid hormones to their functions.

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson covers categories 1A and 1D: the structure and function of biological molecules, and the molecules that fuel metabolism. Book 1, Lesson 25 taught the chemistry: monosaccharide stereochemistry, anomers, reducing sugars, the main lipid classes, and fatty acid melting points. This lesson does not repeat those facts. It asks how that chemistry is used by living systems: why one glycosidic bond gives fuel and another gives fiber, how a sugar on a protein works as an address label, how a membrane stays fluid at different temperatures, and how insoluble lipids get around in watery blood. Passages tend to give you a composition, a transition temperature or a lipid panel and ask what it means. The lipid transport story also leads directly to the metabolism lessons ([Lesson 4](chapter:7) and [Lesson 6](chapter:9)).

Praxis Tip: For each carbohydrate or lipid, hold three things: the linkage or bond, the physical property that follows, and the biological job. A question rarely tests more than one jump, so know where each jump lands.

**Concept Review**

**Storage versus structure.** Glucose polymers differ only in the linkage and the branching, yet their roles are opposite.

| Polymer | Linkage and shape | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Starch (amylose, amylopectin) | α(1→4), α(1→6) branches | Plant energy storage |
| Glycogen | α(1→4), many α(1→6) branches | Animal energy storage |
| Cellulose | β(1→4), straight chains | Plant cell wall |
| Chitin | β(1→4) N-acetylglucosamine | Fungal walls, arthropod shells |

The α linkages bend the chain into a helical coil, giving a compact, accessible store that enzymes can digest. The β(1→4) linkages flip each successive sugar, so the chain stays straight and many chains lie side by side held by hydrogen bonds, forming strong insoluble fibers. Humans make amylase, which cuts α(1→4) bonds, but no enzyme for β(1→4) cellulose; gut microbes in some animals supply it. In a small number of people lactase, which cuts the β linkage of lactose, becomes scarce after childhood, causing lactose intolerance. Chitin shows that structure polymers need not be pure glucose: its unit is a glucose carrying an N-acetylamino group (an amino sugar).

**Why glycogen is branched.** Glycogen is highly branched, roughly one α(1→6) branch every ten or so residues. [VERIFY] The chain has one **reducing end** (its single free anomeric carbon, usually attached to a protein core) and many **nonreducing ends**. Enzymes that mobilize glycogen, such as glycogen phosphorylase, act on nonreducing ends one residue at a time. A branched polymer therefore offers many ends working in parallel, so glucose can be released fast when the body needs it, and a branched molecule is also more soluble and compact than a long linear one. Each branch point creates one extra nonreducing end, so a molecule with b branch points has b + 1 nonreducing ends.

**Sugars on proteins and lipids.** Attaching carbohydrate to other molecules makes **glycoconjugates**.

- **Glycoproteins** carry short, often branched oligosaccharides. In **N-linked** glycosylation the sugar is attached to the amide nitrogen of an asparagine side chain, and it begins in the endoplasmic reticulum with a pre-assembled block of sugars, then is trimmed and extended in the Golgi. In **O-linked** glycosylation sugar is attached to the hydroxyl oxygen of serine or threonine, typically added in the Golgi. Sugars help proteins fold, protect them from proteases, and act as targeting signals.
- **Cell-surface glycans** act as identity tags. The ABO blood groups differ in the terminal sugar of oligosaccharides displayed on red cells, and antibodies recognize those sugars. Sialic acid, a negatively charged sugar, often caps surface chains. **Lectins** are proteins that bind specific sugars, mediating cell-cell recognition and attachment.
- **Proteoglycans** have long unbranched chains of repeating disaccharides, called **glycosaminoglycans**, attached to a core protein. Their many negative charges attract water and cations, forming the hydrated gel of cartilage and the extracellular matrix, which resists compression.
- **Glycolipids** carry sugars on a lipid anchor in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane.
- **Nonenzymatic glycation** is different: glucose sticks to proteins without an enzyme, in proportion to its concentration. The fraction of hemoglobin carrying glucose (hemoglobin A1c) therefore reports average blood glucose over the lifetime of red cells, about two to three months.

Praxis Tip: Enzymatic glycosylation happens in a compartment with a defined order (endoplasmic reticulum, then Golgi) and on defined residues (Asn, Ser, Thr). Glycation is random chemistry that follows concentration. If a stem says "enzyme" and "Golgi," it means glycosylation.

**Fatty acids in biology.** Book 1 gave the melting-point rules. Here are the extensions.

- Fatty acids are written as number of carbons : number of double bonds, and double bond positions can be counted from the carboxyl end (Δ, as in 18:1 Δ9) or from the methyl end (ω or n, as in ω-3 and ω-6).
- Humans cannot make double bonds beyond a certain point in the chain, so the polyunsaturated **linoleic** (ω-6) and **α-linolenic** (ω-3) acids are **essential fatty acids** that must come from the diet. [VERIFY]
- **Arachidonic acid** is a 20-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acid released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A₂. It is the precursor of the **eicosanoids** (prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes), local signals that regulate inflammation, pain, fever and clotting. Cyclooxygenase makes prostaglandins and is inhibited by aspirin and other NSAIDs (Book 1, Lesson 24).
- **Trans** double bonds are straight, so partially hydrogenated trans fats pack like saturated fats and have high melting points.
- A longer chain has more van der Waals contact and a higher melting point, if saturation is the same.

**Triacylglycerols as the energy reserve.** Triacylglycerols (TAGs) are stored in droplets in **adipocytes** (fat cells). The reasons they are the main long-term store:

1. Fatty acid chains are highly reduced (mostly C–H bonds), so oxidation releases about 37 kJ/g, compared with about 17 kJ/g for carbohydrate or protein. [VERIFY approximate values]
2. TAGs are nonpolar and are stored without water, so a gram of fat is a gram of fuel. Glycogen binds a large amount of water, so a gram of hydrated glycogen holds much less energy than a gram of fat.
3. They are chemically stable and can be stored in almost unlimited quantity, unlike glycogen, which is limited to the liver and muscle.

The tradeoff is that fat mobilization is slower than carbohydrate use. Fatty acids released from TAG by lipases cannot be converted to glucose in animals and, in general, are not used well by the brain. [VERIFY] The brain relies mainly on glucose, and on ketone bodies during prolonged fasting ([Lesson 6](chapter:9)).

**Membranes and fluidity.** The cell membrane is a **fluid mosaic**: a bilayer of amphipathic lipids in which proteins float. Phospholipids move quickly sideways within a leaflet (lateral diffusion) but flip between leaflets very slowly without help, so the two leaflets have different compositions (for example, phosphatidylserine is generally kept in the inner leaflet, and its appearance on the outer surface can act as a signal on dying cells). Protein and carbohydrate arrangement is covered in [Lesson 12](chapter:17).

Fluidity matters because proteins must move and conformational changes must occur. A pure phospholipid bilayer changes from a rigid gel to a fluid state at a **transition temperature (Tm)**. What changes Tm?

| Factor | Effect on fluidity | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| More cis double bonds | Increases | Kinks prevent tight packing |
| Longer saturated chains | Decreases | More van der Waals contact |
| Higher temperature | Increases | More thermal motion |
| Cholesterol | Buffers | See below |

**Cholesterol** is a fluidity buffer. Its rigid ring system lies among the first few carbons of the phospholipid tails, with its small hydroxyl group near the polar heads. At warm temperatures it restrains the motion of the chains and makes the membrane less fluid and less permeable. At low temperatures it disrupts regular packing and keeps the membrane from freezing into a gel. The sharp transition is therefore broadened. Organisms also adjust composition: bacteria and cold-blooded animals grown in the cold generally increase the unsaturated fraction of their membrane lipids, which restores fluidity ("homeoviscous adaptation").

**Steroids and signaling lipids.** All steroids share the four-ring skeleton. Cells make cholesterol from acetyl-CoA, and the rate-limiting enzyme, HMG-CoA reductase, is the target of statin drugs. From cholesterol come the steroid hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, estrogens, progesterone, testosterone), bile salts and, in the skin with sunlight, vitamin D. Because steroid hormones are nonpolar, they cross membranes and generally bind **intracellular receptors**; the hormone-receptor complex acts as a transcription factor, changing gene expression. The response is therefore slower but longer-lasting than a response to a water-soluble hormone acting at a surface receptor ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)).

**Bile salts and digestion.** Dietary fat arrives in large drops that enzymes cannot easily reach. Bile salts, made from cholesterol in the liver and stored in the gallbladder, are amphipathic: they **emulsify** fat into smaller droplets and help form **micelles** that carry digested fats and fat-soluble vitamins to the intestinal wall for absorption. A micelle is a single-layer sphere with nonpolar tails inside; a bilayer has two layers enclosing water. If bile flow is blocked, fat absorption falls and so does absorption of the fat-soluble vitamins.

**Lipoproteins: moving fat through blood.** Lipids are insoluble in plasma, so they are packaged in **lipoproteins**: a core of triacylglycerol and cholesteryl esters wrapped in a single layer of phospholipids, free cholesterol and **apolipoproteins** (proteins that stabilize the particle and act as address labels for receptors). The more protein and the less lipid, the **denser** the particle.

| Particle | Main cargo and path | Density |
|---|---|---|
| Chylomicron | Dietary TAG from gut, via lymph | Lowest |
| VLDL | TAG made by the liver, to tissues | Very low |
| LDL | Cholesterol to tissues (from VLDL) | Intermediate |
| HDL | Collects cholesterol, returns it to liver | Highest |

Lipoprotein lipase on capillary walls removes fatty acids from chylomicrons and VLDL for use or storage. As VLDL loses TAG it becomes LDL. Cells take up LDL by binding the LDL receptor (it recognizes apolipoprotein B-100 [VERIFY]) and **receptor-mediated endocytosis**. If the LDL receptor is defective, LDL cannot be cleared and blood cholesterol rises, as in familial hypercholesterolemia. HDL, which returns cholesterol from tissues to the liver for excretion, is why high HDL is generally associated with lower cardiovascular risk. Because LDL deposits cholesterol in artery walls, it is the lipoprotein most associated with atherosclerosis.

**Fat-soluble vitamins.** Vitamins A, D, E and K are nonpolar, absorbed with dietary fat in micelles, transported in chylomicrons and lipoproteins, and stored in the liver and fat, so excess can accumulate (hypervitaminosis), unlike most water-soluble vitamins, which are excreted.

| Vitamin | Key function | Deficiency sign |
|---|---|---|
| A (retinol) | Retinal in vision; gene regulation | Night blindness |
| D | Raises blood calcium and phosphate | Rickets, soft bones |
| E | Antioxidant in membranes | Rare; nerve and red cell damage |
| K | Carboxylates clotting factors | Bleeding, slow clotting |

Vitamin D is made in skin from a cholesterol derivative by UV light and is activated in the liver and kidney. Vitamin K helps convert glutamate residues in several clotting proteins into γ-carboxyglutamate, which binds calcium. Warfarin works by antagonizing vitamin K recycling. [VERIFY]

Praxis Tip: Fat malabsorption (blocked bile, pancreatic insufficiency, certain intestinal diseases) causes problems that look like four different diseases: bad night vision, weak bones, nerve problems, and bruising. Look for the single cause.

**Worked Example 1: Branching arithmetic (skill: structure to rate)**

A glycogen molecule has 12 α(1→6) branch points and a single reducing end. How many nonreducing ends does it have, and how does this compare with a linear chain of the same number of residues? If phosphorylase removes one residue per end per second, how does the potential release rate compare?

Each branch creates one extra nonreducing end, so the molecule has 12 + 1 = 13 nonreducing ends. A linear chain has only one. In the idealized case in which every end is attacked at once, 13 residues are released per second against 1 for the linear chain, a 13-fold difference. (Real enzymes slow down as they approach a branch point, so the real gain is smaller; the principle is what matters.) This is why branching suits glycogen, whose job is rapid mobilization.

**Worked Example 2: Energy per gram (skill: comparing stores)**

Take 37 kJ/g for fat and 17 kJ/g for dry glycogen. (a) How many times more energy does 1 g of fat contain than 1 g of dry glycogen? (b) If glycogen is stored with about 2 g of water per gram, what is the energy per gram of hydrated glycogen, and how does fat compare? (c) A person needs 8,000 kJ per day. Roughly how much fat would cover a day?

(a) 37 / 17 ≈ 2.2. (b) One gram of dry glycogen with 2 g of water makes 3 g of material holding 17 kJ, so about 17 / 3 ≈ 5.7 kJ per gram; fat (stored essentially dry) still gives 37 kJ per gram, about 6.5 times more per gram of tissue. (c) 8,000 / 37 ≈ 216 g of fat. The numbers are rounded, but the conclusions (about two times on a dry basis and several times on a hydrated basis) hold.

**Worked Example 3: Membrane composition (skill: reading a transition temperature)**

Three artificial membranes were studied. Membrane A, made of a saturated 16-carbon phospholipid, melted sharply at 41 °C. Membrane B, made of a phospholipid with one cis double bond in each tail, melted sharply at about −3 °C. Membrane C, made like B but with 30 mole percent cholesterol, showed no sharp transition at all, only a gradual change over a wide range. (a) Explain A versus B. (b) What does C show? (c) A bacterium is moved from 37 °C to 15 °C. What change in its lipids would help it?

(a) The cis kinks in B prevent tight packing, so less thermal energy is needed to fluidize it. A, with straight saturated tails, packs tightly and needs a higher temperature. (b) Cholesterol disrupts the regular packing that allows a sharp gel transition and also limits chain motion in the fluid state: it buffers fluidity over a range of temperatures. (c) Raising the fraction of unsaturated (or shorter) fatty acids in its phospholipids would keep its membrane fluid at the lower temperature.

**Worked Example 4: A lipid panel (skill: linking lipoproteins to function)**

A patient has very high LDL cholesterol since adolescence and cells that fail to bind LDL. Fasting triglycerides are normal. Which step is most likely at fault, and why are the triglycerides normal?

Failed binding of LDL points to a defective LDL receptor, which prevents receptor-mediated uptake and clearance of LDL, leaving it to circulate and rise. Triglycerides are mainly carried by chylomicrons and VLDL, whose clearance depends on lipoprotein lipase rather than the LDL receptor, so they remain normal. A fasting sample has few chylomicrons, which makes a normal triglyceride level even more expected.

**Worked Example 5: Matching molecule to location (skill: following a signal)**

A hormone is a derivative of cholesterol. A second hormone is a short peptide. Compare where each binds and how fast each acts.

The cholesterol derivative is nonpolar, crosses the plasma membrane, binds an intracellular receptor, and changes transcription: the effect takes hours but lasts. The peptide is polar and cannot cross the membrane; it binds a surface receptor and triggers a second messenger cascade or phosphorylation: seconds to minutes, and usually shorter lived.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: When you see α(1→4) think "digestible fuel," and when you see β(1→4) think "fiber." The α/β letter decides whether humans can digest it.

Praxis Tip: Ask "which end?" in polysaccharide questions. A branched polymer has one reducing end and many nonreducing ends, and enzymes working from the ends gain speed from branching.

Praxis Tip: For fluidity problems, list the effects separately: unsaturation, chain length, temperature, cholesterol. Cholesterol is the only one that acts in both directions.

Praxis Tip: Lipoprotein density runs opposite to size and lipid content. The bigger and fattier, the lighter; chylomicrons float, HDL sinks. Mnemonic: "high density, high protein."

Praxis Tip: Steroid hormones are lipids, which is why they cross membranes. Water-soluble hormones, such as peptides and catecholamines, need surface receptors.

Praxis Tip: If a stem mentions micelles, bile or emulsification, you are in fat digestion. Micelles are single-layer, bilayers are double-layer. The distinction is rarely tested in anger but is an easy point.

Praxis Tip: The fat-soluble vitamins are A, D, E and K. A mnemonic of your own, however unlikely, is better than a perfect mnemonic you never write down.

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying humans cannot digest cellulose because it is a different sugar. It is the same sugar with a different linkage.
- Mixing up glycosylation (enzymatic, in ER and Golgi) with glycation (nonenzymatic).
- Saying cholesterol always makes membranes more fluid (or always less).
- Reversing LDL and HDL roles: LDL delivers cholesterol to tissues, HDL returns it.
- Assuming denser lipoproteins carry more fat. They carry more protein per particle.
- Treating fat as unable to supply energy quickly. It supplies a lot, but mobilization is slower than for carbohydrate.
- Assuming excess water-soluble and fat-soluble vitamins behave alike. Fat-soluble ones accumulate.

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **D.** Asparagine nitrogen plus the endoplasmic reticulum is the signature of N-linked glycosylation. (A is wrong because glycation has no enzyme or compartment and follows glucose concentration; B is wrong because O-linked sugars attach to the oxygen of serine or threonine; C is wrong because a glycosidic bond joins a sugar to another molecule, not two side chains.)
2. **B.** Phosphorylase works from the ends, so more ends mean faster release. (A is wrong because branching does not block digestion and glycogen is mobilized readily; C is wrong because each glucose unit yields the same energy; D is wrong because glycogen is a carbohydrate polymer.)
3. **A.** Cholesterol broadens the transition: it fluidizes the gel and stiffens the fluid state. (B is wrong because cholesterol does not alter the tails' saturation; C is wrong because cholesterol interacts noncovalently; D is wrong because the data concern packing and fluidity, not flip rate.)
4. **C.** A gram of fat has more reduced carbon and no associated water. (A is wrong because oxidized molecules carry less energy, and fats have little oxygen; B is wrong because TAGs are nonpolar and stored in droplets; D is wrong because the energy comes from oxidizing the chains.)
5. **A.** Without receptor-mediated uptake, LDL accumulates in blood. (B is wrong because uptake of LDL by cells would fail, so LDL would build up; C is wrong because fat absorption and chylomicron formation do not depend on the LDL receptor; D is wrong because HDL is not removed by the LDL receptor.)
6. **D.** Bile is needed for micelle formation and absorption of the fat-soluble vitamins, and vitamin K is needed for clotting factors. (A is wrong because vitamin C is water-soluble and bile duct blockage does not impair its absorption; B is wrong because vitamin A affects vision and gene regulation, not clotting; C is wrong because vitamin D regulates calcium, while vitamin K modifies clotting factors.)
7. **B.** Lipid solubility lets steroids enter cells, and their receptors act as transcription factors. (A is wrong because steroids are lipid-soluble and can enter cells; C is wrong because that describes surface-receptor signaling; D is wrong because steroids generally travel bound to carrier proteins.)
