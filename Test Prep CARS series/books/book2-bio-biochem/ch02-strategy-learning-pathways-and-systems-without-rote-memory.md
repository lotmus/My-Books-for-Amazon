# Strategy: Learning Pathways and Systems Without Rote Memory

**What you will be able to do**

- Learn a pathway by its logic (purpose, inputs, outputs, energy, regulation) before its enzyme names.
- Draw a pathway map from memory and use it to predict what happens when a step is blocked.
- Build compare-and-contrast tables and one-page system summaries.
- Schedule redrawing and retrieval so that pathways stay learned.

**Why it matters**

Metabolism, signaling cascades and organ systems look like lists, and lists are what the brain is worst at holding. Students who try to memorize them as lists end up with a pile of names that fall over when a question changes one detail. Students who learn the logic can rebuild the names when needed, and can answer the question that changes the detail. The section rewards the second kind of student: a large share of its questions ask you to use a pathway, not recite it ([Strategy: Know the Biology and Biochemistry Section](chapter:1)).

Praxis Tip: Learn the logic of a pathway before the names. The names are labels on a map. A map you can reason about is worth ten labels you cannot.

**The five questions for any pathway**

Ask these in order, and write the answers in your notebook.

1. **Why does the cell run this?** Its purpose: make ATP, store energy, make a building block, dispose of a waste.
2. **What goes in and what comes out?** Name the starting molecule, the end product and the carriers (for example NAD+ and NADH, or ATP and ADP).
3. **Where does it happen?** Cytosol, mitochondrion, membrane. Location often explains the logic.
4. **What is the energy and electron accounting?** Which steps invest ATP, which yield ATP or reduced carriers.
5. **What turns it on, and what turns it off?** The regulation: hormones, substrates, products and energy state.

Notice that none of the questions is "what is the third enzyme." Enzyme names come last, attached to a map that already makes sense. Many are guessable from the reaction: a kinase adds a phosphate, a dehydrogenase removes electrons and hydrogen, an isomerase rearranges. Book 1, Lesson 24 is the reference for enzyme vocabulary; the point here is to read the name for what it tells you.

**Worked Example 1: Glycolysis by logic**

Apply the five questions to glycolysis.

1. *Purpose.* Extract usable energy from glucose without needing oxygen. It also supplies intermediates for other pathways.
2. *In and out.* Glucose in; two pyruvate out; reduced NADH is made, and ATP is made by substrate-level phosphorylation.
3. *Location.* The cytosol.
4. *Accounting.* The early steps invest 2 ATP (phosphorylating the sugar twice). The later steps produce 4 ATP. Net: 4 minus 2 = 2 ATP per glucose. Two NADH are also made [VERIFY the usual textbook count]. The investment explains why the pathway has an "energy-requiring" phase before it pays out.
5. *Regulation.* The early committed step, catalyzed by phosphofructokinase-1 [VERIFY], is inhibited when the cell has plenty of ATP and activated when AMP is high. So the pathway speeds up when the cell is low on energy. The logic: a pathway that makes ATP should be slowed by ATP.

*Reading the logic.* If a question tells you a cell has abundant ATP and asks what happens to glycolytic flux, you do not need the name of the enzyme. The principle (a high-energy signal throttles an energy-producing pathway) gives the answer: flux decreases.

*Skill used:* generating a prediction from the pathway's purpose and regulation.

Praxis Tip: For each pathway, write the regulation in one sentence of the form "this pathway is turned on by ___ because ___, and off by ___ because ___." If you cannot finish the "because," you have memorized, not learned.

**Regulation logic: the same few ideas**

Most regulation falls into a handful of patterns:

- **Feedback inhibition.** The end product inhibits an early, usually committed, step, which prevents the cell from piling up intermediates.
- **Energy state.** High ATP slows ATP-producing pathways and favors storage; high AMP or ADP speeds ATP-producing pathways.
- **Reciprocal regulation.** Opposing pathways (for example breaking down glucose and making glucose) are generally not run at full speed at the same time. Running both would burn ATP for no net change, a futile cycle.
- **Hormonal control.** The fed-state hormone and the fasting-state hormone push the same pathways in opposite directions ([Lesson 6](chapter:9)).
- **Covalent modification and allostery.** Phosphorylation and effector binding change enzyme activity quickly, while changes in enzyme amount (gene expression) are slower.

**Worked Example 2: A futile cycle**

*Setup.* A cell runs a pathway that phosphorylates a sugar using ATP, and a second pathway that removes the phosphate again without making ATP. Each trip around the cycle uses one ATP. What is the net result of running both at full speed?

*Reasoning.* The sugar returns to its starting form, so there is no net sugar change. One ATP is hydrolyzed per cycle with no ATP made back, so energy is released as heat. Ten cycles per second therefore burn 10 ATP per second for nothing. The cell avoids this by regulating the two enzymes reciprocally: the signal that activates one inhibits the other.

*Skill used:* seeing why reciprocal regulation is logical, not arbitrary.

**Compare-and-contrast tables**

Pathways that look alike are the usual source of swapped facts. Build a table for each such pair: same rows, two columns, filled in from memory first and checked second. Choose rows that force a decision: location, carrier, energy form, key regulator.

**Worked Example 3: Fatty acid oxidation versus synthesis**

| Feature | Oxidation | Synthesis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Release energy | Store energy |
| Location | Mitochondrion [VERIFY] | Cytosol [VERIFY] |
| Electron carrier | NAD+ and FAD, reduced | NADPH, consumed |
| Fed or fasted | Favored when fasting | Favored when fed |

*Reading the table.* The opposite directions have opposite carriers: a pathway that releases energy reduces carriers, and a pathway that builds uses reduced carriers. This is general chemistry in disguise: oxidation of a fuel yields electrons that are captured, and building a reduced molecule such as a fatty acid needs electrons delivered. When a question swaps the carriers, the table tells you something is wrong without any memorized sentence.

*Skill used:* using a contrast table to detect a swapped fact.

Praxis Tip: When two pathways seem to be mirror images, check whether they really are. Compare their locations and carriers first. If they differ in both, the cell is keeping them apart on purpose, and that is the fact the exam asks about.

**Predicting a blocked step**

The strongest use of a pathway map is prediction. The rule is simple. Blocking a step causes:

- the substrate of that step, and upstream intermediates, to accumulate;
- the product, and downstream intermediates, to fall;
- any branch that can bypass the block to carry more flux;
- regulators that sense the changed levels (ATP, NADH, intermediates) to shift other pathways.

**Worked Example 4: Blocking the electron transport chain**

*Setup.* A poison blocks the electron transport chain of cells that are otherwise supplied with glucose and oxygen. Predict four consequences.

*Reasoning.*
1. Reduced carriers can no longer give up their electrons to the chain, so NADH accumulates and NAD+ falls.
2. The proton gradient is no longer built, so ATP synthesis driven by it falls.
3. Pathways that need NAD+, such as the TCA cycle, slow because the oxidized carrier is scarce.
4. Glycolysis is needed for ATP, and to keep it running the cell must regenerate NAD+ some other way: fermentation, which converts pyruvate to lactate in animal cells. So lactate rises.

*Reading the logic.* Four consequences follow from three ideas: carriers shuttle electrons, the chain uses them to build a gradient, and glycolysis needs NAD+. No enzyme names are needed.

*Skill used:* tracing a perturbation through carriers and energy state.

**One-page system summaries**

For organ systems (the kidney, the heart, the endocrine axes), use a fixed template on one page of your notebook:

- the job of the system in one sentence;
- the main structures, in the order that matter functionally;
- the signal that controls it and the feedback that limits it;
- two or three typical perturbations and the predicted result;
- a small sketch.

A page you have to fit on one sheet forces you to decide what matters.

**Worked Example 5: A redraw schedule**

*Setup.* You plan to master twelve pathways and systems this term. A weekly redraw of each takes about 5 minutes, drawn from memory and then checked.

*Calculation.* 12 × 5 = 60 minutes a week, one hour. Add a first redraw on the day after learning, then at 3 days, 7 days and 21 days, as in the review schedules throughout this book. The total cost is small compared with the cost of relearning everything the week before the exam.

*Reading the plan.* The work is in the retrieval, not the rereading: the drawing is attempted before checking. A map you can redraw cold is a map you will use under time pressure.

*Skill used:* converting a study goal into a weekly time budget.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Redraw each pathway weekly from a blank page, then check it. Rereading feels productive and teaches less than a single failed attempt to draw.

Praxis Tip: Follow one thing through a pathway: the carbon, the electrons, or the energy. Each trace shows you a different set of rules, and an exam question usually cares about only one.

Praxis Tip: Learn the committed or regulated step first. It is where most questions are set, because it is where the cell makes decisions.

Praxis Tip: When you learn an enzyme name, say aloud what the name predicts ("kinase: adds phosphate"). If the name does not predict anything, tag it for a flashcard.

Praxis Tip: Write "if blocked, what piles up?" beside each step on your first drawing. It turns a diagram into a tool.

Praxis Tip: Keep a one-page "regulators" sheet listing each pathway, its activator and its inhibitor. Seven lines of it will answer a surprising number of questions.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. Which first step is the best way to begin learning a new metabolic pathway?
   A. Memorize the enzymes in order
   B. Memorize the structures of every intermediate
   C. State its purpose, its inputs and outputs, and what regulates it
   D. Read the chapter again without notes

2. Why would a cell regulate the pathway that breaks down a sugar and the pathway that makes the same sugar reciprocally, rather than running both at full speed?
   A. Running both would hydrolyze ATP with no net change in the sugar, wasting energy
   B. Both pathways need the same enzymes, so they cannot be present together
   C. Neither pathway uses ATP
   D. The sugar is destroyed in the process

3. Glycolysis invests 2 ATP in its early phase and produces 4 ATP in its later phase per glucose. What is the net ATP gain per glucose?
   A. 4
   B. 2
   C. 6
   D. 0

4. A drug inhibits the enzyme that catalyzes step 4 of a linear pathway running from A to a final product E. Assuming no bypass, which is the most likely effect?
   A. The final product E accumulates
   B. All intermediates fall equally
   C. Nothing changes because other steps compensate
   D. The substrate of step 4 accumulates and the final product E falls

5. Compared with fatty acid oxidation, fatty acid synthesis generally
   A. consumes reduced carriers (NADPH), where oxidation produces reduced carriers
   B. produces large amounts of NADH
   C. takes place in the same compartment and with the same carriers
   D. is favored when the cell is fasting

6. Which study method is most likely to make a pathway stay learned?
   A. Rereading the same page several times in one evening
   B. Highlighting the enzyme names
   C. Redrawing it from memory on a schedule and then checking the drawing
   D. Copying the diagram while looking at it

7. End-product feedback inhibition usually acts on an early, committed step of a pathway. What is the main advantage?
   A. It speeds up the pathway when product is plentiful
   B. It makes the final step irreversible
   C. It removes the need for any other regulation
   D. It stops the cell from accumulating intermediates and wasting resources when enough product exists

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** Purpose, inputs and outputs, and regulation give a framework onto which names can later be attached. (A puts labels before the logic that makes them reconstructible; B is the heaviest load for the least reasoning payoff; D rereading builds familiarity, not recall.)
2. **A.** A futile cycle spends ATP and returns the sugar to where it started, so cells control the two directions reciprocally. (B they generally use some different enzymes; the problem is wasted energy, not enzyme absence; C at least one step in the cycle is described as costing ATP; D the cycle returns the sugar to its starting form.)
3. **B.** 4 produced minus 2 invested gives a net of 2. (A ignores the 2 ATP invested; C adds invested and produced ATP; D assumes investment cancels production.)
4. **D.** A block raises the substrate and upstream intermediates and lowers the downstream product. (A reverses the direction of the block; B only intermediates after the block fall; C a linear pathway with no bypass cannot compensate.)
5. **A.** Building a reduced molecule needs electrons delivered by reduced carriers, so synthesis uses NADPH while oxidation yields reduced carriers. (B oxidation, not synthesis, is the producer; C the two pathways are generally kept apart by location and carrier; D synthesis is favored in the fed state.)
6. **C.** Attempting retrieval before checking, repeated over days, is what builds durable memory. (A produces familiarity without testing recall; B marks labels without testing logic; D does not require retrieval.)
7. **D.** Throttling the first committed step prevents piling up intermediates and spending resources on product that is not needed. (A feedback inhibition slows the pathway; B it controls early flux, not irreversibility of the last step; C cells use several regulatory layers.)

Praxis Tip: Check whether each wrong choice reverses a direction (accumulates versus falls), swaps two pathways or ignores energy state, and log which. The same slip repeats.

**Common mistakes**

- Memorizing enzyme names in order before understanding the purpose of the pathway.
- Rereading a diagram instead of redrawing it.
- Assuming opposing pathways run simultaneously at full speed.
- Predicting the wrong direction for a blocked step (the substrate accumulates; the product falls).
- Ignoring the energy and electron-carrier accounting.

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "For any pathway I ask its purpose, its inputs and outputs, its location, its energy accounting and its regulation; I learn the regulated step and the logic before the names; I contrast look-alike pathways in tables; and I redraw from memory on a schedule so that I can predict what a block does."

*Review schedule:* redraw glycolysis from memory after 1 day; after 3 days, redo Worked Example 4; after 7 days, build a contrast table for a new pair of pathways; after 21 days, explain the five questions aloud.

*Interleave:* alternate pathway drawing with the experiment reading in [Strategy: Reading Biology Experiments](chapter:3), since both train prediction.

**Do this today**

1. Pick one pathway you half-know and answer the five questions in your notebook.
2. Draw it from memory, then check it, then mark what you missed.
3. Build one compare-and-contrast table for two pathways you confuse.
4. Add three redraw dates to your calendar.

**Recap**

Learn a pathway by purpose, inputs and outputs, location, energy accounting and regulation. The same handful of regulatory patterns recurs. Use contrast tables for look-alikes and one-page summaries for systems. Predict blocked steps by asking what piles up and what falls. Retrieve by redrawing, on a schedule.
