**Timing and conditions**

Fourteen original questions drawn from [Lessons 1 to 6](chapter:4): protein structure and hemoglobin, carbohydrates and lipids, nucleic acid structure, bioenergetics and glycolysis, the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, and the other pathways with their control (categories 1A, 1B and 1D). Several questions deliberately reach back to Book 1, Lesson 16 (acid-base chemistry), Lesson 23 (amino acids) and Lesson 24 (enzymes and kinetics), because the exam does not announce which book a question comes from. Allow about 22 minutes, in one sitting, with scratch paper and no notes or calculator. Write your answer letters, with a confidence level (high, medium or low) for each, on scratch paper or in your notebook; an ebook cannot be written in. Check only when you have finished.

Praxis Tip: In a table of experimental conditions, read the left column as the order of events before you read any numbers. Then ask of each row what changed from the row above. Most questions about such a passage are really "what does this one change tell you?"

Praxis Tip: When a passage gives you an inhibitor or a hormone, write on scratch paper the three things it should do before you look at the data: which step is blocked, what piles up before it, and what runs short after it. A prediction made first is much harder to talk yourself out of.

Praxis Tip: Metabolism questions are won on direction, not on memorized numbers. If you cannot recall an exact value, decide only whether the quantity should go up, down or stay put. The options usually differ in direction.

**Questions**

**Passage 1 (questions 1-4)**

A researcher places isolated, intact liver mitochondria in a buffered medium with an oxygen electrode. Pyruvate, malate and phosphate are present throughout, and the chamber is kept well stirred. Substances are added one after another to the same chamber, in the order listed, and the rate of oxygen consumption (as nmol of oxygen atoms per minute) and the rate of ATP synthesis are recorded during the first minute after each addition. In stage 2 the ADP supply is not limiting. Stage 3 adds oligomycin, stage 4 adds 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP), stage 5 adds rotenone, and stage 6 adds succinate. Because additions accumulate, in stages 4 to 6 every earlier substance is still present. The investigator ran a control without mitochondria and found no oxygen consumption in any stage, so all the changes below are due to the mitochondria.

| Stage | Addition | O consumed | ATP made |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | None (substrates only) | 10 | 0 |
| 2 | ADP | 50 | 125 |
| 3 | Oligomycin | 12 | 0 |
| 4 | DNP | 80 | 0 |
| 5 | Rotenone | 5 | 0 |
| 6 | Succinate | 55 | 0 |

Units for ATP made are nmol per minute.

1. Between stage 1 and stage 2, oxygen consumption rises fivefold. The best explanation is that ADP
   A. is oxidized by Complex I in place of NADH
   B. carries protons across the inner membrane in the way DNP does
   C. allows protons to flow back through ATP synthase, draining the gradient and releasing the chain from respiratory control
   D. stimulates glycolysis, which supplies more NADH

2. Oxygen consumption falls to 12 in stage 3 and then rises to 80 in stage 4. These two stages together indicate that in stage 3 the fall was caused by
   A. direct inhibition of Complex IV by oligomycin
   B. exhaustion of ADP in the chamber
   C. DNP supplying additional electrons to the chain
   D. restraint of electron flow by a gradient that could not be dissipated, with the chain itself intact

3. Using the values in stage 2, the ratio of ATP made to oxygen atoms consumed is closest to
   A. 2.5, as expected when NADH is the source of electrons
   B. 0.4
   C. 1.5
   D. 3.0

4. After rotenone abolishes most oxygen consumption in stage 5, succinate restores much of it in stage 6. This result shows that, in the presence of rotenone,
   A. rotenone inhibits Complex III as well as Complex I
   B. succinate restores ATP synthesis, because the chain is working again
   C. Complexes III and IV and cytochrome c remain functional, and electrons can enter the chain through Complex II
   D. rotenone acts on Complex II, so succinate bypasses the block

**Passage 2 (questions 5-8)**

Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) was purified from rat liver, and its initial rate was measured at several concentrations of fructose 6-phosphate (F6P). Three series were run. In the first, ATP was kept low. In the second, ATP was high. In the third, ATP was high and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP) was also added. All other conditions were identical, and every series used the same amount of enzyme. Rates are reported as a percentage of the largest rate the preparation can give, as estimated by the investigator from separate measurements at very high F6P. The enzyme is a tetramer, and in every series the reaction mixture also contained magnesium ions, so that only the factors named above differ.

| F6P (mM) | Low ATP | High ATP | High ATP + F2,6BP |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 20 | 1.5 | 5.9 |
| 1 | 50 | 5.9 | 20 |
| 2 | 80 | 20 | 50 |
| 4 | 94 | 50 | 80 |
| 8 | 98.5 | 80 | 94 |

In the investigator's notes, all three series rose more steeply than a simple Michaelis-Menten curve at low F6P.

5. Comparing the low-ATP and high-ATP series, the data are best described by saying that high ATP
   A. lowers the maximum rate that can be reached, as a noncompetitive inhibitor would
   B. raises the F6P concentration needed for half-maximal rate (from about 1 to about 4 mM) without lowering the maximum
   C. increases the rate, as an activator would
   D. permanently destroys part of the enzyme

6. At 2 mM F6P, the rate in the low-ATP series is how many times the rate in the high-ATP series?
   A. 2
   B. 4
   C. 0.25
   D. 1.6

7. The pattern in the third series (high ATP plus F2,6BP) would be expected in liver in which state?
   A. Early fasting, with high glucagon
   B. Prolonged fasting, with glycogen exhausted
   C. The fed state, with high insulin
   D. Any state in which citrate is high

8. The investigator wants to learn whether F2,6BP works by raising the maximum rate or only by increasing the apparent affinity for F6P. Which experiment is most informative?
   A. Measure the rate with and without F2,6BP at only 0.5 mM F6P
   B. Increase the ATP concentration further and compare
   C. Add urea to unfold the enzyme and then add F2,6BP
   D. Measure the rate with and without F2,6BP at several F6P concentrations well above 8 mM, and see whether the rates converge

**Passage 3 (questions 9-12)**

Hemoglobin was purified from adult and from newborn blood and stripped of all small molecules. Samples were then equilibrated with oxygen at 37 degrees C in the buffers described below, and the oxygen pressure at which each sample was half saturated (P50) was measured. Where listed, 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) was added at a concentration similar to that inside red blood cells. The values are approximate. The pH was held constant within each sample by a buffer of high capacity, and the temperature and the total hemoglobin concentration were the same in all samples, so that the only differences between samples are those shown in the table.

| Sample | Hemoglobin | pH, 2,3-BPG | P50 (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Adult | 7.4, none | 14 |
| 2 | Adult | 7.4, added | 26 |
| 3 | Adult | 7.2, added | 32 |
| 4 | Fetal | 7.4, added | 19 |

For comparison, the investigator measured myoglobin under the same conditions, as a control for the oxygen electrode and the buffer, and found a P50 of about 3 mmHg, with no change when 2,3-BPG was added.

9. Comparing samples 1 and 2, which conclusion is best supported?
   A. 2,3-BPG lowers the oxygen affinity of adult hemoglobin, consistent with stabilizing the T state
   B. 2,3-BPG raises the oxygen affinity of adult hemoglobin
   C. 2,3-BPG lowers affinity by competing with oxygen for the heme iron
   D. 2,3-BPG has no effect, because the P50 is far above that of myoglobin in both samples

10. Comparing samples 2 and 4, which conclusion is best supported?
   A. Under these conditions, fetal hemoglobin has the higher oxygen affinity, so oxygen would tend to move from adult to fetal hemoglobin
   B. Fetal hemoglobin has the lower oxygen affinity, so it releases oxygen to maternal blood
   C. Fetal hemoglobin binds 2,3-BPG more strongly than adult hemoglobin
   D. Fetal hemoglobin is insensitive to pH

11. Comparing samples 2 and 3, which conclusion is best supported, and what does it imply for exercising muscle?
   A. Lower pH raises oxygen affinity, so hemoglobin in working muscle holds oxygen more tightly
   B. Lower pH lowers oxygen affinity (the Bohr effect), so hemoglobin in acidic, working muscle unloads more oxygen
   C. Lower pH increases the amount of 2,3-BPG bound, which accounts for the change
   D. The pH effect requires 2,3-BPG

12. The pH effect on hemoglobin arises largely from groups that gain or lose a proton in the physiological pH range. Which side chain is most likely to contribute the most?
   A. Aspartate, whose side chain has a pKa of about 4
   B. Lysine, whose side chain has a pKa of about 10.5
   C. Leucine
   D. Histidine, whose side chain has a pKa of about 6

**Answers and explanations**

1. **C.** With ADP and phosphate available, ATP synthase lets protons return to the matrix, so the gradient falls and electron flow speeds up. This is respiratory control: oxidation is coupled to phosphorylation. [1D · Skill 4] (A ADP is not an electron donor to the chain; the electrons come from NADH made by pyruvate and malate; B ADP is not a lipid-soluble proton shuttle, and in stage 2 the protons returned through ATP synthase, as the 125 of ATP made shows; D these isolated mitochondria contain no glycolytic enzymes; pyruvate and malate are already present in stage 1.)
2. **D.** Oligomycin blocks the proton channel of ATP synthase (F0). The gradient builds until it opposes further pumping, so oxygen consumption falls. DNP then carries protons across the membrane, removes the restraint, and oxygen consumption rises above even the ADP-stimulated rate. The chain was working throughout. [1D · Skill 4] (A if the chain were blocked, DNP could not have restored oxygen consumption in stage 4; B ADP was not limiting, and its supply could not explain why DNP then raises the rate; C DNP is a proton carrier that dissipates the gradient; it is not an electron donor, which is why ATP made stays at zero.)
3. **A.** 125 nmol of ATP divided by 50 nmol of oxygen atoms is 2.5. Pyruvate and malate are oxidized to give NADH, whose electrons pass through Complexes I, III and IV (about 10 protons pumped, about 4 per ATP). [1D · Skill 4] (B is the inverse of the ratio asked for (oxygen divided by ATP); C is the value expected for electrons entering from FADH2, not NADH; D is the value in some older textbooks; the data give 125/50, which is 2.5.)
4. **C.** Rotenone blocks Complex I only. Succinate is oxidized by succinate dehydrogenase (Complex II), which passes electrons to coenzyme Q and onward to Complexes III and IV. Restoration of oxygen uptake therefore shows the downstream chain is intact. This is a standard way to locate a block in the chain. [1D · Skill 3] (A if Complex III were blocked, electrons from succinate could not reach oxygen and the rate would stay low; B ATP made stays at 0 in stage 6 because DNP is still present and dissipates the gradient; D rotenone blocks NADH oxidation at Complex I; succinate is oxidized by Complex II, which feeds coenzyme Q beyond Complex I.)
5. **B.** Half-maximal rate (50) occurs at 1 mM F6P in the low-ATP series and at 4 mM in the high-ATP series, while the rates at high F6P approach one ceiling. This is the pattern of an allosteric inhibitor that lowers the apparent affinity for the substrate. It is the same logic as a change in Km with Vmax unchanged (Book 1, Lesson 24). [1D · Skill 4] (A the rates in the high-ATP series keep climbing toward the same ceiling (80 at 8 mM and rising), so what shifts is the half-maximal concentration; C rates in the high-ATP series are lower than in the low-ATP series at every F6P concentration; D that would lower the maximum rate in proportion at all concentrations, rather than move the concentration at which the half-maximal rate occurs.)
6. **B.** 80 divided by 20 is 4. [1D · Skill 4] (A is the F6P concentration itself, not a ratio of rates; C is the inverse of the ratio asked for; D compares the low-ATP rate (80) with the series containing F2,6BP (50).)
7. **C.** Insulin raises F2,6BP in liver, and F2,6BP is the most powerful activator of PFK-1: it activates glycolysis and inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, the gluconeogenic bypass (Lesson 6). It relieves part of the ATP inhibition, as in the third column. [1D · Skill 2] (A glucagon acts through protein kinase A to lower F2,6BP, which would make PFK-1 even less active; B the liver is then making glucose, and low F2,6BP is part of what favors gluconeogenesis over glycolysis; D citrate is an inhibitor of PFK-1 and would shift the curve to the right, like high ATP, not partly back to the left.)
8. **D.** At saturating substrate, a change in apparent affinity disappears, whereas a change in maximum rate does not. If the rates converge at high F6P, the maximum is unchanged and F2,6BP acts on affinity. This is the same logic used to distinguish competitive from noncompetitive inhibition. [1D · Skill 3] (A at a single low concentration, a change in affinity and a change in maximum rate both change the rate, so the two cannot be told apart; B this changes the inhibitor, not the activator being tested; C a denatured enzyme has no activity to compare, so no conclusion about a native enzyme is possible.)
9. **A.** A higher P50 means lower affinity (a right shift). 2,3-BPG binds the central cavity of deoxygenated hemoglobin and stabilizes the T state, so oxygen is held less tightly. [1A · Skill 4] (B the P50 rises from 14 to 26 mmHg, which means that more oxygen pressure is needed for half saturation; C 2,3-BPG binds in the central cavity of the deoxygenated tetramer; it does not bind the heme iron; D the P50 differs between samples 1 and 2, and the comparison with myoglobin does not address the effect of 2,3-BPG.)
10. **A.** Both samples contain 2,3-BPG at pH 7.4, and fetal hemoglobin reaches half saturation at the lower oxygen pressure. Higher affinity lets oxygen pass from maternal to fetal blood across the placenta. [1A · Skill 4] (B a lower P50 (19 versus 26 mmHg) means higher affinity, not lower; C fetal hemoglobin generally binds 2,3-BPG less strongly, which is why its P50 is lower with 2,3-BPG present; D no sample of fetal hemoglobin was measured at a second pH, so the data cannot show this.)
11. **B.** The only variable that changed between samples 2 and 3 is pH, and P50 rose. Protons binding to hemoglobin favor the T state and release oxygen. Working muscle is acidic, so it pulls oxygen off hemoglobin. [1A · Skill 4] (A P50 rises from 26 to 32 mmHg at the lower pH, which indicates lower affinity; C the same 2,3-BPG concentration was added to both samples; only the pH changed; D both samples contained 2,3-BPG, so the data cannot show that the pH effect depends on it.)
12. **D.** The groups whose charge changes with a small change in pH near 7 are those with pKa values nearest 7. For free amino acids that is histidine (imidazole, pKa about 6), and in hemoglobin some histidines in particular environments are important in the Bohr effect (Book 1, Lesson 23; Book 1, Lesson 16). [1A · Skill 2] (A is deprotonated at both pH 7.2 and 7.4, so its charge does not change in this range; B is protonated at both pH values, so its charge does not change in this range; C has a nonpolar side chain with no ionizable group.)
13. **A.** Chargaff's rules (A = T and G = C) hold only when every base has a partner on the other strand. Here A does not equal T and G does not equal C, so the strands are not fully paired; and thymine means DNA. [1B · Skill 2] (B in double-stranded DNA, A equals T and G equals C regardless of overall composition; C the genome contains thymine, not uracil; D the genome contains thymine, not uracil, and the bases do not pair equally.)
14. **B.** Cells take up LDL by binding the LDL receptor and receptor-mediated endocytosis. Without it, LDL stays in the blood and its cholesterol accumulates, as in familial hypercholesterolemia. [1D · Skill 2] (A the receptor is how cells take LDL up, so loss of the receptor slows clearance; C chylomicrons are assembled in the intestine from dietary fat and do not depend on the LDL receptor; D this process needs the LDL receptor, so it would fall.)
