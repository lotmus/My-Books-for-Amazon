# Strategy: Pacing and Test Day for Passage-Heavy Biology

**What you will be able to do**

- Turn the section's published format into a pacing plan with checkpoints at the question level and at the passage level.
- Budget time for an experiment passage, including the reading, and bank time on independent questions.
- Handle an unfamiliar protein, pathway or organism with a fixed five-step routine instead of panic.
- Triage passages as well as questions, and recover from a bad passage before it becomes a bad section.
- Run the test-day checklist from Core Test Skills without rereading the whole plan the night before.

**Why it matters for the exam**

The Biological and Biochemical section is the second one you sit, and it is the most passage-heavy of the four in the sense that matters: most of its questions are attached to a block of text, a table or a figure that you have never seen. Book 1 gave you a pacing plan for the Chemical and Physical section (Book 1, Chapter 38). The arithmetic is similar, but the delays are different. In Book 1 the usual time sink was a long calculation. Here it is a long, unfamiliar description: a protein you have never heard of, a pathway drawn as boxes and arrows, a result stated in six sentences. A student who knows every lesson in this book can still lose ten minutes to one passage that "looks scary."

This chapter is the delivery plan. It is a companion to the general plan in Core Test Skills, "Your Test-Day Plan", which remains the reference for the week, the night and the morning. You will meet its checklist in summary below. Read this chapter twice: once now, and once in the week before the exam.

Praxis Tip: When you meet an unfamiliar protein, ask what the passage tells you about it. The passage is an open-book exam about itself; you are never asked to have memorized a protein that only exists in a research paper.

**Pacing: the arithmetic**

With 59 questions in 95 minutes, the average is 95 / 59 = 1.61 minutes per question, about 1 minute 37 seconds. That average hides two very different kinds of question.

- An **independent question** has no passage. It tests one fact or one short chain of reasoning. Most can be answered in 30 to 60 seconds, and a few take longer.
- A **passage question** comes with a reading cost. The first question in a set quietly pays for the reading; the later ones are cheaper.

Because the independent questions are quick, you can plan to bank time on them and spend it on passages.

Worked Example: Budgeting a section.

Assume 15 independent questions and 10 passages with 44 questions in all.

Step 1. Give each independent question about 1 minute: 15 × 1 = 15 minutes.
Step 2. That leaves 95 − 15 = 80 minutes for the passages.
Step 3. Per passage: 80 / 10 = 8 minutes on average.
Step 4. A passage of 4.4 questions on average. Spend about 2.5 minutes reading and mapping, leaving 5.5 minutes for 4.4 questions, which is 5.5 / 4.4 = 1.25 minutes per question.

A four-question passage therefore gets about 7 to 7.5 minutes (2.5 + 4 × 1.15). A six-question passage gets about 9.5 to 10 minutes (2.5 + 6 × 1.2). Notice that a six-question passage is the better deal per question, because the reading is paid once. Do not rush it.

*Skill used:* converting a format into a per-passage budget.

The questions are not numbered by type, so you will not know in advance where the independent questions fall. Treat the plan as a guide to the average and use the checkpoints below.

| Checkpoint | Questions finished | Time elapsed (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Quarter | 15 | 24 min |
| Half | 30 | 48 min |
| Three-quarters | 45 | 72 min |
| End | 59 | 95 min |

Praxis Tip: Do not check the clock after every question. Check at your checkpoints. Constant clock-watching costs a few seconds each time and a good deal of calm.

**Passage-level checkpoints**

Question-level checkpoints tell you whether you are on schedule. Passage-level checkpoints tell you why not. During a practice exam, note two times for every passage: when you finished reading and mapping, and when you finished the last question. After a few exams you will see a pattern, such as "my reading takes 4 minutes on pathway passages but 2 on data passages." That pattern tells you where to train.

- **Reading and mapping: about 2 to 3 minutes.** If you are past 4 minutes and still reading, stop. Write the one-sentence question of the experiment, the manipulation and the result, and go to the first question. You can return to the text for detail.
- **First question: about 2 minutes.** It often pays for confusion left over from the reading.
- **Each later question: about 1 to 1.25 minutes.** You already own the map.
- **Hard stop: about 10 minutes for any one passage.** If you pass the stop, record your best answer to every remaining question and move on.

Praxis Tip: Reading time belongs to the passage, not to a separate pot. A passage that took 4 minutes to read has 4 minutes less to answer with; the clock does not care which half you were slow in.

**Passage triage: which set first?**

On the computer-based exam you can generally move within the section, though you should confirm the navigation rules on the official page. [VERIFY] That lets you triage passages as well as questions. You do not need to do them in printed order.

A simple triage that costs under 15 seconds per passage:

1. Glance at the first lines and the first figure. Is it a pathway you know, a data table, a gel, a graph, or a wall of text?
2. Count the questions in the set. A passage with six questions is worth more than one with four, for the same reading cost.
3. Rate it **A** (I see the topic and the shape of the data), **B** (partly familiar) or **C** (I do not recognize the topic or the figure type).

Do the A passages as you meet them. Take B passages normally. For a C passage with only four questions, consider skipping it on the first pass, marking its questions to find again, and returning with whatever time is left. Do not skip a C passage with six questions without a good reason: it is a lot of points to defer.

A C passage is usually less hard than it looks; the next section shows why.

Praxis Tip: Triage is a decision, not a feeling. If you cannot decide in 15 seconds, call it B and begin. A passage you started beats a passage you contemplated.

**A plan for an unfamiliar protein, pathway or organism**

The exam deliberately includes topics you did not study. It does so on purpose, to test whether you can reason from a passage plus the basic science in this book. Use the same five steps every time.

1. **Give it a role.** Label the thing by what the passage says it does, not by its name: "the kinase", "the binder", "the transporter", "the repressor". Write the label on scratch paper beside the name. The name is a tag; the role is the information.
2. **Find the verbs.** Circle the verbs the passage uses about it: binds, phosphorylates, cleaves, inhibits, exports, degrades. The verbs fix what kind of molecule it is.
3. **Slot it into a map.** Put it in a pathway you already know: signal in, signal out; substrate to product; gene to RNA to protein. Where does it sit relative to what is known? Upstream or downstream of what?
4. **Use the perturbation.** Look for what happened when it was removed, blocked or overexpressed. This is almost always the information the questions are built on ([Strategy: Reading Biology Experiments](chapter:3)).
5. **Answer from the data, then check with basic science.** Pick the choice the passage supports. Then ask whether it breaks a basic rule you know, such as "enzymes do not change equilibrium" or "a protein needs a start codon".

Worked Example: An unfamiliar protein.

A passage introduces a protein named Brx1. It says Brx1 binds the phosphorylated form of a transcription factor and keeps it in the cytoplasm. Cells lacking Brx1 show high expression of the factor's target genes. A question asks what happens to target-gene expression if Brx1 is overexpressed.

Step 1. Role: "the cytoplasmic anchor". Step 2. Verb: binds, keeps in the cytoplasm. Step 3. Slot: it sits between the signal that phosphorylates the factor and the nucleus, where a transcription factor must go to act. Step 4. Perturbation: without the anchor, expression is high. Step 5. More anchor would hold more factor in the cytoplasm, so target-gene expression generally falls. Check: nothing here contradicts basic science, since transcription factors act in the nucleus.

You did not need to know what Brx1 is. You needed its role, its verb and the perturbation. The name was decoration.

*Skill used:* reasoning about an unfamiliar protein from role and perturbation.

Praxis Tip: Replace an unfamiliar name with a role label the first time you see it, and use only the label thereafter. By the third mention a name like Brx1 is a roadblock; a label like "anchor" is a handle.

Praxis Tip: If the passage gives a pathway as boxes and arrows, label each arrow with a plus (activates) or a minus (inhibits) before you read any question. Half of the questions will turn on a sign.

**Questions: the triage rule**

Not every question deserves your full effort. In the first read of a question, decide:

1. **Answer now.** You see the path, and it will take under about two minutes.
2. **Flag and move.** You see a path but it is long, or you can narrow to two choices and cannot decide. Record your best answer, flag it, and go on. A recorded answer beats a blank one.
3. **Guess and move.** You cannot see a path. Eliminate what you can, pick one, and go on. Wrong answers are generally not penalized beyond earning no credit [VERIFY], so never leave a question blank.

Come back to flagged questions if time remains. Change an answer only when you find a specific reason, such as a misread axis label or a missed sign on an arrow, not because of a vague feeling. Likewise, there is no rule that your first instinct is right or that your second one is; what holds up is a stated reason.

Praxis Tip: The question you cannot do costs the same on the score as the one you can. Four minutes on it is a bad trade when three easier ones are waiting.

**The test-day checklist, imported from Core Test Skills**

The full plan, with the reasoning, is in Core Test Skills, "Your Test-Day Plan". It is not reproduced here. The headings are summarized below as a reference; confirm every rule against the exam owner's current instructions. [VERIFY]

*One week before.* Confirm registration, ID, location and arrival time. Take your last full-length simulation earlier than this week. Use this week for light review of your error log, your pathway maps and your technique notes, and for rest.

*Two days before.* Pack the bag: ID, confirmation, any allowed items, an extra layer, and snacks and water as permitted. Lay out clothes. Stop new material.

*The night before.* Eat a normal dinner. Stop studying early. Set two alarms.

*Morning.* Eat the breakfast you have practiced. Leave with a buffer of at least 30 minutes.

*In the room.* Settle in. If the rules allow, write your checkpoints on the scratch material you are given.

*Breaks.* Use the toilet, drink, eat, move about. Do not replay the last section. It is finished.

If you need testing accommodations, apply early. The paperwork can take weeks, and no study technique helps if you have missed the deadline.

Praxis Tip: Pack the bag the night before and put it by the door. Searching for your ID at 7:15 a.m. is a form of exam stress you can simply decline.

**Stress without drama**

A little arousal sharpens attention; a lot narrows it. Three habits keep you on the useful side:

- **Name it.** "My heart is racing; that is the normal reaction."
- **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Four counts in, six counts out, three times. It takes about thirty seconds.
- **Anchor to the next action.** Not "I'm failing this passage" but "Write the role label for the first protein."

In this section the commonest trigger is the passage that looks like a foreign language. Remember that it is meant to. You are expected to use the passage plus basic science. Read it as a puzzle with all the pieces on the table.

Praxis Tip: Practice discomfort on purpose, early on: take a few timed sets slightly hungry, in a noisy room, or one coffee short. It builds range. Stop doing it in the final week, when comfort and routine are the aim.

**Handling a bad passage, or a bad section**

A passage will occasionally go badly. A section sometimes does. The aim is to stop one bad passage from becoming two, and one bad section from becoming two.

1. At the hard stop, record your best answers and say the words "that one is done."
2. Breathe out slowly, once. Look at the clock only at your next checkpoint.
3. Pick up speed on the next passage by doing its quickest questions first, not by skimming its text.
4. Between sections, do not estimate your score from how it felt. The exam deliberately includes hard material, so a hard section is normal, not diagnostic.

Praxis Tip: If you are behind at a checkpoint, do not sprint. Collect two or three easy questions in the next set and let the hard ones wait. Speed gained by skimming the text is paid back in misread data.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Write units and directions beside every number in the data: "up", "down", "no change". Biology data usually asks you to compare, and a comparison is a direction before it is a size.

Praxis Tip: On the last two minutes, fill every remaining blank. A guess has a chance; a blank has none.

**Common mistakes**

- Reading the rules the night before.
- Reading a hard passage three times before writing anything down.
- Letting one unfamiliar name stall the whole set.
- Checking the clock after every question.
- Spending ten minutes to "prove" a single answer.
- Changing answers on a feeling rather than a reason.
- Trying a new food or drink on exam morning.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. At 1.6 minutes per question, how many minutes does a 30-question practice part take, and where should you be at Question 15?
2. In the worked budget, 15 independent questions get 1 minute each. How many minutes are left for 10 passages, and how many per passage?
3. Why is a six-question passage a better deal than a four-question one, for the same reading?
4. Name the five steps of the plan for an unfamiliar protein, in order.
5. A passage says protein Q "binds and sequesters" a hormone in the blood. What role label would you give Q, and what would losing Q probably do to the free hormone level?
6. When should you hold a passage to a hard stop, and what do you do at it?
7. Name two things to do between sections.

**Stop here.** Answer each question in your notebook before reading the answers.

**Answers and reasons**

1. 48 minutes (30 × 1.6). At Question 15 you should be near 24 minutes (15 × 1.6). These are averages, not guarantees for any one question.
2. 95 − 15 = 80 minutes, and 80 / 10 = 8 minutes per passage on average.
3. The reading cost is paid once per passage, so the more questions share it, the lower the cost per question. It does not mean you can ignore the clock.
4. Give it a role; find the verbs; slot it into a map; use the perturbation; answer from the data and check against basic science.
5. "The carrier" or "the binder". If Q sequesters the hormone, removing Q would generally leave more hormone free, and the effect depends on what the passage says about bound versus free hormone.
6. Hold every passage to about 10 minutes, or the limit you set in practice. At the stop, record your best answer to each remaining question and move on, or flag them for a return if time remains.
7. Breathe out slowly, say "that section is done", drink water or eat as allowed, and avoid replaying questions.

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

In your notebook, explain to an imaginary friend how to turn 59 questions and 95 minutes into four question-level checkpoints and one passage budget. Then walk through the five-step plan on a protein name you invent. After two days, one week and three weeks, redo the budget arithmetic from memory, and apply the five steps to a new invented name.
