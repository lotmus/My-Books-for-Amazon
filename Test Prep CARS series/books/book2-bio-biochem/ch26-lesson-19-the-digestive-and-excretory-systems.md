# Lesson 19: The Digestive and Excretory Systems

**What you will be able to do**

- Trace a meal through the gastrointestinal tract and name the secretions, enzymes and hormones that act at each stage.
- Explain how carbohydrates, proteins and fats are digested and absorbed, and why fats take a different route from sugars and amino acids.
- Describe what the liver, gallbladder and pancreas contribute to digestion and metabolism.
- Follow filtrate through the nephron and say what is filtered, reabsorbed and secreted at each segment, including the countercurrent mechanism.
- Predict how ADH, aldosterone, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and atrial natriuretic peptide change urine volume and blood pressure.
- Use the bicarbonate buffer and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to classify acid-base disturbances and their compensation.

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson covers category 3B, organ systems, and it is the place where Book 1 chemistry is most visible. Enzymes that work at pH 2 or pH 8 ([Lesson 4](chapter:7)), secondary active transport driven by the sodium gradient ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), osmosis and colligative properties, buffers (Book 1, Lesson 16) and fluid pressure (Book 1, Lesson 4) all appear again. Passages often describe a patient, a drug or a lab value and ask which segment of the gut or nephron is affected and what the body does next. The hormones here connect to [Lesson 16](chapter:22), and the pressure and volume consequences connect to [Lesson 17](chapter:23) and [Lesson 18](chapter:24).

Praxis Tip: For every organ in this lesson, ask what it moves (a solute, water, acid), in which direction, and what hormone changes the amount. Most questions are the same three-part question in different clothes.

**Concept Review**

**The gastrointestinal tract: an overview.** The digestive tract is a long tube, running from the mouth to the anus, whose lumen is technically outside the body. Its wall has layers: an inner **mucosa** (epithelium that secretes and absorbs), a **submucosa**, layers of **smooth muscle** and an outer covering. The muscle produces **peristalsis** (waves that push food along) and **segmentation** (mixing contractions). The enteric nervous system coordinates these movements, and the parasympathetic system generally increases digestive activity while the sympathetic system generally decreases it ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)).

| Region | Main action | Key secretions |
|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Chewing; starch digestion begins | Salivary amylase, lingual lipase |
| Stomach | Acid, protein digestion, mixing | HCl, pepsin, mucus |
| Small intestine | Most digestion and absorption | Pancreatic enzymes, bile, brush-border enzymes |
| Large intestine | Water and electrolyte absorption | Mucus; bacteria |

**Mouth and esophagus.** **Salivary amylase** begins to cleave starch into shorter chains, and saliva contains mucus and a little lingual lipase. Peristalsis carries the swallowed bolus down the esophagus.

**The stomach.** Stomach secretions come from several cell types, a classic exam list.

| Cell | Product | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Parietal cell | HCl, intrinsic factor | Acidifies; intrinsic factor allows B12 uptake |
| Chief cell | Pepsinogen | Inactive precursor of pepsin |
| Mucous cell | Mucus and bicarbonate | Protects the wall |
| G cell | Gastrin | Hormone that stimulates acid secretion |

The parietal cell pumps protons into the lumen with an H⁺/K⁺-ATPase ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), bringing the gastric pH to roughly 1 to 2 [VERIFY]. Acid denatures proteins (making them better substrates), kills many microbes, and converts **pepsinogen** into **pepsin**, which is itself able to activate more pepsinogen (positive feedback). Pepsin is a protease with an acidic pH optimum, and it cleaves proteins into shorter polypeptides. A thick mucus layer rich in bicarbonate protects the stomach from its own acid and enzyme. Gastric motility turns the meal into a semi-liquid called **chyme**, released slowly through the pyloric sphincter into the duodenum. Loss of the parietal cells (as in an autoimmune gastritis) causes poor B12 absorption and a form of anemia ([Lesson 17](chapter:23)).

**Hormones of digestion.** Several hormones are released by endocrine cells in the stomach and small intestine, and the pattern is easier to learn by function.

| Hormone | Released by | Main effects |
|---|---|---|
| Gastrin | Stomach (G cells) | Increases HCl secretion and motility |
| Secretin | Duodenum, in response to acid | Pancreas and bile ducts release bicarbonate |
| Cholecystokinin (CCK) | Duodenum, in response to fat and protein | Gallbladder contracts; pancreas releases enzymes; slows gastric emptying |
| GIP | Duodenum, in response to glucose and fat | Enhances insulin release |

Praxis Tip: Match each hormone to its stimulus and its fix. Acid in the duodenum calls for bicarbonate (secretin). Fat in the duodenum calls for bile and enzymes (CCK). The names almost tell you: cholecystokinin moves the gallbladder.

**The pancreas.** The pancreas has two jobs. Its endocrine islets release insulin and glucagon ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)). Its **exocrine** cells release digestive juice into the duodenum through the pancreatic duct: a bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralizes acid chyme (so that pancreatic enzymes, which prefer a neutral to slightly basic pH, can work), together with the enzymes below.

| Enzyme | Substrate | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Pancreatic amylase | Starch | Maltose and short oligosaccharides |
| Pancreatic lipase (with colipase) | Triglycerides | Free fatty acids and 2-monoglyceride |
| Trypsin, chymotrypsin | Proteins, polypeptides | Smaller peptides |
| Carboxypeptidase | Peptide ends | Shorter peptides and amino acids |
| Nucleases | DNA, RNA | Nucleotides |

Proteases are made as inactive **zymogens** (trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidase), so that they do not digest the pancreas. The intestinal brush-border enzyme **enteropeptidase** converts trypsinogen to **trypsin**, and trypsin then activates the other zymogens, an example of a cascade ([Lesson 4](chapter:7) on regulation by proteolytic cleavage). Premature activation inside the pancreas contributes to pancreatitis.

**The liver, gallbladder and bile.** The liver receives blood from the intestine through the **hepatic portal vein**, so everything absorbed except most fat is processed by the liver before it reaches the general circulation. Its functions include:

- **Bile production.** Bile contains **bile salts** (derived from cholesterol), phospholipids, cholesterol and bilirubin. The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile, and CCK makes it contract. Bile salts are amphipathic, and they **emulsify** fat, breaking large droplets into small ones that increase the surface area for lipase. Bile is not an enzyme and does not chemically digest anything. Most bile salts are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and returned to the liver (the enterohepatic circulation).
- **Metabolic control.** The liver stores glycogen, performs gluconeogenesis, makes ketone bodies and lipoproteins, and carries out the urea cycle that converts toxic ammonia to urea ([Lesson 6](chapter:9)).
- **Protein synthesis and detoxification.** It makes albumin (the main plasma protein supporting oncotic pressure) and most clotting factors, and it modifies drugs and toxins, often making them more water soluble.
- **Bilirubin handling.** The liver conjugates bilirubin, a product of heme breakdown from old red cells, and secretes it in bile. Failure of this process, or blockage of the bile duct, causes jaundice, a yellow tint of the skin.

**The small intestine: digestion and absorption.** The small intestine has three parts: the **duodenum** (where chyme meets bile and pancreatic juice), the **jejunum** and the **ileum**. Its absorptive surface is enormous, because the wall is folded, covered in **villi**, and each epithelial cell carries **microvilli** (the brush border). Each villus contains capillaries and a lymph vessel called a **lacteal**. Brush-border enzymes, including lactase, sucrase, maltase and peptidases, finish the digestion of small oligomers right at the surface.

*Carbohydrates.* Starch is digested by amylase to disaccharides, and brush-border disaccharidases produce monosaccharides. **Glucose and galactose** enter the epithelial cell by the sodium-glucose cotransporter (SGLT1), a secondary active transporter ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), and leave across the basolateral membrane by facilitated diffusion (GLUT2). **Fructose** enters by a facilitated transporter (GLUT5). All three go to the liver through the portal vein. Lactose intolerance results from too little lactase: undigested lactose draws water into the lumen (osmosis) and is fermented by colonic bacteria, producing gas.

*Proteins.* Pepsin, then the pancreatic proteases, then brush-border peptidases reduce proteins to amino acids and small peptides (di- and tripeptides). These are taken up by sodium- or proton-coupled cotransporters (secondary active transport), and leave the cell as amino acids into the portal blood.

*Fats.* Fat absorption is more complicated because lipids are insoluble in water. Bile salts emulsify the fat, lipase produces fatty acids and monoglycerides, and these mix with bile salts to form **micelles**, which carry the products to the epithelial surface. The products diffuse across the membrane, and inside the cell they are reassembled into triglycerides, packaged with cholesterol and proteins into **chylomicrons** ([Lesson 6](chapter:9)), which are too large for capillaries. Chylomicrons leave by exocytosis, enter the **lacteals**, travel in lymph and reach the blood through the thoracic duct. They therefore bypass the portal vein and the liver at first. (Short-chain fatty acids are generally absorbed directly into the portal blood.) Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are absorbed with the fat, so fat malabsorption can cause vitamin deficiencies, for example poor clotting from lack of vitamin K.

*Other substances.* Water follows solute osmotically, so most fluid entering the gut is absorbed in the small intestine. Iron is absorbed mainly in the duodenum, and B12 (bound to intrinsic factor) in the terminal ileum.

Praxis Tip: Sugars and amino acids go in through sodium-coupled transporters and ride the portal vein to the liver. Fats go in as micelles, return as chylomicrons and ride the lymph. If a question asks where an absorbed nutrient goes first, this one fork usually decides it.

**The large intestine.** The colon absorbs most remaining water and electrolytes, compacts the residue and houses bacteria that make some vitamin K. Diarrhea can cause large losses of fluid and bicarbonate.

**The kidney: structure.** The kidneys filter blood, return most of what is filtered and excrete wastes, excess ions and water. Each kidney has an outer **cortex** and an inner **medulla**. Its functional unit is the **nephron**, about a million per kidney [VERIFY]. The parts, in order of flow:

1. **Glomerulus** (a tuft of capillaries) inside **Bowman's capsule**. This is where filtration occurs.
2. **Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT).**
3. **Loop of Henle**: a descending limb and an ascending limb (thin, then thick), dipping into the medulla.
4. **Distal convoluted tubule (DCT).**
5. **Collecting duct**, which receives filtrate from many nephrons and carries urine to the renal pelvis, ureter and bladder.

Blood enters through the **afferent arteriole**, passes through the glomerulus and leaves through the **efferent arteriole**, which then divides into the **peritubular capillaries** and the **vasa recta**, vessels that run next to the tubule and the loop.

**Filtration.** Blood pressure in the glomerular capillary is high, because the efferent arteriole is narrow. By the Starling forces (Book 1, Lesson 4; [Lesson 17](chapter:23)), the hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out through the fenestrated capillary, the basement membrane and the filtration slits between **podocytes**. The barrier filters by size and by charge: water, ions, glucose, amino acids and urea pass, while blood cells and most plasma proteins (albumin, which is large and negatively charged) generally do not. Protein or blood in the urine therefore suggests damage to the filtration barrier. The **glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** is normally about 125 mL/min, around 180 L/day [VERIFY]. Filtration is regulated by the afferent arteriole: stretch in the arteriole wall causes constriction (the myogenic response), keeping GFR fairly constant when the systemic pressure changes.

The GFR is estimated by **clearance**, the volume of plasma completely cleared of a substance per minute. For a substance that is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted (inulin is the classic example), clearance equals GFR:

C = (U × V) / P

where U is the urine concentration, V the urine flow rate and P the plasma concentration. If the clearance of a substance is higher than the GFR, it is being secreted; if lower, it is being reabsorbed.

**Reabsorption and secretion by segment.**

| Segment | Main handling | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| PCT | About two thirds of Na⁺ and water; nearly all glucose and amino acids; most HCO₃⁻ | Isosmotic; secretes H⁺ and some drugs |
| Descending limb | Water leaves | Permeable to water, not to salt |
| Thick ascending limb | Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ leave | Impermeable to water; dilutes the fluid |
| DCT | Na⁺ and Cl⁻ reabsorbed; Ca²⁺ regulated by PTH | Thiazide diuretics act here |
| Collecting duct | Water (ADH), Na⁺ (aldosterone), K⁺ and H⁺ secretion | Final adjustment |

*The proximal tubule* does the bulk work. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase on the basolateral side keeps cell sodium low ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), and sodium coupled to glucose and amino acids (symport) enters the cell from the lumen. Water follows through aquaporins, so the fluid stays isosmotic with plasma (the concentration does not change, though the volume falls). Filtered glucose is normally entirely reabsorbed, but the transporters saturate at a **transport maximum**: if blood glucose is high enough (as in untreated diabetes mellitus), glucose is left in the urine and carries water with it, causing high urine volume ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)). Bicarbonate is reabsorbed with the help of carbonic anhydrase, and the proximal tubule also secretes organic acids and some drugs, a route used to clear many medications.

*The countercurrent mechanism.* The kidney can make urine more concentrated than blood, up to roughly four times plasma osmolarity in humans [VERIFY], because the loop of Henle builds a salt gradient in the medulla. The **descending limb** is permeable to water but not to salt, so water leaves into the salty interstitium and the fluid becomes more concentrated as it descends. The **ascending limb** is impermeable to water, and in its thick part it pumps out Na⁺, K⁺ and Cl⁻ actively (through a Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter that loop diuretics block), so the fluid becomes dilute and the interstitium becomes salty. This is a **countercurrent multiplier**: the opposed flow in the two limbs multiplies a small difference at any one level into a large gradient from cortex to inner medulla. Urea recycling in the collecting duct adds to the medullary osmolarity. The **vasa recta**, which run in hairpins beside the loop, are a countercurrent exchanger: they carry away the water and salt that are reabsorbed without washing out the gradient. In the collecting duct, filtrate that has become dilute by the time it leaves the ascending limb passes through this salty medulla. If the duct is permeable to water, water leaves by osmosis and the urine is concentrated. If it is not, the urine remains dilute.

Praxis Tip: The loop makes the gradient, and ADH decides whether you use it. Without ADH, the collecting duct is water-tight and the urine is dilute, however salty the medulla is.

**Hormonal control of water and sodium.**

*ADH (vasopressin).* ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary ([Lesson 16](chapter:22)). Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect a rise in plasma osmolarity, and baroreceptors detect a large drop in blood volume or pressure; both increase ADH release. ADH binds a receptor on the collecting duct cells, and a cAMP-based pathway ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)) inserts **aquaporin** water channels into the apical membrane. Water is reabsorbed, urine becomes small in volume and concentrated, and plasma osmolarity falls. Without ADH (diabetes insipidus), large volumes of dilute urine are produced and thirst is intense. Alcohol suppresses ADH release, which is one reason it increases urine volume.

*The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.* When blood pressure or sodium delivery falls, or when the sympathetic nerves are active, the **juxtaglomerular cells** of the afferent arteriole release the enzyme **renin**. Renin converts the liver protein **angiotensinogen** into angiotensin I, and **angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)**, found mainly on lung capillaries, converts it to **angiotensin II**. Angiotensin II (1) constricts arterioles, raising pressure, (2) stimulates the adrenal cortex to release **aldosterone**, (3) promotes ADH release and thirst. **Aldosterone**, a steroid hormone acting on intracellular receptors ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), increases Na⁺ reabsorption (and so water) in the distal tubule and collecting duct, and increases K⁺ and H⁺ secretion. Blood volume and pressure rise. ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers lower blood pressure by interrupting this chain.

*Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP).* When the atria are stretched by high blood volume, they release ANP, which opposes the RAAS: it increases sodium and water excretion and lowers blood pressure.

Aldosterone changes how much sodium is reabsorbed, but it does not by itself change plasma sodium concentration much, because water follows the sodium. ADH changes water without sodium, and it is the hormone that mainly adjusts the concentration of body fluids.

Praxis Tip: Low volume turns on renin, angiotensin II, aldosterone and ADH together. High osmolarity turns on ADH alone. When a question asks which hormone changes concentration and which changes volume, think ADH for concentration, aldosterone for volume.

**Fluid and electrolyte balance, and acid-base balance.** Plasma pH is tightly held at about 7.4 (7.35 to 7.45 [VERIFY]). The main buffer is the bicarbonate system, linked to the lungs through carbon dioxide:

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

pH = 6.1 + log₁₀ ( [HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × pCO₂) )

With normal values of about 24 mM bicarbonate and a pCO₂ of 40 mmHg [VERIFY], the ratio is 20 to 1 and the pH is 7.40. The equation shows that pH depends on the ratio of a base (set mostly by the kidney) to an acid (set mostly by breathing).

| Disturbance | Primary change | Compensation |
|---|---|---|
| Respiratory acidosis | pCO₂ high (hypoventilation) | Kidney retains HCO₃⁻ (slow) |
| Respiratory alkalosis | pCO₂ low (hyperventilation) | Kidney excretes HCO₃⁻ |
| Metabolic acidosis | HCO₃⁻ low (diarrhea, ketoacids) | Faster breathing lowers pCO₂ |
| Metabolic alkalosis | HCO₃⁻ high (vomiting) | Slower breathing raises pCO₂ |

The lungs respond within minutes by changing the ventilation rate ([Lesson 18](chapter:24)), and the kidneys respond over hours to days. The kidney handles acid by (1) reabsorbing filtered bicarbonate, (2) secreting H⁺ into the tubule, where it is buffered by phosphate and ammonia and excreted, and generating new bicarbonate, and (3) adjusting these rates according to the blood pH. Compensation brings the pH back toward, but generally not fully to, normal. Vomiting loses stomach acid and causes a metabolic alkalosis; severe diarrhea loses bicarbonate-rich intestinal fluid and causes a metabolic acidosis.

**Worked Example 1: Clearance and GFR (skill: quantitative reasoning)**

A patient receives an infusion of inulin. The plasma concentration is 1.0 mg/mL, the urine concentration is 24 mg/mL, and the urine flow rate is 5.0 mL/min. (a) What is the GFR? (b) A second solute has a plasma concentration of 2.0 mg/mL and a urine concentration of 6.0 mg/mL at the same flow rate. Is it reabsorbed or secreted?

(a) C = UV/P = (24 × 5.0) / 1.0 = 120 mL/min, close to the normal GFR. (b) Its clearance is (6.0 × 5.0) / 2.0 = 15 mL/min, far less than 120 mL/min. Only a fraction of the filtered load appears in the urine, so the solute is net reabsorbed.

**Worked Example 2: Acid-base arithmetic (skill: Henderson-Hasselbalch)**

Use pH = 6.1 + log(HCO₃⁻ / (0.03 × pCO₂)). (a) Find the pH when HCO₃⁻ = 24 mM and pCO₂ = 60 mmHg. (b) Find the pH when HCO₃⁻ = 12 mM and pCO₂ = 40 mmHg. Classify each.

(a) 0.03 × 60 = 1.8, and 24 / 1.8 = 13.3, with log = 1.12, so pH = 7.22. The CO₂ is high, so this is a respiratory acidosis (uncompensated). (b) 0.03 × 40 = 1.2, and 12 / 1.2 = 10, with log = 1.00, so pH = 7.10. Bicarbonate is low, so this is a metabolic acidosis. The body would hyperventilate to lower pCO₂, which raises the ratio and moves the pH back toward 7.4.

**Worked Example 3: Following a fatty meal (skill: pathway tracing)**

A patient has a blocked bile duct and passes pale, greasy stools. Explain the symptoms and predict one long-term risk.

Without bile, fat is not emulsified, so lipase has little surface to work on and micelles do not form. Most fat is not absorbed and appears in the stool (steatorrhea). The stool is pale because bilirubin, which gives feces its color, does not reach the gut. Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with fat, so the patient is at risk of deficiency, for example vitamin K deficiency with impaired clotting. The lipase itself is intact, so the problem is solubilization and not enzyme activity.

**Worked Example 4: Predicting a hormonal response (skill: system reasoning)**

A hiker loses blood volume through sweating and has had little water. Predict the changes in renin, aldosterone, ADH, urine volume and urine concentration.

Low volume and pressure reduce delivery of blood to the afferent arteriole, so renin rises, followed by angiotensin II and aldosterone. High plasma osmolarity and low volume also raise ADH. Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption and ADH increases water reabsorption through aquaporins, so the urine volume falls and the urine is concentrated. Angiotensin II also constricts arterioles, supporting blood pressure, and stimulates thirst. ANP, in contrast, is low because the atria are not stretched.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: In any absorption question, first ask whether the molecule is water soluble or lipid soluble. That decides portal vein or lymph, and whether bile is needed.

Praxis Tip: In nephron questions, draw the tubule in your head and mark where the fluid is isosmotic, where it is most concentrated (the bottom of the loop) and where it is most dilute (the top of the thick ascending limb).

Praxis Tip: Diuretic drugs are easiest to predict by site. A loop diuretic blocks the cotransporter that builds the gradient, so it removes the medullary gradient and the kidney loses concentrating ability, and a large volume of urine follows.

Praxis Tip: For acid-base questions, name the primary change first (CO₂ or bicarbonate), and then name the compensation, which always goes in the same direction as the primary change for the other partner.

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying that bile contains digestive enzymes. Bile emulsifies fat, but pancreatic lipase does the chemistry.
- Sending absorbed fat to the liver first through the portal vein. Chylomicrons go through the lymph.
- Mixing up secretin (bicarbonate, from acid) and CCK (bile and enzymes, from fat and protein).
- Thinking that the descending limb pumps salt out, or that the ascending limb is permeable to water.
- Assuming the urine in the proximal tubule becomes concentrated. The fluid stays isosmotic because water follows solute.
- Believing that aldosterone mainly controls the concentration of the blood, when it mainly controls volume through sodium.
- Treating compensation as correction: the pH moves toward normal but usually does not reach it.
- Forgetting that glucose in the urine is a sign that the transport maximum has been exceeded, not that the kidney is making glucose.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** Gastrin acts on parietal cells to increase HCl secretion, so too much gastrin causes too much acid. (A gastrin increases rather than decreases acid; C gallbladder contraction is driven by CCK, not gastrin; D bicarbonate is secreted by mucous cells and the pancreas, not in response to gastrin.)
2. **D.** Fats need emulsification and micelles for absorption, and chylomicrons enter the lymphatic lacteals rather than the blood capillaries. (A pepsin and SGLT1 handle protein and sugar, not fat; B amylase digests starch, and fatty acids are also absorbed; C reverses the roles of lipase and bile, and chylomicrons are too large for capillaries.)
3. **A.** Sodium and other solutes are moved out by transport, and water follows through aquaporins, so the fluid remains isosmotic. (B this would make the filtrate more concentrated; C this would make the filtrate more dilute; D ADH acts mainly on the collecting duct.)
4. **C.** That transporter moves salt out of the ascending limb and builds the medullary gradient. Blocking it reduces the gradient, and less water can be recovered. (A the gradient is weakened, so concentration is poor; B the pump that builds the gradient is blocked; D less medullary salt means less osmotic pull for water.)
5. **B.** Low pressure and volume activate the RAAS and ADH, which conserve sodium and water. (A low volume reduces ANP and increases renin; C both rise when volume falls; D the kidney conserves sodium and water.)
6. **A.** Loss of gastric HCl removes acid from the body and leaves a relative excess of bicarbonate. (B vomiting loses acid, not base; C no ventilation problem is described; D the primary disturbance is in bicarbonate.)
7. **D.** Low pH and low bicarbonate show a primary metabolic acidosis, and the low pCO₂ shows that faster breathing is compensating. (A pCO₂ is low, not high; B pH is acidic and bicarbonate is low; C pH is acidic, and the low pCO₂ is the response, not the cause.)
