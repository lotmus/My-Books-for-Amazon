# Lesson 6: Other Pathways and Metabolic Control

**What you will be able to do**

- Describe gluconeogenesis, including the three bypass reactions, its precursors and its cost.
- Describe glycogen synthesis and breakdown and their hormonal control.
- Describe beta-oxidation, the carnitine shuttle and ketone body formation, and contrast fatty acid oxidation with fatty acid synthesis.
- Explain what the pentose phosphate pathway makes and why cells need it.
- Outline amino acid catabolism and the urea cycle.
- Use reciprocal regulation to predict what insulin, glucagon and epinephrine do in the fed and fasted states, and how organs divide the labor.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Lessons [4](chapter:7) and [5](chapter:8) gave you the core machine for burning glucose. This lesson puts that machine in context. Passages in category 1D often present a hormone, a fasting state, a mutant enzyme or a drug and ask which pathway rises and which falls. The unifying idea is **reciprocal regulation**: pathways that run in opposite directions (building versus breaking down) are controlled in opposite directions by the same signal, so a cell does not waste energy running both at once. Learn this logic and most questions become predictions rather than recall. The hormones themselves are covered more fully in [Lesson 16](chapter:22).

Praxis Tip: For every pathway in this lesson, write three things: what turns it on, what turns it off, and which pathway runs in the opposite direction. Then check that the same signal does opposite things to the two.

**Concept Review**

**Gluconeogenesis** makes glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. It occurs mainly in the **liver** (and the kidney cortex in prolonged fasting). Precursors: **lactate** (from muscle and red blood cells, the **Cori cycle**), **glycerol** (from triacylglycerol breakdown), **alanine and other glucogenic amino acids** (muscle sends alanine to the liver, the **glucose-alanine cycle**), and propionyl-CoA from odd-chain fatty acids. Even-chain fatty acids **cannot** give net glucose, because their acetyl-CoA carbons are lost as CO₂ in the TCA cycle and PDH is irreversible ([Lesson 5](chapter:8)).

Most glycolytic steps are reversible and are simply run backward. The three irreversible glycolytic steps need **bypass** enzymes:

| Glycolysis step | Gluconeogenic bypass | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Pyruvate kinase (PEP → pyruvate) | Pyruvate carboxylase, then PEPCK | Mitochondria, cytosol |
| PFK-1 (F6P → F1,6BP) | Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase | Cytosol |
| Hexokinase/glucokinase | Glucose-6-phosphatase | ER membrane |

Pyruvate carboxylase (biotin; requires ATP; activated by acetyl-CoA) makes oxaloacetate in the mitochondria. PEP carboxykinase (PEPCK) then converts oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate using GTP. Glucose-6-phosphatase is found in the liver and kidney but not in muscle, which is why **only the liver (and kidney) can release free glucose into the blood**. The cost of making one glucose from two pyruvate is 4 ATP + 2 GTP (6 high-energy phosphates), plus 2 NADH, compared with the 2 ATP that glycolysis nets. The extra cost is the price of reversing a downhill pathway.

**Glycogen** is the short-term glucose store (liver and muscle). Muscle glycogen serves the muscle itself; liver glycogen is released to keep blood glucose steady.

- **Synthesis:** glucose-6-phosphate → glucose-1-phosphate (phosphoglucomutase) → **UDP-glucose** (uses UTP). **Glycogen synthase** adds glucose units to a growing chain by α(1→4) bonds (starting on a protein primer, glycogenin). A **branching enzyme** creates α(1→6) branches.
- **Breakdown:** **glycogen phosphorylase** cleaves α(1→4) bonds by **phosphorolysis** (adds Pi, giving glucose-1-phosphate, not free glucose, and so saves ATP). A **debranching enzyme** handles branch points. Glucose-1-phosphate is converted to glucose-6-phosphate, which the liver converts to free glucose using glucose-6-phosphatase, whereas muscle sends it into glycolysis. Phosphorylase uses pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B₆) as a cofactor.

Regulation uses **covalent modification** in a cascade. **Glucagon** (liver) and **epinephrine** (liver and muscle) bind G protein-coupled receptors, raise cAMP and activate protein kinase A (PKA). PKA activates phosphorylase kinase, which phosphorylates and **activates glycogen phosphorylase**; PKA also phosphorylates and **inactivates glycogen synthase**. **Insulin** triggers the opposite: a phosphatase (PP1) removes the phosphates, so synthase is active and phosphorylase is inactive. Because synthesis and breakdown are switched in opposite directions by the same signal, they do not run simultaneously. Muscle has extra local controls: Ca²⁺ (from contraction) and AMP activate phosphorylase, so glycogen breakdown matches demand. [VERIFY]

Praxis Tip: For glycogen enzymes, phosphorylation by PKA means "fasting or fight-or-flight." Phosphorylated phosphorylase is on; phosphorylated synthase is off. Say this aloud until it is boring.

**Fatty acid oxidation (beta-oxidation).** Fatty acids from adipose tissue are activated in the cytosol to fatty acyl-CoA by acyl-CoA synthetase, using ATP (ATP → AMP + PPi, equal to the cost of 2 ATP). Long-chain acyl-CoA cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane on its own, so it uses the **carnitine shuttle**: **CPT-I** (outer membrane) transfers the acyl group to carnitine, a translocase moves it across, and CPT-II returns it to CoA in the matrix. **CPT-I is the regulated step: it is inhibited by malonyl-CoA**, the first intermediate of fatty acid synthesis. [VERIFY wording]

In the matrix, each round of beta-oxidation removes two carbons as acetyl-CoA through four steps:

1. Oxidation by acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (makes **FADH₂**).
2. Hydration of the double bond.
3. Oxidation by 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (makes **NADH**).
4. Thiolysis by thiolase (uses CoA, releases **acetyl-CoA**).

A fatty acid with 2n carbons needs n − 1 rounds and gives n acetyl-CoA. **Palmitate (16:0)**: 7 rounds, 8 acetyl-CoA, 7 FADH₂ and 7 NADH. Using the yields from Lesson 5 (10 ATP per acetyl-CoA, 1.5 per FADH₂, 2.5 per NADH), total = 80 + 10.5 + 17.5 − 2 (activation) = **106 ATP**. [VERIFY] Fats yield far more ATP per gram than glucose because they are more reduced and stored without water. Unsaturated fats need an extra isomerase (and sometimes a reductase) and give slightly less. Odd-chain fatty acids end with propionyl-CoA, which is converted in several steps to succinyl-CoA. Very long-chain fatty acids are first shortened in peroxisomes. [VERIFY]

**Ketone bodies.** When fatty acid oxidation is high and carbohydrate is scarce (fasting, a low-carbohydrate diet, uncontrolled type 1 diabetes), the liver makes acetyl-CoA faster than the TCA cycle can use it. Oxaloacetate is being drawn into gluconeogenesis, so acetyl-CoA is diverted to **ketogenesis** in liver mitochondria: two acetyl-CoA condense to acetoacetyl-CoA, then to HMG-CoA (HMG-CoA synthase, the regulated step), which is cleaved to **acetoacetate**. Acetoacetate is reduced to **β-hydroxybutyrate** (using NADH) or spontaneously loses CO₂ to give **acetone** (exhaled, a fruity breath odor). Ketone bodies are water-soluble fuels exported to the blood and used by the brain (in prolonged fasting), heart and muscle, which convert them back to acetyl-CoA. The **liver cannot use ketone bodies** because it lacks the enzyme that activates acetoacetate. Red blood cells cannot, because they have no mitochondria. Acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate are acids; in uncontrolled type 1 diabetes, overproduction causes **ketoacidosis** (low blood pH, with high glucose because insulin is missing).

**Fatty acid synthesis** occurs in the **cytosol** when energy is plentiful (fed state, in liver and adipose). Mitochondrial acetyl-CoA cannot cross the membrane, so it leaves as **citrate** (a high citrate level signals plenty of energy) and is cleaved back to acetyl-CoA in the cytosol. **Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC)**, the committed step, makes **malonyl-CoA** from acetyl-CoA, using biotin and ATP. **Fatty acid synthase**, a large multienzyme complex, then adds two-carbon units from malonyl-CoA to a growing chain, using **NADPH** as the reductant, and releases palmitate (16:0). ACC is activated by citrate and insulin (dephosphorylation) and inhibited by glucagon, epinephrine and AMP-activated kinase (phosphorylation) and by palmitoyl-CoA. Humans cannot synthesize linoleic or α-linolenic acid, the **essential fatty acids**. Fatty acids are stored as triacylglycerols (with glycerol-3-phosphate).

Fatty acid oxidation and synthesis are a contrast set. Remember it as a table:

| Feature | Oxidation | Synthesis |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Mitochondrial matrix | Cytosol |
| Carriers | NAD⁺, FAD, CoA | NADPH, acyl carrier protein |
| Active when | Fasting | Fed |
| Key regulator | CPT-I (malonyl-CoA inhibits) | ACC (citrate, insulin activate) |

**Pentose phosphate pathway (PPP).** The PPP runs in the **cytosol**, parallel to glycolysis, and uses glucose-6-phosphate for two purposes: **NADPH** and **ribose-5-phosphate**. The **oxidative phase** converts glucose-6-phosphate to ribulose-5-phosphate, releasing CO₂ and making **2 NADPH**; its rate-limiting enzyme is **glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)**, which is stimulated by NADP⁺ and inhibited by NADPH. The **nonoxidative phase** (transketolase, transaldolase) interconverts sugars and can make ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotides or return carbons to glycolysis as fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. The pathway makes no ATP.

NADPH is the cell's biosynthetic reductant: it supplies fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis, keeps **glutathione** reduced (protecting red blood cells from oxidative damage), and fuels the phagocyte respiratory burst. Tissues with a high PPP activity: liver, adipose, adrenal cortex, lactating mammary gland, and red blood cells. **G6PD deficiency** leaves red cells vulnerable to oxidative stress (certain drugs, fava beans, infection), causing hemolytic anemia. Key contrast: NADH (catabolic, oxidized by the ETC) versus NADPH (anabolic, used in biosynthesis and defense).

**Amino acid catabolism and the urea cycle (overview).** When amino acids are used for fuel, the nitrogen is removed and the carbon skeleton enters metabolism. **Transamination** (aminotransferases such as ALT and AST, PLP-dependent) moves the amino group to α-ketoglutarate, forming **glutamate**. **Glutamate dehydrogenase** (oxidative deamination) releases the nitrogen as ammonium ion (NH₄⁺). Free ammonia is toxic, so it is carried to the liver as **alanine** (from muscle) or **glutamine**, and converted to **urea** in the **urea cycle**, which spans the mitochondria and the cytosol:

- Mitochondria: **carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I** (CPS I; activated by N-acetylglutamate) makes carbamoyl phosphate from NH₄⁺ and CO₂; ornithine transcarbamoylase makes citrulline.
- Cytosol: citrulline plus aspartate gives argininosuccinate, which is cleaved to arginine and fumarate; **arginase** hydrolyzes arginine to **urea** and ornithine.

Urea contains two nitrogen atoms, one from NH₄⁺ and one from aspartate. The cycle costs about 4 ATP equivalents per urea. [VERIFY] Defects in the cycle cause hyperammonemia. The carbon skeletons end up as pyruvate or TCA intermediates (**glucogenic**), or acetyl-CoA/acetoacetate (**ketogenic**); leucine and lysine are purely ketogenic. [VERIFY]

**Hormones and reciprocal regulation.** The same logic applies across the pathways.

| Signal | Main targets | Net effect |
|---|---|---|
| Insulin (fed) | Muscle, fat, liver | Store glucose and fat |
| Glucagon (fasted) | Mainly liver and fat | Raise blood glucose |
| Epinephrine (stress) | Muscle, liver, fat | Mobilize fuel quickly |

**Insulin** (from pancreatic β cells; receptor tyrosine kinase) promotes glucose uptake (GLUT4 in muscle and adipose), glycolysis, glycogen synthesis, fatty acid and triacylglycerol synthesis and protein synthesis, and suppresses gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, lipolysis and ketogenesis. **Glucagon** (α cells; G protein, cAMP, PKA) does nearly the opposite, mainly in the liver: glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and (with epinephrine) lipolysis in adipose via hormone-sensitive lipase. **Epinephrine** does what glucagon does but acts in muscle too, and on a faster, stress-driven timescale. Cortisol raises gluconeogenic enzymes more slowly, through gene expression. The three timescales are worth remembering: allosteric (seconds), phosphorylation (seconds to minutes), gene expression (hours).

In the liver, the bifunctional enzyme PFK-2/FBPase-2 ties glycolysis and gluconeogenesis together through fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP). In the fed state, insulin raises F2,6BP, which activates PFK-1 (glycolysis on) and inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (gluconeogenesis off). Glucagon, via PKA phosphorylation, lowers F2,6BP, flipping both. [VERIFY]

**The metabolic states.**

- **Fed (absorptive):** insulin high, glucagon low. Glucose is taken up, oxidized, or stored as glycogen and fat. The liver exports triacylglycerol in lipoproteins ([Lesson 19](chapter:26)).
- **Early fasting (postabsorptive, hours):** insulin falls and glucagon rises. The liver breaks down glycogen and starts gluconeogenesis. Lipolysis begins.
- **Prolonged fasting (days):** glycogen is exhausted. The liver depends on gluconeogenesis (from glycerol, lactate and amino acids) and makes ketone bodies, which the brain uses in place of part of its glucose, sparing muscle protein. [VERIFY]

**Organ specialization.**

| Organ | Characteristic metabolism |
|---|---|
| Liver | Buffers blood glucose; gluconeogenesis, ketogenesis, urea |
| Adipose | Stores and releases fatty acids and glycerol |
| Skeletal muscle | Own glycogen; uses fat; releases lactate and alanine |
| Brain | Glucose; ketones when fasting |
| Red blood cell | Glycolysis only; PPP for NADPH |
| Heart | Mostly oxidizes fatty acids aerobically |

The liver's glucokinase has a high Km, so it takes up glucose when blood glucose is high, and it is not inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate (unlike hexokinase). [VERIFY] Muscle lacks glucagon receptors for practical purposes and lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, so it does not export glucose; it responds to epinephrine and its own energy state. Adipose cannot make much glucose and releases glycerol to be used by the liver.

**Worked Example 1: Labeled pathway steps (skill: bypass logic)**

A patient has no functional liver fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and is fasted. What happens to blood glucose from alanine, and what accumulates?

Alanine becomes pyruvate and then oxaloacetate and PEP, and the carbons move up the pathway to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, where the missing enzyme blocks the way to glucose. Gluconeogenesis from alanine, lactate and glycerol fails, so fasting hypoglycemia occurs, and lactate and precursors accumulate. Glucose from stored glycogen is initially unaffected, so symptoms emerge once glycogen runs out.

**Worked Example 2: Palmitate energy (skill: arithmetic)**

How many ATP are obtained from the complete oxidation of one palmitate, given 10 ATP per acetyl-CoA, 2.5 per NADH, 1.5 per FADH₂?

Palmitate (16 carbons): 8 acetyl-CoA need 7 rounds. Acetyl-CoA: 8 × 10 = 80. FADH₂: 7 × 1.5 = 10.5. NADH: 7 × 2.5 = 17.5. Sum = 108. Activation costs 2 ATP equivalents: 106 net.

**Worked Example 3: Predicting a hormone's effects (skill: reciprocal regulation)**

A fasting person is given a glucagon injection. List the effects on liver glycogen synthase, glycogen phosphorylase, ACC and CPT-I.

Glucagon raises cAMP and PKA activity. Glycogen synthase is phosphorylated and inactivated; glycogen phosphorylase is activated through phosphorylase kinase. ACC is phosphorylated and inhibited, so malonyl-CoA falls. Lower malonyl-CoA relieves CPT-I, so more fatty acids enter the mitochondria for oxidation. One signal produces a coordinated switch from storage to release.

**Worked Example 4: Why ketone bodies form (skill: connect pathways)**

In a fasting patient with low insulin, why do ketone bodies rise even though the liver is oxidizing large amounts of fat?

Beta-oxidation produces acetyl-CoA, but with low insulin and high glucagon, oxaloacetate is drawn into gluconeogenesis, so less oxaloacetate is available to combine with acetyl-CoA in the TCA cycle. The extra acetyl-CoA is diverted to acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate, which carry energy to the brain and muscle. The liver lacks the enzyme to use them, so they are exported.

**Worked Example 5: Why NADPH matters (skill: pathway purpose)**

A person with G6PD deficiency takes an oxidant drug. Why do red blood cells lyse, and why is the problem localized to them?

Red cells have no mitochondria and depend on the PPP as their only source of NADPH. NADPH keeps glutathione reduced, which neutralizes peroxides. Without NADPH the cells accumulate oxidative damage, hemoglobin precipitates, membranes weaken and cells lyse. Other tissues have alternative NADPH sources and less oxidative stress.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: When asked what a hormone does, first decide whether it signals fed or fasted. Then everything it does follows: fed means store, fasted means release.

Praxis Tip: Phosphorylation is neither "on" nor "off" in general. It activates phosphorylase kinase and phosphorylase but inactivates glycogen synthase and ACC, so check each enzyme separately.

Praxis Tip: Malonyl-CoA is the traffic light: it is the first product of synthesis and it blocks the entry of fatty acids into oxidation.

Praxis Tip: Only liver and kidney have glucose-6-phosphatase. Muscle glycogen can never raise blood glucose directly (though muscle lactate and alanine can, through the liver).

Praxis Tip: Fatty acids (even-chain) cannot make net glucose, but the glycerol of a triacylglycerol can. If a passage tracks a label from a fatty acid, do not expect it in glucose.

Praxis Tip: Brain and red cells are the "glucose-dependent organs." Predict hypoglycemia symptoms in the brain first.

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying acetyl-CoA from fatty acids can be converted into glucose in humans.
- Saying muscle glycogen directly maintains blood glucose.
- Reversing the direction of glycogen synthase and phosphorylase regulation.
- Saying fatty acid synthesis uses NADH or occurs in the mitochondria.
- Treating NADPH and NADH as interchangeable.
- Forgetting that the liver makes but cannot use ketone bodies.
- Forgetting that insulin and glucagon act oppositely on both gluconeogenesis and glycolysis.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **D.** Glucose-6-phosphate has to be dephosphorylated before glucose can leave the cell; the liver and kidney have glucose-6-phosphatase, muscle does not. (A is a synthetic enzyme present in both tissues; B adds phosphate to glucose and is present in muscle; C is a glycolytic enzyme found in both tissues.)
2. **B.** Glucagon acts through cAMP and PKA to switch from storage to release, and the fall in F2,6BP removes the stimulation of PFK-1 and the inhibition of fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase. (A describes the response to insulin; C reciprocal control prevents both being active; D glucagon does not activate synthesis.)
3. **A.** The two pathways are kept separate by compartment, carriers and regulation, which is what allows opposite hormonal control. (B the location and the cofactor are both incorrect; C they use different enzymes, locations and carriers; D the hormonal controls are reversed.)
4. **C.** A 12-carbon chain gives 6 acetyl-CoA after 5 rounds: 60 + 5(1.5) + 5(2.5) − 2 = 60 + 7.5 + 12.5 − 2 = 78. (A forgets the activation cost; B counts only the acetyl-CoA and omits the NADH and FADH₂; D is the value for the 16-carbon palmitate.)
5. **D.** Low insulin and high glucagon favor fat mobilization and ketogenesis, and the acidic ketones lower blood pH. (A insulin is low in this condition and suppresses ketogenesis; B glucose is high, and the liver does not oxidize ketone bodies at all; C the brain can use them.)
6. **A.** The PPP supplies NADPH, and red cells have few alternative sources. (B glycolysis is intact in this deficiency; C mature red cells have no nucleus and make little DNA; D red cells have no mitochondria in any case.)
7. **B.** Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-I, preventing newly made fatty acids from being immediately oxidized. (A relieves CPT-I inhibition; C lowers malonyl-CoA through ACC inhibition; D supports the shuttle.)
