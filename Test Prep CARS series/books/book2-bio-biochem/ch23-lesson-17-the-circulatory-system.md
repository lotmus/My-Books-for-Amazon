# Lesson 17: The Circulatory System

**What you will be able to do**

- Trace blood through the heart and both circuits, and link the mechanical events of the cardiac cycle to the heart sounds, the valves and the electrical conduction system.
- Interpret an ECG at the concept level, and predict the effect of a conduction block or a change in autonomic input on heart rate and cardiac output.
- Apply the continuity equation and Poiseuille's law from Book 1, Lesson 4 to vessels, and explain why arterioles control blood pressure and why capillaries are slow.
- Use Starling forces to predict the direction of fluid movement across a capillary wall, and explain edema.
- Describe the components of blood, the steps of clotting, the logic of anticoagulant drugs, fetal circulation and the basics of the lymphatic system.

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson belongs to category 3B, the structure and function of the major organ systems. The circulatory system is a plumbing problem solved by living tissue, and the physics from Book 1 is the quickest route to the answers. Pressure drives flow, resistance falls steeply as radius grows (Book 1, Lesson 4), flow is the same at every point of a series circuit while velocity depends on total cross-sectional area, and a pump that is also an electrical organ (Book 1, Lesson 5) must be told when to squeeze. Passages usually give a pressure, a flow, a drug or a defect and ask what happens next. Gas carriage by hemoglobin ([Lesson 1](chapter:4)) is completed in [Lesson 18](chapter:24), the neural and hormonal controls come from [Lesson 15](chapter:21) and [Lesson 16](chapter:22), and fluid balance is finished in [Lesson 19](chapter:26).

Praxis Tip: For any circulation question, ask what the pressure gradient is, what the resistance is, and which one the question changed. Almost every answer is one of those two.

**Concept Review**

**The heart and the two circuits.** The heart is two pumps side by side. The **right side** receives deoxygenated blood from the body and sends it to the lungs (the **pulmonary circuit**). The **left side** receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and sends it to the body (the **systemic circuit**). Blood travels in series: right atrium, right ventricle, pulmonary arteries, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, left ventricle, aorta, body, then veins back to the right atrium. Because the circuits are in series, both ventricles must pump the same volume per minute. The left ventricle has the thicker wall because it pushes against the far higher resistance of the systemic circuit. The pulmonary circuit is a low-pressure, low-resistance system (a mean pressure of roughly 15 mmHg [VERIFY]), which protects thin alveolar walls.

Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it toward the heart; the oxygen content is not part of the definition. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood, and the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.

| Valve | Location | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Tricuspid | Right atrium to right ventricle | Atrioventricular |
| Pulmonary | Right ventricle to pulmonary artery | Semilunar |
| Mitral (bicuspid) | Left atrium to left ventricle | Atrioventricular |
| Aortic | Left ventricle to aorta | Semilunar |

Valves open and close passively with pressure differences, so blood flows one way. The atrioventricular valves are tethered by tendon-like **chordae tendineae** attached to papillary muscles, which stop them from flipping back into the atria. Valve closure makes the heart sounds: the first sound ("lub") is the closing of the atrioventricular valves at the start of ventricular contraction, and the second ("dub") is the closing of the semilunar valves as the ventricles relax. A leaky or narrowed valve produces a turbulent murmur, because turbulent flow is noisy (Book 1, Lesson 4 and Lesson 6).

**The cardiac cycle.** One beat has two phases. In **diastole** the ventricles relax and fill; most filling is passive, and atrial contraction tops it up at the end. In **systole** the ventricles contract. The sequence for the left ventricle is as follows.

1. **Filling.** The mitral valve is open, and ventricular pressure is low.
2. **Isovolumetric contraction.** The ventricle begins to contract, and its pressure rises above atrial pressure, so the mitral valve shuts. The aortic valve is not yet open, so the volume cannot change.
3. **Ejection.** Ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens, and blood is ejected.
4. **Isovolumetric relaxation.** The ventricle relaxes and pressure falls below aortic pressure, so the aortic valve shuts (the dicrotic notch on the aortic pressure trace). The mitral valve opens only after ventricular pressure drops below atrial pressure.

At the end of filling the ventricle holds the **end-diastolic volume (EDV)**, around 120 mL [VERIFY], and after ejection it holds the **end-systolic volume (ESV)**, around 50 mL [VERIFY]. The difference is the **stroke volume (SV)**, and the **ejection fraction** is SV / EDV, which is generally about 55 to 70 percent in a healthy heart. **Cardiac output (CO)** is the volume pumped per minute:

CO = HR × SV

At rest this is about 5 L/min, which is roughly the whole blood volume each minute. During hard exercise it can rise several-fold, through both a higher rate and a larger stroke volume.

**Control of cardiac output.** Heart rate is set by the autonomic nervous system ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). Sympathetic input (norepinephrine acting on β₁ receptors; epinephrine from the adrenal medulla) raises rate and the force of contraction. Parasympathetic input through the **vagus nerve** (acetylcholine) slows the rate; at rest the vagal influence dominates, which is why the resting rate is generally lower than the intrinsic rate of the pacemaker. Stroke volume depends on three things: **preload** (the filling of the ventricle), **contractility**, and **afterload** (the pressure the ventricle must beat to open the aortic valve). The **Frank-Starling mechanism** says that within limits, the more the ventricle is stretched by filling, the more forcefully it contracts, so the heart automatically matches its output to the venous return. Increased venous return (muscle pump and breathing during exercise) raises preload and therefore stroke volume.

Praxis Tip: Cardiac output is a product, so it can change by changing either factor. When a question gives a drug or a posture change, say which of rate, preload, contractility or afterload moved, and the answer usually follows.

**The conduction system.** Cardiac muscle cells are linked by **intercalated discs** containing gap junctions ([Lesson 12](chapter:17)), so depolarization spreads from cell to cell and the muscle behaves as a functional syncytium. The impulse is generated and routed by specialized tissue.

1. The **sinoatrial (SA) node** in the right atrium is the **pacemaker**. Its cells depolarize spontaneously (automaticity), with the fastest intrinsic rate of any heart tissue, so it sets the rhythm. The atria depolarize and contract.
2. The impulse reaches the **atrioventricular (AV) node**, where conduction is deliberately slow. This **AV delay** lets the ventricles finish filling before they contract.
3. It then runs rapidly down the **bundle of His**, into the left and right **bundle branches** along the septum, and out through the **Purkinje fibers**, which spread it rapidly to the ventricular muscle so that the ventricles contract nearly together, from the apex upward.

The atria and ventricles are electrically separated by a fibrous skeleton, so the AV node is generally the only route between them. If the AV node fails, lower tissue (with a slower intrinsic rate) takes over as a backup pacemaker, which is why a complete block produces a slow ventricular rate that is out of step with the atria.

A ventricular muscle action potential differs from a nerve action potential ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). After the rapid Na⁺-driven upstroke, a **plateau** lasting a couple of hundred milliseconds [VERIFY] is maintained by inward Ca²⁺ current balancing outward K⁺ current. The Ca²⁺ that enters triggers more Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (calcium-induced calcium release), which causes contraction ([Lesson 20](chapter:27) covers the contractile machinery). The long plateau means a long refractory period, so cardiac muscle cannot be tetanized, and the heart can fill between beats.

**The ECG at the concept level.** An electrocardiogram records the summed electrical activity of the heart from the body surface. It does not record contraction directly, only the electrical events that precede it.

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| P wave | Atrial depolarization |
| PR interval | Includes the AV node delay |
| QRS complex | Ventricular depolarization |
| T wave | Ventricular repolarization |

Atrial repolarization happens at the same time as the QRS complex and is hidden by it. A lengthened PR interval suggests slowed conduction through the AV node; a missing QRS after a P wave suggests a block; a rapid, irregular pattern with no clear P waves suggests atrial fibrillation, in which the atria quiver rather than contract. A wide QRS suggests that the ventricles are being depolarized through slow, abnormal routes.

**Blood vessels.** The structure of each vessel fits its job.

| Vessel | Wall features | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| Artery | Thick, elastic and muscular | Pressure reservoir; smooths pulses |
| Arteriole | Thick smooth muscle | Resistance; sets flow to each tissue |
| Capillary | One endothelial layer | Exchange |
| Vein | Thin wall, valves | Capacitance (blood reservoir), return |

Large arteries are elastic: they stretch during systole and recoil during diastole, which keeps blood moving through diastole (the **Windkessel** effect) and turns a pulsing output into steadier flow. **Arterioles** are the main site of resistance, and their smooth muscle is regulated by the sympathetic nervous system, by local metabolites (carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, adenosine, low oxygen cause dilation, matching flow to tissue demand), and by hormones. The endothelium releases **nitric oxide**, a local vasodilator. **Veins** are thin and compliant, hold the majority of the blood volume [VERIFY], and have one-way valves; skeletal muscle contractions and breathing squeeze the veins and push blood toward the heart (the "muscle pump"). Cardiac function depends on the filling from the veins. The liver is fed by the **hepatic portal system**, in which blood from the intestine passes through a second capillary bed before returning to the heart ([Lesson 19](chapter:26)).

**Pressure and flow: Book 1 fluids applied.** Blood is a moving fluid, so the physics of Book 1, Lesson 4 applies, with the caution that blood is a viscous, non-ideal fluid in elastic tubes.

*Flow, pressure and resistance.* A flow driven by a pressure difference follows an Ohm's-law relation:

Q = ΔP / R

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) drives flow through the systemic circuit, and the pressure drops from the arteries to the veins, so MAP − central venous pressure = CO × TPR (total peripheral resistance). With venous pressure near zero:

MAP = CO × TPR

*Poiseuille's law.* For steady laminar flow of a viscous fluid in a rigid tube:

Q = π r⁴ ΔP / (8 η L), so the resistance is R = 8 η L / (π r⁴)

The fourth power makes radius by far the most powerful variable: halve the radius, and resistance rises sixteen-fold. This is why arterioles, whose radius can be adjusted by smooth muscle, are the main controllers of blood pressure and of blood distribution. Plaque narrowing an artery, and the vasoconstriction of the sympathetic nervous system, work through this r⁴ dependence. Higher hematocrit raises viscosity and therefore resistance.

*Continuity.* The volume flow rate through a series of vessels is the same at every level (Q = A × v for a single tube, so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ in Book 1, Lesson 4). When blood passes from the aorta into the branching network, the **total** cross-sectional area of all the parallel vessels grows. The aorta has the smallest total area and the fastest velocity. The capillaries are individually tiny, but there are billions of them, so their combined area is the largest in the system, and the blood velocity is the lowest. Slow flow in a thin, leaky wall is what gives time for exchange. Velocity then rises again in the venous system as total area falls.

*Where the pressure falls.* Pressure falls little in the large arteries and most steeply across the arterioles, which hold most of the resistance. Pressure in capillaries is low, and pulsation has been damped out by the time blood reaches them. Venous pressure is lowest, near zero at the right atrium.

*Arterial pressure.* The pressure measured in the arm is reported as **systolic over diastolic** (for example, 120/80 mmHg). **Pulse pressure** = systolic − diastolic, and the average driving pressure over the cycle is estimated as MAP ≈ diastolic + one third of the pulse pressure, because the heart spends more of each cycle in diastole. Stiff arteries (arteriosclerosis) cannot absorb the ejected volume, so the pulse pressure grows. A cuff pressure measurement (**sphygmomanometer**) works because turbulent flow through the squeezed artery is audible: the first sound appears when cuff pressure falls to systolic, and the sounds disappear when the artery is fully open (diastolic).

Praxis Tip: When Poiseuille is hiding in a passage, write the ratio. New flow over old flow equals (new radius over old radius) to the fourth power, with the rest cancelled. Then there is no algebra left to get wrong.

*Regulation of blood pressure.* **Baroreceptors** in the carotid sinus and aortic arch sense stretch of the arterial wall and signal the medulla. A fall in pressure reduces their firing, and the medulla raises sympathetic output and lowers vagal output: heart rate, contractility and vasoconstriction rise, and pressure returns toward normal. Slower, hormonal mechanisms alter blood volume: the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and antidiuretic hormone raise it, and atrial natriuretic peptide lowers it ([Lesson 16](chapter:22) and [Lesson 19](chapter:26)).

**Capillary exchange and Starling forces.** Capillaries have a wall one cell thick, with water-filled clefts between cells (in some tissues the wall has pores, and in the brain tight junctions make a blood-brain barrier). Gases, small molecules and water cross freely, while plasma proteins mostly stay inside. Two opposing pressures set the direction of net fluid movement.

- **Hydrostatic pressure** (P) pushes fluid out of the capillary. It is higher at the arteriolar end and lower at the venous end.
- **Oncotic pressure** (π) is the osmotic pull exerted by the proteins, chiefly **albumin**, that cannot cross the wall. It draws fluid in and stays roughly the same along the capillary (Book 1, Lesson 13).

Net filtration pressure = (P capillary − P interstitium) − (π capillary − π interstitium)

A positive number means filtration (fluid leaves); a negative number means reabsorption. Typically the arteriolar end has net filtration, and the venous end has net reabsorption. Slightly more fluid is filtered than reabsorbed, and the excess returns through the lymph. **Edema** (excess interstitial fluid) arises when this balance breaks: high venous pressure (heart failure, a blocked vein), low plasma albumin (liver disease, malnutrition, kidney protein loss), increased capillary permeability (inflammation), or blocked lymph drainage.

**Blood.** Blood is a tissue: about 55 percent **plasma** and 45 percent cells by volume, and the cell fraction is the **hematocrit** (generally about 40 to 50 percent [VERIFY]). Plasma is mostly water, with ions, nutrients, hormones, wastes and proteins: **albumin** (oncotic pressure, carrier), **globulins** (including antibodies, [Lesson 21](chapter:28)) and **fibrinogen** (clotting).

- **Erythrocytes (red blood cells)** are biconcave discs, which give a high surface-to-volume ratio for gas exchange. Mature mammalian red cells have no nucleus and no mitochondria, so they make ATP only by glycolysis and do not consume the oxygen they carry. They live roughly 120 days and are removed in the spleen and liver. They are made in bone marrow under the control of **erythropoietin**, a hormone produced mainly by the kidney in response to low oxygen delivery.
- **Leukocytes (white blood cells)** defend the body: neutrophils, monocytes (which become macrophages), lymphocytes (B and T cells), eosinophils and basophils ([Lesson 21](chapter:28)).
- **Platelets** are cell fragments pinched from large bone marrow cells (megakaryocytes) and are essential for clotting.

All blood cells descend from hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow ([Lesson 13](chapter:18) on differentiation). The **ABO** and **Rh** groups are determined by antigens on the surface of red cells; a person generally carries antibodies against the A or B antigen that they lack, so a transfusion must be matched. Type O red cells lack A and B antigens, which is why they are the "universal donor" for red cells.

**Clotting.** After vessel injury, **hemostasis** proceeds in overlapping stages.

1. **Vasoconstriction** of the injured vessel reduces flow.
2. **Platelet plug.** Platelets adhere to exposed collagen (helped by von Willebrand factor), become activated, release chemicals and stick to each other.
3. **Coagulation cascade.** A series of clotting factors, most of them proteases made in the liver, activate each other in turn, each activating the next (amplification, like the signaling cascades of [Lesson 12](chapter:17)). The cascade converges on a common step: the protease **thrombin** is made from its inactive precursor **prothrombin**. Thrombin converts soluble **fibrinogen** to insoluble **fibrin**, which polymerizes into a mesh that traps cells and is cross-linked by a transglutaminase (factor XIII) into a stable clot. Calcium ions are required in several steps.
4. **Dissolution.** Once the vessel heals, the enzyme **plasmin**, made from plasminogen, digests fibrin (fibrinolysis).

Several clotting factors (II, VII, IX and X) require **vitamin K** for a post-translational carboxylation that lets them bind calcium. The drug **warfarin** interferes with vitamin K recycling, so these factors are made in a defective form, and clotting slows. **Heparin** works differently: it enhances antithrombin, a natural inhibitor of thrombin and other factors. Aspirin inhibits platelet aggregation by blocking synthesis of a platelet signal (thromboxane) [VERIFY]. **Hemophilia A** is caused by a deficiency of factor VIII and is X-linked recessive ([Lesson 10](chapter:14)). Clotting is positive feedback: thrombin activates more factors and platelets, and that is why the response is explosive, and why it must be kept local by inhibitors and by rapid flow that washes away activated factors. A clot that forms inside an intact vessel (a thrombus) can break free (an embolus) and lodge downstream.

Praxis Tip: To classify a clotting drug, find the step it hits: vitamin K recycling (warfarin), thrombin inhibition (heparin, through antithrombin), platelet aggregation (aspirin), or fibrin breakdown (tissue plasminogen activator). Then predict bleeding versus clotting.

**Fetal circulation.** The fetus gets oxygen from the placenta, not the lungs, which are filled with fluid and offer high resistance. Three shunts reroute the blood.

- The **umbilical vein** carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus. Two **umbilical arteries** carry deoxygenated blood back to the placenta. (The vessel names follow the direction relative to the heart, not oxygen content.)
- The **ductus venosus** lets much of the umbilical venous blood bypass the liver and reach the inferior vena cava.
- The **foramen ovale** is an opening between the right and left atria. Because pulmonary resistance is high, right atrial pressure exceeds left, and blood passes right to left, bypassing the lungs.
- The **ductus arteriosus** connects the pulmonary artery to the aorta, shunting blood that reaches the right ventricle away from the lungs.

At birth, the lungs inflate and pulmonary resistance falls sharply. More blood flows through the lungs, left atrial pressure rises above right atrial pressure, and the flap of the foramen ovale is pushed shut (it later fuses, leaving a small depression, the fossa ovalis). The ductus arteriosus constricts within hours to days, partly because the oxygen level rises and prostaglandin levels fall, and becomes a ligament. A ductus that fails to close (patent ductus arteriosus) leaves a left-to-right shunt, and drugs that inhibit prostaglandin synthesis are sometimes used to close it. Fetal hemoglobin has a higher oxygen affinity than adult hemoglobin ([Lesson 1](chapter:4)), which helps the fetus to take oxygen from maternal blood.

**The lymphatic system (basics).** Lymphatic vessels begin as blind-ended capillaries in tissues and collect the fluid and protein that were filtered from capillaries but not reabsorbed, perhaps a few liters a day [VERIFY]. The fluid (**lymph**) travels through progressively larger vessels with one-way valves, propelled by skeletal muscle and breathing, passes through **lymph nodes** (where immune cells survey it, [Lesson 21](chapter:28)), and drains into veins near the heart through the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct. In the small intestine, **lacteals** absorb dietary fat packaged as chylomicrons, which enter the lymph and not the blood capillaries ([Lesson 19](chapter:26)). Lymph therefore has three jobs: returning fluid and protein, absorbing fat, and immune surveillance. Failure of lymph drainage causes edema.

**Worked Example 1: Cardiac output (skill: calculation)**

At rest a person has an end-diastolic volume of 120 mL, an end-systolic volume of 50 mL and a heart rate of 70 beats per minute. Calculate the stroke volume, the ejection fraction and the cardiac output. Then predict the effect of a drug that doubles the heart rate but, by shortening filling time, reduces the end-diastolic volume to 90 mL, with the end-systolic volume unchanged.

SV = 120 − 50 = 70 mL. Ejection fraction = 70 / 120 = 0.58, about 58 percent. CO = 70 beats/min × 70 mL = 4,900 mL/min, about 4.9 L/min. For the drug: heart rate is 140 and SV = 90 − 50 = 40 mL, so CO = 140 × 40 = 5,600 mL/min, only slightly higher (5.6 L/min), and the ejection fraction is 40 / 90 = 44 percent. The lesson is that a faster rate does not always increase output, because filling time is shortened at very high rates.

**Worked Example 2: Poiseuille's law (skill: ratio reasoning)**

Atherosclerotic plaque narrows an artery so that its radius falls to 80 percent of normal. If the pressure difference, length and viscosity are unchanged, by what factor do the resistance and the flow change?

R is proportional to 1 / r⁴, so the new resistance is (1 / 0.8)⁴ = 2.44 times the old. Flow is the inverse: 0.8⁴ = 0.41, or 41 percent of normal. A 20 percent narrowing therefore removes about 59 percent of the flow. To restore the flow, the heart must raise the pressure by a factor of about 2.4, which is one reason narrowing is linked to high blood pressure and to heart strain.

**Worked Example 3: Continuity (skill: velocity and area)**

The aorta has a cross-sectional area of 4 cm², and blood in it moves at 30 cm/s during mean flow. The total cross-sectional area of all capillaries is about 3,000 cm². Estimate the mean capillary velocity.

Flow is conserved, so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂: v₂ = (4 × 30) / 3,000 = 0.04 cm/s, or 0.4 mm/s. The flow is slower by a factor of 750. At this speed, a red cell takes a second or so [VERIFY] to pass through a capillary of about a millimeter in length, which gives time for oxygen and nutrients to diffuse (in line with the diffusion-time estimate in [Lesson 12](chapter:17)). Because the capillaries are many in parallel, the velocity in each is low, although the flow in the whole bed equals the cardiac output.

**Worked Example 4: Starling forces (skill: net filtration)**

In a capillary bed, the capillary hydrostatic pressure is 32 mmHg at the arteriolar end and 14 mmHg at the venous end. Plasma oncotic pressure is 24 mmHg, and interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures are both close to zero. (a) Find the net filtration pressure at each end. (b) A patient's albumin falls so that plasma oncotic pressure drops to 16 mmHg. What happens at the venous end?

(a) Arteriolar end: 32 − 24 = +8 mmHg, so fluid leaves. Venous end: 14 − 24 = −10 mmHg, so fluid is reabsorbed. (b) Venous end: 14 − 16 = −2 mmHg, so reabsorption nearly stops. Along the whole capillary, the net outward fluid increases, the lymph cannot return all of it, and edema develops, as in liver disease or nephrotic syndrome.

**Worked Example 5: Mean arterial pressure (skill: estimation)**

A blood pressure reading is 126/78 mmHg, and the cardiac output is 5.0 L/min. Estimate the pulse pressure, the MAP and the total peripheral resistance (ignoring venous pressure).

Pulse pressure = 126 − 78 = 48 mmHg. MAP ≈ 78 + 48 / 3 = 94 mmHg. TPR = MAP / CO = 94 / 5.0 = 18.8 mmHg per (L/min). If the arterioles constrict at the same cardiac output, TPR and MAP both rise.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Arteries versus veins is about direction, not oxygen. The pulmonary vein is oxygenated, the umbilical vein is oxygenated, and the pulmonary and umbilical arteries are not.

Praxis Tip: The "total area" trap: a single capillary is narrow, but the whole capillary bed is the widest part of the circuit. Velocity follows the total, and that is why capillary flow is slow.

Praxis Tip: For edema, check the two forces in the Starling equation, then the lymph. If the question mentions albumin, the answer is oncotic pressure; if it mentions a clot or a failing pump, the answer is hydrostatic pressure.

Praxis Tip: The SA node sets the pace, the AV node waits, and the Purkinje fibers hurry. If a passage describes a lag, a delay or a block, find the AV node first.

Praxis Tip: In a pressure-volume or valve question, the valves open and close only on pressure differences. Ask which pressure is larger, and the state of each valve follows.

**Common Mistakes**

- Defining arteries as vessels carrying oxygenated blood. They are vessels leaving the heart.
- Thinking that velocity is lowest in the aorta because it is the widest single vessel. The total area of the capillaries is the largest.
- Treating flow as proportional to radius. Poiseuille's law has radius to the fourth power.
- Forgetting that the two ventricles pump the same volume per minute, although the pressures differ greatly.
- Calling the QRS complex ventricular contraction. It is the electrical depolarization that comes first.
- Saying that the SA node is slow because the AV node delays the signal. The delay is built into the AV node, and the SA node has the fastest intrinsic rate.
- Mixing up edema from low albumin (oncotic) with edema from high venous pressure (hydrostatic).
- Believing that warfarin dissolves clots. It slows the making of new clotting factors.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **D.** Conduction through the AV node is slow by design, which separates atrial and ventricular contraction. (A starts the impulse and sets the rate; it does not delay it; B conduct rapidly through the ventricles, so they hurry the signal; C are mechanical valves and do not conduct electrical impulses.)
2. **B.** Poiseuille's law gives flow proportional to r⁴, and (1/2)⁴ = 1/16. (A treats flow as proportional to radius; C uses radius squared, as if flow depended only on area; D uses radius cubed, which is not what the law says.)
3. **A.** Albumin provides most of the oncotic pressure that pulls fluid back into the capillary, so a fall in albumin favors net filtration. (B reverses the direction of the oncotic force; C arteriolar constriction lowers downstream capillary pressure and would reduce filtration; D faster drainage would reduce, not cause, swelling.)
4. **C.** Warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, so factors II, VII, IX and X are made in a nonfunctional form. (A describes fibrinolysis by plasmin, not warfarin; B platelet counts are not the main target of warfarin; D this is what thrombin does, and it would promote clotting.)
5. **A.** The foramen ovale is the opening between the atria that bypasses the high-resistance fetal lungs. (B connects the pulmonary artery to the aorta; C routes umbilical venous blood past the liver to the vena cava; D carries deoxygenated blood from the fetus to the placenta.)
6. **D.** The T wave is the electrical recovery of the ventricular muscle after the QRS complex. (A is the P wave; B is the QRS complex; C is part of the PR interval, not the T wave.)
7. **B.** By continuity, v = Q / A, so with Q fixed, the largest total area gives the lowest velocity. (A flow is the same through each level of a series circuit; C capillary pressure is lower than arterial pressure; D the radius of a single capillary does not explain the total velocity.)
