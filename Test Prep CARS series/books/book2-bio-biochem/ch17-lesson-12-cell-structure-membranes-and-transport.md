# Lesson 12: Cell Structure, Membranes and Transport

**What you will be able to do**

- Match each organelle to its structure and function, and trace a protein through the endomembrane system.
- Compare the three cytoskeletal filament systems and the motors that use them.
- Describe the fluid mosaic model and predict how lipid composition, temperature and cholesterol affect fluidity.
- Classify transport as simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, primary or secondary active transport, or vesicular transport, and link each to energy and gradients.
- Use the Nernst equation, Fick's law and osmotic pressure from Book 1 to reason about cells, and predict what a cell does in hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic solutions.
- Describe cell junctions, the extracellular matrix, and the major signal transduction routes (GPCRs, receptor tyrosine kinases, second messengers).

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson covers category 2A, the structure and function of cells and their membranes. It is the part of biology that is most closely tied to physical chemistry. A membrane is a barrier with a dielectric interior (Book 1, Lesson 5), an ion gradient is stored electrical and chemical energy (Book 1, Lesson 17 on the Nernst equation and Lesson 14 on free energy), osmosis is a colligative property (Book 1, Lesson 13), and diffusion rates follow from the physics of random motion. Passages often describe a transporter, a toxin or a drug and ask what happens to a gradient or a signal. The ideas feed forward into [Lesson 15](chapter:21), where the same gradients produce action potentials, [Lesson 16](chapter:22) on hormones, and [Lesson 5](chapter:8), where the proton gradient makes ATP.

Praxis Tip: For every transport or signaling question, ask three things in order: what is moving or being sensed, which way does it want to go (the gradient), and what supplies the energy if it does not go that way?

**Concept Review**

**Compartments and organelles.** Eukaryotic cells divide their chemistry into membrane-bound compartments, which allows incompatible reactions to run side by side.

| Organelle | Structure | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Double membrane with pores | Stores DNA; transcription, RNA processing |
| Nucleolus | Dense region inside the nucleus | rRNA synthesis, ribosome assembly |
| Rough ER | Membrane network with ribosomes | Makes secreted and membrane proteins |
| Smooth ER | Membrane network, no ribosomes | Lipid synthesis, detoxification, Ca²⁺ storage |
| Golgi apparatus | Stacked flattened sacs | Modifies, sorts and ships proteins and lipids |
| Lysosome | Acidic vesicle with hydrolases | Digests macromolecules and worn organelles |
| Peroxisome | Single-membrane vesicle | Fatty acid oxidation, breaks down H₂O₂ |
| Mitochondrion | Double membrane, folded inner membrane | ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation |
| Chloroplast | Double membrane, thylakoids | Photosynthesis (plants and algae) |

Some points that are tested often. The nuclear envelope is a double membrane interrupted by **nuclear pores**, which let small molecules through and control the passage of proteins and RNA, often using signal sequences and the small G protein Ran. The **mitochondrion** has an outer membrane, an inner membrane folded into cristae, an intermembrane space and a matrix; it contains its own circular DNA and ribosomes ([Lesson 11](chapter:15) on endosymbiosis). The **lysosome** keeps an acidic interior (a pH around 5 [VERIFY]), and its enzymes are acid hydrolases that work poorly at the neutral pH of the cytosol, which protects the cell if one leaks. **Peroxisomes** contain oxidases that produce hydrogen peroxide and catalase to break it down. The **cytosol** is the fluid that fills the rest of the cell; most of glycolysis and much protein synthesis happen there. **Ribosomes**, whether free or attached to the ER, are not membrane-bound organelles, but they are essential. Plants also have a large central **vacuole** and a cell wall; animals have **centrosomes** that organize microtubules.

**The endomembrane system and protein trafficking.** The nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vesicles and plasma membrane are linked, directly or by vesicles. A protein destined for secretion, the plasma membrane, the ER, the Golgi or a lysosome is made with a **signal sequence** (a short hydrophobic stretch, usually at the amino end). As it emerges from a free ribosome, the signal sequence is recognized by the **signal recognition particle (SRP)**, which brings the ribosome to the rough ER, where the polypeptide is threaded into the ER lumen (or into the membrane) as it is made. In the ER, proteins are folded with the help of chaperones ([Lesson 1](chapter:4)), disulfide bonds form (the oxidizing environment favors it), and sugars are added (**glycosylation**). Properly folded proteins leave in **transport vesicles** to the Golgi, where they are further modified (for example, carbohydrate trimming and sorting tags) and then sorted into vesicles bound for the plasma membrane (secretion by **exocytosis**), the lysosome, or elsewhere. The default route for a soluble protein that enters the ER and carries no other signal is secretion. Proteins to be returned to the ER carry a retention signal. Proteins with no signal sequence stay in the cytosol, and proteins for the nucleus, mitochondria or peroxisomes use their own targeting sequences and are imported after synthesis.

Lysosomal enzymes are tagged in the Golgi with mannose-6-phosphate, which receptors recognize. A defect in tagging or in a lysosomal enzyme causes storage of undigested material (lysosomal storage diseases, such as Tay-Sachs disease).

Praxis Tip: Trace secretion as a one-way train: ribosome on rough ER, ER lumen, transport vesicle, Golgi, secretory vesicle, plasma membrane. If a drug blocks one stop, everything downstream is affected and the protein piles up at the block.

**The cytoskeleton.** Three filament systems give the cell shape, organize its interior and allow movement.

| Filament | Subunit | Roles |
|---|---|---|
| Microfilaments | Actin | Cell shape, cortex, muscle contraction, cytokinesis, crawling |
| Intermediate filaments | Keratins, lamins, others | Mechanical strength, nuclear lamina |
| Microtubules | Tubulin (α/β dimers) | Mitotic spindle, vesicle tracks, cilia, flagella |

Microfilaments are the thinnest (about 7 nm [VERIFY]), made of actin, polar, and grow and shrink at their ends; with the motor **myosin** they drive muscle contraction and cytokinesis. **Intermediate filaments** (about 10 nm) are the most stable and rope-like, and there are many types: keratin in epithelia and **lamins** that support the inside of the nuclear envelope. They are not polar and are not used as tracks for motors. **Microtubules** (about 25 nm [VERIFY]) are hollow tubes of α/β-tubulin dimers, with a plus end (fast growing) and a minus end anchored near the centrosome. They show **dynamic instability**, in which GTP bound to tubulin is hydrolyzed after incorporation, and cells use this to rapidly rearrange the spindle ([Lesson 13](chapter:18)). The motor proteins **kinesin** (generally toward the plus end, so away from the nucleus) and **dynein** (toward the minus end) carry vesicles along microtubules, using ATP hydrolysis. Cilia and eukaryotic flagella contain a "9 + 2" arrangement of microtubule doublets with dynein arms that slide to produce bending. Drugs that disrupt cytoskeletal dynamics (for example, agents that bind tubulin) are used in cell biology and in chemotherapy because dividing cells are especially dependent on the spindle.

**Membrane structure: the fluid mosaic model.** The plasma membrane is a **phospholipid bilayer** ([Lesson 2](chapter:5)) with proteins embedded in or attached to it. Lipids and many proteins can diffuse sideways within a leaflet (lateral diffusion is fast), but flipping from one leaflet to the other is slow unless an enzyme assists it, so the two leaflets keep different compositions (asymmetry). Membrane components:

- **Integral proteins** span or are embedded in the membrane; **transmembrane** segments are usually α-helices of hydrophobic amino acids. **Peripheral proteins** attach to the surface by weaker interactions and can be removed with mild treatments like changes in salt.
- **Cholesterol** is wedged among phospholipids. It acts as a **fluidity buffer**: at high temperatures it restrains the motion of the phospholipid tails (reducing fluidity), and at low temperatures it prevents tight packing (preventing solidification).
- **Glycolipids and glycoproteins** carry carbohydrate chains on the outer surface, important in recognition (blood group antigens, for instance).
- **Lipid rafts** are small, more ordered domains rich in cholesterol and sphingolipids that may concentrate certain proteins.

Fluidity increases with temperature, with shorter fatty acid tails and with more cis double bonds in the tails. Cells adjust the lipid composition to the temperature (more unsaturated lipids in the cold).

Praxis Tip: Membrane fluidity follows the same logic as melting point in [Lesson 2](chapter:5). Kinks and short chains keep a membrane fluid; long saturated tails make it stiff. Cholesterol is the exception that moderates both extremes.

**Transport across membranes.** The hydrophobic interior of the bilayer allows small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, steroid hormones) and, more slowly, small uncharged polar molecules (water, urea, ethanol) to cross by **simple diffusion**. Ions and large polar molecules cannot cross without a protein.

| Mechanism | Needs protein? | Needs energy? |
|---|---|---|
| Simple diffusion | No | No |
| Facilitated diffusion (channel, carrier) | Yes | No (down gradient) |
| Primary active transport | Yes (pump) | Yes, ATP directly |
| Secondary active transport | Yes (cotransporter) | Yes, from another gradient |

- **Simple diffusion** runs down the concentration gradient. By Fick's law, the flux is proportional to the gradient, the membrane area and the permeability, and inversely proportional to the distance.
- **Facilitated diffusion** uses **channels** (a pore, often gated by voltage, ligand or mechanical force) or **carriers** (which change shape as they bind and release the solute). The movement is still down the electrochemical gradient. Carriers show **saturation** (a maximum rate) and specificity, like enzymes (Book 1, Lesson 24); simple diffusion does not saturate. Water crosses faster through **aquaporins**.
- **Primary active transport** uses ATP directly to move a solute against its electrochemical gradient. The key example is the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase**, which moves **3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in** for each ATP, so it is **electrogenic** (it contributes a small negative effect inside the cell) and maintains the low sodium and high potassium inside. Other pumps include the Ca²⁺-ATPases that remove calcium from the cytosol, and the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase of gastric parietal cells.
- **Secondary active transport** uses the energy stored in an ion gradient, usually Na⁺ or H⁺, to move another solute. **Symport** (cotransport) moves both in the same direction (the sodium-glucose cotransporter in the intestine and kidney), and **antiport** (exchange) moves them in opposite directions (the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger). The ATP is used indirectly, by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump that renews the gradient.
- **Vesicular transport.** **Endocytosis** brings material in: phagocytosis ("cell eating", large particles), pinocytosis (fluid) and **receptor-mediated endocytosis** (specific cargo is concentrated at receptors in clathrin-coated pits, as when cells take up cholesterol in LDL particles). **Exocytosis** fuses vesicles with the plasma membrane to release contents and to add membrane.

**Electrochemical gradients and the Nernst equation.** For an ion, the net force depends on both the concentration difference and the electrical potential. At equilibrium, there is no net flux, and the **equilibrium (Nernst) potential** is

E = (RT / zF) ln([ion]out / [ion]in), which at 37 °C is about E = (61.5 mV / z) log₁₀([ion]out / [ion]in).

(Book 1, Lesson 17.) For a cation, a higher concentration outside gives a negative equilibrium potential. The **resting membrane potential** lies between the Nernst potentials of the ions to which the membrane is permeable, weighted by permeability; at rest, the neuron membrane is much more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺, so the resting potential is near E(K) ([Lesson 15](chapter:21)). The Na⁺/K⁺ pump does not itself set the potential directly; it maintains the gradients that do. Whether an ion moves through an open channel depends on the difference between the membrane potential and that ion's Nernst potential (its driving force).

**Osmosis and tonicity.** **Osmosis** is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane, toward the side with the higher solute concentration (lower water potential). **Osmotic pressure** is the pressure needed to stop it: π = iMRT (Book 1, Lesson 13). What matters is the concentration of **impermeant solutes**. **Tonicity** describes a solution's effect on cell volume:

- **Hypotonic** solution (lower impermeant solute concentration than the cell): water enters, and an animal cell swells and may lyse. A plant cell becomes turgid, which is healthy because the cell wall resists bursting.
- **Isotonic** solution: no net water movement.
- **Hypertonic** solution: water leaves, and an animal cell shrinks (crenation); a plant cell loses turgor and the membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis).

Tonicity is not the same as osmolarity. A solution of urea may have the same total osmolarity as the cell's cytoplasm, but if urea crosses the membrane, it does not hold water out, and the cell will still swell: the solution is effectively hypotonic. Cells control volume actively by pumping ions (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump counteracts the osmotic influx of water), and freshwater protozoa use contractile vacuoles.

Praxis Tip: Water goes toward the higher concentration of impermeant solute. If the question gives you a permeant solute, such as urea, remove it from the calculation of tonicity.

**Cell junctions.** Cells in tissues attach to each other and to the matrix.

| Junction | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Tight junction | Sealing strands (claudins, occludin) | Seals the space between cells; blocks leakage |
| Desmosome | Cadherins linked to intermediate filaments | Spot welds; resists mechanical stress |
| Gap junction | Connexon channels | Passes ions and small molecules between cells |
| Hemidesmosome | Integrins linked to intermediate filaments | Anchors cells to the basement membrane |

Tight junctions also separate the apical from the basolateral membrane domains of epithelial cells, which lets epithelia transport substances in one direction (for example, glucose absorption in the intestine). Gap junctions allow electrical coupling, as in cardiac muscle. In plants, **plasmodesmata** link neighboring cells through the cell wall.

**The extracellular matrix (ECM).** The ECM is the network of secreted macromolecules outside animal cells. **Collagen** is the major structural protein (a triple helix; [Lesson 1](chapter:4)) and gives tensile strength. **Elastin** gives elasticity. **Proteoglycans** (core proteins with long glycosaminoglycan chains) retain water and resist compression, and **fibronectin** and laminin link the matrix to cells. **Integrins** are transmembrane receptors that bind matrix proteins on the outside and the cytoskeleton on the inside, and they convey signals in both directions. Cadherins mediate cell-cell adhesion, and require calcium.

**Cell signaling.** Cells sense signals, convert them to an internal response (**signal transduction**), and amplify them. The steps: **reception** (a ligand binds a receptor), **transduction** (a relay of molecular changes), and **response** (a change in enzyme activity, gene expression or cytoskeleton). Signaling can be endocrine (hormones carried by the blood), paracrine (local), autocrine (the same cell), or direct (gap junctions, or contact between cells).

*Location of the receptor.* Small, nonpolar ligands, such as steroid and thyroid hormones, cross the membrane and bind **intracellular receptors**, which often act as transcription factors ([Lesson 8](chapter:12)). Large or polar ligands (peptide hormones, neurotransmitters, growth factors) cannot cross, and bind **cell-surface receptors**. There are three major families.

1. **Ligand-gated ion channels:** ligand binding opens a channel and changes the membrane potential in milliseconds (the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, for example).
2. **G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs):** seven-transmembrane-helix receptors that work through heterotrimeric **G proteins** (α, β, γ subunits). In the resting state, Gα binds GDP. When the ligand binds, the receptor acts as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor: Gα swaps GDP for GTP and separates from βγ, and Gα-GTP (or βγ) then regulates an effector enzyme. The signal ends because Gα has intrinsic GTPase activity, which hydrolyzes GTP to GDP and reassembles the trimer. Two classic routes:
   - **Gs** stimulates **adenylyl cyclase**, which converts ATP to **cyclic AMP (cAMP)**. cAMP activates **protein kinase A (PKA)**, which phosphorylates target proteins. **Gi** inhibits adenylyl cyclase. cAMP is broken down by phosphodiesterase to AMP.
   - **Gq** activates **phospholipase C**, which cleaves the membrane lipid PIP₂ into **IP₃** (which opens Ca²⁺ channels in the ER, raising cytosolic calcium) and **diacylglycerol (DAG)** (which, with calcium, activates **protein kinase C**).
   Cholera toxin permanently activates Gαs, and cAMP stays high in intestinal cells, which drives the secretion of chloride and water.
3. **Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs):** receptors for growth factors and insulin. Ligand binding causes the receptors to pair (dimerize), and the kinase domains phosphorylate tyrosines on each other (**autophosphorylation**). The phosphotyrosines are docking sites for adaptor proteins, which activate the small G protein **Ras** and a kinase cascade (the MAPK pathway) that ends in changes in gene expression and cell division. Mutations that leave Ras or an RTK always on are common in cancer ([Lesson 13](chapter:18)).

**Second messengers and amplification.** A **second messenger** is a small intracellular molecule made or released in response to a signal: cAMP, IP₃, DAG and Ca²⁺ are the standard examples. Because each activated receptor can activate many G proteins, and each enzyme makes many second messengers, a small number of ligand molecules can produce a large response (**amplification**). Signals must also be terminated: GTP hydrolysis, phosphodiesterases (cAMP), phosphatases (which remove phosphates) and receptor internalization. **Kinases** add phosphate groups from ATP, and **phosphatases** remove them, so phosphorylation acts as a reversible switch ([Lesson 4](chapter:7), the same logic as enzyme regulation). The same ligand can have different effects in different cells, because the receptor, the effectors and the downstream proteins differ.

**Worked Example 1: The Nernst potential (skill: gradients and potential)**

A cell has an intracellular K⁺ concentration of 140 mM and an extracellular concentration of 5 mM at 37 °C. (a) What is the K⁺ equilibrium potential? (b) What happens to the value if the extracellular K⁺ rises to 10 mM and the intracellular concentration is unchanged?

(a) E(K) = 61.5 log(5 / 140) = 61.5 × (−1.447) = about −89 mV. The inside is negative because K⁺ would tend to leave the cell down its concentration gradient, and equilibrium is reached when the negative interior holds it back. (b) E(K) = 61.5 log(10 / 140) = 61.5 × (−1.146) = about −70 mV. Higher external potassium makes the potential less negative (the cell depolarizes), because the concentration gradient driving K⁺ out is smaller. Use the sign convention carefully: the formula uses [out] over [in].

**Worked Example 2: Osmosis (skill: direction and pressure)**

(a) Estimate the osmotic pressure of a 0.15 M NaCl solution at 37 °C (310 K), treating NaCl as fully dissociated (i = 2). (b) A red blood cell has an internal osmolarity of about 300 mOsm/L. What happens when it is placed in 0.05 M NaCl (about 100 mOsm/L, assuming full dissociation)?

(a) π = iMRT = 2 × 0.15 mol/L × 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K) × 310 K = 7.6 atm. (b) The external solution has lower impermeant solute concentration, so it is hypotonic. Water enters the cell, which swells and may lyse (hemolysis) because animal cells have no wall. The same cell in 0.15 M NaCl (about 300 mOsm/L) would be in an isotonic solution and keep its volume.

**Worked Example 3: Tracing a secreted protein (skill: endomembrane pathway)**

Insulin-like peptides are made as precursors with a signal sequence. Describe the route of such a protein from its synthesis to its release, and predict what happens if the signal sequence is deleted.

The mRNA is translated on a free ribosome. When the signal sequence emerges, SRP binds and delivers the ribosome to the rough ER, and the chain is threaded into the lumen. In the ER the signal sequence is removed, the protein folds and disulfide bonds form, and it is packaged into transport vesicles that fuse with the Golgi. After modification and sorting, secretory vesicles carry it to the plasma membrane, where exocytosis releases it. If the signal sequence is deleted, SRP does not recognize the protein: it is made in the cytosol and is neither processed in the ER nor secreted.

**Worked Example 4: Classifying transport (skill: gradient and energy)**

In the intestinal epithelium, glucose enters the cell from the lumen through a sodium-glucose cotransporter, then leaves through a glucose transporter on the blood side. The cell maintains low internal sodium with the Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Classify each step, and predict the effect of ouabain, which inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.

Entry from the lumen is **secondary active transport (symport)**: glucose moves up its gradient, coupled to the downhill movement of Na⁺. Exit on the blood side is **facilitated diffusion**, because glucose is at high concentration inside and low outside. The pump is **primary active transport**. If the pump is inhibited, intracellular sodium rises and the sodium gradient collapses. The cotransporter loses its driving force, and glucose uptake falls, even though the cotransporter itself is not blocked and no ATP is used directly by it.

**Worked Example 5: Signal amplification (skill: second messengers)**

A hormone binds a Gs-coupled receptor. In a simplified model, each activated receptor activates 10 G proteins, each active G protein activates one adenylyl cyclase, and each cyclase makes 100 cAMP molecules before it is turned off. How many cAMP molecules result from one receptor? What happens if a mutation in Gα eliminates its GTPase activity?

One receptor leads to 10 active G proteins and then 10 cyclases, each making 100 cAMP: 10 × 100 = 1,000 cAMP molecules, a thousandfold amplification. If Gα cannot hydrolyze GTP, it stays in the active state, adenylyl cyclase stays on, and cAMP production continues after the hormone is gone: the signal does not turn off (as in cholera toxin).

**Worked Example 6: Diffusion and cell size (skill: scaling)**

For a small molecule with a diffusion coefficient D = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, estimate the time to diffuse a distance x in one dimension using t = x² / (2D), for x = 10 μm and x = 1 mm.

For x = 10 μm = 1 × 10⁻⁵ m: t = (1 × 10⁻¹⁰) / (2 × 10⁻⁹) = 0.05 s. For x = 1 mm = 1 × 10⁻³ m: t = (1 × 10⁻⁶) / (2 × 10⁻⁹) = 500 s, about 8 minutes. The distance rose 100-fold and the time 10,000-fold: diffusion time grows with the square of the distance. This is why diffusion is effective inside a cell but not across a body, why cells are small, and why large organisms need circulatory systems ([Lesson 17](chapter:23)).

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Active transport means energy is spent against a gradient, whether directly (ATP) or indirectly (another gradient). Facilitated diffusion has a protein but no energy. Do not mix up "needs a protein" with "needs energy".

Praxis Tip: For a Nernst problem, put the sign check first: positive ion, more outside, negative equilibrium potential. Then do the logarithm.

Praxis Tip: When the question says a drug "blocks the pump", follow the gradient it maintains and everything coupled to it. One inhibited pump can disable many transporters.

Praxis Tip: In a signaling passage, identify where the cascade is switched on and where it is switched off. Questions about mutations or toxins are nearly always about a switch stuck on or stuck off.

Praxis Tip: Learn one example of each junction by function, not by name: seal (tight), rivet (desmosome), pipe (gap). The names then fall into place.

Praxis Tip: Which organelle? Ask "which job?" Protein export is rough ER and Golgi, lipid synthesis is smooth ER, digestion is lysosome, ATP is mitochondrion. Four jobs cover most questions.

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying that facilitated diffusion requires ATP, or that all transport proteins are pumps.
- Forgetting that secondary active transport depends on the pump that maintains the gradient.
- Assigning water movement to solutes: water moves toward higher impermeant solute concentration.
- Treating a permeant solute (urea, ethanol) as if it contributes to tonicity.
- Using the Nernst potential with the concentrations upside down or ignoring the charge of the ion.
- Saying the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase moves equal numbers of ions (it moves 3 Na⁺ out for 2 K⁺ in).
- Mixing up intermediate filaments (strength) with microtubules (tracks, spindle).
- Assuming a receptor that binds a hormone must be on the surface, when steroid hormones use intracellular receptors.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** Without the signal sequence, SRP does not target the ribosome to the ER, so the protein is synthesized in the cytosol and is not secreted. (A the protein was made, so the gene was transcribed; C the protein never reached a secretory vesicle; D a mitochondrial targeting sequence is a different signal.)
2. **D.** Kinesin and dynein walk along microtubules, powered by ATP hydrolysis. (A provide mechanical strength and are not tracks for motors; B are the track for myosin, not kinesin; C are intermediate filament proteins supporting the nuclear envelope.)
3. **A.** The downhill movement of Na⁺ provides the energy for uphill glucose uptake, and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains the sodium gradient. (B the cotransporter does not hydrolyze ATP directly; C the movement is against the glucose gradient; D glucose is polar and cannot cross the bilayer unaided.)
4. **C.** E = 61.5 × log(145/10) = 61.5 × 1.161 = about +71 mV. (A has the sign wrong: more cation outside gives a positive equilibrium potential; B uses the concentration ratio itself instead of its logarithm; D doubles the value, as if the charge were 2+.)
5. **D.** Tonicity depends on impermeant solutes. Urea equilibrates across the membrane, the cell's own impermeant solutes then draw water in, and the cell swells. (A ignores that urea is permeant and does not hold water out; B urea does not exert a lasting osmotic force on the cell; C urea is a small polar molecule.)
6. **B.** GTP hydrolysis resets Gα to its inactive GDP-bound form. Without it, the active state persists. (A binding of GTP is normal, so activation does occur; C termination depends on GTP hydrolysis, which is lost; D the protein is stuck in the GTP-bound state.)
7. **A.** Dimerization and autophosphorylation of tyrosines creating docking sites for adaptor proteins is the hallmark of RTK signaling. (B signals through heterotrimeric G proteins, not by autophosphorylation; C opens a pore and does not recruit adaptors through phosphotyrosines; D growth factors are large and do not cross the membrane to bind such receptors.)
