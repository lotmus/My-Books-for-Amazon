# Lesson 18: The Respiratory System

**What you will be able to do**

- Describe the airway from nose to alveolus and relate each region to its protective or exchange function.
- Explain ventilation using pressure, volume and Boyle's law, and calculate minute and alveolar ventilation from lung volumes.
- Use Dalton's law and Henry's law to find partial pressures and predict the direction of gas exchange in the lungs and tissues.
- Explain how oxygen and carbon dioxide are carried in blood, including the Bohr and Haldane effects and the bicarbonate system.
- Apply the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to respiratory acid-base disorders, and describe the control of breathing and the role of surfactant.

**Why it matters for the exam**

This lesson belongs to category 3B, the structure and function of the major organ systems. The respiratory system is applied physical chemistry. The gas laws of Book 1, Lesson 12 explain why the chest draws air in and why altitude matters. The buffer chemistry of Book 1, Lesson 16 explains how breathing controls blood pH. The hemoglobin curve of [Lesson 1](chapter:4) gives the oxygen delivery, and the pressure and flow ideas of [Lesson 17](chapter:23) apply to airflow and to the pulmonary capillaries. Passages often give a blood gas result or a lung disease and ask which variable moved, and in which direction the body will respond. The kidney's part in acid-base balance is completed in [Lesson 19](chapter:26).

Praxis Tip: Most respiratory questions are pressure questions. Gas moves from high partial pressure to low partial pressure, so write the two numbers down first and the direction follows.

**Concept Review**

**Anatomy of the airway.** Air passes through a series of tubes that condition it and then reaches a vast exchange surface.

| Region | Features | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Nasal cavity, pharynx | Mucus, hairs, rich blood supply | Filter, warm, humidify |
| Larynx | Vocal cords, epiglottis | Voice; closes over trachea when swallowing |
| Trachea, bronchi | C-shaped cartilage rings, ciliated cells | Conducting airway; clears debris |
| Bronchioles | Smooth muscle, no cartilage | Adjust airflow |
| Alveoli | Thin walls, capillary network | Gas exchange |

The **trachea** divides into two main bronchi, which branch repeatedly into bronchioles and end in clusters of **alveoli**. The incomplete cartilage rings of the trachea keep it open, and the open back of each C lets the esophagus bulge during swallowing. The airway is lined with **goblet cells** that secrete mucus and **ciliated cells** whose cilia beat upward. Together they form the **mucociliary escalator**, which carries trapped particles and microbes toward the throat to be swallowed or coughed out. Smoking paralyzes cilia, and in cystic fibrosis, a defective chloride channel (CFTR, a transport protein of the kind described in [Lesson 12](chapter:17)) leaves the airway mucus thick and difficult to clear.

The airways that carry air without exchanging gas make up the **conducting zone**, whose volume (roughly 150 mL in an adult [VERIFY]) is the **anatomical dead space**. In the bronchioles, smooth muscle sets the radius, and by Poiseuille's law ([Lesson 17](chapter:23)) a small change in radius has a large effect on airflow. Sympathetic input and epinephrine (β₂ receptors) dilate the bronchioles, and parasympathetic input constricts them. In asthma, the bronchioles narrow and inflame, and β₂ agonists are used to open them.

The **alveoli** are small air sacs, a few hundred million in total, with a combined surface area of around 70 m² [VERIFY]. Their walls are mostly thin **type I cells**, and each alveolus is wrapped in capillaries; the barrier between air and blood is generally less than a micrometer thick. **Type II cells** secrete **surfactant**. Alveolar macrophages remove inhaled particles. The right lung has three lobes and the left has two, which leaves room for the heart.

Each lung is enclosed by a double-layered membrane, the **pleura**: the visceral layer on the lung and the parietal layer on the chest wall, separated by a thin film of fluid. The fluid holds the two layers together (like wet glass plates) so that the lung follows the chest wall, and the pressure in the pleural space (intrapleural pressure) is normally below atmospheric pressure. If air enters the space (a **pneumothorax**), the seal is lost, and the lung collapses.

**Ventilation: the mechanics.** Mammals use **negative-pressure breathing**: they lower the pressure in the lungs, and the atmosphere pushes air in. The reasoning is Boyle's law from Book 1, Lesson 12: at a constant temperature, the pressure of a gas is inversely proportional to its volume.

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

*Inspiration* is active. The **diaphragm** (a dome of skeletal muscle, driven by the phrenic nerve) contracts and flattens, and the external intercostal muscles lift the ribs up and out. The volume of the chest cavity grows, the lung follows, and the pressure in the alveoli falls a little below atmospheric. Air flows in until the pressures equalize. *Quiet expiration* is passive: the muscles relax, and the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall shrinks the volume, so the alveolar pressure rises above atmospheric and air flows out. *Forced expiration* (exercise, coughing) is active and uses the abdominal muscles and the internal intercostals.

Two properties of the lungs matter. **Compliance** is the ease of stretching (change in volume per unit change in pressure); a stiff lung, as in fibrosis, has low compliance. **Elastic recoil** is the tendency of stretched tissue to return to its original size, which drives passive expiration; in emphysema, the elastic tissue is destroyed, recoil is lost, and air is trapped. Airway resistance, governed by radius as in Poiseuille's law, is the third factor: **obstructive** diseases (asthma, emphysema, chronic bronchitis) make it hard to get air out, whereas **restrictive** diseases (fibrosis) make it hard for the lung to expand.

*Lung volumes.* A spirometer measures the volumes.

| Term | Meaning | Typical adult |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal volume (TV) | Air moved in a quiet breath | about 500 mL |
| Vital capacity (VC) | Largest breath out after largest breath in | about 4.5 L |
| Residual volume (RV) | Air left after maximal expiration | about 1.2 L |
| Total lung capacity | VC + RV | about 5.8 L |

(Typical values are approximate [VERIFY] and vary with size, sex and age.) The residual volume cannot be exhaled, because the airways and alveoli would otherwise collapse. **Minute ventilation** is the volume moved per minute:

Minute ventilation = respiratory rate × tidal volume

But air in the dead space never reaches the alveoli, so the more useful figure is

Alveolar ventilation = respiratory rate × (tidal volume − dead space)

Shallow, rapid breathing is inefficient, because the dead space takes a larger share of each breath. In an obstructive disease, the ratio of the forced volume exhaled in one second (FEV₁) to the forced vital capacity (FVC) is reduced, whereas in a restrictive disease the ratio is normal or high and both volumes are small.

Praxis Tip: Boyle's law is a seesaw: volume up, pressure down. Every inhalation question reduces to saying which muscles enlarged the chest. The same seesaw works in reverse for a ventilator, which pushes air in with positive pressure instead.

**Surfactant and alveolar stability.** The alveoli are lined with a thin layer of water, and water has a high surface tension that pulls the surface inward. For a sphere, the Law of Laplace gives the collapsing pressure as P = 2T / r, where T is the surface tension and r the radius. A smaller bubble has a higher pressure, so a small alveolus would empty into a larger one connected to it, and many would collapse. **Surfactant** (a mixture of phospholipid, notably dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, and some proteins) is amphipathic ([Lesson 2](chapter:5)): it spreads over the water surface and lowers surface tension, and it does this more effectively in smaller alveoli, where it is more concentrated. It does three useful things: it raises compliance (less work to inflate), stabilizes alveoli of different sizes, and keeps them from collapsing on expiration. Premature infants born before the type II cells make enough surfactant develop **respiratory distress syndrome**: stiff, collapsing lungs and difficult breathing. Glucocorticoids given to the mother before preterm birth speed the maturation of fetal lungs [VERIFY], and artificial surfactant can be given to the infant.

**Partial pressures and gas exchange.** Two gas laws from Book 1, Lesson 12 and Lesson 13 are needed.

*Dalton's law:* the partial pressure of a gas is its mole fraction times the total pressure.

P(gas) = fraction × P(total)

At sea level the total pressure is about 760 mmHg, and air is about 21 percent oxygen, so dry-air PO₂ = 0.21 × 760 = 160 mmHg. In the airways, the inspired air is humidified, and water vapor takes up about 47 mmHg at body temperature, so the inspired PO₂ is about 0.21 × (760 − 47) = 150 mmHg. At altitude the fraction of oxygen is unchanged (still 21 percent), but the total pressure is lower, so the PO₂ falls. This is why mountain air is "thin".

*Henry's law:* the concentration of gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure over the liquid. Oxygen dissolves poorly in plasma, about 0.003 mL of O₂ per dL of blood for each mmHg [VERIFY], so in plasma at a PO₂ of 100 mmHg, only about 0.3 mL per dL is dissolved. Carbon dioxide is about twenty times more soluble than oxygen [VERIFY]. Gases move across the alveolar membrane by **diffusion**, from high to low partial pressure, and (by Fick's law, [Lesson 12](chapter:17)) the rate rises with the surface area and the pressure difference and falls with the thickness of the barrier. That explains why fibrosis (thickened wall), emphysema (lost surface) and pulmonary edema (fluid in the way) all impair exchange.

Typical values (approximate [VERIFY]; they are also the numbers around which passages are built):

| Site | PO₂ (mmHg) | PCO₂ (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|
| Inspired air (humidified) | 150 | 0 |
| Alveolar air | 100 | 40 |
| Systemic arterial blood | 100 | 40 |
| Systemic venous blood | 40 | 46 |

Oxygen therefore diffuses from alveolar air (100) into blood returning with a PO₂ of 40, and equilibrium is reached in a fraction of the transit time through the capillary, so healthy lungs have a reserve. Carbon dioxide diffuses the other way, though the pressure difference is small (46 to 40), because CO₂ is so soluble and diffuses readily. In the tissues the gradients are reversed: oxygen flows from blood into cells, which have a low PO₂ because the mitochondria use it ([Lesson 5](chapter:8)), and CO₂ flows out of the cells.

Alveolar PO₂ is set by the balance of oxygen delivered by ventilation and oxygen taken up by blood. A useful simplified relation is

PAO₂ ≈ PIO₂ − PaCO₂ / R

where PIO₂ is the inspired PO₂, PaCO₂ the arterial CO₂ pressure and R the respiratory quotient, about 0.8 [VERIFY]. Underventilation (hypoventilation) raises PCO₂ and therefore lowers alveolar PO₂.

Blood flow must be matched to airflow. In the lung, if an alveolar region is poorly ventilated, the low PO₂ in it causes the local pulmonary arterioles to constrict, which diverts blood to better-ventilated regions (hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction). This is the opposite of systemic vessels, which dilate in low oxygen.

Praxis Tip: Do not confuse the percentage of oxygen with its partial pressure. Altitude changes the pressure, not the percentage, so it is the partial pressure that decides the gradient across the alveolar wall.

**Oxygen transport.** About 98 percent of the oxygen in arterial blood is carried by hemoglobin ([Lesson 1](chapter:4)), and only about 2 percent is dissolved. Each gram of hemoglobin can bind about 1.34 mL of oxygen [VERIFY], and the oxygen content of blood is therefore:

O₂ content ≈ 1.34 × [Hb] × saturation + 0.003 × PO₂

Because of the sigmoidal binding curve, arterial blood at a PO₂ of 100 mmHg is nearly fully saturated, so a modest drop in alveolar PO₂ lowers the saturation only a little (the flat part of the curve gives a safety margin), whereas a fall in tissue PO₂ unloads a lot of oxygen from the steep part of the curve. Several factors shift the curve to the right (lower affinity, easier unloading) at active tissue: more CO₂, more H⁺ (lower pH, the **Bohr effect**), higher temperature and more 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate. The opposite changes, and fetal hemoglobin, shift it to the left. Carbon monoxide binds the heme iron with far higher affinity than oxygen and shifts the curve left, so it blocks loading and impairs unloading.

Note that the oxygen content depends on the hemoglobin concentration as well as the saturation. In **anemia**, there is less hemoglobin, so the oxygen content is low even though the PO₂ and the saturation are normal. Pulse oximeters read saturation, not content, and cannot see carbon monoxide poisoning reliably.

At high altitude, the lower PO₂ stimulates the peripheral chemoreceptors, breathing speeds up, and over days the kidney makes more erythropoietin and the hematocrit rises ([Lesson 17](chapter:23)).

**Carbon dioxide transport.** CO₂ is carried to the lungs in three forms (percentages are approximate [VERIFY]).

1. **Bicarbonate, about 70 percent.** In red blood cells, **carbonic anhydrase** speeds the reaction: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. The H⁺ is buffered by hemoglobin, and the HCO₃⁻ leaves the cell in exchange for Cl⁻ (the **chloride shift**), so most CO₂ travels as bicarbonate in the plasma.
2. **Carbaminohemoglobin, about 20 to 25 percent.** CO₂ binds reversibly to amino groups on globin.
3. **Dissolved CO₂, about 5 to 10 percent.**

In the lungs, the reactions run in reverse: bicarbonate re-enters the cell, combines with H⁺ and becomes CO₂, which diffuses into the alveoli. Oxygenation of hemoglobin helps this, because oxygenated hemoglobin binds H⁺ and CO₂ less well than deoxygenated hemoglobin (the **Haldane effect**). It is the mirror image of the Bohr effect: the binding of CO₂ and H⁺ lowers oxygen affinity, and the binding of oxygen lowers CO₂ and H⁺ affinity, so each gas helps to unload the other.

**The bicarbonate buffer and acid-base balance.** The CO₂ system is the major buffer of the blood. From Book 1, Lesson 16, the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation for this system is

pH = 6.1 + log₁₀ ([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × PCO₂))

where PCO₂ is in mmHg, and 0.03 converts it to the concentration of dissolved CO₂ in mM. With normal values ([HCO₃⁻] about 24 mM, PCO₂ about 40 mmHg), the ratio is 24 / 1.2 = 20, log 20 = 1.30, and the pH is 7.40; the normal range of arterial pH is about 7.35 to 7.45. The system is "open": the lungs can remove the acid component (CO₂) by changing the ventilation rate, while the kidneys control the base component (HCO₃⁻) by changing its reabsorption and regeneration ([Lesson 19](chapter:26)). The pH depends on the ratio, so each organ's contribution can be read off.

| Disorder | Primary change | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|
| Respiratory acidosis | PCO₂ up | Hypoventilation, airway disease |
| Respiratory alkalosis | PCO₂ down | Hyperventilation (anxiety, altitude) |
| Metabolic acidosis | HCO₃⁻ down | Ketoacids, lactic acid, diarrhea |
| Metabolic alkalosis | HCO₃⁻ up | Vomiting, antacid excess |

Compensation moves the other component in the same direction. In metabolic acidosis, the lungs breathe faster and deeper, which lowers PCO₂ and moves the ratio toward normal within minutes; in a respiratory disorder, the kidneys change bicarbonate over days. Compensation reduces the pH change but rarely restores it entirely. To diagnose, check the pH first (acidosis or alkalosis), then see whether the PCO₂ or the bicarbonate was the one to move the pH in that direction; the other is compensation.

**Control of breathing.** Breathing is automatic, but the rhythm is generated in the brainstem. Groups of neurons in the **medulla** generate the basic rhythm of inspiration and expiration, and regions in the **pons** modulate and smooth it. They drive the phrenic and intercostal nerves to the respiratory muscles. The cortex can override them for a time (holding the breath or speaking), but not forever. Stretch receptors in the lung wall limit over-inflation (the Hering-Breuer reflex).

The main controlled variable in a healthy person is **arterial PCO₂**, through its effect on pH. **Central chemoreceptors** in the medulla respond to the H⁺ in the cerebrospinal fluid. CO₂ crosses the blood-brain barrier easily and forms H⁺ there, whereas H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ in the blood cross poorly, so these receptors are sensitive to CO₂. A rise in PCO₂ increases ventilation, which removes CO₂ (negative feedback). **Peripheral chemoreceptors** in the **carotid bodies** and aortic bodies sense arterial PO₂, PCO₂ and pH; they respond to oxygen only when it falls substantially (generally below about 60 mmHg [VERIFY]). Voluntary hyperventilation lowers PCO₂ and raises pH, which explains why it makes a person dizzy and tingly, and why a person who hyperventilates before swimming underwater may lose consciousness without feeling the urge to breathe.

**Worked Example 1: Boyle's law (skill: pressure and volume)**

At the end of expiration the lungs hold 2.50 L of air at atmospheric pressure (760 mmHg). The respiratory muscles then enlarge the lungs at constant temperature before any new air has entered. By how much must the volume grow to bring the alveolar pressure down to 758 mmHg?

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂, so V₂ = (760 × 2.50) / 758 = 2.507 L. An increase of only about 7 mL (0.3 percent) lowers the pressure by 2 mmHg, enough to draw air in. This is why alveolar pressure swings only a few mmHg during quiet breathing: air enters as soon as the pressure drops, so the pressure never moves far from atmospheric.

**Worked Example 2: Partial pressures at altitude (skill: Dalton's law)**

At a high mountain camp the total pressure is 480 mmHg. (a) What is the PO₂ of dry air? (b) If the alveolar PCO₂ is 40 mmHg and R = 0.8, estimate the alveolar PO₂ at sea level (inspired PO₂ 150 mmHg) when the person hypoventilates and PCO₂ rises to 60 mmHg.

(a) PO₂ = 0.21 × 480 = 101 mmHg, about 63 percent of the sea-level value, although the air is still 21 percent oxygen. (b) Normal: 150 − 40 / 0.8 = 100 mmHg. With hypoventilation: 150 − 60 / 0.8 = 75 mmHg. Less ventilation means less oxygen delivered and more CO₂ retained, so oxygen falls as carbon dioxide rises.

**Worked Example 3: Oxygen content (skill: anemia)**

Calculate the oxygen content of arterial blood (PO₂ 100 mmHg, saturation 98 percent) for a person with hemoglobin 15 g/dL, and for a person with anemia and hemoglobin 8 g/dL.

Bound O₂ (normal) = 1.34 × 15 × 0.98 = 19.7 mL/dL. Dissolved = 0.003 × 100 = 0.3 mL/dL. Total is about 20.0 mL/dL. In anemia, bound O₂ = 1.34 × 8 × 0.98 = 10.5 mL/dL, and the total is about 10.8 mL/dL, about half the normal. The PO₂ and saturation are normal, so breathing does not help much; the transport capacity is what is missing.

**Worked Example 4: Acid-base calculation (skill: Henderson-Hasselbalch)**

A patient has hypoventilation, with arterial PCO₂ = 60 mmHg and [HCO₃⁻] = 24 mM. (a) Calculate the pH. (b) After several days the kidneys raise bicarbonate to 30 mM. What is the new pH?

(a) Dissolved CO₂ = 0.03 × 60 = 1.8 mM. Ratio = 24 / 1.8 = 13.3, log = 1.125, so pH = 6.1 + 1.125 = 7.22 (acidosis). (b) Ratio = 30 / 1.8 = 16.7, log = 1.222, so pH = 7.32. The kidney partially compensates, but the pH is still below 7.40: compensation is usually incomplete.

**Worked Example 5: Alveolar ventilation (skill: dead space)**

Two people each have a minute ventilation of 6.0 L/min. Person A breathes 12 times per minute at 500 mL. Person B breathes 24 times per minute at 250 mL. The dead space is 150 mL. Compare their alveolar ventilation.

A: 12 × (500 − 150) = 4,200 mL/min. B: 24 × (250 − 150) = 2,400 mL/min. Both move 6,000 mL/min, but B's alveolar ventilation is 43 percent lower, because the dead space takes 150 of every 250 mL breath. Deep, slow breaths ventilate the alveoli better than shallow, fast ones.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: For acid-base, use two steps: the pH direction names the disorder, and the PCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ that moved the pH that way names its type. The other number is compensation.

Praxis Tip: Bohr effect in one sentence: where there is work (CO₂, acid, heat), hemoglobin lets go of oxygen. Haldane effect in one sentence: where there is oxygen, hemoglobin lets go of CO₂ and acid.

Praxis Tip: Anemia and carbon monoxide poisoning both cut oxygen delivery without lowering the dissolved PO₂. If a passage shows a normal PO₂ and a sick patient, look at hemoglobin.

Praxis Tip: Breathing is driven chiefly by CO₂ (through pH), not by oxygen. A person who breathes into a bag and lets CO₂ build up will breathe harder, whereas one who breathes pure nitrogen may not notice until too late.

Praxis Tip: In a lung disease question, ask whether the problem is getting air in (compliance), getting air out (resistance and recoil), or getting gas across the wall (diffusion). Each has its own signature.

**Common Mistakes**

- Saying that oxygen concentration falls at altitude. The percentage is constant, and the partial pressure falls.
- Forgetting that inspiration is active and quiet expiration is passive.
- Stating that CO₂ is carried mostly dissolved or on hemoglobin, when most is carried as bicarbonate.
- Reading the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation upside down, so that more CO₂ looks like more alkalosis.
- Treating compensation as the primary problem, or expecting it to restore the pH completely.
- Believing that low oxygen is the main normal stimulus of breathing. The main stimulus is PCO₂ and pH.
- Confusing the Bohr effect (CO₂ and H⁺ lower the oxygen affinity) with the Haldane effect (oxygen lowers the CO₂ and H⁺ affinity).
- Assuming that surfactant raises surface tension, or that it acts only to keep the lung open at inspiration.

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** By Boyle's law, a larger volume means a lower pressure, so air flows from the atmosphere into the lungs. (A relaxation of the diaphragm reduces the chest volume; B reverses the volume change caused by contraction; D the lungs have no muscle and follow the chest wall.)
2. **A.** Dalton's law gives PO₂ as 0.21 times the total pressure, which is about 101 mmHg here. (B the percentage of oxygen is essentially constant with altitude; C solubility is not the cause of the lower inspired pressure; D the CO₂ fraction of air is tiny and does not change with altitude.)
3. **B.** Raised CO₂, H⁺ and temperature all shift the curve to the right, so hemoglobin gives up more oxygen at a given PO₂ (the Bohr effect). (A would hold oxygen more tightly where it is needed; C the Bohr effect applies to adult hemoglobin; D the changes shift the curve and do not abolish saturation at high PO₂.)
4. **D.** Carbonic anhydrase in red cells converts CO₂ to H₂CO₃, which dissociates to H⁺ and HCO₃⁻, and the bicarbonate leaves in exchange for chloride. (A is only a small fraction of the total; B accounts for a minority, perhaps a fifth of the total; C H₂CO₃ dissociates quickly, so little exists as such.)
5. **B.** The pH is high (alkalosis), and the PCO₂ that moved it is low because the person is blowing off CO₂, which makes the change respiratory. (A would show a raised PCO₂ and a pH below 7.35; C would show a low pH and a low bicarbonate; D would show a primary rise in bicarbonate, not a fall in PCO₂.)
6. **C.** Central chemoreceptors respond to the pH change produced by CO₂, and negative feedback keeps arterial PCO₂ near 40 mmHg. (A oxygen sensors respond strongly only when PO₂ falls well below this; B stretch receptors in the lungs limit inflation but do not set the chemical drive; D bicarbonate crosses into the cerebrospinal fluid poorly and is not the main signal.)
7. **D.** Surfactant lowers surface tension, which reduces the pressure that tends to collapse alveoli and makes the lungs easier to inflate. (A cartilage defects are not the explanation for alveolar collapse; B wall thickness affects diffusion, not collapse; C muscle failure is not caused by a lack of surfactant.)
