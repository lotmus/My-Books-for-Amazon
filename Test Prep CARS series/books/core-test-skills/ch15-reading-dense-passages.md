# Strategy: Reading Dense Passages

**What you will be able to do**

- Read a long passage for structure and purpose, not for every detail.
- Build a one-line map of each paragraph as you go.
- Handle unfamiliar words and unfamiliar topics without stalling.

**Why it matters**

Many exams hand you a passage on a subject you may never have thought about, written in sentences that seem designed to postpone the verb. The surprise is not that these passages are hard to read. It is that people try to read them like novels. A passage on an exam is a tool: it contains a point, a structure and some evidence, and the questions ask about those. You are not asked to enjoy it, although a faint sense of enjoyment is permitted.

Praxis Tip: Before you begin, note how many questions follow the passage. Knowing the stakes tells you how carefully to read.

**What to extract**

For any passage, aim to know:

1. **The topic** (what it is about).
2. **The main point** (what the author wants you to believe or understand).
3. **The structure** (how the author builds it: problem and solution, claim and objection, cause and effect, chronology, comparison).
4. **The author's attitude** (neutral, critical, supportive, doubtful).
5. **Where to find the details**, not the details themselves. You can look them up when a question asks.

Praxis Tip: Do not try to memorize details on the first read. Locate them. "Third paragraph, an example about railways" is a perfectly good note.

**Signal words**

Certain words tell you what a sentence is doing:

- **Contrast:** however, but, yet, although, nevertheless, on the other hand. The point often follows.
- **Continuation:** moreover, furthermore, in addition.
- **Cause and effect:** because, therefore, thus, as a result.
- **Example:** for instance, such as.
- **Conclusion:** in short, ultimately, in sum.
- **Opinion:** claims, argues, suggests, fortunately, unfortunately, surprisingly.

Praxis Tip: Jot down the contrast words on your scratch paper as you read. The claim the author actually cares about often follows a "but."

**The paragraph map**

After each paragraph, write a short phrase of about five words on your scratch paper, noting its job: "defines term," "gives example," "objection," "author's reply." At the end you have a skeleton of the whole argument.

**Worked Example: Mapping an original passage**

*Passage.*

(1) Before railways, every town kept its own time. Clocks were set by the sun, so noon in one town arrived a few minutes before noon in the next town to the west. For travelers moving by horse, this mattered little.

(2) Railways changed that. A timetable listing departures from a dozen stations is useless if each station disagrees about the hour. Railway companies therefore began to use a single standard time along their lines, and in time the practice spread beyond the stations.

(3) Not everyone welcomed the change. Some townspeople argued that the sun, not a company, should set the hour. Yet the convenience of a shared time proved hard to resist, and local time gradually gave way.

*Map.*
(1) Old situation: each town used local solar time; didn't matter much.
(2) Railways needed one time; standard time spread.
(3) Objection; but convenience won.

*Topic:* the adoption of standard time. *Main point:* the practical needs of the railways led standard time to replace local solar time, despite objections. *Structure:* old situation, cause of change, resistance and outcome. *Attitude:* neutral, descriptive.

*Sample questions.*

1. The passage is primarily concerned with
 A. the invention of the mechanical clock
 B. how railways led to the use of standard time
 C. why the sun is an unreliable guide to time
 D. objections to the use of timetables
 
 **Answer: B.** The map shows the whole passage turns on railways causing standard time. (A is never mentioned. C overstates paragraph 3. D mistakes a detail for the main point.)

2. According to the passage, local solar time mattered little before railways because
 A. all towns agreed on the hour
 B. travelers moved relatively slowly
 C. people did not use clocks
 D. towns were too far apart to compare times
 
 **Answer: B.** Paragraph 1: "For travelers moving by horse, this mattered little." (A contradicts the passage. C and D are not stated.)

3. The author's attitude toward the objection described in paragraph 3 is best described as
 A. dismissive
 B. neutral and reporting
 C. enthusiastic
 D. alarmed
 
 **Answer: B.** The author reports the objection and the outcome without judging either. ("Proved hard to resist" describes events; it is not a scornful comment.)

*Skill used:* reading for structure, then returning to the text only when a question asks for a detail.

Praxis Tip: For detail questions, return to the passage and find the sentence. Do not trust memory, especially when two choices both sound plausible.

**Unfamiliar words and topics**

- **Unknown word:** read the sentence for context, guess a rough meaning (positive, negative, large, small), and keep going. You rarely need the precise definition.
- **Unfamiliar topic:** the passage contains what you need. Resist the urge to supplement with outside knowledge, which can lead you to answers the passage does not support.
- **Difficult sentence:** find the subject and the verb first, then add modifiers.

Praxis Tip: If a sentence has defeated you after two tries, jot a question mark on your scratch paper and move on. If a question depends on it, you will return with a purpose.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph with extra care. They are where authors tend to put their signposts.

Praxis Tip: Practice with dull material on purpose: government reports, scientific summaries, committee minutes. If you can map those, a bit of exam prose should not alarm you.

Praxis Tip: Read the question stems before the passage when the exam has a lot of detail questions. Then you know what to watch for. (For main-idea questions, read the passage first.)

Praxis Tip: Answer from the passage, not from what you believe is true. The exam is checking your reading, not your opinions.

**Common mistakes**

- Trying to understand and memorize every sentence.
- Importing outside knowledge.
- Missing a contrast word that changed the point.
- Confusing a detail with the main idea.
- Rereading the whole passage when one sentence is what you need.

**Do this today**

1. Pick a 400-word article and write a five-word map for each paragraph.
2. State the main point in one sentence.
3. Write the author's attitude and where it shows.

**Recap**

Read for topic, point, structure and attitude. Map each paragraph, note the contrast words, and look up details only when asked.
