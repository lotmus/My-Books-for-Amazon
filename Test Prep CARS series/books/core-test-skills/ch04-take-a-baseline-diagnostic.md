# Strategy: Take a Baseline Diagnostic

**What you will be able to do**

- Run a diagnostic under realistic conditions and avoid the usual ways of spoiling it.
- Read the results by topic and by cause of error, not just by total.
- Decide what a baseline does and does not tell you.

**Why it matters**

You cannot choose where to spend 200 hours without knowing where you start. A diagnostic is a measuring tool, not a grade. Its job is to show which topics are shaky, which are solid, and whether your mistakes come from not knowing the material or from something else. Taking it honestly means taking it cold, which is unpleasant, and useful in direct proportion.

Praxis Tip: Think of the diagnostic as a medical test. Nobody gets better by arguing with the thermometer.

**Setting it up**

1. **Use legitimate material.** Use official sample material the exam owner provides or a reputable set of original practice questions. Never use leaked or pirated questions; that is unreliable and often a violation of the owner's rules.
2. **Take it cold.** Do not prepare first. If the baseline is inflated by cramming, your plan will be built on an optimistic picture.
3. **Time it for {EXAM}.** Use the real time limits from your fact sheet (Chapter 2), in one quiet block.
4. **No notes, no phone.** Conditions should match the real test as closely as is practical.
5. **Note your confidence.** For each answer, write high, medium or low confidence on your answer sheet. This pays off in Chapter 12.
6. **Do not look anything up** until you have finished and recorded your answers.

Praxis Tip: If you must split the diagnostic across two sittings, split at a section boundary, not in the middle of a section.

**Reading the results**

A total score is the least useful number on the page. Break the results down three ways.

**By topic.** Group questions by subject area from the official content outline, and compute accuracy for each. Topics well below your overall accuracy are candidates for early attention; topics well above can wait for review.

**By cause of error.** For every wrong answer, assign one cause:

- *Content gap:* you did not know the material.
- *Misread:* you knew it, but misread the question or a number.
- *Rushed:* you ran out of time or hurried.
- *Trap choice:* you were tempted by a plausible wrong answer.
- *Calculation slip:* right method, wrong arithmetic.

**By confidence.** High-confidence wrong answers deserve the most attention. They mean you believe something false, which is worse than knowing you do not know.

Praxis Tip: Do the cause-of-error classification the same day, while you can still remember why you picked each answer. By the next morning you will have invented a flattering story.

**Worked Example: Reading a made-up diagnostic**

An invented learner answers 60 questions across four topics. Results:

| Topic | Questions | Correct | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 15 | 12 | 80% |
| B | 15 | 9 | 60% |
| C | 15 | 6 | 40% |
| D | 15 | 11 | 73% |
| Total | 60 | 38 | 63% |

Check: 12 + 9 + 6 + 11 = 38; 38 ÷ 60 ≈ 63.3 percent. Topic C is the clear first target.

Now classify the 22 wrong answers: 10 content gaps, 5 misreads, 3 rushed, 3 trap choices, 1 calculation slip. (10 + 5 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 22 ✓.) Content gaps account for 10 ÷ 22 ≈ 45 percent of errors, so content study matters most, but misreads plus rushed together are 8 of 22 (about 36 percent) and can be improved without learning a single new fact. A plan that treats all 22 errors as content gaps would waste that opportunity.

*Skill used:* separating "what I do not know" from "how I take the test."

Praxis Tip: Always compute the share of errors you could fix without new content. It is usually larger than people expect, and it is the cheapest improvement available.

**What a baseline cannot tell you**

One diagnostic is a rough guide, not a verdict. Small samples bounce around, and you may have been tired, nervous, or lucky. Do not set a target from it alone. Treat it as the first data point on a line, and take another after a few weeks of work.

Praxis Tip: If your baseline is lower than you hoped, resist the urge to recalculate your entire life. If it is higher than you hoped, resist the urge to relax.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Do not look up the answers mid-test, however tempting. The point is to find out what you do not know, and looking it up hides it from you.

Praxis Tip: Write the date and conditions on the diagnostic sheet (sleep, time of day). Later comparisons will need them.

Praxis Tip: Keep your confidence marks. A learner who is often confident and wrong has a different problem from one who is often unsure and right.

**Common mistakes**

- Studying first, so the "baseline" is not one.
- Skipping the cause-of-error step.
- Reading the total only.
- Using unofficial or pirated questions.
- Deciding your fate from one test.

**Do this today**

1. Schedule the diagnostic as a block in your calendar this week.
2. Open your fact sheet from Chapter 2 (the copy in your notebook or spreadsheet).
3. After finishing, classify each wrong answer by cause and each answer by confidence.
4. Compute topic accuracy and the share of errors not caused by content gaps.

**Recap**

A baseline is a measurement. Break it down by topic, cause and confidence, and use it to aim your first weeks.
