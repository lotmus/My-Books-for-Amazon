**Why it matters**

Most people prepare for {EXAM} by doing what feels productive: reading the book again, highlighting in three colors, watching a video at 1.5 times speed. These activities are pleasant, and they are not worthless. They are, however, rather like polishing the car you intend to sell: it looks better, and the engine is no different.

This chapter explains why, and sets out how the rest of the course is organized. Every later chapter is built on the four habits below. If you read only one chapter in Part 1, make it this one.

**The four habits**

**1. Retrieval: pull the answer out, do not push it in.** Retrieval practice means trying to recall information from memory before checking. A flashcard, a practice question, a blank-page summary, or explaining an idea aloud all count. Research on learning generally finds that this kind of effortful recall produces more durable memory than the same time spent re-reading. The effort is the point. If it feels slightly uncomfortable, it is probably working.

**2. Spacing: distribute the practice.** Studying the same material in several shorter sessions spread over days or weeks usually produces better retention than one long session. Cramming can work for tomorrow's quiz, but it tends to evaporate shortly afterward. If {EXAM} is months away, spacing is the single cheapest improvement available.

**3. Interleaving: mix the problem types.** Practicing one type of problem 20 times in a row feels smooth and builds a false sense of mastery. Mixing types forces you to work out which method applies, which is exactly what the real exam does. Mixed practice feels harder and usually helps more.

**4. Feedback: find out what you got wrong, and why.** A wrong answer with no explanation teaches little. A wrong answer you can diagnose ("I misread the question" versus "I never learned this") tells you precisely what to fix. Chapter 9 turns this into a system.

Praxis Tip: When a study method feels easy and smooth, ask whether you are retrieving or merely recognizing. Recognizing ("yes, that looks familiar") is much weaker than recalling.

**The illusion of knowing**

Fluency is the feeling that you understand something because it reads smoothly. It is easy to confuse with learning. You read a page about acid-base chemistry, nod along, and close the book feeling informed. Then a question appears and the information is nowhere to be found. Nothing mysterious has happened: the information was on the page, not in your head.

The cure is a quick self-test: close the book and try to say or write the main points. Whatever you cannot produce is what you did not learn. This is deflating the first time. It is also the most useful information you will get all week.

Praxis Tip: After every study session, spend the last five minutes writing down everything you remember without looking. Check against the material. The gaps become tomorrow's first task.

**Worked Example: Two ways to spend 10 hours**

Imagine two learners preparing for the same section. These numbers are invented for illustration, not from a study.

*Learner A* reads the textbook chapter on a topic twice, highlights, and re-reads the highlights. Total: 5 hours on the chapter. Then she does 20 practice questions on that topic only. Total: 5 hours. She feels well prepared.

*Learner B* reads the chapter once (1.5 hours), then closes the book and writes a one-page summary from memory (0.5 hours), checks it, and notes the gaps. Two days later, he spends an hour answering mixed practice questions from several topics, reviews every miss (0.5 hours), and repeats a shorter version five days later. The rest of his 10 hours follows the same pattern across different topics.

Both spent the same time. Learner B will probably feel less sure of himself along the way, because his sessions kept exposing gaps. That discomfort is information. Learner A felt confident because nothing challenged her. Which one is better prepared depends on what the real exam asks, and the exam does not hand over the chapter.

*Skill used:* comparing study strategies by what they require your memory to do, not by how they feel.

Praxis Tip: Judge a study method by one question: "Did this force me to produce an answer from memory?" If not, add that step.

**Common mistakes**

- Re-reading as the main study method.
- Highlighting nearly everything.
- Practicing one topic for hours, then never revisiting it.
- Skipping the review of wrong answers because it is unpleasant.
- Treating "I recognize this" as "I know this."
- Waiting to feel ready before testing yourself. Testing yourself is how you get ready.

**Do this today**

1. Pick one topic you studied recently.
2. Close every book and tab. Set a timer for five minutes and write everything you remember.
3. Check your notes against the source. Note every gap in your notebook, in a different color of ink.
4. Write the date, then schedule a repeat of this exercise in two days.

**Recap**

Four habits drive this course: retrieve, space, mix, and get feedback. Smooth study sessions feel good and often teach little. Whatever you cannot recall is what you have not yet learned, and finding it early is the whole game.
