# Strategy: Writing Under Time

**What you will be able to do**

- Plan a short essay or response in about two minutes.
- Use a simple structure that works for most prompts.
- Edit quickly with a checklist, in the time that remains.

**Why it matters**

If {EXAM} includes a written section, it asks for a clear, organized response in a short time, usually with no chance of a second draft. The task is not to write beautifully. It is to take a position, support it, and be easy to follow. Graders are reading many responses and rewarding the ones that make their reasoning visible.

If {EXAM} has no writing section, this chapter still pays off in short-answer responses, application essays, and every email you will ever write.

Praxis Tip: Check your fact sheet from Chapter 2 for the writing format: number of prompts, time limit, and how responses are scored in general terms. Prepare for that format, not an imagined one.

**A time budget**

For a 30-minute response, a reasonable split is:

- Planning: 3 minutes (10 percent)
- Writing: 22 minutes (about 73 percent)
- Editing: 5 minutes (about 17 percent)

Scale the numbers for your own time limit. The key point is that planning and editing both get time. Writers who skip the plan tend to wander; those who skip editing leave in errors they would have caught.

Praxis Tip: Practice the proportions with a timer. The first few attempts will feel too rushed in the writing phase; this is normal and gets better quickly.

**The two-minute plan**

1. **Read the prompt twice** and note what it asks you to do (explain, argue, compare, evaluate).
2. **Choose a position** in one sentence. This is your thesis.
3. **List two or three supporting points,** each with a specific example or piece of evidence.
4. **Consider an objection** and decide how you will answer it.
5. **Number your points** in the order you will write them.

Praxis Tip: Choose the position you can support best, not necessarily the one you believe. Graders are assessing your reasoning, not your opinion.

**A simple structure**

- **Introduction (2-3 sentences):** restate the issue briefly and state your thesis.
- **Body paragraphs (one per point):** a topic sentence that makes the point, then evidence or an example, then a sentence tying it back to the thesis.
- **Counterargument paragraph (optional but powerful):** state the strongest objection fairly, then respond.
- **Conclusion (1-2 sentences):** restate the thesis in a fresh way and say why it matters. Do not introduce new points.

Praxis Tip: Make the first sentence of each paragraph a mini-thesis. A reader skimming only those sentences should still follow your argument.

**Worked Example: Prompt, plan and response**

*Prompt.* "Some people say it is better to specialize early in one field than to explore many fields before choosing. To what extent do you agree?"

*Plan.* 
- Thesis: Exploring first is usually better, but only for a limited period.
- Point 1: Exploration helps people find where their strengths and interests lie (example: a student who tries three courses and discovers a fit).
- Point 2: Skills from one field often transfer to another.
- Objection: Early specialists gain a head start in demanding fields.
- Reply: A head start matters less than choosing a path one will sustain, and exploration can be time-limited.

*Sample response (about 180 words).*

> Specializing early offers a head start, but I believe a short period of exploration usually serves people better. The main reason is that interests and strengths are hard to predict. A student who samples three courses before choosing is far more likely to pick a path she will stay with than one who commits to the first field she meets, and persistence matters more over a long career than the first year's progress.
>
> A second reason is that skills travel. Someone who studies both statistics and writing, for example, can explain data clearly, a combination that neither field alone would provide. Breadth therefore tends to enrich depth rather than compete with it.
>
> Critics rightly point out that in demanding fields, such as elite music performance, late starters can struggle to catch up. This is a fair concern. But it argues for keeping exploration brief and purposeful rather than abandoning it. A year of well-chosen sampling costs little against the cost of a wrong decade.
>
> In short, specialization is the goal, but exploration is the sensible way to reach it.

*Skill used:* planning in two minutes and writing a complete response in a few paragraphs, using the prompt's own terms.

Praxis Tip: Use specific, small examples. "A student who samples three courses" is more convincing than "many people."

**The edit checklist**

With your last five minutes, check, in this order:

1. Does the first paragraph state a clear position?
2. Does each paragraph begin with a sentence that makes a point?
3. Is any sentence confusing? Shorten it.
4. Are there missing words, wrong words, or repeated words?
5. Does the conclusion match the thesis?

Do not rewrite. Fix what is wrong and stop.

Praxis Tip: Read your response aloud in your head. Your ear catches missing words that your eye slides over.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Write in plain, direct sentences. Long, ornate sentences are easy to lose control of at minute 25.

Praxis Tip: Practice writing the introduction and the first sentence of each body paragraph for ten different prompts. This is a fast way to build fluency in structure.

Praxis Tip: Use transitions that show relationship ("however," "as a result," "for example"), not decorative ones. They tell the reader how your ideas connect.

Praxis Tip: If you run short of time, finish the conclusion with a single sentence rather than leaving the essay unfinished. An ending, even a brief one, shows the argument is complete.

**Common mistakes**

- Starting to write without a plan.
- No clear thesis.
- Listing points without evidence.
- Ignoring the counterargument.
- Using the whole time to write and none to edit.
- Introducing new ideas in the conclusion.

**Do this today**

1. Pick a sample prompt and set a 30-minute timer.
2. Spend 3 minutes planning, 22 writing, 5 editing.
3. Check the topic sentence of each paragraph and see that they tell the story alone.
4. Note the one habit that cost you the most time.

**Recap**

Plan in two minutes, take a position, support it with specific points, answer an objection, and edit with a checklist. Clarity beats cleverness.
