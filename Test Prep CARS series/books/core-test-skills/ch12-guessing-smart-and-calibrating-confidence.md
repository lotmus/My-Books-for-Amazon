# Strategy: Guessing Smart and Calibrating Confidence

**What you will be able to do**

- Decide when guessing is right, using your exam's actual scoring rule.
- Calculate the expected value of a guess after eliminating choices.
- Use confidence ratings to find where your self-assessment is off.

**Why it matters**

Everyone guesses on a hard exam. The only question is whether you guess well. The right guessing policy depends on one fact: does a wrong answer cost anything? Many exams have no penalty; some do. A mistaken policy in either direction can cost points: leaving blanks where guessing is free, or guessing wildly where it is not.

Separately, your *confidence* is a kind of data. Learners who are sure and wrong, or unsure and right, have different problems with different fixes.

Praxis Tip: Check the penalty rule in your fact sheet (Chapter 2) before reading anything else in this chapter. The answer decides everything below.

**Step one: know the scoring rule**

- **No penalty for wrong answers:** always answer every question. A blank can only lose points.
- **Penalty for wrong answers:** the penalty is usually set so that a *completely random* guess has an expected value of zero. In that case, a guess helps only if you can eliminate at least one choice.

The expected value (EV) of a guess is: (probability of being right × points for right) − (probability of being wrong × penalty).

Praxis Tip: Read the exam owner's wording on scoring carefully, and check it again before test day. If it is unclear, ask the owner. Do not rely on what your friend remembers.

**Worked Example 1: Is a guess worth it?**

Suppose a question has 5 choices, a right answer earns 1 point, and a wrong answer loses 1/4 point.

- *Pure random guess (5 choices):* EV = (1/5)(1) − (4/5)(1/4) = 0.20 − 0.20 = 0. A guess neither helps nor hurts on average.
- *After eliminating 1 choice (4 left):* EV = (1/4)(1) − (3/4)(1/4) = 0.25 − 0.1875 = 0.0625. Slightly positive.
- *After eliminating 2 choices (3 left):* EV = (1/3)(1) − (2/3)(1/4) = 0.3333 − 0.1667 = 0.1667. Clearly positive.

So under this rule, guess when you can eliminate at least one choice, and more confidently as you eliminate more. Under a no-penalty rule, the EV of any answer is positive, so answer everything.

*Skill used:* computing the value of a decision instead of arguing from fear or optimism.

Praxis Tip: Elimination is the whole skill. Practice crossing out at least one choice on every question you are unsure of, even in ordinary study.

**Step two: guess consistently**

If you must guess blindly, pick the same letter every time on those questions rather than switching around. Over many questions, a single letter gives you the same expected result as any other policy, with less dithering. (Do not read patterns into answer positions; a good exam does not leak them.)

Praxis Tip: Decide your blind-guess letter before the exam. It saves a few seconds each time and removes the temptation to re-argue the choice.

**Step three: calibrate your confidence**

During practice, note a confidence level for each answer on your answer sheet: high, medium or low. Afterward, compare confidence with accuracy.

*Well calibrated:* high-confidence answers are mostly right, low-confidence answers are right far less often.
*Overconfident:* high-confidence answers are wrong too often. You believe false things.
*Underconfident:* low-confidence answers are right more often than you expect. You know more than you trust.

**Worked Example 2: Reading a calibration table**

An invented learner rates 60 answers:

| Confidence | Answers | Correct | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | 20 | 14 | 70% |
| Medium | 20 | 12 | 60% |
| Low | 20 | 10 | 50% |
| Total | 60 | 36 | 60% |

Check: 14 + 12 + 10 = 36; 14/20 = 70%; 12/20 = 60%; 10/20 = 50%; 36/60 = 60%.

*Reading it.* Her "high" answers are right only 70 percent of the time, which is not very high, and her "low" answers are right half the time, so her confidence tells her little. The pattern suggests two jobs: study the six high-confidence misses (they are false beliefs worth finding), and learn to recognize what a truly solid answer feels like. The signal she most wants is a bigger spread between high and low.

*Skill used:* treating confidence as a measurement and checking it against results.

Praxis Tip: Always review high-confidence misses first. They are the most expensive errors, because you will not catch them by feeling uncertain.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Before guessing, spend ten seconds on elimination. Ten seconds of elimination is worth more than ten seconds of staring.

Praxis Tip: If you change an answer, write a reason beside the question number on your answer sheet. Review later how often changes helped versus hurt.

Praxis Tip: Do not use "it feels like C" as a reason. If you cannot say why, record it as low confidence and move on.

Praxis Tip: After a section, resist the urge to replay the guesses in your head. The next section will not benefit from it.

**Common mistakes**

- Leaving blanks when there is no penalty.
- Guessing wildly when there is a penalty and you have eliminated nothing.
- Treating confidence as proof.
- Never recording confidence, so never learning where it misleads you.
- Believing there is a magic "most common answer" letter.

**Do this today**

1. Find your exam's wrong-answer rule in the fact sheet.
2. If there is a penalty, compute the EV for 0, 1, 2 and 3 eliminated choices.
3. Take a short practice set and record confidence for every answer.
4. Build the calibration table.

**Recap**

Know the scoring rule, eliminate whenever you can, and treat confidence as data. Review the answers you were sure about and got wrong, since those are where the real surprises live.
