# Strategy: The Quantitative Toolkit

**What you will be able to do**

- Estimate before calculating, and use the estimate to catch mistakes.
- Handle percents, ratios and powers of ten quickly and reliably.
- Track units through a calculation and use them as a free error check.

**Why it matters**

Numbers appear on nearly every exam, sometimes where you least expect them: a science passage, a data table, a reading-comprehension chart. The people who handle them well are not necessarily the best at arithmetic. They are the ones who have a few dependable habits: estimate first, simplify, keep track of units, and check whether the answer is plausible. Mental arithmetic is a pleasant bonus. These habits are the real skill.

Praxis Tip: Where {EXAM} allows a calculator, do not use it for anything you could estimate in five seconds. Where it does not, you needed these habits already.

**Estimate first**

Round to friendly numbers, compute, and see which answer choice is closest. If the choices are far apart (say, 10, 100, 1,000), an estimate settles it. If they are close together, the estimate at least tells you the region to be careful in.

Praxis Tip: Look at the answer choices *before* deciding how precisely to calculate. Widely spaced choices reward rough work.

**Percents**

- 10 percent of a number: shift the decimal point left by one place.
- 5 percent: half of 10 percent. 15 percent: 10 plus 5. 1 percent: shift two places.
- 50 percent = 1/2, 25 percent = 1/4, 75 percent = 3/4, 12.5 percent = 1/8, 37.5 percent = 3/8, 33.3 percent ≈ 1/3, 20 percent = 1/5.
- Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old. The base is the **old** value.
- A rise from 40 to 50 is +25 percent (10 ÷ 40), but a fall from 50 to 40 is −20 percent (10 ÷ 50). Same gap, different base.

Praxis Tip: When a percent question feels tangled, ask "percent of what?" Most errors come from choosing the wrong base.

**Ratios and proportions**

A ratio of 3:2 means the whole splits into 3 + 2 = 5 parts. If the whole is 50, one part is 10, giving 30 and 20.

For proportional relationships, set up a table: quantity A over quantity B stays constant. Cross-multiplying works, but so does asking "how many times bigger?" (Scaling by a factor is quicker than algebra.)

Praxis Tip: Before computing with a ratio, say it in words: "for every 3 of these there are 2 of those." It prevents flipping the ratio.

**Powers of ten and scientific notation**

Multiply: add the exponents. Divide: subtract them. Handle the coefficients separately.

- (3 × 10⁵)(2 × 10⁻³) = (3 × 2) × 10⁵⁻³ = 6 × 10² = 600.
- (6 × 10⁸) ÷ (3 × 10³) = 2 × 10⁵.

Praxis Tip: Move to scientific notation as soon as numbers have more than three zeros. Dropping a zero is the most common arithmetic slip in the world.

**Logs without a calculator**

You will rarely need much, but two values go a long way: log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.30 and log₁₀(3) ≈ 0.48. Then log₁₀(2000) = log₁₀(2) + log₁₀(1000) ≈ 0.30 + 3 = 3.30. And log₁₀(6) = log₁₀(2) + log₁₀(3) ≈ 0.78. Remember that log₁₀(10ⁿ) = n.

Praxis Tip: Only learn log facts if {EXAM} tests logs. Check your fact sheet. Otherwise, save the memory space.

**Units as a check**

Carry the units through every step, treating them like algebra. If a speed question ends in "meters times seconds," something went wrong. Conversions are multiplication by a fraction equal to 1.

Praxis Tip: Write the conversion as a fraction with the unit you want on top and the unit you are removing on the bottom, so the unwanted unit visibly cancels.

**Worked Example 1: Estimating a product**

*Question.* About what is 4,812 × 0.198?
A. 95   B. 950   C. 9,500   D. 95,000

Round: 4,812 ≈ 4,800 and 0.198 ≈ 0.2. Then 4,800 × 0.2 = 960. The closest choice is **B (950)**. (The exact product is 952.776.) The choices are a factor of ten apart, so an estimate is all that is needed.

**Worked Example 2: A unit conversion**

*Question.* Convert 90 km/h to meters per second.

90 km/h × (1000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3600 s) = 90,000 / 3,600 m/s = 25 m/s. 

Shortcut: dividing km/h by 3.6 gives m/s: 90 ÷ 3.6 = 25. ✓

**Worked Example 3: A percent trap**

*Question.* A quantity rises from 40 to 50, then falls back to 40. What are the two percent changes?

Rise: (50 − 40)/40 = 10/40 = 25 percent. Fall: (40 − 50)/50 = −10/50 = −20 percent. The percents differ because the base changed, and a quick sanity check (the second change starts from a larger number, so the same drop is a smaller fraction) confirms it.

*Skill used:* choosing the correct base, then checking that the answer behaves sensibly.

**Sanity checks**

Ask three quick questions of every numerical answer: Is the size plausible? Is the sign right? Do the units match what was asked? An answer that says a person walks at 300 m/s fails the first test before you have looked at the working.

Praxis Tip: Keep a handful of reference values in your head for common contexts (walking speed about 1.5 m/s; a standard classroom about 10 m across). Absurd answers announce themselves quickly.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Break awkward multiplication into friendly parts: 18 × 25 = 18 × 100 ÷ 4 = 450.

Praxis Tip: For fractions, compare by cross-multiplying instead of converting to decimals: 5/8 versus 3/5 gives 25 versus 24, so 5/8 is bigger.

Praxis Tip: Write intermediate results down. Mental arithmetic is quick until it is wrong, and then it is very quick.

Praxis Tip: If your answer disagrees with your estimate by more than a small amount, trust neither until you have checked both.

**Common mistakes**

- Using the new value as the base for percent change.
- Misplacing a decimal or a zero.
- Flipping a ratio.
- Forgetting to convert units before calculating.
- Over-calculating when the choices are widely spaced.

**Do this today**

1. Make a one-page card with the percent facts, the 10-based exponent rules and your unit conversions.
2. Do five estimation problems, writing the rounded versions.
3. Take any recent wrong answer involving a number and ask which sanity check would have caught it.

**Recap**

Estimate first, choose the right base, keep your units, move to scientific notation for big numbers, and ask whether the answer makes sense. These habits matter more than speed at multiplication.
