# Strategy: The Question Method

**What you will be able to do**

- Apply a four-step routine (read, predict, eliminate, verify) to any multiple-choice question.
- Spot the common traps in answer choices.
- Slow down at the right moments without losing time overall.

**Why it matters**

Under time pressure, most people fall back on a habit: skim the question, glance at the choices, pick the one that looks right. The trouble is that wrong choices are *written to look right*. Item writers know the common mistakes and put them in as answer choices. A method turns your attention from "which one feels right" to "what does the question actually ask, and which choice survives checking."

Praxis Tip: Practice the method on easy questions. It becomes automatic only if you use it when it is not needed.

**The method**

**Step 1: Read the question carefully.** Find the actual task. Note the qualifiers (NOT, EXCEPT, LEAST, MOST, always, never, only) on your scratch paper, or highlight them on screen if the exam allows. Read the last sentence first if a long passage precedes it, so you know what to look for.

**Step 2: Predict the answer before looking at the choices.** Form a rough answer in your own words, or at least the *type* of answer (a number near X, a statement about cause, an increase or decrease). This protects you from being steered by the choices.

**Step 3: Eliminate.** Set aside choices (use the strikethrough tool on screen, or a line on scratch paper) that contradict the question, are outside the scope, are too extreme, or repeat the stem without answering it. Even removing one choice improves your odds if you must guess (Chapter 12).

**Step 4: Verify.** Check your remaining choice against the question once more, and against the passage or data if there is one. Ask: does this answer *exactly* what was asked?

Praxis Tip: Jot a few words on your scratch paper about what you are being asked to find, so that you answer the question asked. This takes one second and prevents answering a nearby question.

**Common traps**

| Trap | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Half-right | Starts correct, ends wrong or adds an unsupported claim |
| Reversal | Right idea, wrong direction (increase vs decrease) |
| Too extreme | Uses always, never, only, all, none when the situation is mixed |
| True but irrelevant | A correct statement that does not answer this question |
| Familiar phrase | Repeats words from the passage without meeting the question |
| Partial calculation | The result of step one, offered as the final answer |

Praxis Tip: Name the trap when you see it. Having a word for it ("that is a reversal") makes it far easier to spot next time.

**Worked Example: A percent question**

*Question.* A store raises a price by 20 percent, then offers 20 percent off the new price. Compared with the original price, the final price is
A. the same
B. 4 percent lower
C. 4 percent higher
D. 20 percent lower

*Step 1, read.* The task: final price versus original, as a percent change. Key words: "new price" (the discount applies to the raised price).

*Step 2, predict.* Many people think the two changes cancel (answer A). But they apply to different bases. The discount is on a bigger number, so it removes more than the increase added. Prediction: final price is lower, by a small amount.

*Step 3, eliminate.* A (same) is the tempting cancel-out error. C (higher) contradicts the prediction. D (20 percent lower) ignores the raise. That leaves B.

*Step 4, verify.* Use a convenient number: original = 100. After the raise: 100 × 1.20 = 120. After the discount: 120 × 0.80 = 96. Compared with 100, that is 4 percent lower. B is correct. ✓

*Skill used:* predicting from structure, then testing with a convenient number.

Praxis Tip: When a question has no actual numbers, pick easy ones yourself: 100, 10, 2. Your arithmetic will be faster and more reliable than symbolic algebra under pressure.

**Pace without panic**

The method adds seconds to the start of each question and removes minutes from the cost of errors. Practice it at a deliberate pace first, then speed up. Do not drop steps to go faster; instead, make each step smaller. Step 2 can be a three-second guess.

Praxis Tip: If you cannot form a prediction in about ten seconds, that is information: either the question needs a different approach or it is one to flag and return to (Chapter 11).

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Cover the choices with your hand while you read the question and form your prediction. It looks eccentric. It also works.

Praxis Tip: Choose the best of the remaining answers, not a perfect one. Some choices are only the least wrong.

Praxis Tip: When two answers look nearly identical, find the single word that differs. The question usually turns on it.

Praxis Tip: Do not change an answer without a reason you can state in a sentence. Changing it because of a feeling usually trades a right answer for a wrong one. Changing it because you spotted a misread is a different matter.

**Common mistakes**

- Reading the choices first and letting them steer you.
- Missing NOT or EXCEPT.
- Answering a nearby question (solving for x when the question asks for 2x).
- Spending the same time on every question regardless of difficulty.
- Skipping verification because you feel certain.

**Do this today**

1. Take ten practice questions and apply all four steps to each, even the easy ones.
2. Note the qualifiers and the last line of each question on scratch paper.
3. For each wrong answer, name the trap that caught you.
4. Add the trap names to your error log.

**Recap**

Read, predict, eliminate, verify. The method protects you from answer choices written to look right. It costs a few seconds per question and saves more than that in avoided errors.
