# Strategy: Set a Target and Plan Backwards

**What you will be able to do**

- Choose a target from real requirements instead of a vague wish.
- Work backward from the test date to a weekly study-hours budget.
- Split that budget between learning, practice and review.

**Why it matters**

"I want a good score" is not a plan. It cannot be scheduled, measured, or argued with. A target plus a deadline gives you something to subtract from: how many weeks, how many hours, how many practice tests. The arithmetic is not complicated, but doing it once will stop you from the two classic errors: starting too late, and making a plan for a person with 40 free hours a week who does not exist.

Praxis Tip: Do the planning arithmetic once, write the result on one page, then follow it. Re-planning every Sunday is a hobby, not preparation.

**Choosing a target**

Start from the outside in.

1. **Find the requirement.** Check the programs, scholarships, or courses you are aiming at. What do they publish about scores or results? Many publish ranges for admitted students; some have hard minimums. Use the program's own pages.
2. **Choose a range, not a point.** A target such as "{SCORE_SCALE} at or above the program's stated midpoint, with a stretch goal above it" is easier to live with than a single number.
3. **Compare with your baseline.** After the diagnostic (Chapter 4), you will know the gap. Use that to judge whether the date and the target are realistic together.
4. **Decide what you will do if you miss it.** Retake? Apply elsewhere? Settling this in advance removes the dread from the last weeks. (Chapter 28 covers retake decisions.)

Praxis Tip: Write the target and the test date on one card and tape it where you study. A visible deadline is less abstract than a calendar entry.

**Backward planning**

Work from the test date toward today.

1. **Count weeks.** Weeks from today to the test date.
2. **Subtract buffer.** Remove one week in four, or at least two weeks total. Life will interfere; a buffer is an honest acknowledgment of this.
3. **Subtract the final week.** The last week is for light review and rest, not new material.
4. **Estimate weekly hours.** Be realistic: look at your actual week, not your ideal one. Count hours you can reliably protect.
5. **Multiply.** Available weeks × weekly hours = total study hours.
6. **Allocate.** A reasonable starting split is about half to learning content, about 30 percent to practice questions and full-length simulations, and about 20 percent to review of errors and spaced repetition. Adjust after your first month.

Praxis Tip: Plan with the hours you have, not the hours you wish you had. If the plan needs 20 hours a week and you honestly have 8, the plan is the problem, and it is cheaper to discover that now.

**Worked Example: A 20-week plan**

A learner has 20 weeks until {EXAM} and can reliably study 12 hours a week.

- Weeks available: 20.
- Buffer: 2 weeks. Final lighter review week: 1 week. Study weeks: 20 − 2 − 1 = 17.
- Total hours: 17 × 12 = 204.
- Allocation: content learning 50 percent = 102 hours; practice and simulation 30 percent = 61.2 hours; error review and spaced repetition 20 percent = 40.8 hours. (102 + 61.2 + 40.8 = 204 ✓)

*Reading the plan.* If the full-length simulation takes about half a day, scheduling five of them uses roughly 5 × 4 = 20 hours of the practice budget, leaving about 41 hours for question sets. If content looks too large for 102 hours, the learner knows to cut scope (focus on weaker areas) or find more hours, now, with 20 weeks to spare.

*Skill used:* backward planning, with a sanity check against a realistic weekly capacity.

Praxis Tip: Put the five simulations on the calendar immediately, spaced about three weeks apart. Unscheduled practice tests have a way of not happening.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Write the target as a range with a floor you can live with and a stretch above it. A single number invites a bad week to feel like a catastrophe.

Praxis Tip: Tell one person your test date. Mild social pressure is cheap and surprisingly effective.

Praxis Tip: Re-check the plan after the first month with real data: hours actually studied, practice scores, error causes. Adjust once, deliberately.

Praxis Tip: Put the five simulation dates in your calendar before you add anything else. Everything else can flex around them.

**Common mistakes**

- Setting the target from a friend's score.
- Planning for ideal weeks and ignoring exams, holidays, work.
- Putting all the content first and all the practice at the end.
- No buffer weeks.
- Never revisiting the plan after the first diagnostic.

**Do this today**

1. Write your target range and your test date on one card.
2. Count weeks, subtract buffer and the final week.
3. Estimate your honest weekly hours.
4. Multiply, then split 50/30/20 and write the three numbers down.

**Recap**

Target, date, hours. Count backward from the exam, protect a buffer, and divide the hours among learning, practice and review. A plan you can keep is better than a perfect plan you cannot.
