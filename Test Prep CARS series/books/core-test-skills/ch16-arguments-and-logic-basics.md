# Strategy: Arguments and Logic Basics

**What you will be able to do**

- Break any argument into premises and a conclusion.
- Identify the assumption that links them.
- Reason correctly with "if-then" statements, including the contrapositive.
- Strengthen and weaken arguments, and spot common fallacies.

**Why it matters**

Even exams that are not "about" logic contain arguments: a science passage with a conclusion drawn from data, a reading selection with a claim and an objection, a quantitative question with a hidden assumption. Skills here transfer widely. They also make a habit of friendly, systematic doubt, which is the opposite of cynicism and rather more useful.

Praxis Tip: When you read an argument, ask two questions immediately: What is the conclusion? What would have to be true for it to follow?

**Parts of an argument**

- **Conclusion:** the claim the author wants you to accept.
- **Premises:** the reasons offered for it.
- **Assumption:** an unstated link between the premises and the conclusion. It is the thing that must be true for the argument to work.

Signal words help: *therefore, thus, so, hence* often introduce conclusions; *because, since, given that* often introduce premises.

Praxis Tip: To find the conclusion, ask "What is the author trying to convince me of?" The conclusion is not always the last sentence.

**Worked Example 1: Finding the assumption**

*Argument.* "Since the city installed street cameras, the crime rate has fallen. Therefore, the cameras reduced crime."

- Conclusion: the cameras reduced crime.
- Premise: crime fell after the cameras were installed.
- Assumption: nothing else caused the drop, such as a change in policing, the economy, or a general trend.

*Weaken it:* find evidence that crime fell by the same amount in nearby cities without cameras. That suggests another cause.
*Strengthen it:* evidence that crime fell only in areas covered by the cameras, and not elsewhere in the city, or a pattern of reductions at the times cameras were turned on.

Rule of thumb: to weaken, attack the link or offer an alternative explanation; to strengthen, support the link or rule out alternatives.

Praxis Tip: For "weaken" and "strengthen" questions, restate the argument as "Because P, therefore C, assuming A." Then look for the choice that hits A.

**Conditional statements**

An "if P, then Q" statement says: whenever P is true, Q is true. It does **not** say the reverse.

For "If it rains, the match is canceled":

| Statement | Valid? |
|---|---|
| **Contrapositive:** if the match is not canceled, it did not rain | Valid (always equivalent) |
| **Converse:** if the match is canceled, it rained | Not valid (it could be canceled for another reason) |
| **Inverse:** if it does not rain, the match is not canceled | Not valid |

Only the contrapositive is guaranteed. The converse and inverse are the most common logical errors on exams.

**Necessary and sufficient**

- **Sufficient:** if P is true, Q must be true. (P is enough.)
- **Necessary:** Q cannot be true without P. (P is required, but may not be enough.)

For "Passing requires at least 60 percent": scoring 60 percent is *necessary* for passing, but might not be *sufficient* if there is also an attendance requirement. In "if P, then Q," P is sufficient for Q, and Q is necessary for P.

Praxis Tip: Translate "only if," "required," "must have" into the necessary side, and "if," "whenever," "guarantees" into the sufficient side. A short table of your own helps.

**Worked Example 2: A conditional question**

*Statements.* "If the committee approves the budget, the project goes ahead. The project did not go ahead."

*Question.* Which must be true?
A. The committee approved the budget.
B. The committee did not approve the budget.
C. The committee has not yet met.
D. The project will never go ahead.

*Reasoning.* The contrapositive of the conditional is: if the project did not go ahead, the committee did not approve the budget. The second statement gives exactly that. **B** follows. (A contradicts it. C and D go beyond what is stated: the committee might have met and refused, and the project might go ahead later after a different approval.)

*Skill used:* applying the contrapositive and refusing to over-read.

**Common fallacies**

| Fallacy | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Correlation as causation | A and B occur together, so A causes B | Ice-cream sales and sunburns rise together, so ice cream causes sunburn (heat is the common cause) |
| Sampling problem | Conclusion about a group from an unrepresentative sample | Surveying only gym members about exercise habits of the city |
| False dichotomy | Presents two options as the only ones | "Either we ban it or we do nothing" |
| Ad hominem | Attacks the person rather than the claim | Dismissing a study because its author is unpopular |
| Appeal to authority | Treats a claim as true because someone prominent said it | Without evidence the authority has relevant expertise |
| Circular reasoning | The conclusion is also a premise | "It's true because it says so" |

Praxis Tip: Do not shout "fallacy!" at everything. Many arguments with a gap are simply incomplete. Your task on an exam is usually to find the gap, not to give it a Latin name.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Treat extreme words (all, never, only, always) in an answer choice with suspicion. They are the easiest choices to defeat with a single counterexample.

Praxis Tip: Test a conditional with a concrete case. "If a number is divisible by 4, it's even" is true; its converse, "if even, then divisible by 4," fails for 6.

Praxis Tip: In a "strengthen" question, an answer that simply restates the premise does nothing. Look for the choice that makes the link more believable.

Praxis Tip: Draw simple pictures. Circles for "all A are B" (A inside B) make quantified statements much easier to check.

**Common mistakes**

- Treating the converse as equivalent to the original.
- Confusing necessary with sufficient.
- Treating a restatement of the premise as support.
- Missing the unstated assumption because it seems too obvious.
- Overreading: concluding more than the statements allow.

**Do this today**

1. Take three short arguments from news or practice sets and label conclusion, premise and assumption.
2. Write the contrapositive, converse and inverse of two conditional sentences.
3. For each argument, write one strengthener and one weakener.

**Recap**

Find the conclusion, find the premises, find the gap. Conditionals run in one direction, and only the contrapositive is safe to flip. Strengthen or weaken by acting on the assumption.
