# Strategy: Reading Tables, Graphs and Experiments

**What you will be able to do**

- Read a table or graph in a fixed order and avoid the standard traps.
- Identify variables and controls in a described experiment.
- Separate what the data show from what they merely suggest.

**Why it matters**

Data questions look intimidating because they come with a lot of numbers, but they usually ask for something modest: a trend, a comparison, a prediction, or a judgment about what the data can and cannot support. The strongest candidates are not the best mathematicians. They are the ones who look at the labels first.

Praxis Tip: Spend your first ten seconds on a data item reading the title, axes, units and legend. Skipping this is the single most common reason for confidently answering the wrong question.

**A fixed reading order**

1. **Title and caption:** what is this about?
2. **Axes or column headings:** what is measured, and in what units?
3. **Scale:** does the axis start at zero? Is it linear or logarithmic? Are intervals even?
4. **Legend and symbols:** which series is which?
5. **Overall pattern:** rising, falling, flat, peaked, cyclic?
6. **Exceptions:** outliers, gaps, changes in trend.
7. **Only then** read the question and return for specifics.

Praxis Tip: Put your finger (or cursor) on the exact data point or row you are using. Reading the wrong row is much more common than doing the arithmetic wrong.

**Experiments: the vocabulary**

- **Independent variable:** the thing the experimenter changes on purpose.
- **Dependent variable:** the thing that is measured to see the effect.
- **Controlled variables:** things kept the same so that they do not confuse the result.
- **Control group:** the comparison group that does not receive the treatment.
- **Sample size (n):** how many subjects or measurements.
- **Replication:** repeating to see if the result holds.

Praxis Tip: Before reading results, name the independent and dependent variables in one sentence: "They changed ___ and measured ___."

**Worked Example 1: A plant experiment**

*Setup.* A researcher grows 10 identical plants in each of five groups. All receive the same light, water and soil. The groups receive 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8 grams of a fertilizer per pot. After four weeks, mean plant heights are:

| Fertilizer (g per pot) | Mean height (cm) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 10 |
| 2 | 14 |
| 4 | 18 |
| 6 | 19 |
| 8 | 17 |

- *Independent variable:* amount of fertilizer. *Dependent:* mean height after four weeks. *Controlled:* light, water, soil, plant type, time. *Control group:* 0 g.
- *Pattern:* height rises quickly from 0 to 4 g, flattens by 6 g, and drops at 8 g.
- *Percent change, 0 to 4 g:* (18 − 10) ÷ 10 = 80 percent increase.
- *Sample questions and answers.*
 1. Which dose produced the tallest mean height? **6 g (19 cm).**
 2. Does the data show 8 g is harmful? **Not on its own.** The mean fell from 19 to 17, but with only 10 plants per group and no information on spread, the difference might be chance. The data are *consistent with* a decline at high doses, which is a reason to test further, not to announce a conclusion.
 3. What would you predict at 10 g? **A cautious answer:** the trend suggests no further gain and possibly a decline, but this is beyond the data range, so any prediction is speculation. Extrapolating past the tested range is a classic trap.

*Skill used:* describing the pattern, quantifying change, and stating what the data can support.

**Worked Example 2: The suspicious axis**

A bar chart compares two products: Product P scores 96 and Product Q scores 98. The y-axis starts at 95. The bar for Q appears three times as tall as the bar for P (3 units above the baseline versus 1 unit).

The real difference is 2 points, or 2 ÷ 96 ≈ 2.1 percent. The truncated axis makes a small difference look large.

*Rule:* always check where the axis starts. A bar's *height* is only meaningful as a proportion if the axis begins at zero. 

**Correlation, causation and design**

In a controlled experiment where the experimenter assigns the treatment (ideally at random), a difference in outcome can support a causal claim. In an observational study, two things varying together may share a hidden cause, or the direction of cause may be reversed. The question to ask is always: "Could something else explain this?"

Praxis Tip: When a result sounds causal, ask who decided which subjects got the treatment. If it was the subjects themselves, be careful.

**Common data traps**

| Trap | What to do |
|---|---|
| Truncated axis | Check where the scale begins |
| Dual axes | Check which series uses which axis |
| Log scale | Equal spacing means equal *ratios*, not equal differences |
| Averages hide spread | Ask about variability and sample size |
| Extrapolation | Don't extend beyond the observed range with confidence |
| Percent of what | Check the base, as in Chapter 13 |
| Small samples | Treat dramatic effects from few subjects with caution |

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Before you compute anything, estimate the answer by eye. A bar roughly twice as tall means a ratio near 2.

Praxis Tip: When the question says "which statement is supported by the data," choose the weakest claim that is clearly true. Strong claims usually need more evidence than a single figure provides.

Praxis Tip: Note words like "most," "all," "increased," "caused." Each makes a claim the data may not carry.

Praxis Tip: Practice with graphs from real sources (public statistics offices, scientific summaries) for ten minutes a day. Reading figures is a skill that improves quickly with reps.

**Common mistakes**

- Reading the question before the figure labels.
- Ignoring units.
- Treating a mean as if every subject had that value.
- Drawing a causal conclusion from an observational pattern.
- Extrapolating beyond the range.

**Do this today**

1. Find a chart in a news article and read it using the seven-step order.
2. Write one thing it shows and one thing it does not.
3. Check the axis start and the units.
4. Describe an experiment you know in terms of independent, dependent and controlled variables.

**Recap**

Labels first, then pattern, then the question. Name the variables, check the scale, and claim only what the data support.
