# Strategy: Interleaving and Mixed Practice

**What you will be able to do**

- Explain why mixed practice feels harder and why it helps.
- Build a mixed practice set from your own topic list.
- Schedule blocked and mixed practice in the right proportions.

**Why it matters**

On the exam, nobody announces which kind of problem is coming. The questions arrive in a mixed order and you must decide, in seconds, what the question is and which tool applies. Practicing one problem type 20 times in a row builds speed at the tool but not at the choosing. Mixed practice trains both.

It also feels worse. You will make more mistakes and your sessions will feel less smooth. This is, unusually, a good sign.

Praxis Tip: If your practice sets always feel comfortable, you are probably in a rut. A feeling of mild struggle is the right setting.

**Blocked and interleaved practice**

*Blocked practice* means working on one topic or problem type at a time: all ratio problems, then all geometry problems. It is a sensible way to *learn* a new idea, because you need a few clean repetitions to understand it.

*Interleaved practice* means mixing topics or problem types in one session: a ratio problem, then a logic question, then a graph, then geometry, and so on. It is a better way to build the ability to *choose* a method and to keep older material fresh.

A good rule: block when you are first meeting a topic; interleave once you have the basics.

Praxis Tip: After finishing a new topic, do 5 to 8 blocked problems to build understanding, then immediately file the topic into your mixed rotation.

**Building a mixed set**

1. **List your topics** (the content areas from your fact sheet in Chapter 2).
2. **Pick the proportions.** A starting point is about 70 percent from recent topics and 30 percent from older ones, which keeps earlier material from fading.
3. **Randomize the order**, by shuffling cards or using a random-number list, so that you cannot predict the next topic.
4. **Do not label the problems** by topic before you attempt them. Part of the challenge is identifying the type.
5. **Record misses by cause** (Chapter 9).

Praxis Tip: Keep a small "spiral" pile: one question from each past topic, refreshed weekly. Use it as the warm-up for each session.

**Worked Example: A one-hour mixed session**

A learner has four topics (call them A, B, C, D). A and B are recent; C and D are older. In a one-hour session, she plans 30 questions at 2 minutes each.

- Recent topics (70 percent): 21 questions, so about 10 from A and 11 from B.
- Older topics (30 percent): 9 questions, so about 4 from C and 5 from D.
- Check: 10 + 11 + 4 + 5 = 30 ✓.

She writes each question number on a slip, shuffles, and works in the shuffled order. Her sequence might begin C, A, B, B, D, A, C, and so on. After finishing, she reviews every miss (taking, say, 20 more minutes), and tags each with a cause.

*What to expect.* Her accuracy on a mixed set will likely be lower than on a blocked set of the same material. That gap is not evidence that mixing is bad. It is a map of what she cannot yet do when the cue "this is a topic-A problem" is removed, which is exactly the condition of the real test.

*Skill used:* designing practice that matches the demands of the exam rather than the comfort of the learner.

Praxis Tip: Compare blocked and mixed accuracy occasionally. A large gap means you recognize the method when told the topic but not when left to spot it, so add more mixed practice.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: When two topics keep getting confused, build a set that puts them side by side. Learning to tell similar problems apart is the whole value of mixing.

Praxis Tip: Mix problem types within a topic too: easy and hard, short and long, word problems and bare equations.

Praxis Tip: Use a timer on mixed sets. The pressure of the clock exposes whether you can choose a method quickly.

Praxis Tip: Re-mix old questions you have missed before. Doing the same question in a new context is a good test of whether you learned the idea or the answer.

**Common mistakes**

- Mixing before learning the basics, which turns into confusion.
- Never mixing, then being surprised on test day.
- Sorting problems by topic before attempting them.
- Reading a lower mixed score as failure, and retreating to blocked sets.
- Forgetting to review the misses, which is where the learning is.

**Do this today**

1. List your topics in your notebook and note which are recent and which are older.
2. Build a 15-question mixed set at 70/30, shuffled.
3. Do it without topic labels, then review every miss.
4. Note one pattern: which topics did you misidentify?

**Recap**

Block to learn, interleave to master. Mixed sets feel harder because they remove the cue that tells you which method to use. That removal is the point, since the real exam does it too.
