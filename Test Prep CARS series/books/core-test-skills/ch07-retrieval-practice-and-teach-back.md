# Strategy: Retrieval Practice and Teach-Back

**What you will be able to do**

- Use four retrieval techniques beyond flashcards.
- Turn a passage of notes into a set of useful self-test questions.
- Run a teach-back session that exposes gaps, and fix them.

**Why it matters**

Chapter 1 argued that recalling information beats rereading it. This chapter turns that into techniques. Retrieval can be done with no equipment, in a few minutes, anywhere: on a walk, on a train, while the kettle boils. The only requirement is the willingness to be wrong in private, which is far cheaper than being wrong during the exam.

Praxis Tip: Start retrieval *before* you feel ready. A failed attempt at recall also strengthens learning, as long as you check the answer afterward.

**Four techniques**

**1. The blank-page summary.** After studying a topic, close everything and write what you remember: the main ideas, definitions, steps, and one example. Then compare with your source and mark gaps. Takes 5 to 10 minutes.

**2. Practice questions, early.** Answer questions before you feel ready, and treat the explanations as the lesson. Questions are a learning tool, not only a measurement tool.

**3. The question list.** Convert your notes into questions (see the example below) and answer them later from memory.

**4. Teach-back.** Explain the idea aloud to a friend, a family member, a pet, or an empty room, as if to someone intelligent who has not met the topic. Stumbling points show where the understanding is thin. This is sometimes called the Feynman technique, after the physicist known for explaining things plainly. Its value does not depend on the name.

Praxis Tip: Teach-back works best when you ban jargon you cannot define. If you say "equilibrium" and cannot say what it means in plain words, you have found your gap.

**Worked Example: From notes to questions**

Imagine you read this short note (a deliberately small, simple fact set):

> In many temperate lakes, water is densest at about 4 °C. In summer the warm water floats on top and the cold sits below. In autumn the surface cools until the water is a similar temperature top to bottom, so wind can mix the layers, a process called turnover.

Now turn it into questions of different types.

*Recall:* At about what temperature is fresh water densest?

*Cloze:* In autumn, surface water cools until the lake is a similar temperature top to bottom, allowing wind to mix the layers, a process called ______. (turnover)

*Why:* Why does warm summer water stay at the top of the lake rather than mixing downward?
(Warm water is less dense than cold water, so it floats on top. Strictly, this is the rule for water above the density maximum.)

*Application:* A lake is surface-warm in July and the water near the bottom is about 5 °C. What would you predict about mixing in July, and why?
(Little mixing; the density difference keeps the layers separate.)

*Prediction:* What would you expect to happen to mixing if a calm autumn follows a cool summer? Explain.
(Mixing still occurs, but it may be slower without wind; the cool surface and bottom water are closer in density.)

Notice the progression: recall, understanding, application, prediction. A set of five questions like this takes two minutes to write and tests far more than rereading the paragraph.

*Skill used:* converting passive notes into active questions of rising difficulty.

Praxis Tip: For every topic you study, write at least one "why" question and one "what would happen if" question. Those are the kinds that exams reward.

**Running a teach-back session**

1. Choose a topic and set a 3-minute timer.
2. Explain it aloud from memory, with one concrete example.
3. When you stall, note the spot (a tally on scratch paper will do).
4. Check your source for those spots only.
5. Repeat the explanation, shorter, the next day.

Praxis Tip: Record yourself on your phone once. Listening back is awkward and revealing: the places where you speed up or hedge are usually the places where you are guessing.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Use the commute, the queue, the walk to class. Retrieval needs no desk, only a topic and a minute.

Praxis Tip: When you cannot recall something, guess first, then check. The guess is not wasted; it prepares the memory to accept the answer.

Praxis Tip: Alternate retrieval with a different topic after each blank-page summary so you do not just rehearse what you just saw.

Praxis Tip: Keep your question lists. They make excellent flashcards later and a ready-made revision set before the exam.

**Common mistakes**

- Calling a second reading "review."
- Checking the answer before attempting recall.
- Skipping retrieval because it feels slower.
- Only writing recall questions and never application ones.
- Teaching-back to someone who will just nod.

**Do this today**

1. Choose a short note or paragraph you studied recently.
2. Write five questions: recall, cloze, why, application, prediction.
3. Close the source and answer them in writing.
4. Check, note the gaps, and schedule a repeat in two days.

**Recap**

Four techniques: blank-page summaries, early practice questions, self-made question lists, and teach-back. Aim your questions up the ladder from recall to prediction, and treat every stall as a free pointer to what needs work.
