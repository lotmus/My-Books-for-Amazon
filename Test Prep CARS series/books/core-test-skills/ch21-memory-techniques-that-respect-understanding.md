# Strategy: Memory Techniques That Respect Understanding

**What you will be able to do**

- Use chunking, mnemonics and the method of loci for material that really is just a list.
- Tell when a memory trick helps and when it hides a gap in understanding.
- Combine tricks with retrieval and spacing so they last.

**Why it matters**

Some things must simply be remembered: a formula, a sequence, a set of terms, a list of exceptions. There is no deep reason that the order is what it is, and understanding will not conjure it. For these, memory techniques are legitimate and efficient. For everything else, they are a dangerous way to feel you know something you do not. This chapter is about telling the two apart.

Praxis Tip: Before you reach for a trick, ask: "Could I derive or explain this?" If yes, learn the reasoning. If it is an arbitrary fact, use a trick.

**Chunking**

Working memory is small. People can hold only a handful of separate items at once (figures of about four to seven are often quoted, and they vary by task). Grouping items into meaningful chunks lets you hold more.

*Example.* The digits 8005551234 are hard to hold as ten items. As 800-555-1234, they are three chunks. A formula sheet works the same way: group formulas by topic rather than memorizing a flat list.

Praxis Tip: Chunk by meaning when you can: by topic, by function, by shape. Chunking by sound or position is a fallback.

**Mnemonics**

A mnemonic is a memory aid built from something already familiar.

- **Acronym:** a word formed from first letters (ROY G. BIV for the order of colors in a rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet).
- **Acrostic:** a sentence built from first letters ("My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Nachos" for the planets from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune).
- **Rhyme or rhythm:** "i before e, except after c" (with its famous exceptions).
- **Story or image:** link items into a short, vivid, slightly absurd story.

The weirder and more visual, the better. Plain mnemonics are forgotten because they are plain.

Praxis Tip: Make your own. A mnemonic you build takes work, and that work is part of why it sticks. Ready-made ones are fine when they exist, but make a card for them too.

**The method of loci (memory palace)**

1. Pick a familiar place and a fixed route through it (your home, from the front door to the back garden).
2. Choose 5 to 10 distinct stops along the route (the door mat, the coat rack, the kitchen table, and so on).
3. For each item to remember, create a vivid image and "place" it at a stop.
4. To recall, walk the route mentally and look at what is at each stop.

The method works because it uses spatial memory, which people tend to be good at, and gives items an order.

**Worked Example: Memorizing a list of seven**

*Task.* Remember, in order: lamp, river, bread, anchor, violin, ladder, key.

Route: front door, hallway, stairs, bedroom, bathroom, kitchen, back door.

- Front door: a giant lamp wearing a welcome mat.
- Hallway: a river flowing along the carpet.
- Stairs: bread loaves stacked as steps.
- Bedroom: an anchor dragging the bed across the floor.
- Bathroom: a violin playing itself in the bath.
- Kitchen: a ladder leaning from the ceiling into the sink.
- Back door: an enormous key in the lock, turning slowly.

Walk the route in your head and read off the items. Test yourself the next day and again after a week. If it fades, add retrieval on the ladder from Chapter 6.

*Skill used:* converting abstract items into vivid images and attaching them to a fixed structure.

Praxis Tip: Use the same palace for several lists by changing the images, and wait a day before reusing a stop so old images do not blur into new ones.

**When a trick hides a gap**

Mnemonics store a *pointer*, not an *understanding*. Suppose you remember the planets' order with the nacho sentence. You can say the order, but you cannot say why the inner planets are rocky and the outer ones gaseous. If the exam asks the second thing, the trick is no help.

A good test: after using a mnemonic, ask yourself a "why" or "what if" question about the same material (Chapter 7). If you stall, you have stored the label without the idea.

Praxis Tip: Pair every mnemonic with a one-sentence explanation of why it matters. "Order of planets" is a fact; "why the order matters for temperature" is understanding.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Memorize formulas by *using* them in worked problems, not by staring at them. A formula you have used ten times needs no mnemonic.

Praxis Tip: Say it, draw it, write it. Using more than one channel adds hooks.

Praxis Tip: Study a list in different orders. Otherwise you may only know item four because it follows item three.

Praxis Tip: Test yourself after sleep. Sleeping on it gives the memory a chance to settle, and what you can recall after a night is a more honest measure than what you recall after five minutes.

**Common mistakes**

- Using a mnemonic for something you should understand.
- Making mnemonics so tidy that they are forgettable.
- Never revisiting them. A trick without review fades.
- Overloading one palace with too many items.
- Assuming that remembering the label means knowing the concept.

**Do this today**

1. Pick a list you must memorize (7 to 10 items).
2. Build a loci route and attach one image per item.
3. Recall it tomorrow, then in three days, then in a week.
4. For each item, add one "why" question and answer it.

**Recap**

Use chunking and mnemonics for arbitrary lists, and understanding for everything else. Make the tricks vivid, review them on a ladder, and always check that you know the idea behind the label.
