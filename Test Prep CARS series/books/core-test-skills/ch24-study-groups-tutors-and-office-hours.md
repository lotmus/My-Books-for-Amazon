# Strategy: Study Groups, Tutors and Office Hours

**What you will be able to do**

- Decide when to study alone and when to study with others.
- Run a productive group session in about an hour.
- Ask questions that get useful answers from tutors and instructors.

**Why it matters**

Other people can be the best or worst thing about studying. A good partner finds your blind spots and makes you explain things clearly. A bad one turns the session into a pleasant conversation about everything except the syllabus. Tutors and instructors can save weeks, if you come prepared. If you do not, they are an expensive way to feel supervised.

Praxis Tip: Study alone to learn and test yourself; study with others to explain, compare and fill gaps. The first builds your memory. The second reveals what the first missed.

**When to go alone and when not to**

- **Alone:** first exposure to new material, flashcards, timed practice, error-log work.
- **With others:** explaining a concept aloud, comparing methods for a problem, quizzing each other, discussing a puzzling mistake.

**Choosing partners**

Look for people who are reliable, roughly at your level or a bit ahead, and serious about the same exam. A group of three to four is usually the sweet spot: enough variety of views, not enough room to hide.

Praxis Tip: Test a new partnership with one session. If it turns into a chat, say kindly that you want to be more structured, or move on.

**A one-hour group session**

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-5 | Agenda: each person names one topic or question they want help with |
| 5-15 | Quick-fire recall quiz on last week's material, no notes |
| 15-40 | Work two or three practice problems, with each person explaining their reasoning, not just the answer |
| 40-55 | Teach-back: each person explains one concept to the others in two or three minutes |
| 55-60 | Assign next session's topics and homework |

Check: 5 + 10 + 25 + 15 + 5 = 60 minutes.

Praxis Tip: Rotate who leads. The leader prepares the quiz and the problems; the preparation is itself good study.

**Rules that keep it useful**

1. Start and end on time.
2. Attempt problems individually before comparing.
3. Explain the *why*, not only the answer.
4. When someone is wrong, ask a question rather than announce the correct answer. ("What made you choose B?")
5. Keep phones away.
6. Do not share or circulate actual exam questions or leaked material. It is unfair, often against the exam owner's rules, and can put your score at risk.

**Tutors and office hours**

Tutors and instructors are best used for specific problems, not general rescue. Prepare so the time goes to what only a person can do: diagnose your thinking.

*Before the meeting:*
- Bring your error log and two or three specific questions.
- Attempt the questions first and record where you got stuck.
- Know what you want to leave with: a method, a corrected misconception, or a plan.

*During:*
- Show your own work and explain your reasoning aloud. The expert can then see where it went wrong.
- Ask "What is the general principle here?" so the answer applies beyond one question.
- Take notes, then restate the answer in your own words.

*After:*
- Redo the problem without notes within a day and add the lesson to your log.

**Worked Example: Turning a vague question into a good one**

*Vague:* "I don't understand percent change questions."

*Better:* "On question 7, the price rose from 40 to 50 and then fell back to 40. I computed both changes as 25 percent. The key says the fall is 20 percent. I used the new value as the base for the first step and the old value for the second. When do I pick which?"

The second version shows the expert exactly where the error lies, and the answer ("the base is always the starting value of that step") is fast and permanent.

*Skill used:* describing your own thinking so that someone can fix it.

Praxis Tip: A question that includes "I chose ___ because ___" is nearly always answerable in a minute. A question that says "I don't get it" takes ten.

**Using paid help wisely**

Tutoring and courses vary in quality and cost. Before paying, ask for a sample session or a trial, and check whether the tutor has experience with {EXAM}. Remember that material you buy is not a substitute for the practice you do. Be wary of promises of guaranteed score gains. Check credentials and reviews from independent sources.

Praxis Tip: Decide in advance what you want from a tutor (accountability, content help, strategy). Evaluate after a few sessions against that goal.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Record your group sessions' key insights in a shared document. The notes become a free revision resource.

Praxis Tip: If you are always the explainer in a group, you are learning but you may not be getting what you need. Ask to swap roles.

Praxis Tip: Thank your helpers specifically ("your explanation of the base finally made it click"). It encourages them to help again and keeps the relationship good.

Praxis Tip: Arrive with a problem you got wrong, not a problem you got right. People enjoy explaining success, but it fixes nothing.

**Common mistakes**

- Turning the group into a social event.
- Letting one person dominate.
- Bringing no questions to a tutor.
- Passive attendance: listening instead of attempting.
- Sharing or seeking actual exam questions.
- Relying on a tutor to replace your own practice.

**Do this today**

1. Pick two possible study partners and propose a one-hour trial session.
2. Draft an agenda using the table above.
3. Prepare one error from your log in the "I chose ___ because ___" format.

**Recap**

Study alone to learn, with others to explain and compare. Keep sessions structured, ask specific questions, and never use or share actual exam content.
