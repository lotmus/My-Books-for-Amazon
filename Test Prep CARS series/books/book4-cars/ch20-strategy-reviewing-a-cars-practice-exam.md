# Strategy: Reviewing a CARS Practice Exam

**What you will be able to do**

- Turn a CARS practice score into a diagnosis, not just a number.
- Tag every miss with a cause specific to reading: misread scope, extreme-word trap, outside-knowledge intrusion, author versus reported view, ran out of time, or chose true-but-irrelevant.
- Re-read the passages without the answers in view, and compare your map with a fresh one.
- Use confidence ratings to find lucky guesses and dangerous certainties.
- Build a one-week repair plan, and decide when to take the next exam.

**Why it matters**

A practice exam is worth about as much as the review you do afterward. Two readers can sit the same exam and earn the same score; one learns three things from it and the other learns nothing. In a reading section the difference is even larger than in a content section, because a missed question rarely means "I did not know a fact." It means "I read something in a particular wrong way," and that way of reading will come with you to the real exam unless you name it.

This chapter is the review routine, tuned to the CARS section. It builds on Core Test Skills, "Review a Practice Test", and it assumes you have been keeping an error log (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9) and have met the trap catalog in [Strategy: When You Are Stuck: Elimination and Trap Answers](chapter:17). Use it after **Full-Length CARS Exam 1** ([Part A](chapter:18) and [Part B](chapter:19)) and again after Exam 2 ([Part A](chapter:21) and [Part B](chapter:22)). The timing figures it refers to come from [Strategy: Pacing, Triage and Stamina for CARS](chapter:16).

The exams in this book are original and are not calibrated to any official scale. Do not convert your number into a predicted real score. The number measures your progress against yourself, and that is plenty.

Praxis Tip: Review within 24 hours of the exam, while you can still remember why you chose what you chose. After a week you will be reviewing the answer key, not your own thinking, and the key is a poor witness for the defense.

**The review in five passes**

**Pass 1: Score and feelings (10 minutes).** Count correct answers for each part and for the whole exam. Then write down, in a notebook, how you felt during each passage: steady, rushed, bored, lost, pleased with yourself. A passage that felt lost and went well is a surprise worth understanding; a passage that felt easy and went badly is a warning. Also write your checkpoint times against the plan in [Strategy: Pacing, Triage and Stamina for CARS](chapter:16).

**Pass 2: Tag every miss and every guess.** For each question you missed, or guessed and got right, write in your notebook:

- the passage and question number, and its skill family (foundations, reasoning within the text, reasoning beyond the text; see [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3));
- the question type (main idea, detail, function, inference, application, strengthen or weaken, and so on);
- the cause, from the list below;
- the trap name, using the thirteen names in [Chapter 17](chapter:17), for the choice you picked;
- the fix, in one sentence, and the date you will re-attempt it.

The causes:

| Cause | Meaning | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Misread scope | Chose an answer too narrow or too broad for the question | Say the main idea or the scope aloud first |
| Extreme-word trap | Chose a choice with always, never, only or all | Find the author's hedge before you read choices |
| Outside knowledge | Answered from what you know, not from the page | Point at a line, or reject the choice |
| Author vs reported view | Gave the author a view the passage only reports | Mark who speaks in every paragraph |
| Ran out of time | Rushed or guessed because the clock ran out | Apply the ceiling and triage rules |
| True but irrelevant | Chose a correct statement that did not answer the question | Restate the question in five words |

(Your device shows the table as an image, so you cannot change it. Copy the six causes into your notebook as headings, and add a seventh, "other," for the occasional miss that fits none.)

Two notes on tagging. First, a miss has one *primary* cause, even if two were present. Choose the one that, if fixed, would have changed your answer. Second, be strict about "outside knowledge." If you can honestly say "I picked it because I know it is true from somewhere else," tag it; if you picked it because it sounded plausible, that is probably unsupported inference or true-but-irrelevant, which is a different habit with a different cure.

Praxis Tip: Tag the cause *before* you read the explanation. A tag chosen after you have been told the answer is not diagnosis; it is hindsight with a pen.

**Pass 3: Re-read the passages without the answers.** This is the pass most readers skip, and it is the one that repairs reading. For each passage with two or more misses:

1. Cover the questions and the key. Read the passage again, once, as if for the first time.
2. Without looking at your earlier notes, write a new map: topic, point, tone, and one job per paragraph ([Strategy: The Active Reading Method](chapter:2)).
3. Now compare it with the map you made during the exam. Where do they differ? A difference is a finding. Perhaps you took a reported view for the author's. Perhaps you missed a concession word and read a qualified claim as a flat one. Perhaps your first map was fine and the error came later, at the choices.
4. Only then reopen the questions you missed and retry them from the passage, not from memory of the choices. Write which line supports the key.

Praxis Tip: If your second map is clearly better than the first, the trouble was in the reading, and drilling traps will not help much. If the two maps agree, the trouble was in the questions, and mapping more carefully will not help much either. One re-read tells you which half to repair.

**Pass 4: Confidence and calibration.** During the exam you were asked to jot a small mark beside any question you were unsure of (a question mark is enough; see Core Test Skills, Chapter 9). Now place every question in one of four groups:

| Group | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Sure, correct | Working as designed | Nothing |
| Unsure, correct | Lucky or fragile | Review as if wrong |
| Unsure, wrong | You knew you were shaky | Find the cause; ordinary repair |
| Sure, wrong | You believed something false | Study first; rewrite the idea |

Calibration is the match between how sure you felt and how often you were right. Count the questions in which you felt sure and see what fraction were correct. If that fraction is lower than about nine in ten, your certainty is outrunning your evidence, and the fix is to require a line number for every "sure" answer. If your unsure answers are mostly correct, you are too modest; trust the text-anchored choice sooner and save the time.

The "sure, wrong" group is where the traps live. A confident wrong answer in CARS usually means a trap was written to match a habit of yours: the extreme choice that "sounded like the author," the reported view you took for the author's, or the true statement that was not asked.

Praxis Tip: Do not trust a feeling of certainty that you cannot defend with a line from the page. Certainty is a mood; a line number is evidence.

**Pass 5: Patterns, timing and a plan.** Count your misses by cause, by question family and by passage type (humanities or social science). Then look at timing: which passage cost the most time, and did it also cost the most points? Pick the top two issues, and for each write one concrete action: a lesson to re-read, a set of questions to redo, or a habit to practice. Two issues, not eight.

A compact way to see the pattern is a tally by cause in your notebook, followed by a tally by position. If most of your "ran out of time" misses fall in the last two passages, you have a pacing problem or a stamina problem; if they are spread across the section, one slow habit is eating minutes everywhere. If your misses cluster in one passage type, read the matching lesson again ([Lesson 7](chapter:10) for humanities, [Lesson 8](chapter:11) for social science).

Praxis Tip: Rates beat counts. Six misses out of seven reasoning-beyond-the-text questions is a bigger signal than six misses out of twenty foundations questions, even though the count is the same.

**Worked Example: Reading an error summary**

All numbers here are invented for illustration. Suppose after Exam 1 you find that an exam of 53 questions [VERIFY] produced 15 misses or lucky guesses, tagged as follows:

| Cause | Count | Share of 15 |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme-word trap | 4 | 27% |
| Author vs reported view | 4 | 27% |
| True but irrelevant | 3 | 20% |
| Ran out of time | 3 | 20% |
| Misread scope | 1 | 7% |

The shares are the count divided by 15: 4/15 is about 27%, 3/15 is 20%, 1/15 is about 7%. Two causes tie for first, and together they are 8 of 15, or 53%, of the problem. Notice what you do *not* conclude. You do not conclude "I am bad at CARS." You conclude "two habits," and each has a cure.

Now suppose Pass 3 showed that on the two passages where you missed "author versus reported view," your second map marked the sympathetic voice in paragraph 2 as a critic's, while your first map treated it as the author's. That is a reading error, so the fix is in the map: for every paragraph, write a two-letter voice mark (A for author, R for reported) before the first question. For the extreme-word trap, the fix is in the questions: before reading the choices of any inference or main-idea question, underline the author's hedge (a "may," a "tends to," a "some") on your scratch paper.

Then Pass 4 shows that 3 of the 4 extreme-word misses were "sure, wrong." That is a calibration problem as well, so you add a rule: any choice containing an absolute word gets a one-line justification from the text, or it goes.

*Skill used:* separating count, cause and fix, and using the second map and the confidence group to choose the repair.

Praxis Tip: When two causes tie, repair the one with a smaller fix first. A habit you can change in an afternoon is a quicker win than a skill that takes a month.

**Re-attempt rules**

1. Re-attempt each missed question after three days, from the passage, without looking at the explanation.
2. If you get it right, try once more a week later.
3. If you miss it again, it is no longer a slip; it is a skill. Re-read the matching lesson section, and write two similar questions of your own on a passage from the book, with a wrong choice for each named trap.
4. After two successful re-attempts, retire the question.

**A one-week repair plan**

- **Day 1:** Passes 1 and 2 (score, feelings, tag every miss).
- **Day 2:** Passes 3 to 5. Re-read the passages without the answers. Write the two priorities and the plan.
- **Day 3:** Re-read the lesson sections for your two priorities. Do one short practice passage from an earlier set ([Chapter 12](chapter:12), [Chapter 13](chapter:13) or [Chapter 14](chapter:14)), untimed, mapping with care.
- **Day 4:** Do a second short passage under time. Use your new habit on every question and note whether it slows you.
- **Day 5:** Re-attempt Day-1 misses from the passages. Update the error log.
- **Day 6:** Light review. Reread your trap shorthand and the wrong-answer wall (see the Praxis Tips). Rest.
- **Day 7:** Take the next full-length exam, with the clock.

If you are short on time, compress the plan, but keep the order: diagnose, repair, retest. One thing you should not compress is the rest day. Reading ability is the first thing to go when you are tired.

Praxis Tip: Do not start the second exam the moment you finish this chapter. Two exams are a before-and-after. Take the second after the repairs, or you will have measured nothing but your stamina.

**How to read your results after Exam 2**

Compare the two exams on four numbers: total correct, the number of "ran out of time" misses, the number of "sure, wrong" misses, and the minutes you finished early or late. A score that rises while "sure, wrong" falls is real progress. A score that rises only because you were luckier on topic is not, and you will find that out when you read the passages again. Whatever the outcome, repeat the five passes, because the point of the routine is to make the next review shorter, not to produce a verdict.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Keep a "wrong-answer wall" in your notebook: one line for each false habit you caught ("I took the critic's view as the author's"), corrected in one sentence. Reread it the night before every practice exam.

Praxis Tip: Review the lucky guesses with the same seriousness as the misses. A right answer you cannot defend is a loan from the exam, and the exam charges interest.

Praxis Tip: For every "outside knowledge" miss, write down the exact fact you used and the sentence in the passage that never mentions it. The gap between the two is the lesson.

Praxis Tip: If you got a question wrong and the key's reasoning feels annoying rather than clear, you probably misread the stem. Reread the stem word by word: *except*, *least*, *according to*, *most likely to agree*.

Praxis Tip: Compare the first and second halves of each part. If the second half is much worse, you have a stamina or pacing issue and not a reading issue; rehearse longer sittings ([Strategy: Pacing, Triage and Stamina for CARS](chapter:16)).

Praxis Tip: Do not chase a score from one exam. One exam is a sample of nine passages, and the sample has moods.

**Common Mistakes**

- Reading the explanation before retrying the question.
- Reviewing only the misses, not the lucky guesses or the "sure, wrong" answers.
- Tagging every miss as "careless," which tells you nothing about what to do next.
- Skipping the re-read of the passage, and going straight to the choices.
- Treating the practice score as a prediction of the real score.
- Trying to repair every cause at once instead of the top two.
- Taking the next exam before the repair week is done.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. A reader reviewing a missed question finds that she chose a statement that was true according to the passage but did not answer the question asked. Which cause tag fits best?
   A. Outside knowledge
   B. True but irrelevant
   C. Misread scope
   D. Ran out of time

2. During Pass 3 you write a new map of a passage and find that you now mark paragraph 2 as a critic's view, while on the exam you took it as the author's. Which conclusion is best supported?
   A. The questions on this passage were poorly written
   B. You need to memorize more vocabulary
   C. The passage is too hard for you at present
   D. The error was probably in how you read the passage, not in how you read the choices

3. A reader notes that 4 of her 5 "sure" answers on one exam were wrong. What does this most clearly suggest?
   A. Her confidence is well calibrated
   B. She should stop using confidence ratings altogether
   C. Her certainty is outrunning her evidence, so she should require a supporting line for every "sure" answer
   D. She is too cautious and should trust her first impression more

4. Which of the following should be reviewed "as if wrong"?
   A. A correct answer rated as unsure
   B. A correct answer rated as sure
   C. A wrong answer rated as sure
   D. A question left unanswered at the end of time

5. A reader chose "Critics are mistaken in every case" for a main-idea question; the author wrote that critics "may be partly right but miss the larger point." Which cause tag and trap name fit best?
   A. Extreme-word trap; too extreme
   B. Outside knowledge; outside-knowledge lure
   C. Ran out of time; none
   D. Author versus reported view; misattributed view

6. Why does this chapter advise repairing the top two issues, not all of them?
   A. Most readers have only two issues
   B. The other issues are caused by chance and cannot be repaired
   C. The exam only tests two skills
   D. Fixing a few habits well changes more answers than patching many badly, and you can only practice a couple of new habits at once

7. When is the best time to take the next full-length exam?
   A. Immediately, so that the memory of the first one is fresh
   B. Only after you have read every lesson again
   C. After the repair week, so the second exam measures the effect of the repairs
   D. Never, because practice scores are unreliable

**Stop here.** Answer each question in your notebook before you read the answers.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** A statement that the passage supports but that is not what the question asks is the true-but-irrelevant trap. (A would apply only if the reader used a fact from outside the passage; C would apply if the answer were too narrow or too broad for the question; D would apply if the reader had guessed because the clock ran out.)
2. **D.** The new map differs from the exam map in a way that points to the reading, so the voice error probably came before the questions. (A is not supported, since nothing suggests the questions were faulty; B is unrelated to the finding; C is a discouraging conclusion not supported by a single difference between two maps.)
3. **C.** Four wrong out of five "sure" answers means confidence is higher than accuracy; the cure is to demand a line of text. (A reverses the finding; B throws away useful data; D has it backward, because she is already too confident.)
4. **A.** An unsure correct answer is likely to have been lucky or fragile and may not repeat. (B is working as designed; C is a miss and is reviewed anyway as the most valuable group; D is a miss from timing, which is handled by pacing, not by treating it as a lucky guess.)
5. **A.** "In every case" is an absolute where the author hedged with "may be partly right," which is the extreme-word cause and the too-extreme trap. (B is wrong because the choice is about something stated in the passage; C is wrong because nothing suggests time was the issue; D is wrong because the choice does not give the author a view the passage only reports, but exaggerates the author's own view.)
6. **D.** A few habits practiced deliberately change more answers than many practiced weakly. (A is not true in general; B is false because habits can be repaired; C is false because the section tests three skill families, and the claim has nothing to do with the number of repairs.)
7. **C.** Two exams are a before-and-after, and the second means little unless the repairs are made first. (A would measure only memory and stamina; B is excessive; D mistakes the purpose of the scores, which is to compare against yourself.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, count how many you got right without looking at the chapter, and tag any miss with the cause list above. Review of the review is allowed.
