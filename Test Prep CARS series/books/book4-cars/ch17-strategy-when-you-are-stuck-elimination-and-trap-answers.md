# Strategy: When You Are Stuck: Elimination and Trap Answers

**What you will be able to do**

- Follow a short routine for a question that has you stuck, without panic and without losing the clock.
- Name the thirteen most common trap answers in passage-based questions, and recognize each from its tell.
- Reduce four choices to two by eliminating on named reasons rather than on feelings.
- Break a two-way tie by finding the single point on which the remaining choices differ.
- Turn every miss into a named trap in your error log.

**Why it matters**

In a passage-based section, the right answer is not usually mysterious. What makes questions hard is that the wrong answers are written by people who know exactly how readers go wrong. They are plausible on purpose. A reader without a vocabulary for this feels only that "B sounds right," and that feeling is the one the question writer is counting on. A reader with a vocabulary can say "B is a half-right: its second clause goes beyond the passage," and move on.

This chapter extends the short trap table in Core Test Skills, Chapter 10 (half-right, reversal, too extreme, true but irrelevant, familiar phrase) with the traps particular to reading passages. It pairs with [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3), [Lesson 6](chapter:9) on strengthen and weaken questions, and the timing rules in [Strategy: Pacing, Triage and Stamina for CARS](chapter:16). A longer reference version of the catalog appears in [Appendix: Passage-Mapping Templates and Trap Catalog](chapter:24).

Praxis Tip: A trap is only useful as a name. Say it in your head, not aloud; the examiner may, quite reasonably, hear you muttering "reversal" and take it personally.

**The routine when you are stuck**

1. **Restate the question in five words** on your scratch paper ("author's attitude to critics"). Many stuck questions are stuck because you are solving a nearby question.
2. **Return to the map.** Which paragraph does this question live in? Reread the key line, not the passage.
3. **Predict** a short answer in your own words. If you cannot, predict the *type* (a reason, a contrast, a qualification).
4. **Eliminate by name.** Cross out any choice for which you can name the trap.
5. **Compare the survivors.** If two remain, find the one word or claim on which they differ and test only that against the passage.
6. **Decide and release.** If the 2-minute ceiling arrives, choose the more modest and more text-anchored of the two, flag it, and move on ([Strategy: Pacing, Triage and Stamina for CARS](chapter:16)).

**The catalog, in brief**

| Trap | The tell | The cure |
|---|---|---|
| Too extreme | always, never, only, all | Find a hedge in the text |
| Reversal | Right topic, wrong direction | Check the sign |
| Half-right | Good start, extra clause | Test every clause |
| True but not asked | A fact the passage states | Recheck the task |
| Familiar phrase | Passage words, new meaning | Paraphrase it |
| Out of scope | Never mentioned | Find the line |
| Unsupported inference | A leap from the text | Ask what is stated |
| Misattributed view | "Some say" given to author | Find who speaks |
| Wrong scope | Too narrow or too broad | Match the main idea |
| Tone mismatch | Attitude too hot or cold | Match the hedges |
| Role confusion | What a line says, not does | Name the job |
| Wrong direction | Strengthens when asked to weaken | Reread the stem |
| Outside-knowledge lure | True in life, silent in text | Use only the text |

Each trap is described below with an original example. All examples use the same short passage, which you should read once and then keep in view.

*The garden passage.* "City planners have long treated community gardens as a pleasant extra, to be tolerated on empty lots until a better use arrives. A closer look at one district's gardens suggests this view is too modest. Over a decade, blocks with gardens showed more neighbors who could name one another, and more residents who attended local meetings. Some observers conclude that gardens create community. That may be too simple: gardens tend to be started by people who already know each other. Still, the gardens plainly sustain the ties they begin with, because the work of watering and weeding gives neighbors a reason to meet each week. If so, the gardens are less an ornament of community than a piece of its machinery, and they should be judged by that standard, not by the price of the land."

**1. Too extreme.** The choice uses an absolute where the author hedges. *Example:* "Community gardens always create strong neighborhoods." The passage says gardens "tend to" be started by people who already know each other and calls the creation claim "too simple." The cure is to find the author's hedge ("tend to," "may," "plainly sustain") and measure the choice against it.

**2. Reversal.** The topic is right but the direction is wrong. *Example:* "Gardens are usually started by people who do not yet know one another." The passage says the opposite. Reversals are common in detail questions and survive mostly because they sound like the passage.

**3. Half-right.** The first half is faithful and the second half adds a claim. *Example:* "Gardens help sustain neighborly ties, and for that reason no empty lot should ever be built on." The first clause is correct; the second goes beyond a passage that says gardens should be judged by a different standard, not that land must never be used otherwise. Read every clause.

**4. True but not asked.** The choice is stated in the passage, but it is not what the question asks. *Example:* a question asks what the author concludes about gardens; the choice reads "Blocks with gardens showed more neighbors who could name one another." That is evidence, not the conclusion. Always recheck the task.

**5. Familiar phrase.** The choice borrows memorable words from the passage and recombines them into a claim the passage does not make. *Example:* "Gardens are a piece of the machinery that should be priced by the cost of the land." The words "machinery" and "land" appear; the passage says gardens should *not* be judged by the price of the land. Paraphrase the choice in your own words before you accept it.

**6. Out of scope.** The choice raises a point the passage never discusses. *Example:* "Gardens reduce crime on the blocks where they appear." Crime is never mentioned. The cure is simple but strict: you must be able to put your finger on a line.

**7. Unsupported inference.** The choice draws a conclusion that sounds like a natural next step but is not licensed by the text. *Example:* "Gardens caused the rise in meeting attendance." The passage reports the pattern and cautions against reading it as simple creation. Ask: what does the text actually state, and what am I adding?

**8. Misattributed view.** The choice states a view the passage reports but does not endorse. *Example:* "Gardens create community." The author says "some observers conclude" this and then calls it "too simple." Check who is speaking before you accept a statement as the author's.

**9. Wrong scope.** The choice is too narrow or too broad for the question, most often a main-idea question. *Too narrow:* "Watering and weeding give neighbors a weekly reason to meet." That is a supporting point. *Too broad:* "All public land should be judged by its social usefulness." The passage is about gardens, not all land. The right main idea covers the whole passage and no more.

**10. Tone mismatch.** The choice describes an attitude hotter or colder than the author's. *Example:* "The author is outraged at planners' neglect." The author calls the planners' view "too modest," which is mild. Match the hedges: "too modest" and "may be too simple" signal a measured tone.

**11. Role confusion.** On a function question ("in order to"), the choice restates what the lines say instead of what they do. *Example:* "The author mentions watering and weeding in order to describe how gardens are maintained." The lines are there to give a reason neighbors keep meeting, which supports the claim that gardens sustain ties. Name the job in two words (supports, concedes, rebuts, defines) before reading the choices.

**12. Wrong direction.** On strengthen or weaken questions, the choice does the opposite of what is asked. *Example:* asked for a finding that would *weaken* the claim that gardens sustain ties, a choice says "Neighbors report that they mostly meet at the garden each week." That strengthens it. Reread the stem's verb before you read the choices.

**13. Outside-knowledge lure.** The choice may be true in the real world but the passage never says so. *Example:* "Gardens improve residents' diets." It may sound plausible, but nothing in the passage supports it. The exam asks you to read, not to recall.

Praxis Tip: Keep a one-line shorthand for each trap on your scratch paper during practice (EXT, REV, HALF, TRUE, FAM, OUT, INF, MIS, SCOPE, TONE, ROLE, DIR, LURE). By the day of the exam you will no longer need to write them.

**Worked Example 1: A main-idea question with every choice labeled**

*Question.* Which statement best expresses the main point of the garden passage?
A. Watering and weeding give neighbors a reason to meet each week.
B. Community gardens create community.
C. Community gardens are better understood as part of how neighborhoods function than as an extra, because they sustain ties.
D. Community gardens will always improve any neighborhood in which they appear.

*Reasoning.* A is a supporting detail (wrong scope, too narrow). B is the view of "some observers" that the author calls too simple (misattributed view). D uses "always" and "any" (too extreme). **C** covers the argument: the planners' view is too modest, gardens sustain ties, and they should be judged accordingly.

*Skill used:* eliminating three choices by name before affirming the fourth.

**Worked Example 2: A weaken question**

*Question.* Which finding would most weaken the claim that gardens sustain existing ties?
A. Residents who stopped gardening for a season went on meeting weekly in other places
B. Many neighbors say they joined a garden in order to meet people
C. Planners rarely consult residents before closing a garden
D. Gardens are more common in districts with more public meetings

*Reasoning.* The claim is that the work of gardening gives neighbors a reason to meet. A shows that the ties continued without that reason, which weakens the link (**A**). B is wrong direction (it strengthens). C is true but not asked, because consultation is not about ties. D is an unsupported correlation that does not bear on whether gardening *sustains* ties.

*Skill used:* saying the claim as "X sustains Y," then looking for the finding that cuts the link.

**Worked Example 3: Two choices remain**

*Question.* The passage most strongly suggests that gardens
A. cannot create ties among neighbors who do not already know one another
B. may sustain ties more reliably than they create them
C. should replace all other uses of empty land
D. are valued by planners more than any other amenity

*Reasoning.* Eliminate C (not stated; too extreme) and D (reverses the planners' stance). A and B remain. The difference is one word: A says *cannot*, B says *may*. The passage calls the creation claim "too simple," which is a hedge, not a denial. A is too extreme; **B** keeps the hedge. This is the typical two-way tie: the modest, text-anchored choice wins.

*Skill used:* testing the single point of difference against the author's hedge.

**Worked Example 4: Function and role confusion**

*Question.* The author mentions that gardens "tend to be started by people who already know each other" in order to
A. argue that gardens are unimportant to community life
B. show that the claim that gardens create community may be too simple
C. report how many neighbors join new gardens
D. suggest that planners should help neighbors meet

*Reasoning.* The job of the sentence is to qualify the creation claim. **B.** A is a reversal; the author goes on to say gardens are "machinery" of community. C is role confusion and unsupported (no numbers). D is out of scope.

*Skill used:* naming the job (qualify) before reading the choices.

**Worked Example 5: Converting a miss into a log entry**

*Situation.* You answered "Gardens cause more meeting attendance" and the key says the answer was "Gardens may help sustain ties."

*Log entry.* Question type: inference. My answer's trap: unsupported inference (causal overreach). What the text said: "more residents attended meetings" (a pattern) and the author warned the creation claim was "too simple." What I should have noticed: the author's hedge. Fix: before choosing an inference, underline the author's qualifier. Re-test in three days.

*Skill used:* logging the miss by trap name, so that the next occurrence is recognized instead of repeated (see [Strategy: Reviewing a CARS Practice Exam](chapter:20)).

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: The most common wrong answer is not absurd but a little too confident. Reading the stem, ask yourself how confident the author is, then reject choices that exceed it.

Praxis Tip: When two answers differ by a single word, that word is the question. Everything else is a decoy.

Praxis Tip: Do not eliminate an answer because it sounds odd. Eliminate it because you can name its fault. Some correct answers are written in prose of heroic dullness.

Praxis Tip: Beware the answer that is longer, more elegant or cleverly balanced. Item writers do not make the correct choice systematically longest, and neither should you assume it is.

Praxis Tip: When the passage is silent, the answer is not the one that seems most reasonable; it is the one the passage supports. If nothing supports any choice, you have misread the question, not found a gap.

Praxis Tip: After a miss, name the trap before you read the explanation. If you cannot, read the explanation twice.

Praxis Tip: Do not hunt for a trap in the correct answer. Once three choices have been eliminated by name, take the one that remains and verify it against the text.

**Common Mistakes**

- Choosing an answer because it repeats words from the passage.
- Treating "true" as meaning "answers the question."
- Ignoring the verb in the stem (weaken versus strengthen; except versus is).
- Attributing "some argue" to the author.
- Eliminating the correct answer because it is blunt, short or unexciting.
- Changing a correct answer because of a vague unease, with no named reason.
- Spending five minutes to break a tie that a one-word difference would have settled in thirty seconds.

Questions 1 to 4 refer to the following passage. Questions 5 to 7 ask about the catalog.

*Passage.* "Lullabies are usually dismissed as simple tunes for the sleepy, but their simplicity is a feat of design. A lullaby must work on a listener who cannot follow words, cannot be argued with and will punish a mistake with wakefulness. Its melody therefore moves in small steps, its rhythm rocks at about the pace of a walking adult, and its lyrics, when there are any, say nothing urgent. Composers of ambitious music sometimes borrow these features to produce an effect of calm, and they are often praised for their daring. The lullaby's anonymous makers, who solved the same problem under harder conditions, are rarely mentioned. This is not to say a lullaby is great art. It is to say that it meets a standard of fitness to purpose that great art often meets only by accident."

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. Which statement best expresses the main point of the lullaby passage?
   A. Lullabies are well fitted to their purpose, a skill that is rarely noticed
   B. Lullabies are great art and deserve to be ranked beside the most ambitious music
   C. Composers of ambitious music are praised more than they deserve
   D. Lullabies are simple tunes and rightly dismissed

2. According to the passage, which feature helps a lullaby work on its listener?
   A. Lyrics that carry an urgent message for the listener
   B. A melody that moves in wide leaps across a large range
   C. A rhythm that rocks at about a walking adult's pace
   D. Words that the listener can follow closely and carefully

3. The author mentions composers of ambitious music in order to
   A. accuse those composers of stealing the melodies of the lullaby's anonymous makers
   B. claim that ambitious music is calmer and more effective than any lullaby could be
   C. show that lullabies qualify as great art in the same way as ambitious music
   D. illustrate borrowing from lullabies while the original makers go unmentioned

4. Which statement would the author most likely accept?
   A. A lullaby is great art only if it succeeds in soothing its hearer
   B. A lullaby that fails to soothe has failed by its own standard
   C. Only anonymous makers are able to compose a really good lullaby
   D. Ambitious music is less skillful in design than a lullaby is

5. A choice for a main-idea question states a view that the passage attributes to "some observers" and the author calls too simple. This trap is best named
   A. outside-knowledge lure
   B. tone mismatch
   C. misattributed view
   D. role confusion

6. A choice repeats two vivid phrases from the passage but joins them in a claim the passage never makes. This trap is best named
   A. reversal
   B. familiar phrase
   C. wrong direction
   D. true but not asked

7. Two choices remain after eliminating the others. What is the best next move?
   A. Find the one point where they differ and test it in the text
   B. Choose the longer and more carefully balanced of the two choices
   C. Choose the choice that sounds more polished and sophisticated
   D. Choose the one that borrows more of the passage's own wording

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** The passage argues that the simplicity of a lullaby is the result of skilled design that meets a standard of fitness to purpose. (B goes beyond the passage, which says "This is not to say a lullaby is great art"; C is unsupported, because the author notes the praise but does not say it is undeserved; D reverses the author, who calls the simplicity "a feat of design".)
2. **C.** The rhythm is described directly as rocking at about a walking adult's pace. (A is a reversal, since the passage says the lyrics "say nothing urgent"; B is a reversal, since the passage says the melody moves in small steps; D is a reversal, since the listener "cannot follow words".)
3. **D.** The composers are praised for borrowing, while the anonymous makers who solved the problem are rarely mentioned. (A is too extreme and unsupported, as no accusation of copying is made; B changes the meaning, since the composers borrow features to produce calm and no comparison of calm is made; C contradicts the final sentences, which decline to claim that.)
4. **B.** The passage says a lullaby meets a standard of fitness to purpose and punishes a mistake with wakefulness, so failing to soothe is failing its standard. (A goes beyond the passage, which gives no condition for great art; C is too extreme, since the anonymity of the makers is a fact about history, not a requirement; D is unsupported, because the passage compares fitness to purpose and not skill overall.)
5. **C.** The choice gives the author a view that the author only reports and rejects. (A applies to choices that may be true in life but are not in the text, while this view appears in the text; B applies to attitudes that are too hot or cold, while this choice is a claim; D applies to function questions, while this is a main-idea question.)
6. **B.** Familiar phrases borrow the passage's words but change the meaning. (A applies when the direction of a claim is flipped, and not when words are recombined; C applies to strengthen and weaken questions, and this is not one; D applies to a statement that the passage does make, which this choice does not.)
7. **A.** The two survivors usually differ on one point, such as an absolute versus a hedge, and that point decides the question. (B relies on length, which item writers do not make systematic; C relies on style, and trap answers are often the most polished; D falls for the familiar-phrase trap.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, go back to the thirteen-item catalog and write, for each wrong choice you considered, the abbreviation of its trap.

1. Copy the catalog table onto an index card with your own abbreviations.

**Recap**

When you are stuck: restate, return to the map, predict, eliminate by name, compare the survivors on the single point of difference, and release at the ceiling. The thirteen traps are not clever; they are predictable, and that is what makes them beatable.
