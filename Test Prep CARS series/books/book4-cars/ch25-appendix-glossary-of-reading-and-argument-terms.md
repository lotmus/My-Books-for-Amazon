# Appendix: Glossary of Reading and Argument Terms

This glossary collects the terms printed in bold through the lessons and strategy chapters of this book. Each entry gives a short definition and a pointer to the lesson or strategy chapter where the idea is developed. Use it for quick review, and then return to the chapter for the reasoning behind the term. The names of the thirteen trap answers are defined here and catalogued with examples in [Appendix: Passage-Mapping Templates and Trap Catalog](chapter:24).

Praxis Tip: A glossary is for checking, not for first meetings. If a term is new to you, go to its lesson and read it in a passage, where it has a job to do.

**A**

**Abstract vocabulary** — Words naming ideas and qualities rather than things, common in humanities prose; read them by finding the concrete example the author attaches. (Lesson 7)

**Abstraction** — A general idea standing above particular cases; moving up or down the ladder of abstraction is the core of application questions. (Lesson 5)

**Ad hominem** — An argument that attacks the person making a claim instead of the claim itself. (Lesson 3)

**Allusion** — A brief indirect reference to a work, event or idea, which the passage itself must make clear enough to answer questions. (Lesson 7)

**Ambiguity** — The quality of admitting more than one reading; a word or sentence may be ambiguous until context settles it. (Lesson 4)

**Analogy** — A comparison of two things on the strength of shared structure, not shared surface. (Lesson 8)

**Analogy question** — A question asking which new situation has the same structure as one in the passage. (Lesson 5)

**Anecdote** — A short account of a particular event used as an illustration; it supports a claim weakly unless it is typical. (Lesson 8)

**Application question** — A question asking you to apply a principle from the passage to a new case. (Lesson 5)

**Argument** — A set of reasons offered in support of a conclusion. (Lesson 3)

**Assertion** — A claim stated without support at that point in the text. (Lesson 3)

**Assumption** — An unstated claim that the argument needs in order to work. (Lesson 3)

**Attitude** — The author's evaluative stance toward a subject, such as admiring, skeptical or neutral. (Lesson 2)

**Author versus reported view** — The distinction between what the author holds and what the author only describes others as holding. (Lesson 3)

**Author's purpose** — What the author is trying to do with the passage, expressed as a verb such as explain, argue, warn or reconcile. (Lesson 2)

**B**

**Background paragraph** — A paragraph that supplies context before the argument starts, usually carrying no claim of the author's own. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Begging the question** — An argument whose conclusion is already assumed in its premises. (Lesson 3)

**Bias** — A tilt in selection or presentation that favors one view; a source of weakness in evidence. (Lesson 8)

**Burden of proof** — The responsibility of a claimant to give reasons; a passage may place it on one side of a dispute. (Lesson 3)

**C**

**Calibration** — The match between how sure you feel about answers and how often you are right. (Strategy Chapter 20)

**Causal claim** — A claim that one thing produces another, which requires more than a correlation to support. (Lesson 8)

**Ceiling** — The longest time you allow yourself on one question before you choose the best option, flag it and move on. (Strategy Chapter 16)

**Checkpoint (time)** — A planned time by which you should have finished a given number of passages. (Strategy Chapter 16)

**Claim** — A statement that something is true, good or advisable, which may need support. (Lesson 3)

**Comparison structure** — A passage organization that sets two things side by side to show likeness or difference. (Lesson 1)

**Concession** — A point the author grants to the other side, usually before limiting it. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Concession word** — A signal such as admittedly or of course that warns a point is about to be granted and then reduced. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Conclusion** — The claim an argument aims to establish. (Lesson 3)

**Conditional** — An if-then statement; the truth of the first part guarantees the second, but not the reverse. (Lesson 4)

**Confidence rating** — A quick mark of how sure you are of an answer, used in review to find lucky guesses and dangerous certainties. (Strategy Chapter 20)

**Connotation** — The emotional or evaluative color of a word beyond its literal meaning. (Lesson 2)

**Context clue** — A nearby phrase that fixes the meaning of an unfamiliar or shifting word. (Lesson 7)

**Contradiction** — A statement that cannot be true together with another statement. (Lesson 3)

**Contrapositive** — The logically equivalent form of a conditional: not-B implies not-A. (Lesson 4)

**Contrast word** — A signal such as but, yet or however that marks a turn; the clause after it often carries the weight. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Controlled comparison** — A comparison in which other factors are held roughly equal, so that a difference can be credited to one factor. (Lesson 8)

**Correlation** — A pattern in which two things vary together, which does not by itself show that one causes the other. (Lesson 8)

**Counterargument** — A rival view or objection to the author's claim. (Lesson 3)

**Counterexample** — A case that contradicts a general claim and so weakens it. (Lesson 6)

**Critical reading** — Reading a passage actively to find its structure, claims, evidence and limits. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**D**

**Deductive argument** — An argument meant to guarantee its conclusion if the premises are true. (Lesson 3)

**Definition (stipulated)** — A meaning the author fixes for a term for the purposes of the argument. (Lesson 8)

**Description** — Prose that tells what something is like without arguing a claim. (Lesson 3)

**Detail question** — A question asking for something the passage states directly. (Strategy Chapter 3)

**Diction** — The author's choice of words, which carries tone and attitude. (Lesson 2)

**Distractor** — A wrong answer choice written to attract readers who make a particular mistake. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**E**

**Elimination** — Removing choices for named reasons, not for a feeling. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Emphasis** — A signal that the author thinks a point matters, such as in fact or above all. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Equivocation** — Using a word in two senses within one argument as though it had only one. (Lesson 3)

**Error log** — A notebook record of each miss with its cause, its trap and its fix. (Strategy Chapter 20)

**Evaluative word** — A word that expresses judgment, such as elegant, flawed or crude. (Lesson 2)

**Evidence** — Facts, examples or reasons offered to support a claim. (Lesson 3)

**Evidence assessment** — Judging how well the evidence supports a claim: its size, typicality, relevance and source. (Lesson 3)

**Example** — A particular case offered to support or illustrate a point. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Except question** — A question asking for the choice that is not true or not stated; read the stem verb twice. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Extension question** — A question asking where the author's reasoning would lead if carried further. (Lesson 5)

**Extrapolation** — Extending a pattern beyond the range in which it was observed. (Lesson 8)

**Extreme word** — An absolute such as always, never or only, which makes a choice easy to refute. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**F**

**Figurative language** — Language used in a non-literal way, such as metaphor, to make a point vividly. (Lesson 7)

**Flag** — A mark in the exam interface noting a question to return to. (Strategy Chapter 16)

**Foundations of comprehension** — The skill family covering main idea, detail, meaning in context and structure. (Strategy Chapter 3)

**Frame** — The three-part header of a passage map: topic, point and tone. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Function question** — A question asking what a sentence or paragraph does, not what it says. (Lesson 1)

**G**

**Generalization** — A claim that something holds for a whole class, supported by cases. (Lesson 8)

**Genre** — A kind of writing with its own conventions, such as a critical essay or a fieldwork account. (Lesson 7)

**H**

**Half-right** — A trap choice that is faithful in one part and unsupported in another. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Hasty generalization** — Drawing a broad conclusion from too few or atypical cases. (Lesson 8)

**Hedge** — A word that limits a claim, such as may, tends to or in some cases. (Lesson 2)

**Hypothesis** — A proposed explanation that can be tested against evidence. (Lesson 8)

**I**

**Illustration** — A case or image used to make an idea clearer, not necessarily to prove it. (Lesson 1)

**Implication** — What follows from a statement, whether or not it is said outright. (Lesson 4)

**Inference** — A conclusion supported by the text without being stated in it. (Lesson 4)

**Inference question** — A question asking what must be or is likely true given the passage. (Lesson 4)

**Intermediate conclusion** — A conclusion drawn from some premises that is then used as a premise for the main conclusion. (Lesson 3)

**Irony** — Saying one thing while signaling a different or opposite meaning. (Lesson 2)

**J**

**Jargon** — Specialized vocabulary of a field, which a passage defines enough for you to use it. (Lesson 8)

**L**

**Lead-in** — The opening sentences of a passage that set the topic and often hint at the point. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Literal meaning** — The plain meaning of a word or phrase as opposed to its figurative sense. (Lesson 7)

**Logical gap** — A step in an argument not supported by what is said. (Lesson 6)

**M**

**Main conclusion** — The final claim the whole argument supports. (Lesson 3)

**Main idea** — The central point of the whole passage, which covers every paragraph and no more. (Lesson 1)

**Map (passage map)** — Scratch-paper notes of a passage's frame and paragraph jobs, made as you read. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Meaning in context** — A question asking what a word or phrase means as used in a particular place. (Strategy Chapter 3)

**Metaphor** — A figure that describes one thing as another to bring out a likeness. (Lesson 7)

**Misattributed view** — A trap in which a view the author reports is given to the author as their own. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Model (social science)** — A simplified description of how some part of society works, resting on stated assumptions. (Lesson 8)

**N**

**Narrative structure** — A passage organized as a story or chronology, often with a thesis behind it. (Lesson 1)

**Necessary assumption** — An assumption without which the argument fails. (Lesson 6)

**Negation test** — Checking whether an assumption is required by asking whether denying it breaks the argument. (Lesson 6)

**Neutral tone** — A tone that reports without evident judgment; not the same as mixed. (Lesson 2)

**New-information question** — A question asking how a new fact would affect an argument or claim. (Lesson 5)

**O**

**Objection** — A challenge to a claim, raised by the author or by someone the author reports. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Operationalization** — Turning an abstract concept into something that can be measured. (Lesson 8)

**Organization** — How the parts of a passage are arranged to build its point. (Lesson 1)

**Out of scope** — A trap choice that raises a matter the passage never discusses. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Outside knowledge** — Information not in the passage; the section does not require it and answers must not depend on it. (Strategy Chapter 1)

**Outside-knowledge lure** — A trap choice that may be true in life but is not supported by the passage. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**P**

**Paraphrase** — Restating a statement in other words without changing its meaning. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Passage** — A block of prose, about 500 to 600 words, followed by a set of questions [VERIFY]. (Strategy Chapter 1)

**Pattern of reasoning** — The form of an argument, abstracted from its content, as used in analogy questions. (Lesson 5)

**Point (of a passage)** — The author's main claim, stated in your own words in the frame. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Prediction** — An answer or kind of answer you form from the passage before reading the choices. (Strategy Chapter 3)

**Premise** — A statement offered in support of a conclusion. (Lesson 3)

**Presupposition** — What a statement takes for granted in order to make sense. (Lesson 4)

**Primary purpose** — The main thing the author does in the passage, usually asked with a verb. (Lesson 2)

**Principle** — A general rule underlying particular cases, extracted for application questions. (Lesson 5)

**Q**

**Qualifier** — A word or clause that limits how far a claim goes. (Lesson 2)

**Qualify (paragraph job)** — To limit or narrow a claim already made. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**R**

**Reasoning beyond the text** — The skill family covering application, strengthen and weaken, analogies and new information. (Strategy Chapter 3)

**Reasoning within the text** — The skill family covering inference, function, relationships and the author's reasoning. (Strategy Chapter 3)

**Rebuttal** — A reply that answers an objection. (Lesson 3)

**Reconcile** — To show that two apparently opposed views can both be partly right. (Lesson 2)

**Reported view** — A view the passage describes without endorsing. (Lesson 3)

**Rhetorical question** — A question asked for effect, not to be answered. (Lesson 2)

**Rival explanation** — A different account of the same evidence, which may weaken the author's. (Lesson 8)

**Role confusion** — A trap in which a choice restates what a line says instead of what it does. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**S**

**Sample** — The group of cases actually studied, which may or may not represent the whole. (Lesson 8)

**Sarcasm** — A bitter or mocking form of irony. (Lesson 2)

**Scale of intensity** — The range from mild to strong used to match the strength of a tone choice to the passage. (Lesson 2)

**Scare quotes** — Quotation marks used to signal doubt or distance from a word. (Lesson 2)

**Scope** — The range of subject matter that a claim or a passage covers. (Lesson 1)

**Selection effect** — A distortion arising because the cases examined were not chosen at random. (Lesson 8)

**Signal word** — A connector that shows how sentences relate, such as however, for instance or therefore. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Skim** — To read quickly without notes; discouraged for the first paragraph of a passage. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Stem** — The question as asked, before the answer choices. (Strategy Chapter 3)

**Strengthen** — To make a conclusion more likely, by supporting a premise or an assumption. (Lesson 6)

**Structure** — The arrangement of claims, evidence and reply within a passage. (Lesson 1)

**Sufficient assumption** — An assumption that, if added, guarantees the conclusion. (Lesson 6)

**Support (paragraph job)** — Evidence or an example that backs a claim. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Synthesize** — To combine two or more views into one account. (Lesson 2)

**T**

**Teach-back** — Explaining a lesson in your own words to test whether you can. (Strategy Chapter 20)

**Thesis** — The central claim a passage sets out to defend. (Lesson 1)

**Tone** — The author's attitude as it comes through in word choice and emphasis. (Lesson 2)

**Tone mismatch** — A trap in which a choice describes an attitude hotter or colder than the author's. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Topic** — What a passage is about, which is not the same as its point. (Lesson 1)

**Transition** — A word or sentence that moves a passage from one part to the next. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Triage** — Deciding the order in which to spend limited time on passages and questions. (Strategy Chapter 16)

**True but not asked** — A trap in which a choice is stated in the passage but does not answer the question. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**U**

**Unsupported inference** — A trap in which a choice goes a step past what the passage allows. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**V**

**Verb of the stem** — The word in a question that tells the task, such as weaken, support or except. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Voice** — Whose view is being expressed in a given sentence: the author's or another's. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**Voice tracker** — A short list of the views in a passage with a note of who holds each. (Strategy Chapter 2)

**W**

**Weaken** — To make a conclusion less likely, by attacking a premise or an assumption. (Lesson 6)

**Wrong direction** — A trap in which a choice strengthens when you are asked to weaken, or the reverse. (Strategy Chapter 17)

**Wrong scope** — A trap in which a choice is too narrow or too broad for the question. (Lesson 1)

Praxis Tip: Pick five terms you could not define without looking, and write each in your notebook with a one-line example from a passage you have read. A term you can illustrate is a term you own.
