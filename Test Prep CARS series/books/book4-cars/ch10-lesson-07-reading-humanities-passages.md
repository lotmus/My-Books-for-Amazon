# Lesson 7: Reading Humanities Passages

**What you will be able to do**

- Recognize the five moves that humanities authors make: interpret, evaluate, compare, define and reconsider.
- Separate the author's own view from the views of critics, schools and "some people" that the author reports.
- Judge what an example, an analogy or a figure of speech is doing in an argument.
- Read tone, irony and hedging accurately, and answer questions about them.
- Apply a passage's reasoning to a new case without importing outside knowledge.

**Why it matters for the exam**

CARS passages are understood to come from the humanities and the social sciences, with no outside knowledge required [VERIFY] (check the exam owner's current free format page). Humanities passages (art, literature, music, architecture, philosophy, ethics, the history of ideas) are where many readers lose their footing, because the argument is often carried by interpretation, tone and image rather than by data. There are no numbers to hold on to. What you hold on to is structure: who says what, why, and with how much confidence.

This lesson builds on the mapping method in [Strategy: The Active Reading Method](chapter:2), the argument anatomy in [Lesson 3](chapter:6) and the inference skills in [Lesson 4](chapter:7) and [Lesson 5](chapter:8). It assumes you have met the reading-structure material in Core Test Skills, Chapter 15, and the argument basics in Core Test Skills, Chapter 16.

Praxis Tip: Humanities prose rewards patience more than speed. Read for the sequence of moves, not for the beauty of the sentences, though you are permitted to notice the beauty on your way past.

**Concept Review**

**The five moves.** Almost every humanities argument is built from a small set of moves.

| Move | What the author does | Typical signal |
|---|---|---|
| Interpret | Claims that a work or practice means something | "reads as," "is best understood as" |
| Evaluate | Judges a work, a theory or a reading | "mistaken," "richer," "thin" |
| Compare | Sets two views or works side by side | "whereas," "unlike," "by contrast" |
| Define | Fixes what a term will mean | "what I mean by," "in this sense" |
| Reconsider | Turns against a common view | "but," "yet," "the real question" |

A typical passage opens with a common view, reconsiders it, supports the new view with an example or two, and ends with a qualification. Mapping each paragraph by its move (see Core Test Skills, Chapter 15, for the paragraph map) gives you the skeleton quickly.

**Whose voice is it?** Humanities authors constantly report other people's views: "critics have long held," "a familiar rule says," "it is often said." These phrases mark a view that is on the table but not yet endorsed. The author's own view usually arrives after a turn ("this seems to me mistaken," "the difficulty is that") or is signaled by evaluative words. Questions love to test this, so write **A:** beside the author's lines and **C:** beside the reported views on your scratch paper.

Praxis Tip: When a view is introduced by "some critics" or "it is said," treat it as a suspect until the author vouches for it. Many wrong answers attribute a reported view to the author.

**Examples are evidence, and each one is there for a reason.** A humanities author rarely gives an example for decoration. Ask of each example: does it illustrate the claim, support it, or test it (a counterexample)? A flat style in a diary, a plastered wall, a funeral custom: each is chosen because it behaves in a way the claim must explain. Questions often ask what an example is *for*, or what a new example would show.

**Analogies compare structure, not surface.** When an author compares moral cases to a physics experiment, the comparison is about one feature (isolating a factor). It does not claim ethics is physics. Wrong answers stretch an analogy beyond the point it was made to carry.

**Tone and hedging.** Humanities authors use irony, understatement and qualifiers heavily. "Mostly true," "one suspects," "perhaps" and "tends to" lower the claim's strength; "always," "never" and "the only" raise it. An answer choice that is more confident than the author is too extreme. An answer choice that is more timid than the author may be too weak. Match the strength.

**Figurative language.** When an author writes that a rule "purchases its strength at the price of another's," read the literal claim underneath (a trade-off). Ask what ordinary sentence the image stands in for, and choose the answer that paraphrases it without adding to it.

**Abstract vocabulary.** Words such as "convention," "expectation," "lineage," "canon" and "authenticity" are usually defined by the passage's own examples. Do not supply a dictionary meaning from memory. Use the sentence.

**The questions you will meet.**

| Family | Typical humanities question | Move you make |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations | Main idea; what a phrase means | Paraphrase the map |
| Within the text | Why the author mentions X; tone | Name the job of the line |
| Beyond the text | Apply the view to a new case; weaken | Rebuild the reasoning |

Praxis Tip: Before you read the choices on a "why does the author mention" question, say the job aloud in two words (illustrates, rebuts, concedes, defines). Then find the choice that matches.

**Worked Example 1: Whose view is it? (skill: author versus critics)**

*Passage.* "Readers of the Harbor Notebook, an invented nineteenth-century travel diary, have long treated its flat style as a defect. The diarist records a shipwreck in the same tone as a purchase of rope, and some critics conclude that she lacked feeling. This reading seems to me mistaken. Flatness is not the absence of emotion but a way of managing it: a writer who cannot afford to dwell on terror, because the next task is waiting, will learn to set it down in the grammar of inventory. The ledger tone is therefore the notebook's most eloquent feature, not its weakest."

*Map.* (1) Common critical view: flat style is a defect. (2) Author: mistaken; flatness manages emotion. (3) Conclusion: flatness is a strength.

*Question.* Which of the following does the author reject?
A. The claim that the diarist's tone is flat
B. The inference that a flat tone shows a lack of feeling
C. The claim that the diarist recorded a shipwreck
D. The suggestion that the notebook has a style at all

*Reasoning.* The author calls the critics' reading "mistaken," and the critics' reading is the inference "flat, therefore unfeeling." **B.** The author actually accepts the flatness (A is true but is accepted, not rejected), and C and D are never challenged. Wrong choices A and C are "true but not asked": they are facts the author grants.

*Skill used:* separating what the author accepts (the observation) from what the author rejects (the inference drawn from it).

**Worked Example 2: What a case is for (skill: function of an example)**

*Passage.* "Philosophers who ask why promises bind us usually offer one of two answers. The first says that a promise creates an expectation, and breaking it harms the person who relied on it. The second says that a promise is a kind of act, like a vow, whose very point is to create an obligation, whatever anyone expects. The first answer struggles with a case in which the listener forgets the promise entirely: no expectation remains, yet most people feel the obligation survives. The second answer struggles with the worry that it makes promising a mere ritual, binding by definition rather than by reason. Neither account is idle, but each buys its strength at the price of the other's."

*Question.* The case of the listener who forgets the promise is mentioned in order to
A. show that promises should never be kept
B. illustrate a difficulty for the expectation account
C. demonstrate that the act account is correct
D. argue that forgetting is morally blameworthy

*Reasoning.* The sentence says the first answer "struggles" with this case, so the example is a difficulty for the expectation account: **B.** A is too extreme and unmentioned; C goes beyond the passage, since the second account has its own worry; D is out of scope.

*Skill used:* naming the job of the example (a counterexample) before reading the choices.

**Worked Example 3: Applying a refined rule (skill: new case)**

*Passage.* "A familiar rule of architecture holds that a building should be honest about its materials: concrete should look like concrete, and timber like timber. The rule appeals because it sounds like a moral principle applied to stone. But the plastered house is a difficulty. Plaster conceals the brick behind it, yet no one feels deceived by a plastered wall, because the convention of plastering is universally known. Honesty, it seems, concerns what a viewer is entitled to expect, not what the surface literally shows. The rule is best read as shorthand for a narrower demand: do not exploit a viewer's expectations to mislead."

*Question.* Which case would the author most likely count as a violation of the narrower demand?
A. A wooden wall painted to resemble stone, in a town where stone walls are known to signal sturdiness and sell for more
B. A plastered brick house in a town where plastering is common
C. A concrete wall left bare to show its texture
D. A building that uses both timber and concrete

*Reasoning.* The narrower demand is about misleading expectations. Only A uses a convention (stone means sturdy) to mislead. B is the passage's own case, where no one is misled. C would satisfy the original rule and says nothing about misleading. D is irrelevant to expectation. **A.**

*Skill used:* rebuilding the refined rule in one sentence, then testing each new case against it.

**Worked Example 4: Irony and its target (skill: tone)**

*Passage.* "It has become customary to read a few lines of a dead poet at a funeral, and the custom is harmless enough. It is also, one suspects, a small act of delegation. The mourner who cannot find words borrows someone whose professional business was finding them, and the congregation, relieved, nods at the borrowed eloquence as though it were a family trait."

*Question.* The phrase "as though it were a family trait" most nearly suggests that the congregation
A. knows the mourner is related to the poet
B. credits the mourner with an eloquence that actually belongs to the poet
C. disapproves of the mourner's choice of poem
D. believes eloquence is inherited

*Reasoning.* The literal image is a trait passed in the family; the point is gentle mockery of attributing borrowed eloquence to the borrower. **B.** A takes the image literally. C is unsupported, because the congregation is "relieved." D uses the passage's language ("trait") but changes its meaning. Notice the hedges ("harmless enough," "one suspects"): the tone is mild and wry, not hostile.

*Skill used:* translating a figure into its literal claim and matching the strength of the tone.

**Worked Example 5: Structure of a history-of-ideas passage (skill: how the argument moves)**

*Passage.* "In earlier eras, a person of unusual creative power was said to have a genius, as one might have a guardian spirit: the gift was a visitor, and it could leave. Later, the word came to name the person rather than the spirit: one no longer had a genius but was one. This change is often celebrated as a promotion of the artist. It was also a burden. A visiting spirit can be blamed when it fails to appear; a person who is a genius has no one to blame. Modern anxiety about creative drought is thus partly a linguistic inheritance: we made the gift a permanent possession, and so made its absence a personal failing."

*Question.* The passage proceeds by
A. refuting a critic with a counterexample
B. tracing a change in how a term was used and arguing that the change had an unwelcome consequence
C. comparing two living schools of thought and declining to choose
D. listing evidence for the date of a historical event

*Reasoning.* Sentence by sentence: old usage, new usage, common celebration, reversal ("It was also a burden"), consequence. **B.** A has no critic or counterexample. C invents two schools. D has no dates at all. Note that "thus partly" is a hedge; an answer claiming the change *caused* all creative anxiety would be too extreme.

*Skill used:* describing the passage's movement in one sentence.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: If you cannot say in one sentence what a paragraph is doing (as opposed to what it is about), reread its first and last sentences before moving on.

Praxis Tip: When the author reports a view and then writes "but," the sentence after the "but" is usually the author's. Underline it mentally.

Praxis Tip: For "which would the author most likely agree with" questions, the correct choice is usually modest. Treat sweeping words with the same suspicion you would apply to a salesman with a very short list of exceptions.

Praxis Tip: An analogy proves one thing at a time. Identify the single feature being compared, and reject answers that borrow other features from the comparison.

Praxis Tip: If the passage names a topic you know well (a poet, a philosopher, a style), pretend you know nothing about it. The questions concern the passage's account, which may well differ from what you learned in school.

Praxis Tip: Keep a list in your error log of the humanities signal phrases that fooled you ("it might be said," "one is tempted to think"). Reported views hide in polite language.

**Common Mistakes**

- Attributing a reported view ("critics say") to the author.
- Treating an illustrative example as the author's main point.
- Stretching an analogy beyond the single feature being compared.
- Reading irony literally, or reading mild irony as hostility.
- Choosing an answer that is more extreme than the author's hedged claim.
- Answering from what you remember about a famous work or thinker instead of from the passage.

**Checkpoint Passages**

*Passage A.* "Why do we find ruins beautiful? The common answer is melancholy: a broken colonnade reminds us that nothing lasts, and the pleasure is the gentle sadness of that thought. But melancholy cannot be the whole story, for a heap of rubble reminds us of time's work just as well, and no one crosses a continent to admire rubble. What the colonnade offers, and the rubble does not, is a task. Enough of the original survives that the eye begins, almost against its will, to restore the missing roof and the vanished wall. The pleasure of a ruin is the pleasure of this half-finished act of imagination. It follows that a ruin can be too ruined or not ruined enough: restore it fully and the task disappears, reduce it to stones and there is nothing to build from."

*Passage B.* "Moral philosophers often defend their theories by appeal to cases, and the cases are usually extreme: a runaway trolley, a drowning child, a lifeboat too small for its passengers. Some critics object that extreme cases are poor guides to ordinary conduct, since few of us will ever redirect a trolley. This objection misses what the cases are for. A physicist who removes air resistance from a falling object is not claiming that air is absent from the world; she is isolating one factor so that its effect can be seen. Extreme cases isolate one moral factor in the same way. The real danger lies elsewhere. A case stripped of its surroundings also strips away the history, relationships and uncertainty that shape real decisions, and a theory tested only on stripped cases may fit them perfectly and the world poorly."

*Passage C.* "The history of any art, we are told, is a history of influence: this painter learned from that one, who had learned from another. The story is tidy and mostly true, and it carries a hidden bias. It rewards the artist who resembles a predecessor, since resemblance is the evidence of influence, and quietly punishes the artist who resembles no one, since there is no one to cite. The result is that the most original figures are the hardest to place in a lineage, and the lineage-makers, unable to place them, do the next best thing and invent a teacher. A little scholarly effort is then devoted to proving that a painter who owed nothing to anyone owed a great deal to someone obscure."

Questions 1 and 2 refer to Passage A, questions 3 to 5 to Passage B, and questions 6 and 7 to Passage C.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. The author brings up a heap of rubble in order to
   A. argue that a pile of rubble is more beautiful than a standing colonnade
   B. show melancholy cannot explain why ruins are admired and debris is not
   C. claim that contemplating the passage of time is not worth the effort
   D. recommend that ruins be restored completely so that they look new

2. Suppose a partly standing arch has had its missing stones replaced so that it looks new and complete. According to the passage's reasoning, the arch would most likely
   A. be more pleasing, because a finished work is always preferable to a broken one
   B. be equally pleasing, because melancholy comes from age alone
   C. be admired mainly by viewers who dislike ruins
   D. give less of the pleasure ruins give, since no gap is left to fill

3. The author's response to the critics' objection about extreme cases is best described as
   A. rejecting its reasoning but naming a different limitation of such cases
   B. agreeing with it fully and urging philosophers to abandon such cases altogether
   C. dismissing it and denying that extreme cases have any limitation
   D. conceding it for physics but defending it for ethics

4. The author's comparison to the physicist serves mainly to
   A. show that moral theories can be tested exactly as physical theories are
   B. suggest that physical experiments are more reliable than moral cases
   C. illustrate that a simplified case can legitimately isolate one factor
   D. argue that moral questions have numerical answers

5. Which finding, if true, would most weaken the author's final claim that a theory tested only on stripped cases "may fit them perfectly and the world poorly"?
   A. Real decisions are shaped by history, relationships and a good deal of uncertainty
   B. Theories that fit stripped cases well fit detailed real decisions equally well
   C. Few philosophers use extreme cases in their published work
   D. Some critics of extreme cases have never faced a drowning child

6. The tone of the passage toward the makers of artistic lineages is best described as
   A. bitterly hostile
   B. warmly admiring
   C. neutral and purely descriptive
   D. wryly critical

7. According to the passage, a painter who resembles no predecessor is likely to
   A. be given a teacher by scholars who cannot otherwise place the painter
   B. be left out of the histories of art altogether, having no lineage to cite
   C. have in fact learned from several obscure teachers of the period
   D. be more admired than the many painters who imitate others

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** The rubble is a test case for the melancholy view: it prompts the same thought about time but not the same admiration. (A reverses the point, since the author says no one crosses a continent to admire rubble; C is unsupported, because the author grants that rubble reminds us of time's work; D contradicts the final sentence, which says full restoration removes the task.)
2. **D.** The author says that fully restoring a ruin makes the imaginative task disappear, so the distinctive pleasure goes with it. (A is too extreme and reverses the passage's claim about the task; B is unsupported; the author denies that melancholy is the whole story and never says it comes from age alone; C is out of scope, because the passage says nothing about who admires restored buildings.)
3. **A.** The author says the objection "misses what the cases are for" and then says "the real danger lies elsewhere." (B reverses the author, who says the objection misses what the cases are for; C is too extreme, because the author names a real danger; D is out of scope, since the physicist appears only in an analogy.)
4. **C.** The analogy makes one point: removing air resistance, like stripping a case, isolates a factor so its effect can be seen. (A stretches the analogy beyond the single feature being compared; B is unsupported, because the analogy compares method, not reliability; D is out of scope, as numbers are never mentioned.)
5. **B.** The claim predicts a gap between performance on stripped cases and performance on real ones, so evidence of no gap weakens it. (A would support the claim, since those are the features the stripped cases remove; C is irrelevant, because the claim concerns theories that are tested only on such cases; D is irrelevant, because the critics' experience does not bear on how well theories fit the world.)
6. **D.** The author concedes the story is mostly true but gently mocks its bias and its habit of inventing teachers. (A is too extreme, since phrases such as "tidy and mostly true" and "a little scholarly effort" are mild; B reverses the attitude; the author points out a bias and mocks the invented teacher; C ignores the evaluative and ironic language such as "quietly punishes".)
7. **A.** The lineage-makers, unable to place the most original figures, "invent a teacher." (B is too extreme; the passage says a teacher is invented, not that the painter is omitted; C reverses the passage, which says the teacher is invented and the painter owed nothing to anyone; D is unsupported, because admiration is not discussed.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, name the trap in each wrong choice you were tempted by (reversal, too extreme, true but not asked, misattributed view) and add it to your error log ([Strategy: When You Are Stuck: Elimination and Trap Answers](chapter:17) catalogs the traps).
