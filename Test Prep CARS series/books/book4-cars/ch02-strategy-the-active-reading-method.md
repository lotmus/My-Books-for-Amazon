# Strategy: The Active Reading Method

**What you will be able to do**

- Read a CARS passage while building a short map on scratch paper, without losing the thread.
- Name the job each paragraph does in the argument.
- Use signal words to predict where the author is going.
- Keep the author's own view separate from views the author quotes, reports or rejects.
- Use your map to answer questions quickly and locate evidence.

**Why it matters**

Strong readers do not read more slowly than weak ones. They read with a purpose. A passage is not a pile of sentences; it is an argument with a shape, and the shape is what the questions ask about. Students who read passively, hoping that the important parts will announce themselves, tend to reach the end of a paragraph with a pleasant sense of having read it and no idea what it did. Students who read actively finish each paragraph knowing its job.

The method in this chapter takes a few minutes per passage and produces a map of perhaps forty to sixty words. That map is not a summary for its own sake. It is a tool for finding things fast and for keeping the author's position straight when the answer choices begin to blur it.

Praxis Tip: The aim of a passage map is not to impress anyone. It is to be so short that you will actually make it, and so specific that you will actually use it.

**What goes on scratch paper**

Your device cannot be written on, so take a sheet of scratch paper and set up a map for each passage. It has two parts.

**The frame**, written after the first paragraph or two and revised at the end:

- **Topic:** what the passage is about, in a few words.
- **Point:** the author's main claim, as soon as you can tell what it is.
- **Tone:** admiring, skeptical, neutral, ironic, regretful, and so on.

**The line-by-line notes**, one for each paragraph, each at most about eight words:

- the paragraph number,
- its job (see below),
- a few key words that capture its content.

Use shorthand that you can read back instantly. A plus sign for support, a minus for an objection, and a question mark for something puzzling are enough. The shorthand matters less than the habit. If you have to translate your own notes, they are too elaborate.

Praxis Tip: Never copy sentences. If you are copying, you are not reading; you are typing, and typing has never been proven to cause understanding.

**What paragraphs do**

Every paragraph in a well-built passage has a job. A passage rarely has a paragraph with no job, although it often has one with two. The common jobs are:

| Job | What it does | Typical cue |
|---|---|---|
| Set up | Introduces topic or background | "Consider...", a description |
| Claim | States the author's position | "I will argue", "in fact" |
| Support | Gives evidence or an example | "for instance", a case |
| Objection | Presents a rival or critic | "Critics say...", "Some hold" |
| Concession | Grants a point to the other side | "admittedly", "granted" |
| Reply | Answers the objection | "but", "however", "yet" |
| Qualify | Limits the claim | "only if", "in some cases" |
| Conclude | Draws the point together | "thus", "so", "then" |

When you finish a paragraph, ask: **What was that paragraph for?** If you can answer in two or three words ("objection to the claim," "example of the problem"), you have understood it better than if you could recite its sentences.

It is common to find the job at the beginning or the end of a paragraph. The first sentence often announces it; the last often draws it out. The middle supplies the proof. You do not have to skip the middle, but you can read it faster when you know what the opening promised.

**Signal words**

Signal words are the small connectors that show how sentences relate. They are the closest thing to road signs in a passage.

| Type | Examples | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | but, yet, however, although, whereas | A turn; the author's view often follows |
| Continuation | also, moreover, furthermore, similarly | More of the same |
| Cause and result | because, since, therefore, thus, so | A reason or a conclusion |
| Example | for instance, such as, consider | Support for a claim |
| Concession | admittedly, granted, of course, to be sure | A point the author will soon limit |
| Emphasis | above all, in fact, chiefly, in truth | What the author thinks matters |

Two of these deserve special respect. **Contrast words** mark a turn, and the clause after the turn usually carries the weight. In "The method is elegant, but it fails in practice," the author's verdict is in the second half. **Concession words** warn that what follows is a point the author grants and will then reduce. "Of course the older theory explained a good deal" nearly always leads to a "but" that explains what it did not.

Praxis Tip: When a sentence begins with "of course" or "admittedly," keep one eye on the next sentence. The author is clearing his throat before disagreeing with you.

**The author's voice and other voices**

Many CARS passages discuss other people's views. Some critics say this, a school of thought claims that, an earlier thinker held another thing. Your most important bookkeeping job is to keep track of **whose view is whose**. Wrong answers often borrow a view that appears in the passage but belongs to someone the author is arguing against.

Signs that the author is reporting a view rather than holding it:

- Attributions: "some critics argue," "it is often said," "the traditional account holds."
- Distancing verbs: "claims," "insists," "purports," "would have us believe."
- Quotation marks around a loaded term, which can signal skepticism ("so-called" has the same effect).
- A following turn: a "but" or "however" after a view often means the author is about to reject it.

Signs that the view is the author's own:

- First-person phrasing: "I think," "I will argue."
- Evaluative words with no attribution: "a mistake," "a better reading," "remarkably."
- Ending the passage by building on the view.

When the author seems to agree only partly, record that. A note such as "author: partly agrees with critics, but not the fraud claim" is far more useful than a note that merely says "critics."

**Worked Example 1: The passage**

Read the passage below once at a steady pace, as you would on the test, with scratch paper beside you. Make your own map as you go, then compare it with the one in Worked Example 2.

*Passage.*

Visitors to certain landscape gardens laid out in the eighteenth century are sometimes startled to find a ruined abbey, a crumbling tower or a broken colonnade standing among the lawns. These structures are not ruins at all. They were built new, often cheaply, and sometimes with only a single wall, to look as though time had already done its work. Critics have long treated such follies as a kind of lie in stone, a patron's attempt to buy an ancestry he did not possess. The complaint is easy to understand, since a wall that pretends to be older than it is does resemble a forged signature.

The charge has some force. Certain owners no doubt hoped that a ruin on the estate would lend an air of old lineage to a family whose money was recent. But the accusation of fraud assumes that visitors were meant to be fooled, and that assumption is doubtful. A ruin made of fresh stone, with a tidy path leading to it and a bench placed at the best angle for viewing, announces its own artifice. Visitors of the period were perfectly able to tell a building from a building made to look old. No one who followed a gravel walk to the tower expected to find a genuine medieval survivor waiting at the end of it.

A better reading is that the follies were instruments for producing a mood. A ruin invites a visitor to reflect on the passing of empires while remaining comfortably alive on a pleasant afternoon. The garden builder was less a forger than a stage designer, supplying the prop that made contemplation easy. Nobody calls a theater set fraudulent because it is painted canvas. The comparison has limits, however. A theater audience pays to enter a fiction, whereas garden visitors were guests of an owner who also profited socially from the impression of antiquity. Both things can be true of the same wall.

What the follies chiefly reveal, then, is a taste for pleasurable melancholy that was cultivated rather than inherited. A fake ruin moved its viewers not because it was old but because they already knew what ruins mean. The emotional effect depended on the viewer's own contribution, and so did not depend on the authenticity of the stone. This suggests a modest conclusion. It is a mistake to judge such objects by the standard we apply to historical documents. They are better judged by the standard we apply to music, which asks whether they produce the feeling they were built to produce. Even that standard, it should be said, does not excuse an owner who lied about their origins when asked directly.

**Worked Example 2: The map**

Here is a map of the passage as a strong reader might write it. It is deliberately short.

| Para | Job | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Set up, objection | Follies = fake ruins; critics: lie |
| 2 | Concession, reply | Some owners wanted lineage; but not meant to fool |
| 3 | Claim, qualify | Mood-making, like stage sets; limit: owners gained |
| 4 | Conclude | Judge as music, not documents; lying still bad |

*Frame.* **Topic:** fake ruins in eighteenth-century gardens. **Point:** follies are best judged by the mood they produce, not as frauds, though dishonesty about their origin is still wrong. **Tone:** measured, reasonable, lightly amused.

Look at what the map records. The first paragraph holds an object (follies) and the critics' view that they are lies. The words "have long treated" are a cue that this is a reported view, and "easy to understand" is a courtesy that precedes a turn. The second paragraph opens with a concession ("The charge has some force"), and the "But" in the third sentence is the turn: the author's reply is that visitors were not being fooled. The third paragraph is the positive claim (the follies produce a mood), and it ends with a qualification ("The comparison has limits"). The fourth paragraph concludes by naming a standard of judgment and, in its last sentence, limits it again.

Notice, too, what the map leaves out: the abbey, the colonnade, the bench, the gravel walk. These are the examples, and you can find them again in seconds if a question asks. A map holds the structure and leaves the furniture where it is.

Praxis Tip: On your first pass, mark where each paragraph turns. The turns, more than the topics, tell you where the author stands.

**Worked Example 3: Using the map to answer questions**

*Question 1.* Which of the following best expresses the main point of the passage?
A. The critics are right that follies were deceptions, and the owners who built them deserve blame.
B. Follies are better judged as mood-makers than as deceptions, though lying would still be wrong.
C. Garden follies and theater sets are essentially the same kind of object, built for the same audience.
D. Visitors to gardens in the eighteenth century expected to find authentic medieval buildings.

*Working.* The frame says it. The point is that follies should be judged by the mood they produce, with a reservation about lying. That is B. Choice A gives the critics' view, which the author reports and mostly resists. Choice C turns a qualified analogy into an identity; the author says the comparison "has limits." Choice D reverses the claim, since the author says no one expected a genuine survivor. **Answer: B.**

*Question 2.* The author's mention of a painted theater set in the third paragraph serves mainly to
A. show that follies cost far more to build than stage scenery did
B. offer a limited analogy against the fraud charge
C. suggest that gardens should be designed by professionals from the theater
D. concede that the critics' charge of fraud is entirely correct

*Working.* The map labels paragraph 3 "mood-making, like stage sets; limit: owners gained." The sentence is an analogy ("Nobody calls a theater set fraudulent...") and it is followed by "The comparison has limits." That is B. Choice A introduces cost, which is not discussed in this comparison; choice C invents a recommendation; choice D overstates, since the author concedes only a limited point, and not here. **Answer: B.**

*Question 3.* Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the author's claim that visitors were not meant to be fooled?
A. Many diaries from the period praise the beauty of the follies in glowing terms.
B. Letters survive in which owners tell guests a folly is a genuine medieval relic.
C. Most follies were reached by a gravel path laid out for strolling visitors.
D. Follies cost less to build than real ruins would have cost to maintain.

*Working.* The claim lives in paragraph 2: visitors knew the follies were artifice. Evidence that owners presented them as genuine would show an intent to fool, and that is B. Choice A is consistent with the author's view (beauty is part of the mood); choice C is a detail the author uses in support, not against; choice D is about cost, which does not bear on intent. **Answer: B.**

Notice how fast the questions went once the map was there. You did not search the passage for the "theater" sentence or for the sentence about visitors; the map told you the paragraph, and the paragraph held the sentence.

**A routine for a passage**

Here is a workable routine for ten minutes of passage time. [VERIFY] The exact split depends on the real time limit.

1. **Skim nothing; read the first paragraph with care.** It sets topic and often hints at the thesis. Write the topic.
2. **Read each later paragraph for its job.** Note it in a few words. Do not stop to puzzle over a hard sentence for more than a few seconds; mark it with a question mark and move on.
3. **Watch the turns.** Underline mentally every "but," "however," "yet" and "although."
4. **Track voices.** Note whose view is whose.
5. **At the end, write the frame.** Topic, point and tone, one line each.
6. **Then, and only then, read the first question.**

On a passage of five or six hundred words, the reading and mapping together should take about three to four minutes, leaving six or seven minutes for the questions. [VERIFY] Fast but unfocused reading does not save time: a student who reads in two minutes and then rereads three times in the questions has spent more.

Praxis Tip: If you cannot say in one sentence what the passage is arguing, you are not ready for the questions. Spend a few seconds finding that sentence now rather than a few minutes finding it later.

**Common Mistakes**

- Mapping too much: a map that is half the length of the passage is a second passage.
- Mapping too little: "OK" is not a note.
- Writing topics instead of jobs ("gardens" instead of "objection to the claim").
- Missing the turn: reading the first half of "X, but Y" and stopping.
- Attributing a quoted view to the author, or the author's view to a critic.
- Rereading the whole passage for every question rather than going back to the right paragraph.
- Treating examples as the main point.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Read the author's tone as seriously as the author's claims. A single sarcastic adjective can reverse a sentence.

Praxis Tip: The conclusion of a passage is not always its last sentence. It often arrives one sentence earlier, with the last sentence adding a caution.

Praxis Tip: If you are lost in paragraph three, the fix is not to read harder. Return to your map, find the last place you were sure, and read forward from there.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. *Passage.* Some historians insist that the printing press single-handedly ended manuscript culture. The view is tidy, and it is wrong. Scribes went on copying books by hand for generations, for patrons who wanted a particular look or who distrusted the new machines.

The claim that the printing press single-handedly ended manuscript culture is
   A. the author's own claim, which the passage supports
   B. the author's tentative suggestion
   C. a view held by some historians, which the author rejects
   D. a view held by patrons of scribes

2. *Passage.* Most reviewers praised the film's photography. Yet the story never earns its ending.

The word "Yet" signals that the next statement
   A. contrasts with the praise and probably carries the author's main criticism
   B. repeats the reviewers' praise in different words
   C. defines the term "photography"
   D. gives an example of the film's photography

3. *Passage.* Of course, a cheaper school lunch program would not by itself raise attendance, since absences have many causes, including transport and illness. Still, in the districts that adopted it, attendance rose by a noticeable margin.

The job of this passage is best described as
   A. introducing the topic of school attendance
   B. concluding an argument about transport
   C. defining the term "attendance"
   D. conceding a limit and then offering support for the claim

4. *Passage.* The committee's report runs to four hundred pages and, with admirable consistency, recommends that further study be conducted.

The tone of the sentence is best described as
   A. admiring
   B. dryly ironic
   C. furious
   D. neutral and descriptive

5. A student's map note for a paragraph reads "Author loves the new bridge." The paragraph says: "Admirers call the bridge a triumph. Its cost, though, ran to double the estimate, and its lanes are narrower than the old crossing's." The best correction of the note is
   A. no change, since the paragraph opens with praise
   B. "Author hates all bridges"
   C. "Bridge was cheap"
   D. "Admirers praise it; author stresses cost and narrow lanes"

6. On a passage of about 550 words, with about ten minutes in total per passage, which allocation for first-pass reading and mapping is most reasonable [VERIFY]?
   A. Under one minute
   B. About three to four minutes
   C. About seven to eight minutes
   D. The entire ten minutes

7. A sentence beginning "Granted, ..." most often signals
   A. a point the author concedes and will probably limit
   B. the author's central thesis
   C. the final conclusion of the passage
   D. a detailed example

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** "Some historians insist" is an attribution, and "and it is wrong" rejects the view. (A reverses who holds it; B misreads a flat rejection as a suggestion; D takes the patrons from a later sentence, but they are the customers, not the holders of the claim.)
2. **A.** "Yet" marks a turn from praise to a complaint, and the complaint is usually the author's point. (B treats a contrast word as a continuation; C and D describe sentence types that the passage does not contain.)
3. **D.** "Of course... would not by itself" concedes a limit, and "Still" turns to supporting evidence. (A and C describe jobs the text does not perform; B names a topic that is only mentioned as a cause of absence.)
4. **B.** "Admirable consistency" applied to a recommendation of more study is mockery. (A takes the adjective at face value; C reads anger into a dry sentence; D ignores the evaluative phrase.)
5. **D.** The note should record both voices: the admirers' praise and the author's objections. (A takes the opening praise for the author's view; B and C go beyond or contradict the text.)
6. **B.** Three to four minutes is enough to read and map and still leaves time for the questions. (A is too fast to map; C leaves too little time for the questions; D leaves none.)
7. **A.** "Granted" introduces a concession. (B confuses a concession with a thesis; C misplaces it, as concessions come before the author's reply; D mistakes a concession for an example.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, name the slip behind each miss (missed the turn, wrong voice, wrong job) and log it in your error log (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9).

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "I read for the job of each paragraph, write a short map with a frame of topic, point and tone, watch the turns and concessions, and keep the author's voice separate from the voices the author reports."

*Review schedule:* revisit this chapter after 1 day (map the gardens passage again without looking at the sample map), after 3 days (redo the checkpoint questions from a blank page), after 7 days (map a new passage from any later set and compare it with the explanation), and after 21 days (teach the routine aloud to a friend, or to a houseplant).

*Interleave:* alternate mapping a humanities passage with mapping a social-science passage ([Lesson 7](chapter:10) and [Lesson 8](chapter:11)), and then answer the questions from the map alone before rereading.

Praxis Tip: Keep every map you make for a week. When you miss a question, compare the map with the passage. Often the map shows exactly where your understanding went wrong.
