# Lesson 8: Reading Social-Science Passages

**What you will be able to do**

- Identify the kind of claim a social-science author is making: descriptive, causal, explanatory, predictive or normative.
- Name the kind of evidence offered (survey, case study, historical record, comparison, model) and say what it can and cannot show.
- Spot the standard weak points: selection effects, one-sided samples, loose measures, hidden assumptions and rival explanations.
- Answer strengthen, weaken and application questions by reasoning about evidence rather than by recalling facts.
- Recognize when an author is cautious about a conclusion and match that caution in your answer.

**Why it matters for the exam**

The second half of the CARS passage pool is understood to be social science: economics, sociology, anthropology, political thought, education and related fields [VERIFY] (check the exam owner's current free format page). These passages differ from humanities passages in one useful way. They are usually about evidence. Someone claims that something is true, causes something or should be done, and then the passage shows how good the support is. Your job is to read the way a careful referee reads a report: what is being claimed, on what basis, and what would change the picture.

You do not need any social-science knowledge for this. If a passage uses a technical term, it defines it or lets the context do so. What you need is a small stock of questions to ask of any argument, which this lesson supplies. It builds on [Lesson 3](chapter:6) (anatomy of an argument), [Lesson 6](chapter:9) (strengthen, weaken and assumption) and Core Test Skills, Chapter 16.

Praxis Tip: Read a social-science passage the way you would read a friend's claim that a new diet changed their life. Pleased for them, but curious about what else changed that month.

**Concept Review**

**What kind of claim is it?** Before you weigh evidence, name the claim, because each type needs different support.

| Claim type | Example shape | What would support it |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | "Most households do X" | Representative counts |
| Causal | "X leads to Y" | Comparison that rules out rivals |
| Explanatory | "Y happened because of X" | Evidence of timing and mechanism |
| Predictive | "If X, then Y will follow" | A model plus its assumptions |
| Normative | "Society should do X" | Values as well as facts |

A frequent source of wrong answers is a choice that quietly shifts the type: a descriptive finding presented as proof of cause, or a causal claim presented as a recommendation.

**What kind of evidence is it?** Social-science passages lean on a handful of evidence types. Each one has a standard strength and a standard limit.

| Evidence | Good for | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Survey | What people report | Wording; who answered |
| Case study | Showing a pattern can occur | Not how common it is |
| Historical record | Timing and sequence | Gaps; survivor bias |
| Comparison of groups | Testing a cause | Groups may differ otherwise |
| Model | Clarifying logic | Rests on assumptions |

**Correlation, cause and the third factor.** Two things that move together may be linked by cause, by a third factor, by chance, or by reverse direction (Y causing X). A passage that reports a pattern and then insists on a cause is inviting you to ask: what else differs between the groups? Strengthen questions often supply a comparison that rules out the third factor; weaken questions supply the third factor.

**Selection.** If the people in a sample chose to be there, or survived to be counted, they may differ from the people left out. "Students who finished the program improved" says little until you know what happened to those who left.

**Measures and definitions.** A social concept (trust, wellbeing, literacy) is measured by something narrower: a survey question, a test, a count. Passages often argue that a measure fails to capture the concept, or that two groups mean different things by the same word. When the measure changes, the number can change without the thing changing.

**Models and assumptions.** A model simplifies on purpose. Its predictions follow from its assumptions, so the most useful questions are: what does it assume, and what happens if the assumption fails? A model is not refuted by being simple; it is limited by what it leaves out.

**Rival explanations and timing.** When two explanations compete, the author usually looks for evidence that fits one better: a change that happens suddenly points away from a cause that builds slowly. Look for the timing detail.

**Scope and caution.** Good social-science authors qualify: "in this sample," "tends to," "suggests." They separate showing that something *can* happen (existence) from showing how *often* it happens (prevalence). Wrong answers inflate a cautious conclusion into a general law.

Praxis Tip: Underline mentally every qualifier in the author's conclusion ("may," "partly," "in this case"). Then check that your chosen answer is no stronger than the author.

Praxis Tip: When a question asks what would strengthen or weaken a causal claim, first say the causal claim in the form "X causes Y." Then ask what could break the link: a third factor, reverse direction, or a selection filter.

**Worked Example 1: A model and its hidden assumptions (skill: assumptions)**

*Passage.* "A simple model of downtown parking treats each driver as choosing between a cheap curb space and a costly garage. If the curb is cheaper, the model says, drivers will circle the block hunting for a space until the time they lose is worth about as much as the fee they save. The model explains why streets with cheap curb parking so often look busy with slow traffic. Yet it quietly assumes that every driver values an hour in the same way. A courier in a hurry and a retiree with an empty afternoon would, on the model's own logic, make different choices, and the model's single predicted circling time hides that difference."

*Map.* (1) Model: drivers trade search time against fee. (2) What it explains. (3) Hidden assumption; objection.

*Question.* The author's criticism of the model is that it
A. cannot explain why curb parking is cheaper than garage parking
B. treats drivers as more alike in their valuation of time than they are
C. predicts that nobody will ever use a garage
D. ignores the cost of fuel

*Reasoning.* The criticism is the assumption "every driver values an hour in the same way." **B.** A is out of scope: the model takes prices as given. C is too extreme and reverses the model, which has some drivers choosing garages. D is out of scope, since fuel is never mentioned.

*Skill used:* finding the assumption by asking what the model's single prediction hides.

**Worked Example 2: A causal claim with a rival (skill: strengthen)**

*Passage.* "Districts that moved their school start times later report higher test scores, and advocates cite the pattern as proof that more sleep improves learning. The inference is tempting. But the districts that adopted later starts were also, on average, those that had recently raised spending per pupil, and better-funded schools tend to post higher scores whatever time the day begins. The advocates may be right. Their evidence, however, cannot distinguish the effect of sleep from the effect of money."

*Question.* Which finding would most strengthen the advocates' claim?
A. Later-start districts also had longer school years
B. Among districts with similar spending, those with later starts had higher scores
C. Advocates have a long record of promoting school reform
D. Test scores rose in all districts in the same year

*Reasoning.* The rival is spending. To strengthen the sleep claim, remove the rival: compare districts matched on spending. **B.** A adds another possible cause and weakens. C is about the advocates, not the evidence. D, a rise everywhere, suggests an influence unrelated to start times.

*Skill used:* naming the third factor and choosing the evidence that controls for it.

**Worked Example 3: What a case study can show (skill: scope)**

*Passage.* "A fieldworker spent a year in a single mountain village and concluded that kin loyalty there outweighs any loyalty to employers. Critics say one village cannot represent a region. The fieldworker replies that she never claimed to describe the region; she claimed only to show that such loyalty can be strong enough to shape hiring, which one well-documented case can establish. What a case study cannot do is say how common the pattern is. The fair reading of her work is therefore existence, not prevalence."

*Question.* The author would most likely agree that the fieldworker's study
A. shows that most mountain villages favor kin in hiring
B. shows that the critics have misunderstood what the study claims to do
C. proves that kin loyalty is stronger than employer loyalty everywhere
D. has no value because it covers only one village

*Reasoning.* The author sides with the fieldworker's modest claim: the study shows the pattern *occurs*. **B** captures the reply to the critics. A and C inflate existence into prevalence (too extreme). D swings to the opposite extreme.

*Skill used:* matching the strength of an answer to the scope of the evidence.

**Worked Example 4: A loose measure (skill: definitions)**

*Passage.* "Surveys that ask, 'Would you say most people can be trusted?' are widely used to measure social trust. The question is simple, and that is both its appeal and its flaw. A respondent who pictures neighbors may answer yes, while one who pictures strangers online may answer no, though the two hold identical attitudes toward the same people. When the measure shifts, researchers read a change in trust; it may only be a change in who the respondent imagines."

*Question.* The main concern raised about the survey question is that
A. respondents are likely to lie about their attitudes
B. the question may not measure one stable thing
C. trust cannot be studied at all
D. too few people answer surveys

*Reasoning.* The concern is validity: the same words call up different people. **B.** A is unsupported; the passage never accuses respondents of lying. C is too extreme, because the author criticizes one measure, not the study of trust. D is out of scope; response rates are not discussed.

*Skill used:* distinguishing a flawed measure from a flawed concept.

**Worked Example 5: Timing as evidence (skill: rival explanations)**

*Passage.* "Historians offer two explanations for the decline of weavers' guilds in an invented market town. The first points to competition from cheaper cloth carried in on new roads. The second points to a tax on guild-held workshops that made membership costly. The records favor the second: membership fell sharply in the very year the tax began, while imports of cloth rose only slowly over the next twenty years. This does not rule out competition as a contributing cause, but a cause that arrives gradually cannot easily explain a drop that arrives all at once."

*Question.* The author's argument depends on the assumption that
A. a gradual cause will tend to produce a gradual change
B. taxes always cause institutions to decline
C. competition could never harm a guild
D. historians seldom disagree

*Reasoning.* The last sentence states the assumption nearly outright. **A.** B is too extreme ("always"). C contradicts "does not rule out competition." D is out of scope.

*Skill used:* spotting the principle that links the evidence to the conclusion.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: For any "study found X" sentence, ask who was studied, who was left out, and what else differed. That is three questions and about eight seconds.

Praxis Tip: The phrase "the figures show" and the phrase "the figures suggest" are not interchangeable. Pick the answer whose verb matches the author's.

Praxis Tip: When a passage argues against a conclusion, it does not follow that the author believes the opposite conclusion. "The evidence does not show X" is not "X is false," a distinction that exam writers adore.

Praxis Tip: Choose answers that resist rivals. A strengthen answer usually removes an alternative explanation; a weaken answer usually supplies one.

Praxis Tip: Do not let a number impress you. A big percentage from a small or self-selected group is still a small or self-selected group in a good hat.

Praxis Tip: If a normative passage ("schools should...") gives only facts, the logical gap is a value. Spot that gap and you will see why a tempting answer fails.

**Common Mistakes**

- Treating a correlation as proof of a cause because the passage's tone is confident.
- Ignoring selection (who dropped out, who volunteered).
- Turning "can happen" into "usually happens."
- Treating a model's simplification as a refutation of the model.
- Reading a criticism of a measure as a criticism of the whole field.
- Bringing in statistics or history you remember instead of the passage's evidence.

**Checkpoint Passages**

*Passage A.* "When a neighborhood library extended its opening hours, borrowing rose by a fifth. Local officials took this as proof that the hours had been too short. A sociologist reviewing the figures was less sure. Many of the new visits, she found, were by people who stayed for the heated reading room and borrowed nothing; the rise in borrowing came almost entirely from a few weeks in which a nearby school assigned a group project. Longer hours may well have helped, she conceded, but a single before-and-after comparison cannot separate the hours from the assignment. What the figures do show is a rise; what they cannot show is why. To learn the cause one would need to compare libraries that changed their hours with similar libraries that did not, over the same period."

*Passage B.* "A tutoring program announced that students who completed it improved their grades by a full letter on average. The figure is impressive, and the program's directors repeat it often. But completion is the catch. Students who drop out of a demanding program tend to be those who were struggling with it, so the group that finishes has already been filtered for motivation and ability. A comparison between completers and the general student body therefore measures partly the program and partly the filter. To be convincing, the program would have to report what happened to everyone who enrolled, or compare enrollees with an equivalent group who applied but were placed on a waiting list. Until then the headline figure is best read as a promise rather than a result."

*Passage C.* "Anthropologists who study gift exchange often report that givers deny expecting anything in return. Cynics hear this denial as a polite fiction covering a strict ledger. But the denial may be doing real work. A gift that is openly repaid on schedule looks like a sale, and the relationship it creates is one of trade, which can be ended by settling the account. A gift whose return is left vague keeps the parties in each other's debt, and a lasting tie is the point. On this view the fiction is not a cover for the ledger but a device for keeping the ledger open. The pretense of not counting is what allows a lasting bond to form."

Questions 1 and 2 refer to Passage A, questions 3 to 5 to Passage B, and questions 6 and 7 to Passage C.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. The sociologist's main point is that
   A. longer opening hours had no real effect on borrowing at that library
   B. the officials deliberately misread the figures to justify the change
   C. a rise in borrowing after the change does not show why it rose
   D. heated reading rooms attract more borrowers than books do

2. Which finding would most strengthen the officials' conclusion that longer hours caused the rise?
   A. A similar library with unchanged hours saw no rise during the same weeks
   B. Most of the new visitors came to use the heated reading room
   C. The school's group project was assigned at several neighboring libraries as well
   D. Officials had judged the old hours too short before the change

3. The author's attitude toward the program's headline figure is best described as
   A. scornful: the figure has almost certainly been invented by the directors
   B. cautious: it may be right, but it does not yet show what is implied
   C. enthusiastic: it proves the program works
   D. indifferent: its accuracy does not matter

4. The author mentions students who drop out of the program in order to
   A. show that the program is too demanding for most of the students enrolled
   B. argue that completers did not really improve their grades
   C. suggest that the directors have hidden the dropout rate on purpose
   D. identify a difference between the groups unrelated to the program

5. Which new piece of evidence would the author find most convincing?
   A. A larger collection of testimonials from students who completed the program
   B. A report of the average number of hours that each completer attended the sessions
   C. Grade changes for all enrollees, against equivalent waiting-list applicants
   D. A statement from the directors that the figure was carefully and fairly calculated

6. Which situation would the author's account most likely predict to produce the strongest lasting bond?
   A. Two neighbors who trade favors for years and never agree when any is to be repaid
   B. Two merchants who settle every debt in full at the end of each month
   C. A neighbor who announces the value of each favor and the date by which it must be repaid
   D. A buyer who pays a stranger exactly the stated price

7. The author describes the cynics' view in order to
   A. endorse the view that gift-givers are being dishonest about their motives
   B. show that anthropologists disagree about almost everything they study
   C. prove that givers expect repayment on a fixed schedule
   D. present a reading that the author says overlooks what the denial does

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** She grants a rise but argues that the comparison cannot separate the hours from the school assignment. (A goes beyond the passage, since she concedes the hours may well have helped; B is unsupported; the passage says only that they took the rise as proof; D uses passage details but changes the point, because the reading-room visitors borrowed nothing.)
2. **A.** A comparison library without the change but with the same school assignment would isolate the effect of the hours, which is the comparison the passage says is missing. (B is consistent with the sociologist and says nothing about why borrowing rose; C supplies a rival cause, so it weakens the officials' claim; D is irrelevant, because a prior opinion is not evidence of cause.)
3. **B.** The author accepts that the figure may be true but explains why it fails to demonstrate the program's effect. (A is unsupported, because the author calls it "impressive" and never alleges fabrication; C reverses the author, who says it is "a promise rather than a result"; D is contradicted by the author's detailed concern about how the figure was produced.)
4. **D.** Dropouts show how the group that finishes has been filtered, so the comparison partly measures the filter. (A is unsupported, as the author gives no figure for how many leave; B is too extreme, since the author questions the comparison, not the improvement; C attributes a motive that the passage never mentions.)
5. **C.** The author's own suggestion is to report on all enrollees or to compare them with an equivalent waiting-list group. (A repeats the same filtered group and so does not address the author's concern; B describes effort among completers and does not compare them with anyone else; D gives assurance rather than evidence about the comparison.)
6. **A.** The passage says that leaving the return vague keeps the parties in each other's debt and builds a lasting tie. (B would, on the author's view, be conducting trade, which ends when the account is settled; C makes the exchange look like a sale, which the passage says ends the tie; D describes a sale and creates no continuing debt.)
7. **D.** The cynical reading is introduced, then answered with the claim that the fiction keeps the ledger open. (A reverses the author, who treats the denial as a device with a function; B is too extreme and unsupported, since only one disagreement is described; C contradicts the passage, which says fixed repayment would turn the gift into a sale.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, sort each miss by cause: misread the claim type, missed a rival explanation, ignored selection, or stretched the scope. Log it in your error log ([Strategy: Reviewing a CARS Practice Exam](chapter:20) shows how).
