# Lesson 1: Main Idea, Scope and Structure

**What you will be able to do**

- State the main idea of a passage as a complete sentence with a subject, a verb and a point, not as a topic label.
- Define the scope of a passage (what it covers and what it leaves alone) and use scope to reject answer choices that are too broad, too narrow or off to one side.
- Map the structure of a passage paragraph by paragraph, naming the job each paragraph does.
- Answer main-idea, primary-purpose, organization and function-of-detail questions from your map, returning to the text only when a question asks for a detail.

**Why it matters for the exam**

CARS is understood to contain 53 questions in 90 minutes, built on about nine passages of 500 to 600 words, with roughly five to seven questions per passage [VERIFY: confirm the current format on the exam owner's free format page]. The public description groups the questions into three skill families: foundations of comprehension, reasoning within the text, and reasoning beyond the text, with approximate shares of 30, 30 and 40 percent [VERIFY]. This lesson is the first of the three families. If you cannot say what a passage is for and how it is put together, every later skill (inference, application, strengthening and weakening) is built on sand.

The method here extends the habits from [Strategy: The Active Reading Method](chapter:2) and the question types in [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3). It also assumes you have met the basic paragraph-mapping routine in Core Test Skills, Chapter 15. No outside knowledge is required for any passage in this book, and none is required here.

Praxis Tip: Treat every passage as a short argument in a hurry. Someone had a point to make and about 550 words in which to make it. Your first job is to find the point, which is a good deal easier than finding the point of most meetings.

**Concept Review**

**Topic, main idea and scope are three different things.**

- The **topic** is what the passage is about, in a few words: "lighthouse logbooks."
- The **main idea** is what the author says about the topic: "Lighthouse logbooks, long dismissed as dull, are valuable evidence precisely because they are routine." A main idea is a full sentence. If your summary has no verb, it is still a topic.
- The **scope** is the boundary of the discussion: which period, which people, which kind of evidence, which question. The logbook passage is about nineteenth-century coastal logbooks as historical evidence. It is not about lighthouse architecture, and it is not about all historical records.

**Where the main idea lives.** In many passages the author states it outright, often after a pivot. Look in three places: the end of the first paragraph (the thesis), the sentence that follows a contrast word such as *yet*, *but* or *however*, and the last paragraph (the conclusion). In other passages the main idea is never stated in one sentence, and you build it by combining the paragraph jobs. Either way, the main idea must account for the whole passage, including the last paragraph.

**The three-move method for a main-idea sentence.**

1. Name the topic.
2. Name the author's move: argues, explains, defends, corrects, compares, reconciles, warns.
3. Add the qualification the author attaches (the "only," "mostly," "in some cases," "but not").

The third move matters more than it looks. Authors on this kind of exam are rarely absolute, and the correct main idea usually preserves their qualification.

**Scope and the five ways a choice goes wrong.** When you test a choice against the passage, ask whether it fits the passage's boundary exactly. Choices that fail usually fail in one of these ways:

| Trap | What it does | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| Too broad | Covers far more than the passage does | Could this sentence head a different passage? |
| Too narrow | Describes one example or one paragraph | Does it ignore the last paragraph? |
| Off to one side | Uses the topic but asks a different question | Is it about the same claim? |
| Reversal | States the opposite of the author's view | Does the author say this in her own voice? |
| Too extreme | Adds "always," "never," "only," "must" | Does the text support that strength? |

Two further traps appear all the time. A choice may be **true but not the main idea** (a true detail, promoted). Or it may **borrow the passage's words but change their meaning**, which makes it look safe to a tired reader.

Praxis Tip: When two choices both look plausible for a main-idea question, ask which one could replace the passage's title. The one that is merely a true statement from the middle of the passage will not fit as a title.

**Structure: what each paragraph is doing.** Each paragraph has a job. Common jobs are: set up a common view; define a term; give an example; raise an objection; concede a point; reply; qualify; draw a consequence; conclude. Write the job in about five words on scratch paper (remember that you cannot write in this book). A few shapes recur:

- **Common view, then correction.** The author sets out what "most people say," then explains why it is incomplete or wrong. The pivot word is the signal.
- **Claim, objection, reply.** A thesis is stated, a challenge is raised, and the author answers it.
- **Problem, then proposal.** A difficulty is described and a remedy is argued for.
- **Comparison.** Two things are set side by side to show a difference or a shared pattern.
- **Example to principle.** A case is described, and the author draws the general lesson from it.
- **Chronology with a thesis.** A sequence of events narrated in order to support a claim about change.

Knowing the shape lets you predict. When you see "Some people say" at the start, expect a reply. When you see a long example, expect a principle.

**The question types this lesson covers.**

- **Main idea / primary purpose:** "The passage is primarily concerned with..." or "The main point of the passage is..." Purpose stems ask what the author is *doing* (arguing, describing, correcting); main-idea stems ask what the author *concludes*. The correct choices usually line up.
- **Organization:** "Which best describes the structure of the passage?" Use your map of paragraph jobs.
- **Function of a detail:** "The author mentions the fountain bench in order to..." Do not ask what the detail says. Ask what job it does in the argument, usually illustrating or supporting a nearby claim.
- **Scope:** "Which of the following would the passage be least likely to discuss?" or "The passage does NOT address which of the following?" Test each choice against what is actually said.

Praxis Tip: For function-of-detail questions, find the sentence just before or after the detail. The detail usually serves the claim sitting next to it.

**Worked Example 1: Mapping a passage and finding the main idea (skill: topic, move, qualification)**

*Passage.*

(1) For most of the nineteenth century, the logbooks of coastal lighthouses were treated as dull paperwork: columns of wind directions, lamp-oil quantities and passing ships. Historians of the sea mostly ignored them in favor of captains' diaries, which promised drama.

(2) Yet the keepers' entries, precisely because they were routine, offer something the diaries cannot. A captain wrote when something happened; a keeper wrote every night, including the nights when nothing did. Taken together, thousands of such ordinary entries reveal patterns of traffic, weather and seasonal rhythm that no single dramatic voyage could show. The logbooks' dullness, in other words, is not a defect in the evidence but the source of its value.

*Map.* (1) Old view: logbooks dull; historians prefer diaries. (2) Pivot at "Yet": routine is the logbooks' strength; contrast with diaries; conclusion about value.

*Three moves.* Topic: lighthouse logbooks as evidence. Move: corrects the neglect by arguing for their value. Qualification: their value comes from the very routine that made them seem dull.

*Main idea.* Lighthouse logbooks, long overlooked as dull, are valuable historical evidence because their routine nature records patterns that dramatic accounts miss.

Notice the pivot. Paragraph 1 describes a view the author is about to reject. A reader who stops at the end of paragraph 1 would conclude the passage is about dull records. The author's point arrives after "Yet."

**Worked Example 2: Ranking choices by scope (skill: too broad, too narrow, off to one side)**

Use the logbook passage. Which statement is the best main idea, and why is each of the others wrong?

A. Historical records of the sea are valuable to scholars.
B. Keepers recorded lamp-oil quantities every night.
C. Lighthouse logbooks, though long dismissed as dull, are valuable because they are routine.
D. Captains' diaries are less trustworthy than the logs kept by lighthouse keepers.

*Reasoning.* **C** fits the topic, the author's move and the qualification. **A** is too broad: it could head a passage on any maritime archive, and it loses the paradox that dullness is the value. **B** is too narrow: it is a detail from the first sentence and ignores paragraph 2. **D** is off to one side and extreme: the passage contrasts diaries and logbooks on what they capture, never on trustworthiness.

**Worked Example 3: Function of a detail (skill: what job does this sentence do?)**

*Passage.*

(1) Urban planners tend to treat public benches as an afterthought, a line in the budget somewhere below paving and above flower beds. The usual justification is comfort: tired walkers need a place to sit.

(2) That justification undersells the bench. Consider a bench placed at a bus stop and one placed beside a fountain at the far end of a square. The first is used by people waiting; the second, remarkably, is used by people who have come for no purpose except to sit. Strangers on such benches begin to nod to one another, and over time a loose acquaintance forms among people who would never otherwise meet. The bench, then, is less a convenience for individuals than a small device for producing neighbors.

*Question.* The author mentions the bench beside the fountain primarily in order to

A. show that comfort is the only reason people use benches
B. illustrate that benches can create social contact beyond mere comfort
C. argue that fountains should be built in every square
D. criticize planners for ignoring bus stops

*Reasoning.* Map: (1) benches treated as comfort items, an afterthought; (2) pivot: benches do more, with a contrast example. The fountain bench is used by people with no purpose except to sit, and the sentence that follows describes strangers becoming acquaintances. Its job is to illustrate the author's claim that benches produce neighbors. **B**. **A** reverses the claim ("only" and "comfort"). **C** is out of scope; the fountain is only part of an illustration. **D** uses the planners from paragraph 1 but gives them a criticism the passage does not make about bus stops.

Praxis Tip: In function questions, the wrong choices are often true statements about the detail's content. You are choosing the job, not the content.

**Worked Example 4: Scope with a qualification (skill: the "only," "mostly," "in part")**

*Passage.* Critics of writing have long warned that anyone who can write things down will stop remembering them. The charge is not baseless: a person who keeps a phone number in a notebook rarely learns it. But the charge is aimed at the wrong target. What written records relieve is the memory of arbitrary facts: numbers, dates, lists. What they cannot relieve, and indeed demand more of, is the memory of how things connect. A reader who has stored nothing in his head cannot follow a page of argument, since each sentence depends on the ones before it. Writing, then, moves memory rather than abolishing it, from the storehouse toward the thread.

*Question.* Which statement best expresses the main point?

A. Writing has weakened human memory, as its critics claim.
B. Writing shifts the burden of memory from isolated facts toward connections rather than eliminating it.
C. Readers who store nothing in their heads can still follow arguments.
D. Memory for numbers, dates and lists is not important.

*Reasoning.* Three moves: topic, writing and memory; move, answers critics; qualification, the critics are partly right (arbitrary facts) but wrong about the whole. **B** carries that. **A** accepts the critics' charge in full, which the author calls "aimed at the wrong target." **C** reverses a sentence of the passage. **D** is extreme; the passage says only that written records relieve memory of these things, not that they are unimportant. Note that "The charge is not baseless" is a concession: the author's view includes a partial agreement. Choices that drop the concession tend to be too extreme.

**Worked Example 5: Purpose verbs and structure (skill: matching what the author does to the shape of the passage)**

Return to the bench passage. Which of these best states the author's purpose?

- *to describe* how benches are positioned in squares (too narrow; ignores the claim),
- *to argue* that benches deserve more attention because they foster community (fits the pivot and the conclusion),
- *to complain* about the budget priorities of city planners (too strong; budget placement appears in the opening only as an illustration of neglect).

The structure is a common view (benches provide comfort), then a pivot with a contrast example, then a conclusion. The author's verb is *argues*. If a passage shows an argumentative shape, the primary purpose will be an argumentative verb: argues, defends, challenges, corrects. If the shape is chronological or enumerative with no pivot, the verb is likelier to be *explains* or *describes*.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Do not accept the first sentence as the main idea merely because first sentences are tidy. Passages on this kind of exam like to open with the view they plan to argue against.

Praxis Tip: After finishing the passage, say the main idea aloud, in one sentence, before you look at any question. If you cannot, read the first and last paragraphs again. A main idea you built yourself is much harder to trade away for a tempting wrong answer.

Praxis Tip: Underline mentally (on your scratch paper, since the page is not yours to deface) every contrast word. The author's own position tends to sit right after the last one.

Praxis Tip: A choice with the word "only" or "always" has to earn it. Check the text for a sentence that says as much. Usually it does not.

Praxis Tip: When a main-idea choice mentions only the first half of the passage, it is too narrow. When it would fit a dozen other passages, it is too broad. The right answer is a snug fit and a little dull.

Praxis Tip: Do not use outside knowledge to decide what the passage "really means." The passage may be wrong about the world, but you are being asked what it says.

**Common Mistakes**

- Writing a topic ("the history of logbooks") when the question wants a claim.
- Stopping at the end of paragraph 1 and mistaking the view the author is about to reject for her own.
- Choosing a true detail because it is vivid, instead of the idea that organizes the whole passage.
- Choosing an answer that is too broad because it sounds safe.
- Dropping the author's qualification, which turns a measured view into an extreme one.
- Answering a function question by restating what the detail says rather than what it does.
- Assuming the passage's structure must be chronological because it contains dates.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. *Passage.* Museum labels are usually written as if visitors had already decided what to think. "A masterpiece of restraint," says one; "a landmark of the period," says another. Such labels do their work before the visitor has looked. A better label would ask a question the object can answer (why is the left hand unfinished?) and then leave the visitor to look for the answer. Looking, after all, is what the visitor came for, and a label that does the looking in advance leaves the visitor with nothing to do but agree.

   Which statement best expresses the main idea of the passage?
   A. Museums should remove their labels altogether so that visitors are left to look in peace.
   B. Labels would serve visitors better by prompting looking than by announcing verdicts.
   C. Visitors seldom read museum labels, and a masterpiece of restraint needs no explanation.
   D. Labels that describe an object as a landmark of its period are inaccurate.

2. *Passage.* Most of what a shopper pays for a loaf of bread goes to milling, baking, packaging and transport, so even a doubling in the price of wheat raises the price of bread only a little. Commentators who blame the price of bread on wheat harvests are therefore explaining a small part of the price and treating it as the whole.

   The primary purpose of the passage is to
   A. predict how the price of bread will change after next year's harvest
   B. defend commentators who stress the importance of wheat harvests
   C. describe the stages by which wheat becomes bread and reaches the shopper
   D. correct a familiar explanation by showing how little of the price it covers

3. *Passage.* Letters written by soldiers in one regiment during a long winter siege rarely mention fear. A historian argues that this silence is not courage but convention: the letters were read by officers, and writers learned what could be said. To read the silence as bravery is to mistake a rule for a feeling.

   Which statement best captures the historian's main point without going beyond the passage?
   A. The letters' silence about fear reflects what could be said, not necessarily what was felt.
   B. Letters are unreliable as historical evidence of any kind, whether from war or from peacetime.
   C. The soldiers of the regiment felt no fear at any point during the long winter siege.
   D. Officers in the regiment read the soldiers' letters before they were sent home.

4. *Passage.* Early cinemas were built to resemble theaters, with curtains, boxes and gilt. The reason was social. Theater was respectable and the new medium was not, so owners borrowed the look of the stage to borrow its standing. In one such cinema a curtain rose before every film, though there was nothing behind it but a screen.

   The author mentions the curtain that rose before every film primarily to
   A. argue that cinema screens were less impressive than the sets used on a theater stage
   B. explain why cinemas eventually abandoned the theater style for plainer buildings
   C. show how far cinemas imitated the stage to seem respectable
   D. suggest that the curtain was a practical necessity for projecting films onto a screen

5. *Passage.* It is often said that street names are merely labels, as arbitrary as serial numbers. There is something in this: nobody consults the history of Mill Lane to find the mill. But names accumulate meanings the labeler never intended. A street called New Road for two centuries becomes a small joke; one named for a forgotten benefactor becomes a prompt to ask who he was. Names are arbitrary at the start and anything but by the end.

   Which best describes the organization of the passage?
   A. A term is defined and then illustrated by a single extended example.
   B. Two competing theories are described, and one is rejected on the basis of an experiment.
   C. A common view is partly granted, then replaced by a fuller one.
   D. Events are narrated in chronological order and a prediction is made about future street names.

6. *Passage.* (1) The conductor is often pictured as the person who makes the sound. (2) In rehearsal, however, most of what a conductor does is subtractive: asking the brass to play less, the strings to wait, the percussion to hold back. The sound already exists; the conductor decides what to leave out.

   The second paragraph serves mainly to
   A. challenge the first paragraph's picture by describing what conductors do
   B. provide further evidence that the conductor is the person who makes the sound
   C. explain why brass players are the most difficult section of an orchestra to rehearse
   D. define the technical vocabulary that conductors use during rehearsal

7. *Passage.* Public libraries are sometimes said to be obsolete because information is free online. For retrieving a fact, that is largely so. But a library has always offered more than facts: a quiet room, a librarian who can tell a good source from a bad one, and the sight of other people reading. A search engine duplicates none of these, and some patrons need them more than ever.

   Which of the following topics is NOT addressed in the passage?
   A. Whether facts can be retrieved easily online
   B. The cost to a community of running a library
   C. The role of librarians in judging sources
   D. The value of seeing other people read

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** The author argues that labels should invite looking instead of pre-judging, and the qualifying move is "a better label" rather than no label. (A goes beyond the passage, which wants better labels, not none; C is unsupported, since the passage never says visitors seldom read labels, and it borrows a sample label phrase as if it were the author's point; D is a narrow detail about two sample phrases and never questions their accuracy.)
2. **D.** The passage names a common view and then argues that it is incomplete; its verb is corrects. (A is out of scope; the passage makes no prediction; B reverses the author's stance toward those commentators; C is a detail used as evidence, not the author's purpose.)
3. **A.** The historian's claim is about this set of letters and about reading silence as evidence of bravery. It is qualified ("not necessarily"). (B is too broad; the passage concerns one regiment's letters; C reverses the historian, who denies that silence shows courage; D is true but too narrow; it is a premise, not the conclusion.)
4. **C.** The curtain is an example of the claim in the previous sentences, an imitation of theater for its prestige. (A is unsupported; the passage makes no comparison of impressiveness; B is out of scope; abandonment is not discussed; D reverses the point, since the curtain concealed nothing and had no practical purpose.)
5. **C.** The passage opens with a view ("it is often said"), concedes part of it ("there is something in this"), pivots at "But," and gives examples. (A describes a pattern the passage does not follow, since it uses two brief examples and a pivot; B is out of scope; no experiment or formal theory appears; D imposes a chronology and a prediction that the passage never offers.)
6. **A.** "However" signals a pivot, and the paragraph replaces the common picture with an account of the conductor as an editor. (B reverses the paragraph, which says the sound already exists; C is out of scope and an unsupported inference from one example; D is not what the paragraph does; it uses no definitions.)
7. **B.** The passage never mentions funding or cost; each other choice is explicitly covered. (A is addressed in the concession ("largely so"); C is addressed when the passage mentions a librarian who can tell good sources from bad; D is addressed in the list of things a library offers.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, label each wrong choice with its trap from the table (too broad, too narrow, off to one side, reversal, too extreme) and log the one you chose in your error log (see Core Test Skills, Chapter 9, and [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3)).

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "The topic is what a passage is about, the main idea is what the author says about it, and the scope is the boundary of the discussion; I find the main idea by naming the topic, the author's move and the author's qualification, I map each paragraph's job, and I reject choices that are too broad, too narrow, off to one side, reversed or too extreme."

*Review schedule:* revisit this lesson after 1 day (redo the logbook map without looking), after 3 days (the seven checkpoint questions from a blank page), after 7 days (map two new passages and write a main idea for each), and after 21 days (explain the five traps aloud).

*Interleave:* pair this lesson with [Lesson 2](chapter:5) on tone and [Lesson 3](chapter:6) on argument, since main idea questions often turn on whether a view is the author's own. When you are ready for timed practice, begin with [Practice: Passage Set 1](chapter:12).

Praxis Tip: End each study session by writing the main idea of that session's passage in one sentence, then underline its verb. If there is no verb, you have written a topic. Try again.
