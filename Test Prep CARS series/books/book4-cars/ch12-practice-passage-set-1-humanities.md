**Passage 1 (questions 1-5)**

Readers who distrust translated poetry often say that the original is lost on the way between languages, as though the translator had been handed a full cup and spilled some of it in transit. The picture is tidy, and it is mistaken. It assumes that a poem is a substance that arrives whole or does not arrive. A poem is better described as a set of instructions for an experience: it tells a reader where to pause, which sound to linger on, which word to be surprised by. Instructions cannot be poured from one cup into another; they can only be carried out again, in a different material.

Consider what happens when a line depends on a rhyme that the second language cannot supply. The literal translator keeps the meaning and abandons the music, and the result is accurate in the way a floor plan is accurate: everything is present except the feeling of being inside the house. The imitative translator does the reverse, inventing new images to preserve the sound, and is accused of betrayal. Both accusations rest on the same premise, that fidelity means keeping some separable part of the poem intact. If fidelity is instead measured by the experience produced, neither translator is simply faithful or unfaithful. Each has judged which effect matters most in a given line and has paid for it with another.

This does not mean that anything goes. A translator who turns a restrained elegy into a lament full of exclamation points has not made a bold choice; she has misread the instructions. The test is whether a reader who knew the original well would recognize, in the new poem, the same sort of experience: the same pace, the same coolness or heat, the same turn near the end. Such recognition is a demanding standard, because it requires the translator to read with unusual care and to write with unusual restraint. Nobody pretends that many translations meet it, and the difficulty of the standard is a reason to take it seriously, not to abandon it. A bad translation is a failure of that kind of reading and writing, and it is the failure we should be trying to describe.

It follows that the best translations are rarely the most self-effacing. A version that tries to vanish, offering a transparent window onto the original, usually produces flat English and calls the flatness loyalty. The translators who succeed tend to behave like conductors: they do not compose the score, but no one would say they merely transmit it. Their interpretation is visible, and it is also answerable to something outside themselves.

A reader who accepts this view gains something practical. She stops asking whether a translation is the poem and asks instead what kind of performance it gives, and she becomes willing to read two or three versions side by side, each revealing what the others chose to sacrifice. The poem, on this view, is not found in any single rendering. It is approached by triangulation, and the approach is itself a pleasure. Readers who care about a poem in another language have, in the end, no better option, and no reason to look for one.

**Passage 2 (questions 6-11)**

Every season a reviewer announces, with the air of someone reporting a rescue, that a new novel is "deeply relatable." The word has become the highest compliment in the trade, which is odd, because it describes the book's usefulness to the reader and says nothing about the book. A hammer is relatable, in this sense: it suits the hand that holds it. The standard is easy to defend, and its defenders are not fools. A story that no one can enter, they say, is a story no one will read, and an art that is not read is a private hobby. They add that the great novelists of any period knew perfectly well whom they were writing for. Writers have always sought the reader's sympathy; why should critics be embarrassed to praise success?

The difficulty is what the word measures. To find a character relatable is to recognize something already in oneself: the same disappointment, the same sort of kitchen, the same small cowardice at the same sort of dinner. Nothing is risked and nothing is learned. The reader returns from such a book unchanged but flattered, like a traveler who has crossed an ocean to find a hotel identical to the one at home. Whatever pleasure this gives is real. But a literature judged chiefly by recognition will drift toward the already recognized, because publishers can predict what readers already contain and cannot predict what they might become.

Consider the opposite experience, which most serious readers can recall. One begins a novel about a person whose motives are opaque, whose habits seem strange, whose sense of honor appears to belong to another century. For fifty pages the reader is irritated. Then, somewhere, the irritation turns into curiosity, and the reader catches herself reasoning as the character reasons. Nothing about this reader was "relatable" in advance; the book made the relation. That is a harder achievement than mirroring, and a rarer one, and it is exactly the achievement the new vocabulary has no room to praise.

It would be unfair to claim that relatable books are bad. Some are excellent, and their excellence usually has nothing to do with being relatable. A critic who writes that an accomplished novel is relatable has, in effect, noticed the least interesting thing about it and mistaken that for the whole. The remedy is not to ban the word, which would only make it glamorous, but to ask a follow-up question every time it appears: relatable to whom, and at what cost to whoever is not that person?

None of this is a plea for difficulty as such. Obscurity is as easy to manufacture as comfort, and a book that baffles is not thereby profound. The aim is a critical language able to distinguish the book that confirms a reader from the book that enlarges her, and honest enough to say which of the two it is praising. A review that did so would be longer and less quotable than a review of one word, and it would be worth more.

**Passage 3 (questions 12-16)**

Suppose a painting hangs in a museum for a century, admired by thousands, until researchers establish that it was painted last week by a skilled imitator. Nothing about the canvas has changed. The colors are what they were, and visitors standing before it would see exactly what they saw yesterday. Yet most people feel that something has been lost, and museums remove such works from the wall, usually with some embarrassment. The puzzle is why. If the value of a painting lay wholly in how it looks, the discovery would be irrelevant. Three answers are worth considering.

The first answer holds that the value was never in appearance alone. On this view, a painting is also an act: the record of a particular person solving particular problems at a particular time. To admire a painting is partly to admire what someone did. The forgery displays the same surface but conceals a different act, and because the admiration was directed at the act, it has lost its object. The strength of this account is that it explains why we are not disturbed by honest copies, openly labeled; nobody is deceived about which act they are looking at. Its weakness is that it seems to make our pleasure depend on information we cannot perceive, and many people doubt that a pleasure so dependent was ever aesthetic.

The second answer is blunter. It says the lost value is not aesthetic at all but economic and historical. A painting by a famous hand is a rare object, a witness to its period, and a unique piece of property; a forgery is none of these things. The viewer's disappointment is then the disappointment of someone who has been sold a lesser product. This account is simple and has the virtue of not inflating the mystery. But it struggles with the fact that people who will never own the painting, and who care nothing for its price, still feel the loss.

The third answer treats the feeling as a mistake, a leftover from the habit of valuing things by their origin. If two paintings give the same experience, it says, preferring one is no more rational than preferring a signed glass of water to an unsigned one. A viewer who understood this would be untroubled by the discovery. The difficulty is that the view asks us to give up a response that seems to run through nearly all of our valuing, including our valuing of people, whom we do not regard as interchangeable with perfect duplicates.

None of the three accounts is complete, and they are not mutually exclusive. The first captures something about the reasons viewers give; the second something about the institutions museums run; the third something about what a purely visual standard would require. A fair conclusion is modest: the forgery case does not show that appearance is irrelevant, but it does show that it is rarely the only thing we are valuing when we value a work of art.

**Answers and explanations**

1. **D.** The first paragraph replaces the picture of a spilled cup with the picture of instructions carried out again, and the remaining paragraphs build on that replacement. [Foundations] (A reverses the author's position: the author denies that keeping the meaning alone amounts to fidelity; B is too extreme: the author argues that translation is possible, and says that some translators succeed; C contradicts paragraph 4, which says that self-effacing versions tend to produce flat English.)
2. **D.** The sentence says that in a floor plan 'everything is present except the feeling of being inside the house,' which is the author's description of what the literal translator loses. [Foundations] (A reverses the point: the floor plan is accurate, and the loss lies elsewhere; B borrows the architectural image but changes the meaning: the author says the structure is not the experience; C is unsupported: the passage says nothing about which readers prefer which kind of translation.)
3. **C.** Paragraph 2 says that if fidelity is measured by the experience produced, neither the literal nor the imitative translator is simply faithful or unfaithful, so an imitative version can be faithful if it produces the right experience. [Within] (A reverses paragraph 2, where the author rejects the premise that fidelity means keeping a separable part intact; B is too extreme: paragraph 3 begins by saying that anything does not go; D reverses paragraph 4, which says the successful translators' interpretation is visible.)
4. **B.** The final sentence of the paragraph states the point directly: the interpretation 'is visible, and it is also answerable to something outside themselves.' [Within] (A is too extreme: the conductor 'does not compose the score'; C reverses the claim: 'no one would say they merely transmit it'; D is out of scope: the passage never discusses how translators are trained.)
5. **A.** The ornate version resembles the elegy turned into a lament: a misreading of pace and tone, judged by whether a knowledgeable reader recognizes the original's experience. [Beyond] (B is true but misapplied: the author separates considered sacrifices from misreadings of the poem's instructions; C relies on a standard the author rejects, that preserving meaning alone is enough; D twists the final paragraph, which says no single version contains the poem, not that versions cannot be judged.)
6. **A.** The author grants that the standard has defenders who 'are not fools' and that the pleasure 'is real,' but calls recognition a poor measure of what a book achieves. [Foundations] (B is too extreme: paragraph 4 says some relatable books are excellent, and paragraph 5 says difficulty is no virtue in itself; C ignores the author's repeated objections to what the word measures; D is unsupported: the author cares enough to propose a remedy.)
7. **B.** The sentence before the hammer says that the word 'describes the book's usefulness to the reader and says nothing about the book,' and the hammer illustrates that. [Foundations] (A uses the image but changes the meaning: the author is criticizing, not recommending, a standard of usefulness; C contradicts the text, which says the defenders are not fools; D is unsupported: the comparison is offered as a criticism of the vocabulary.)
8. **D.** The reason is given in the last sentence of paragraph 2, introduced by 'because.' [Within] (A is unsupported: no such loss is claimed; B is too strong: the passage says only that such books are harder to predict; C is not stated: the passage criticizes the vocabulary of reviews, not a refusal to review.)
9. **C.** Paragraph 1 states the standard and its defense, paragraphs 2 and 3 argue the limitation with an opposite experience, and paragraphs 4 and 5 qualify and propose the remedy. [Within] (A misdescribes the passage, which has one extended contrasting example and offers no test or confirmation; B is out of scope: the passage makes no historical claims; D is wrong because the author sides against one view and does not reconcile it with another.)

[Beyond] (B would strengthen the claim by showing that familiarity attracts readers; C is irrelevant to the drift in contemporary writing, since it concerns only how classics are described; D is irrelevant: it concerns spending on promotion, not the familiarity of what is published.)

[Within] (B is a strength claimed for the first answer, not a difficulty for the second; C is a fact the puzzle starts from, which no answer is said to struggle with; D reverses the text, which says the second answer has the virtue of not inflating the mystery.)
14. **B.** On the first answer, admiration is partly directed at the act of solving problems; a hidden tracing replaces that act with a different one while the surface looks the same. **A model passage map (Passage 2)**

Here is one way to map the passage on scratch paper, described in words. Paragraph 1: the author announces that the compliment "relatable" is everywhere and says it measures the book's usefulness to the reader. Note the concession: its defenders are not fools, and their argument is that unreadable art is a private hobby. Paragraph 2: the author's first objection. Recognition is flattering but changes no one, and a literature judged by it will drift toward what is already known. Paragraph 3: the contrasting example, the opaque character who wins the reader over; the author's thesis lives in the sentence "the book made the relation." Paragraph 4: the qualification (relatable books can be excellent) and the remedy (ask "relatable to whom?"). Paragraph 5: a second qualification (difficulty is not virtue), ending on the criterion the author actually wants: the book that confirms versus the book that enlarges. In the margins of your map, put a star beside the sentence "That is a harder achievement than mirroring" as the author's central evaluation, and a note beside the hammer, marking it as an illustration only. With this map in hand, Question 6 comes from the whole shape, Question 7 from the first paragraph, Question 8 from the second, Question 9 from the overall order of paragraphs, and Questions 10 and 11 from paragraphs 2, 3 and 5 together.

Praxis Tip: After you finish a passage, spend ten seconds writing a one-sentence main idea before you look at question 1. If the first question is a main-idea question and your sentence matches the right answer, you have confirmed your map; if it does not, you have found the problem before it cost you anything.

Praxis Tip: Notice how often a wrong answer was true, or at least plausible, and wrong for the question. Several of the wrong choices in Questions 3 and 5 sound sensible. None of them answers what was asked. Before you pick, say the question's job aloud in a few words ("what the author accepts," "what the author would say about this case"), and let only the choice that does that job survive.

Praxis Tip: In Beyond questions that ask you to strengthen or weaken, first name the claim being tested in your own words, then ask which direction each choice pushes. Choices that push the wrong way, or do not push at all, are the usual traps, and the careful reader notices that "supports a different claim" is a way of being wrong.

Praxis Tip: If a passage has made you cross, amused or bored, say so in the margin of your map, because tone is a form of information. Authors whose irony you have missed will be taken for sincere ones, and the answer choices at the other end of the scale are written for exactly that reader.
