**Passage 1 (questions 1-6)**

Ask someone today what privacy is and the answer will probably involve information: who may know what about me, and who may collect, keep or sell it. This feels like the natural meaning of the word, but it is a recent one. For most of the history of settled households, privacy was a matter of space. A person was private to the degree that a wall, a door or a distance stood between that person and others. In a one-room dwelling shared among parents, children, servants and sometimes animals, there was little to be had, and the thought that one might want more of it was slow to appear. Historians of domestic architecture note that the arrival of hallways and rooms with single purposes made it possible, for many families, to be alone indoors without leaving the house.

A second stage came when the idea moved from rooms to persons. As towns grew and strangers began to live beside one another, people acquired a new worry: not that others might stand too close, but that they might know too much. Privacy became a claim against intrusion, against the opened letter and the eavesdropper and, eventually, the curious newspaper. It came to be described as a right, and the right was defended as a protection of the self from public view. Notice what stayed constant. The threat was still someone gaining access, and the remedy was still a barrier, now of law and custom instead of timber.

The present stage differs in a way that is easy to miss. Because information about a person is now gathered continuously and mostly without anyone's presence, the barrier model fits poorly. There is no door to close on a record of one's purchases. Some theorists have responded by defining privacy as control: the ability to decide how information about oneself flows. This is an advance on the barrier picture, but it has a cost. Control suggests that a person who has consented has lost nothing, and it treats each disclosure as a transaction between an individual and a collector. Yet much of what people care about concerns context rather than consent. A person may tell a physician what she would never tell an employer, and neither listener has done wrong by hearing it. The wrong comes when the information moves from one setting to another, where its meaning changes.

I do not claim that this history is a march toward a final definition. Each stage kept something of the one before it. Architecture still matters, since a person denied any private room is diminished; the right against intrusion still matters; and control is sometimes exactly what one should ask for. The lesson is rather that the word has been repeatedly stretched to cover a new threat, and that each stretching revealed assumptions in the old definition that had gone unnoticed, because the old threat had never tested them. If this is right, we should expect the present definition to prove inadequate in its turn, and we should be wary of any account that presents itself as the natural meaning of privacy rather than the latest one.

**Passage 2 (questions 7-12)**

The redemption memoir follows a shape so familiar that its readers could recite it. A life goes badly wrong, through illness, addiction, poverty or loss; a turning point arrives; and the writer emerges, wiser and usually better off, to report that the suffering was worth having. Its popularity is easy to explain. Readers who are themselves struggling find company in it, and publishers find in it the promise of an ending. I do not wish to sneer at either. My complaint is narrower: that the genre, by its very form, cannot tell the truth about the experiences it most wants to honor.

The difficulty is the ending. A memoir written from the far side of recovery must arrange earlier events so that they lead to it. Episodes that led nowhere are trimmed; the dull years in the middle are compressed; people who were merely in the way become obstacles, or guides. Motives are tidied, and doubts that lasted for years are settled in a sentence. The writer is not lying. Hindsight does this to everyone's memory, and a reader should not expect otherwise. But the result is that suffering appears in the book as raw material, a stage that was necessary and so was justified by what it produced. Readers learn, without being told, that pain is a kind of investment. That is a doctrine, not an observation, and many sufferers have good reason to resist it, particularly those whose lives did not end in a turning point.

Defenders reply that the genre gives hope, and that hope is not a small thing. This is a fair point, and I take it seriously. But hope offered through a pattern has a price: it implies that those who do not recover in the pattern's way have failed to supply their half of the story. Readers whose illness is chronic, or whose losses were never repaired, are handed a vocabulary in which their lives are unfinished books. A genre that comforts the people it resembles may thus quietly reproach the people it does not.

It would be unfair to suggest that no memoir escapes. Some of the best refuse the final chapter: they end in the middle of the ordeal, or they report the turning point and then report that it changed less than anyone expected. Such books are often less comforting, and they sell fewer copies than their tidier rivals. What they offer instead is the thing the genre promises but cannot deliver, an account in which suffering is permitted to be only what it was. It is telling that readers who write to the authors of such books say, more often than not, not that they felt cheered but that they felt believed.

The redemption memoir will not disappear, and it should not. But a reader who knows its shape reads it differently, as a made thing and not as a transcript, and learns to ask what was left on the floor during the trimming.

**Passage 3 (questions 13-18)**

Two researchers set out to learn whether people who live under cooperative institutions are more willing to trust strangers. They recruited 240 adults from twelve settlements in a river valley. Six of the settlements maintain a communal granary, to which households contribute grain and from which they may draw in poor harvests; six do not. Each participant was paired, anonymously and by computer link, with a person from a different settlement. The first player received ten tokens and could send any number to the second player. Whatever was sent was tripled, and the second player then decided how many tokens to send back. Because the pair would never meet again and would never learn each other's identities, the amount sent was taken to measure trust, and the amount returned to measure trustworthiness.

The results were clear in one respect and surprising in another. First players in granary settlements sent an average of six tokens, while those in settlements without granaries sent an average of four. Second players, however, returned about the same share of what they received in both kinds of settlement, roughly a third. The gap in sending was large enough that the researchers judged it unlikely to be a matter of chance, and the gap in returning was too small to be worth reporting as a difference. The researchers conclude that communal institutions raise trust without raising trustworthiness: people in granary settlements were not more generous, only more confident of the generosity of others.

The researchers' interpretation is that life with a granary supplies repeated experience of contributions being returned in kind, and that this experience generalizes into an expectation about people one has not met. Remarks collected after the game fit this reading, though they are anecdotes and not measurements; several first players in granary settlements said that people "usually come through." The researchers raise two cautions themselves, and neither is a small one. First, they cannot rule out that settlements already inclined to cooperate were the ones that built granaries, in which case the institution would be an effect of trust and not a cause. They offer partial evidence against this: two of the six granaries were built by a relief agency after a flood, not by the residents, and senders in those settlements gave as much as senders in the other four. Second, the game paired participants only with fellow valley dwellers, so the expectation may not extend to outsiders.

A critic adds a third caution: tokens are not grain, and people may send more freely where the stakes feel like play. The researchers answer that this applies equally to both groups of settlements and so cannot explain the difference between them, though it may limit how far the absolute figures can be generalized.

Whatever the verdict on cause, the least noticed result may be the most instructive. Folk wisdom holds that communal living makes people better. The data suggest something more modest: it makes people reasonable in expecting others to be good, and expectations are what grease cooperation among strangers.

**Passage 4 (questions 19-24)**

Political thinkers who defend tradition often make a claim that sounds like an appeal to ignorance: since we cannot see why an inherited practice works, we should not tear it down. Their critics reply that this is a recipe for preserving every injustice that has survived long enough to look natural. Both sides are partly right, and the quarrel persists because each speaks as if tradition were one thing, a single bundle to be kept or discarded whole, when in fact it is a collection of practices with very different histories.

The strongest version of the defense is not an appeal to ignorance but an argument about information. A practice used by many generations has been tested, though not in any laboratory: it has met many circumstances and been quietly revised when it failed. Its present form therefore embodies knowledge that no individual possesses and that no one could state in full. A reformer who sees only its apparent purpose, and removes it, may discover too late that it served another purpose too. Hence the conservative presumption, which is a rule about who must explain themselves and not a claim that the past was wise: those who propose to change an inherited practice bear the burden of showing that they understand what it does.

This argument has a limit that its friends rarely mention. Testing by experience works only when failure is felt by someone who is able to change the practice. A practice that fails only the powerless is not corrected, since those who suffer have no voice in revision and those who revise do not suffer. Its long survival shows nothing about its merit; it shows only that its costs fell on the quiet. A tradition's authority as accumulated knowledge is therefore proportional to how widely its failures could be registered. By this standard, a custom governing weights and measures, which every buyer and seller feels, has a stronger claim than a custom governing the status of people who cannot vote.

Critics of tradition err in the opposite direction when they treat this limit as a refutation. Showing that a practice might embody injustice is not showing that it does, and the reformer's own proposal is untested, and so has no earned presumption of its own. The sensible course, then, is to ask the same question of both: who would have noticed if this were failing? Where the answer is "everyone affected," an inherited practice deserves caution about change. Where the answer is "no one with power," it deserves scrutiny, however old it is, since its age tells us only how long the silence has lasted.

The result is an unexciting politics, one that will satisfy neither the partisans of continuity nor the partisans of change, since it supplies reasons to preserve some inherited things and to dismantle others and gives no general rule for deciding in advance. I count that in its favor. A doctrine that tells us always to preserve, or always to reform, has stopped being an argument about particular practices and has become a temperament.

**Score-and-review protocol**

1. Total your Part B score and add it to your Part A score.
2. Mark every miss with its tag, its cause and your confidence rating.
3. Redo each miss untimed, restating in one sentence why the right answer is right before you look at the explanation.
4. For a fuller method of turning the whole exam into a study plan, see "Strategy: Reviewing a CARS Practice Exam" (chapter 20).

Praxis Tip: When you review, state aloud (quietly, if others are present) the named reason each wrong choice fails. If you can only say "it felt wrong," you have not yet learned the trap, merely survived it.

Praxis Tip: For a strengthen or weaken question, write "claim" and "gap" on scratch paper before reading the choices. The right answer will bear on the gap; the decoys will bear on something nearby, which is the cheapest place to build a decoy.

Praxis Tip: For an analogy question, strip the passage's example to its bare structure ("selection by outcome, without lying") and discard choices that differ in structure, however similar their subject matter.

Praxis Tip: In an attitude question, look for the author's own evaluative words, and ignore the attitudes the author attributes to others. A critic's scorn is not the author's scorn unless the author shares it.

Praxis Tip: If you finish a passage early, spend the spare minute rereading your two least confident answers against the text, and not rereading the passage for pleasure. The passage will forgive you; the scoring will not.
