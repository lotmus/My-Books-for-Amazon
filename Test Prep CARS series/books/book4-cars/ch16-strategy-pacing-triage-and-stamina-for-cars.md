# Strategy: Pacing, Triage and Stamina for CARS

**What you will be able to do**

- Turn the section's time limit into a budget per passage and a set of clock checkpoints.
- Split a passage's ten minutes sensibly between reading, mapping and answering.
- Decide, quickly and calmly, whether to take passages in order or defer a dense one.
- Apply skip-and-return rules at the question level without losing the points you already hold.
- Build the stamina to read at full attention for ninety minutes.

**Why it matters**

Most learners who run short of time on a passage-based section are not slow readers. They lose time in three places: rereading passages they have already read, defending a close call between two choices for three minutes, and arriving at passage seven with a tired brain. This chapter deals with all three. It imports the general method from Core Test Skills, Chapter 11 (budget, buffer, checkpoints and the flag-and-move rule) and adds only what is particular to a section built from passages.

Praxis Tip: Time on this section is spent by the passage, not by the question. A passage that goes badly costs you in all five to seven of its questions at once.

**The numbers**

Two quick consequences:

- Average time per question: 90 ÷ 53 ≈ 1.7 minutes (about 1 minute 42 seconds), but this average includes reading time, so it overstates the time you have for any one question.
- Average time per passage: 90 ÷ 9 = 10 minutes.

**Building the passage budget**

Keep a buffer of about 4 to 5 minutes at the end for flagged questions (Core Test Skills, Chapter 11). Then:

- Working time: 90 − 4.5 = 85.5 minutes, which is a clean **9 minutes 30 seconds per passage** across nine passages.
- Checkpoints on the clock: after passage 3, 28:30; after passage 6, 57:00; after passage 9, 85:30.

Write the three checkpoints at the top of your scratch paper when the section opens. Looking at the clock three times is control; looking every ninety seconds is a hobby.

**Inside one passage**

A 9:30 passage divides roughly like this:

| Questions in the set | Read and map | Time per question |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | about 2:45 | about 1:20 |
| 6 | about 2:45 | about 1:10 |
| 7 | about 2:45 | about 1:00 |

Every row adds up to roughly 9:30 (6:45 of question time divided by 5, 6 or 7). Notice what the table implies: sets with more questions give you less time per question but more points per read, so a seven-question passage is the best value in the section.

Reading a 550-word passage at 220 words per minute takes 550 ÷ 220 = 2.5 minutes; the extra 15 seconds is for the map ([Strategy: The Active Reading Method](chapter:2)). If you read more slowly, do not skip the map to compensate. A good map makes later questions faster; skipping it moves time from the front of the passage to the back and usually adds some.

Praxis Tip: Test your own reading rate once with a timed 500-word passage, then do the division. Pacing built on a number you guessed is a rumor with a spreadsheet.

**Passage order and triage**

If the interface allows you to move between passages [VERIFY], you have a choice about order. Taking passages in order is the default and is perfectly respectable. Triage is an adjustment, not a fashion.

Use a 20 to 30 second look at the first few lines of each passage you are about to start, only to answer one question: **can I state what this passage is about?** If yes, begin. If the opening is dense and abstract and you cannot state the topic, you have a candidate for deferral.

Rules for deferring:

1. Defer **at most one or two** passages in a section, never more.
2. Defer on **density signals** (abstract nouns, long sentences, no concrete example in sight), not on topic. You need no outside knowledge for any passage, so a topic you dislike is not a reason; unfamiliarity is not difficulty.
3. Put a time reservation in writing: "Passage 4 deferred; back by the 70-minute mark."
4. Never defer a passage and then forget it. An undone passage is five to seven blanks.
5. If navigation is limited or you are unsure, do not defer. Apply question-level triage instead.

Praxis Tip: A dense passage is not necessarily a hard set of questions. Several questions on it may ask only about main idea or tone. That is another reason to read it with a rested brain rather than skip it with a worried one.

**Skip-and-return at the question level**

Core Test Skills, Chapter 11, gives the flag-and-move rule. For a passage-based section, tune it as follows.

1. **Ceiling.** About 2 minutes for any single question (roughly twice the average).
2. **At the ceiling:** pick the best remaining choice now, flag, and move on. Do not leave a question blank when a wrong answer costs nothing [VERIFY the scoring rule].
3. **Order inside a passage.** Within a set, take the questions in order unless one stalls. Questions with line or phrase references and main-idea or tone questions tend to go faster; long reasoning-beyond-the-text questions are the usual stallers.
4. **Return within the same passage when you can.** Your map is fresh in your mind for a few minutes and cold after an hour. If a question stalls, circle back at the end of that set if you have 30 seconds to spare, before the next passage pushes the map out of mind.
5. **Use the end buffer** for flagged questions on the clock, not for rechecking questions you were confident about.

Praxis Tip: The elimination moves in [Strategy: When You Are Stuck: Elimination and Trap Answers](chapter:17) are your tools for a flagged question. Use them once, give your best choice, and release the question.

**When you are behind or ahead**

*Behind at a checkpoint:* resist the urge to skim the next passage without mapping. Instead, tighten the question side: apply the 2-minute ceiling strictly, stop rechecking confident answers, and flag more eagerly. If you are more than five minutes behind at passage 6, consider deferring the densest remaining passage, if allowed.

*Ahead at a checkpoint:* bank nothing. Hard passages arrive in bunches. Use extra seconds to verify the answers you flagged, or to read the next passage a little more carefully.

**Stamina**

Ninety minutes of dense reading is a physical task of its own kind. Many learners' accuracy falls in the last third of the section for no reason connected to the content. Stamina is trained the way running distance is trained: build up gradually, then test.

| Weeks | Block | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | 2 passages (about 19 min) | Full method, review after |
| 3 to 4 | 4 passages (about 38 min) | Timed, no phone |
| 5 to 6 | 6 passages (about 57 min) | Timed, mixed subjects |
| 7 to 8 | 9 passages (85 to 90 min) | Full simulation |

Between blocks, pay attention to the basics from Core Test Skills, Chapters 22 and 25: sleep, a normal meal beforehand, water, and practicing at the hour of day when the real exam will occur. Then track one number per full practice section: accuracy on passages 1 to 3 versus passages 7 to 9. A gap of more than ten points suggests a stamina problem rather than a skill problem.

**Passage reset.** Between passages, take 10 seconds: lower your shoulders, look away from the screen, breathe out slowly, and write the next passage number on your scratch paper. It costs less than 15 seconds and it separates the passages in your head so that one confusing set does not leak into the next.

**Worked Example 1: Building a plan (skill: arithmetic budgeting)**

*Problem.* A learner reads 200 words per minute, expects passages of about 550 words with 6 questions, and wants a 5-minute buffer. Build the plan.

*Solution.* Working time is 90 − 5 = 85 minutes, so each of 9 passages gets 85 ÷ 9 ≈ 9.44 minutes (9 minutes 27 seconds). Reading 550 words at 200 per minute takes 2.75 minutes (2:45); allowing 15 seconds to map, the front end is 3:00. That leaves about 6:27 for six questions: about 65 seconds each. Checkpoints: after passage 3, 28:21; after passage 6, 56:42; after passage 9, 85:00.

*Skill used:* converting a time limit and a personal reading rate into checkpoints. Notice the plan exposes the real problem: this learner has little slack, so improving reading speed or map speed will pay more than agonizing over questions.

**Worked Example 2: Behind at the first checkpoint (skill: adjusting)**

*Problem.* The plan says 28:30 after passage 3. The clock reads 33:00. What do you do?

*Solution.* You are 4:30 behind, which is the length of your whole buffer. Do not respond by skipping the map. Instead: (1) hold the 2-minute ceiling firmly; (2) stop verifying questions you are already sure of; (3) flag eagerly in the next two passages and expect to give some of those flagged questions only a guess; (4) accept that you may enter the final passage without a buffer. If you are still more than five minutes behind at passage 6, consider deferring a dense passage if allowed.

*Skill used:* recovering time from the question side rather than the reading side.

**Worked Example 3: A triage decision (skill: the 30-second look)**

*Problem.* You have three fresh passages ahead. The openings read:

- Passage 1: "Sociologists have long asked why neighbors who barely speak will lend one another a ladder without being asked."
- Passage 2: "To say that a predicate resists reduction is not yet to say what it resists, nor in virtue of what the resistance holds."
- Passage 3: "When the city of Marl rebuilt its market hall, the architect insisted that every beam remain visible."

*Solution.* Passages 1 and 3 each open with a concrete scene or question, so you can state the topic (why neighbors help; a building's visible beams). Passage 2 is abstract and dense, and you probably cannot state its topic after 30 seconds. Defer passage 2 only if you are allowed to and if you have written down a return time. Do 1 and 3 first, then give 2 the time you saved, with a rested brain and a clear budget. If you are unsure whether you may navigate, simply read in order.

*Skill used:* triaging on density, not on subject.

**Worked Example 4: A stalled question (skill: the ceiling)**

*Problem.* You have spent 1:50 on a question that has narrowed to two choices, B and D, and the clock is on your checkpoint.

*Solution.* You are at the ceiling. Use one 15-second move from the elimination toolkit: restate the question, then ask which choice goes further than the passage. Suppose D uses a word the passage never supports ("always"). Choose B, flag, and move on. If you cannot separate them, still choose one: a guess costs nothing and a blank earns nothing [VERIFY]. Return only if the buffer allows.

*Skill used:* a decision rule instead of a debate.

**Worked Example 5: Diagnosing stamina (skill: reading your own data)**

*Problem.* Over two full practice sections a learner scores 8 of 10 on passages 1 to 3 and 5 of 10 on passages 7 to 9, with no pattern by topic. What should she do?

*Solution.* The 30-point gap with no topic pattern points to fatigue, not knowledge. Lengthen training blocks in steps (see the table), keep the passage-reset routine, check meals and sleep before practice, and re-measure the gap after two weeks. If the gap persists despite good conditions, check whether she is rushing at the end because she spent too long early; a pacing chart for each passage will show it.

*Skill used:* separating a stamina problem from a content problem.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Write your three checkpoints and the words "2 min ceiling" on the first line of your scratch paper. Two seconds now; many minutes later.

Praxis Tip: Do not use the last passage as a place to make up lost time. A rushed final map is the most expensive way to save two minutes.

Praxis Tip: Practice pacing with timed single passages, not just timed sections. A skill that is used only once a month is an acquaintance.

Praxis Tip: Practice the passage reset on easy sets. A ritual that has never been rehearsed tends to be forgotten at the moment it is needed.

Praxis Tip: If a passage starts well and then loses you, mark the paragraph where you lost the thread with a question mark and keep reading. Questions rarely require the whole passage; they require a sense of structure plus specific lines.

Praxis Tip: Treat a flagged question as a debt, not an enemy. Pay what you can at the end and let the rest go with a calm guess.

**Common Mistakes**

- Using the same time for every passage regardless of its number of questions.
- Dropping the map to save time, then spending more time rereading.
- Deferring a passage because the topic seems dull and then never returning.
- Staying with a close call past the ceiling.
- Rechecking confident answers in the buffer instead of attempting flagged ones.
- Training stamina only in single passages and meeting 90 minutes for the first time on exam day.
- Letting a bad passage spoil the next one.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. A learner plans a 90-minute section of 9 passages with a 5-minute buffer and divides the rest equally. About how long is each passage's budget?
   A. 10 minutes
   B. 8 minutes 30 seconds
   C. 7 minutes 44 seconds
   D. 9 minutes 27 seconds

2. Using a plan of 9 minutes 30 seconds per passage, what should the clock read after the fourth passage?
   A. 40 minutes
   B. 38 minutes
   C. 36 minutes
   D. 42 minutes 30 seconds

3. After the third passage the clock reads 34:00 against a planned 28:30. Which response best follows the chapter's advice?
   A. Skip the map on the next few passages in order to save reading time
   B. Defer every remaining dense passage to the end
   C. Hold the 2-minute ceiling, skip rechecks and flag more eagerly
   D. Spend the whole review buffer now and give up on the final passage

4. Which is the best reason to defer a passage when navigation is allowed?
   A. You cannot state the topic after a 30-second look at its opening
   B. The topic is poetry, which you happen to find less interesting than most
   C. It has the most questions in the section
   D. It is the first passage in the section

5. A question reaches the 2-minute ceiling with two choices remaining. What should the learner do?
   A. Leave it blank and flag it so the decision can be made later
   B. Keep going until certain, because accuracy matters most
   C. Reread the whole passage from the beginning
   D. Choose the better of the two, flag the question and move on

6. A learner scores 80 percent on passages 1 to 3 and 55 percent on passages 7 to 9, with no topic pattern. The most likely issue and first remedy are
   A. fatigue, so lengthen timed blocks gradually and keep a reset routine
   B. a knowledge gap in the last passages' topics, so study those subjects
   C. late passages are harder by design, so nothing can be done
   D. poor early pacing, so cut the time spent on passages 1 to 3

7. A learner reads 220 words per minute. About how long will a 550-word passage take to read, before mapping?
   A. 2 minutes 45 seconds
   B. 2 minutes 30 seconds
   C. 3 minutes 20 seconds
   D. 5 minutes

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **D.** Working time is 90 − 5 = 85 minutes, and 85 ÷ 9 ≈ 9.44 minutes. (A ignores the buffer, which leaves 85 minutes; B divides 85 minutes by 10 passages, not 9; C divides 85 minutes by 11 passages, not 9.)
2. **B.** 4 × 9.5 = 38 minutes. (A uses 10 minutes per passage, not 9:30; C uses 9 minutes per passage, not 9:30; D counts four and a half passages.)
3. **C.** The chapter recovers time from the question side: strict ceiling, no rechecking of confident answers, eager flagging. (A moves time from the front of the passage to the back and usually costs more in the questions; B breaks the rule of deferring at most one or two passages; D abandons five to seven questions when recoverable time remains in the question side.)
4. **A.** The chapter says to defer on density signals after a 20 to 30 second look. (B is a matter of preference; the chapter says defer on density, not on topic; C reverses the logic, because a long set is the best value per read; D has nothing to do with density or with how well the passage can be read.)
5. **D.** The flag-and-move rule says to guess first, flag, and move on, returning only if time allows. (A earns nothing if no answer is given, and a wrong answer is understood to cost nothing; B spends time that other questions in the set could use to earn points; C costs about 2.5 minutes, which is more than the question deserves.)
6. **A.** A large late-section drop with no topic pattern points to stamina, which is trained in steps. (B is unsupported, since no outside knowledge is needed and no topic pattern appears; C is an unsupported assumption that excuses the gap; D may be true but is not the first remedy suggested by this pattern; the data point to fatigue first.)
7. **B.** 550 ÷ 220 = 2.5 minutes. (A uses a rate of 200 words per minute rather than 220; C uses a rate of 165 words per minute; D uses a rate of 110 words per minute.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, redo any arithmetic that went wrong with your own reading rate and save the result on an index card. A learner who knows their rate can recompute the plan in a minute.

**Do this today**

1. Time yourself reading one 500-word article, and compute your words per minute.
2. Build your own passage budget for a 90-minute, nine-passage section with a buffer you choose.
3. Write your three checkpoints and your ceiling on an index card.
4. Do one timed passage ([Practice: Passage Set 1](chapter:12)) using the 9:30 plan, and log where the time went.

**Recap**

Budget by the passage, set three checkpoints, defer a dense passage only with a reason and a return time, release stalled questions at the ceiling with a guess, and train stamina in steps. The method for reviewing a timed section is in [Strategy: Reviewing a CARS Practice Exam](chapter:20).
