# Lesson 6: Strengthen, Weaken and Assumption Questions

**What you will be able to do**

- Break an argument into conclusion, premises and the gap between them.
- Name the common kinds of gap: causal, sampling, analogy, scope and necessary-versus-sufficient.
- Predict what would strengthen or weaken an argument before reading the options.
- Find an assumption and confirm it with the negation test.
- Tell strengtheners, weakeners, assumptions and irrelevant facts apart, and avoid the five standard traps.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Strengthen, weaken and assumption questions belong to the reasoning-beyond-the-text family, which the public description is understood to weight most heavily, roughly 40 percent of the section [VERIFY]. In the CARS section they appear as "Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the author's argument?" or "The author's reasoning depends on which assumption?" They test whether you can see how an argument works, which is different from whether you agree with it.

This lesson builds directly on [Lesson 3](chapter:6) (the anatomy of an argument) and uses the new-information skill from [Lesson 5](chapter:8). Core Test Skills, Chapter 16, gives a short introduction to the same ideas on logic basics, and you may want to reread its section on parts of an argument and common fallacies before you start. The examples here use invented arguments on general topics; you do not need to know anything about them.

Praxis Tip: You do not need to agree or disagree with an argument to attack or support it. Your task is to find the one thing that, if changed, would change the strength of the conclusion.

**Concept Review**

**The skeleton.** Every argument has three working parts.

- *Conclusion:* what the author wants you to accept.
- *Premises:* the evidence or reasons offered.
- *Assumption:* the unstated claim that connects the premises to the conclusion. It is the bridge.

In shorthand: *Because P, therefore C, assuming A.* Strengthen, weaken and assumption questions are three ways of asking about A.

| Question | It is asking you to... |
|---|---|
| Assumption | Name the bridge the argument needs |
| Strengthen | Add support to the bridge or remove a threat to it |
| Weaken | Attack the bridge or offer a rival route |

**Common kinds of gap.** Most gaps in CARS arguments fall into a few families. Learn to name them quickly.

| Gap | Pattern | Typical assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Causal | A came before B, so A caused B | No rival cause, and no reverse cause |
| Sampling | A claim about a group from some members | The sample represents the group |
| Analogy | X resembles Y, so what holds of Y holds of X | The similarity is relevant |
| Scope | Evidence about one thing, conclusion about more | The evidence extends |
| Measure | A number or sign is read as the thing itself | The measure reflects the thing |

**Strengthening.** You strengthen an argument by making its bridge more likely to hold. There are four standard moves.

1. *Rule out a rival cause.* "The same decline did not occur in a similar town without the change."
2. *Support the link directly.* "The mechanism the author proposes has been observed."
3. *Show the sample is representative.* "The people surveyed resemble those not surveyed."
4. *Add a new premise that points to the same conclusion.* "A second, independent piece of evidence agrees."

**Weakening.** The mirror image has four moves.

1. *Offer a rival cause.* "A second change occurred in the same period."
2. *Reverse the direction.* "The supposed effect actually came first."
3. *Show the sample is skewed.* "Those who responded were unusual."
4. *Break the analogy or measure.* "The two cases differ in a way that matters here," or "The number does not track the thing."

A weakener need not destroy the argument. It only has to make the conclusion less likely. A strengthener likewise need not prove anything. When two options both weaken, the one that attacks the central bridge more directly is stronger.

**Assumption questions.** A **necessary assumption** is something that must be true for the argument to work. A **sufficient assumption** would, if added, guarantee the conclusion. CARS most often asks for the necessary kind, with stems like "The argument assumes that..." or "depends on which of the following?" Use the **negation test**: negate the option; if the argument then falls apart, the option is a necessary assumption. If the argument survives the negation, the option is not needed. This test beats a feeling of "does that sound right?"

*Example.* Argument: "This novel will endure, because critics currently admire it." Candidate assumption: "Critical admiration now predicts a long life for a work." Negation: "Critical admiration now does not predict a long life." The argument loses its support, so the candidate is necessary. A different candidate: "Critics are never wrong about literary quality." Negation: "Critics are sometimes wrong." The argument survives, because admiration could still be a decent guide; so the candidate is not necessary. It is also too strong, a typical trap.

**Strengthener or assumption?** They overlap, but they differ in standard. An assumption must be required; a strengthener need only help. A statement can strengthen an argument without being required by it, and a required assumption may add little extra support once you grant it. When the stem says "assumes," use the negation test. When it says "strengthen," ask whether the option makes the conclusion more likely.

**Five traps in these questions.**

| Trap | What it does |
|---|---|
| Reversed direction | Strengthens when asked to weaken, or the reverse |
| Irrelevant | Concerns a topic the argument does not use |
| Out of scope | Talks about a different group, time or place |
| Too strong | Demands more than the argument needs (always, never, all) |
| Consistent only | Agrees with the conclusion but does not touch the bridge |

Of these, "consistent only" is the hardest to resist. An option that merely says the conclusion could be true, or repeats the premise in other words, feels supportive but adds nothing.

**Reading the stem.** Words such as "most," "if true" and "depends" matter. "If true" means you must accept the option as fact and judge its effect on the argument. "Most" means more than one option might help or hurt, and you must pick the strongest. "Depends" and "assumes" signal the necessary-assumption test.

**Arguments inside CARS passages.** In a CARS passage the argument may not be neatly marked. The author may argue against someone else's view, or may report an argument without endorsing it. First decide whose argument the question targets. If the stem says "the author's argument," use the author's conclusion, not a view the author quotes only to dismiss. On scratch paper, mark the argument as "C: ...; P: ...; gap: ..." in a short line.

**A routine for these questions.**

1. Find the conclusion the stem refers to.
2. Note the premises (the evidence offered).
3. Name the gap in one phrase (rival cause, skewed sample, relevant difference, measure).
4. Predict the shape of a good answer. For weaken: "something that points to the rival cause." For strengthen: "something that rules it out."
5. Test the options one at a time. Ask "does this hit the gap, and in which direction?" For assumption, apply the negation test to the finalists.

Praxis Tip: Write the gap as a question: "What else could explain this?" or "Are those people typical?" Each option then answers or ignores the question.

**Worked Example 1: A causal gap (skill: rival causes)**

> Since the museum began offering audio tours, visitors have stayed longer. The director concludes that audio tours deepen visitors' engagement with the exhibits.

*Conclusion:* audio tours deepen engagement. *Premise:* visitors stay longer. *Gap:* a longer stay is taken to mean deeper engagement; but it might only mean slower walking or waiting.

*Question.* Which of the following would most weaken the director's conclusion?

A. Most visitors who take an audio tour say they enjoyed it.
B. Visitors with audio tours spend much of the extra time waiting at numbered stations for recordings to finish.
C. The museum has added a new gallery since the tours began.
D. Audio tours cost the museum little to produce.

*Reasoning.* B hits the gap exactly: it offers a rival explanation for the longer stay (waiting) that has nothing to do with engagement. A is consistent but only reports enjoyment, not depth. C is out of scope unless the new gallery explains the longer stay, which the option does not say. D is irrelevant to the conclusion.

*Skill used:* noticing that a measure (time spent) was read as the thing itself (engagement).

Praxis Tip: When the evidence is a proxy such as time, attendance, applause or volume, the best weakener often shows that the proxy changed for a reason unrelated to the quality.

**Worked Example 2: A sampling gap (skill: representativeness)**

> A sociologist interviewed fifty people at a free weekly community lunch. Most said they often felt lonely. She concluded that loneliness is widespread among the neighborhood's elderly residents.

*Question.* Which of the following, if true, would most strengthen the sociologist's conclusion?

A. The people at the lunch were friendly and spoke freely.
B. The lunch is advertised mainly to elderly residents who have said they eat alone.
C. Most of the neighborhood's elderly residents attend the lunch, whatever their circumstances.
D. The sociologist has studied loneliness for many years.

*Reasoning.* The gap is whether lunch-goers are typical. C makes the sample nearly the whole group, so it strengthens. B would weaken, because the lunch recruits the lonely, the opposite of a random sample. A concerns candor, which is not the problem. D concerns the sociologist's experience, which is not evidence about these residents.

*Skill used:* asking "who is in the sample, and who is not?"

**Worked Example 3: The negation test (skill: necessary versus too strong)**

> The critic maintains that this novel will endure, since critics today overwhelmingly admire it.

*Question.* The critic's argument most depends on which assumption?

A. Critics are never mistaken about literary quality.
B. The admiration of today's critics is a reasonable guide to a work's lasting appeal.
C. The novel is the most admired of its decade.
D. Readers always agree with critics.

*Reasoning.* Apply negation. B negated ("Critics' admiration is no guide to lasting appeal") removes the support, so B is needed. A negated ("Critics are sometimes mistaken") leaves a good guide intact, so A is too strong. C, negated, leaves the argument unharmed, because admiration need not be the greatest to predict lasting. D is too strong and not required.

*Skill used:* using negation to separate necessary from merely related.

Praxis Tip: Treat "always," "never," "all" and "none" in an assumption option as red flags. Most arguments need a bridge, not a perfect one.

**Worked Example 4: An analogy gap (skill: relevant similarity)**

> Just as a doctor who explains a disease in detail is not promoting the disease, a novelist who depicts cruelty in detail is not promoting cruelty.

*Question.* Which of the following would most strengthen the argument?

A. Doctors usually have more years of training than novelists.
B. In both cases the audience reads in order to understand the thing described.
C. Novelists are paid less than doctors.
D. Some novelists admire cruelty.

*Reasoning.* The analogy works if the purpose of the audience's reading is similar. B supports that. A and C compare training and pay, which are irrelevant differences. D is a consideration against the analogy at most, not for it.

*Skill used:* checking whether the shared feature is the relevant one.

**Worked Example 5: A passage-level argument (skill: choosing the target)**

> Public monuments ought to be temporary, the essayist says. A permanent monument claims that the community agrees on whom to honor, but communities rarely agree, and a monument that cannot be removed forces the disagreement on every generation that follows.

*Conclusion:* monuments ought to be temporary. *Premises:* permanence claims agreement; agreement is rare; permanence forces the disagreement on later generations. *Gap:* that permanent monuments really claim agreement, and that being forced to live with them is a harm.

*Question 1.* The essayist's argument assumes that

A. monuments are rarely popular.
B. a permanent monument implies a consensus that does not exist.
C. every community disagrees about its history.
D. temporary monuments cost less than permanent ones.

*Question 2.* Which of the following would most weaken the argument?

A. Many permanent monuments were openly contested when unveiled but are now accepted by wide majorities.
B. Temporary monuments are often well attended.
C. Some communities have removed monuments after lengthy debate.
D. Most people never look at monuments.

*Reasoning.* For Question 1, B is the bridge from "permanent" to "problem"; negate it and the objection vanishes. A and C are too strong and not required, and D is out of scope. For Question 2, A attacks the harm premise: if disagreement fades with time, then permanence does not force a lasting disagreement. B is irrelevant to the harm, C is consistent with either view, and D is out of scope.

*Skill used:* separating the question about the bridge (assumption) from the question about its strength (weaken).

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: For "weaken," do not choose an option that merely says the author is wrong. The right option gives a reason that fits the argument's own terms, for example a rival cause or a skewed sample.

Praxis Tip: For "strengthen," beware of answers that restate a premise. If the information is already in the passage, it adds nothing.

Praxis Tip: "If true" means accept the option as fact, even when it seems unlikely. Do not evaluate whether it is probable, only what it would do.

Praxis Tip: In a causal argument, always test the reverse: "Could B have caused A?" Reverse causation is a favorite.

Praxis Tip: If you cannot name the gap in a few words, reread the conclusion and ask what the author would need you to believe about the evidence.

Praxis Tip: When two options both help or both hurt, choose the one that connects most directly to the conclusion, not to a side detail.

**Common Mistakes**

- Weakening the premise when the question targets the conclusion, or the reverse.
- Choosing an option that is consistent with the conclusion but does not support it.
- Choosing an option that makes the argument sound false rather than less certain.
- Treating a necessary assumption as a sufficient one, and choosing a statement that is too strong.
- Missing the direction of the question (strengthen versus weaken).
- Importing a favorite counterexample from outside the passage.
- Attacking a view the author only quotes.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. A linguist notes that in towns where street signs are in two languages, residents are more likely to speak both languages. She concludes that bilingual signage encourages bilingualism.

   Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the linguist's conclusion?

   A. Bilingual signs cost more to produce and to maintain than signs in one language.
   B. Some of the bilingual towns have signs in three or more languages.
   C. In many bilingual towns, most visiting tourists speak only one language.
   D. Towns adopted bilingual signs only after residents were already bilingual.

2. The gallery's director says that the redesigned lighting has improved visitors' satisfaction. As evidence, she cites exit questionnaires: scores averaged 4.3 out of 5 after the redesign, compared with 3.9 before.

   Which of the following, if true, would most strengthen the director's claim?

   A. Lighting designers generally recommend the approach used in the gallery's redesign.
   B. Satisfaction scores at other galleries also rose over the same period.
   C. The same questionnaire, at the same exit, went to similar visitors in both periods.
   D. The director commissioned the redesign herself.

3. A critic argues that no film adapted from a novel can match the novel, because a film must choose a single image where a novel allows each reader to imagine one of their own.

   The critic's argument most depends on which of the following assumptions?

   A. Part of a novel's value lies in each reader imagining scenes freely.
   B. Every film that has been adapted from a novel has been unsuccessful.
   C. Most readers enjoy novels more than they enjoy films.
   D. Films cannot convey the inner thoughts of a character.

4. Some economists compare a city's streets to the circulatory system of a body. A body is healthier when blood flows without obstruction, they say, so a city is healthier when its traffic flows without obstruction.

   Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the economists' argument?

   A. Blood vessels are much narrower than the streets of a city.
   B. A city's vitality comes largely from markets and lingering, which need slow movement.
   C. Cities carry a good deal more traffic now than they did in the past.
   D. Traffic engineers usually measure flow in vehicles per hour on each road.

5. A university press argues that it should publish only books likely to sell at least five hundred copies. Otherwise, it says, it will run out of funds and be unable to publish anything.

   The press's argument most depends on which of the following assumptions?

   A. Every book that sells five hundred copies is a good book.
   B. The scholarly value of a book is entirely unrelated to its sales.
   C. The press has never lost money on a book.
   D. The press cannot cover losses on low-selling books from other revenue.

6. An archaeologist argues that the settlement was abandoned because of drought, since tree-ring samples show unusually thin rings in the final decade of its occupation.

   Which of the following, if true, would most strengthen the archaeologist's argument?

   A. Pollen records show a decline in water-loving plants near the site in that decade.
   B. Neighboring settlements that also had thin rings were not abandoned in that period.
   C. The settlers kept written records of their harvests and storehouses.
   D. Tree rings are also used to date wooden beams.

7. A sociologist maintains that the decline of the village fair reflects declining community spirit, since attendance has fallen by half over thirty years.

   Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the sociologist's reasoning?

   A. Attendance at the fair was once higher than attendance at the school plays.
   B. Membership in the choir, the football club and the gardening society has also fallen by half.
   C. The village's population has also fallen by half over the same thirty years.
   D. The attendance figures were gathered by volunteers.

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **D.** If the signs came after the bilingualism, the supposed cause followed the supposed effect, which reverses the direction of the argument. (A is irrelevant; the cost of signs does not bear on whether signs encourage bilingualism; B is irrelevant; the number of languages on a sign does not address the direction of cause; C is out of scope; the argument concerns residents, not tourists.)
2. **C.** Holding the measure and the visitors steady rules out two rival explanations for the change in scores. (A is irrelevant; a general recommendation does not show an effect on these visitors; B would weaken the claim by suggesting a general cause; D is irrelevant; who commissioned it says nothing about its effect.)
3. **A.** The argument works only if reader-supplied imagery counts as a merit; negating the option removes the reason a fixed image is a loss. (B is too strong; the argument concerns what films cannot do, not what all existing ones have done; C is out of scope; enjoyment is not the argument's measure; D is not needed, since the argument rests on images and not on thoughts.)
4. **B.** It points to a relevant difference between the two cases: what makes a city healthy depends partly on slow movement, unlike a body's blood. (A is an irrelevant difference; width does not bear on whether free flow is healthy; C is irrelevant; the amount of traffic does not address the comparison; D is irrelevant; how flow is measured does not address the comparison.)
5. **D.** If the press could cover losses elsewhere, the funding worry would disappear; negating the option removes the reason for the policy. (A is not needed; the argument concerns funds, not quality; B is too strong and not required by a funds-based argument; C is too strong, and it conflicts with the press's worry about losses.)
6. **A.** An independent line of evidence for dry conditions in the same decade supports the proposed cause. (B would weaken the argument, since thin rings did not lead to abandonment elsewhere; C is irrelevant; the existence of records does not bear on the cause of abandonment; D is irrelevant; it concerns a use of tree rings, not the settlement.)
7. **C.** If the population halved too, attendance as a share of residents is unchanged, so the drop does not show declining spirit. (A is irrelevant; a comparison between events does not bear on community spirit; B would strengthen the reasoning by suggesting a wider decline in participation; D is irrelevant unless the volunteers were unreliable, which the option does not say.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, label each wrong option with its trap (reversed, irrelevant, out of scope, too strong, consistent only). A fast label means you saw the structure; a slow one means reread the routine.

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "Every argument is a bridge from premises to conclusion; I name the gap, predict what would support or break it, and test each option for direction and relevance, using the negation test for assumptions and refusing options that are reversed, irrelevant, out of scope, too strong or merely consistent."

*Interleave:* alternate an argument passage with a tone passage and an inference passage. Then take [Passage Set 1](chapter:12), and use [Elimination and Trap Answers](chapter:17) to review your errors.

Praxis Tip: End each study session by taking one argument from a newspaper column or a conversation and writing its bridge, one strengthener and one weakener. Be generous to the author; it is good practice, and it makes you more pleasant at dinner.
