# Appendix: Passage-Mapping Templates and Trap Catalog

**What this appendix is for**

This is a reference to keep open while you practice. It collects, in one place, the templates for mapping a passage on scratch paper and the full catalog of thirteen trap answers named in [Strategy: When You Are Stuck: Elimination and Trap Answers](chapter:17). Nothing here is new; it is the method of [Strategy: The Active Reading Method](chapter:2) and [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3) laid out for quick use.

Because the ebook cannot be written in, the templates below are described in words. Copy each one into a notebook or onto scratch paper, ruling the columns yourself. In the real exam you will have scratch paper and nothing else, so practice copying a template in under thirty seconds. If it takes longer, simplify it.

Praxis Tip: A template is a scaffold. Once you can map without it, put it away. The aim is the habit, not the stationery.

**Part 1: Passage-mapping templates**

**Template 1: The frame and the paragraph notes.** Use this for every passage.

Rule your page with a short heading line and a numbered list.

- Heading line, written in three short pieces: **Topic** (a few words), **Point** (the author's claim, in one short line), **Tone** (one or two words).
- Below it, one numbered line per paragraph: the paragraph number, a two- or three-word *job* ("claim," "objection," "example," "reply," "qualify," "conclude"), and at most five key words.
- Mark the voice at the start of each line with A for the author or R for a reported view, and a small question mark where you are unsure.

Fill in the Topic after the first paragraph. Fill in the Point and Tone at the end, and revise the Topic if the passage turned. A finished line looks like this in prose: "Paragraph 2, R, objection, critics say gardens only follow community." If you cannot say what a paragraph was for, reread its first and last sentences.

**Template 2: The argument map.** Use this for any passage, or any paragraph, that argues.

Make five labeled lines, in this order.

1. **Conclusion.** The main claim, in your words. If there is an intermediate conclusion, label it separately.
2. **Evidence.** The reasons or facts offered for it, as short phrases, each numbered.
3. **Assumption.** What must be true for the evidence to support the conclusion. Test it by negation: if the opposite makes the argument fail, it is required (see [Lesson 3](chapter:6) and [Lesson 6](chapter:9)).
4. **Counterargument.** The best rival view, and whether the author concedes, rebuts or ignores it.
5. **Qualifier.** The hedges that limit the claim ("may," "in some cases," "tends to").

Write one line for each, not a paragraph. The qualifier line is the one most often left empty, and it is the one most often tested.

**Template 3: The voice tracker.** Use this for passages with many views.

Make a short list with two columns, written side by side: *Who* and *What they hold*. Common rows are "the author," "some critics," "earlier scholars," "a common view," "a reader" and "an example's subject." Next to each row put a plus if the author agrees, a minus if the author rejects it, a zero if the author only reports it, and a tilde if the author agrees in part. Before you answer any "most likely to agree" or "according to the author" question, find the row.

**Template 4: The structure strip.** Use this when a question asks what a paragraph or sentence is for.

Write the paragraph numbers in a line, and under each put one of the jobs from the list in [Strategy: The Active Reading Method](chapter:2): set up, claim, support, objection, concession, reply, qualify, conclude. Draw an arrow between paragraphs that answer each other (a reply points back to the objection). Function questions ("in order to") are nearly always answered by reading the strip, then checking the choice's verb against it.

**Template 5: The question stem card.** Use this for each question.

Before reading the choices, write five items on one line: the **family** (F for foundations, W for within the text, B for beyond the text); the **type** (main idea, detail, function, inference, application, strengthen or weaken, and so on); the **stem restated in about five words**; the **prediction** (an answer, or the kind of answer); and the **paragraph** it lives in. The prediction is what you compare against the choices. If you cannot write one, you have misread the stem.

**Template 6: The two-choice test.** Use this when two choices survive.

Write the two letters. Underneath, write the single word or claim on which they differ. Find the line of the passage that decides it, and write its paragraph number. Then choose. If the ceiling arrives before you can find the line, choose the more modest and more text-anchored of the two, and flag the question ([Strategy: Pacing, Triage and Stamina for CARS](chapter:16)).

**Template 7: The error log entry.** Use this for every miss in practice (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9, and [Strategy: Reviewing a CARS Practice Exam](chapter:20)).

One entry has six parts, in this order: the passage and question number; the family and type; the trap name from the catalog below, in capitals if you like; what the text actually said, in a short phrase; the fix, in one sentence; and the date of the re-attempt. If you cannot name the trap, write "unclear" and read the matching entry below twice.

**Template 8: The confidence mark.** Use this during practice and exams.

Beside each question in your notes, put one mark: a tick for sure, a dash for neither sure nor unsure, a question mark for unsure. During review, count the sure-wrong group first.

Praxis Tip: Every template has a rule that its output must be shorter than the thing it describes. A map as long as the passage is a copy, and a copy is a way of not reading.

**Part 2: The trap catalog**

The thirteen names, with their abbreviations, are the same ones used in [Chapter 17](chapter:17). Each entry gives the tell, the question types where it is most common, and the cure. All examples below come from one short invented passage, so read it first and keep it in view.

*The bridge passage.* "Engineers who praise a bridge usually mean that it stands, but the people who cross it each day mean something else: that it is pleasant to cross. These two standards do not always agree. The older river bridge in the town is, by every calculation, over-built, and many residents find its heavy railings and narrow walkway dull. The newer footbridge is slender and graceful, and, at first glance, a triumph. Yet its deck sways slightly in wind, and some walkers avoid it for that reason. The lesson is not that beauty and strength compete. It is that the person who designs a bridge must treat the walker's experience as a part of the structure, not as an afterthought."

**1. Too extreme (EXT).** *Tell:* always, never, only, all, none, completely, "the sole reason." *Common in:* main idea, inference, author's attitude. *Example:* "Strength and beauty in a bridge can never be combined." The author says the lesson is *not* that they compete. *Cure:* find the hedge in the text, then measure the choice against it.

**2. Reversal (REV).** *Tell:* the right topic with the direction flipped. *Common in:* detail, inference, tone. *Example:* "Residents find the older bridge delightful to cross." The passage says many find it dull. *Cure:* check the sign of the claim (praise or blame, more or less, cause or result).

**3. Half-right (HALF).** *Tell:* a faithful first clause followed by a clause the passage does not support. *Common in:* main idea, inference, detail. *Example:* "The footbridge is graceful, and it is therefore unsafe." Graceful is stated; unsafe is not (it sways, which some walkers dislike, but safety is never the question). *Cure:* test every clause on its own.

**4. True but not asked (TRUE).** *Tell:* the choice is stated in the passage but answers a different question. *Common in:* function, main idea, detail. *Example:* a question asks why the author mentions the sway; the choice says "The older bridge has heavy railings." True, but about a different point. *Cure:* restate the question in five words before you read the choices.

**5. Familiar phrase (FAM).** *Tell:* vivid words from the passage recombined into a claim the passage does not make. *Common in:* main idea, inference. *Example:* "Heavy railings make a bridge a triumph." The passage uses "heavy railings" and "triumph" in different sentences, and about different bridges. *Cure:* paraphrase the choice in your own words before accepting it.

**6. Out of scope (OUT).** *Tell:* a topic the passage never touches. *Common in:* inference, application, main idea. *Example:* "The footbridge cost more to build than the older bridge." Cost is never mentioned. *Cure:* point to a line, or reject it.

**7. Unsupported inference (INF).** *Tell:* a natural-sounding next step the text does not license. *Common in:* inference, application. *Example:* "Walkers who avoid the footbridge use the older bridge instead." The passage says some avoid the footbridge; it does not say where they go. *Cure:* ask what is stated and what you are adding.

**8. Misattributed view (MIS).** *Tell:* a view the passage reports (a critic's, an earlier scholar's, a common belief) given to the author. *Common in:* author's view, tone, main idea. *Example:* "The author holds that a bridge succeeds if it stands." The first sentence reports what engineers *usually mean*; the author then qualifies it. *Cure:* mark A or R on every paragraph and check who speaks.

**9. Wrong scope (SCOPE).** *Tell:* too narrow (a detail posing as the main idea) or too broad (a claim about everything). *Common in:* main idea, primary purpose. *Example, too narrow:* "The footbridge sways slightly in wind." *Too broad:* "All public structures should be designed for pleasure." *Cure:* match the choice to the Point on your map; the right one covers the whole passage and no more.

**10. Tone mismatch (TONE).** *Tell:* an attitude hotter or colder than the author's. *Common in:* tone, author's attitude. *Example:* "The author is scornful of engineers." The author notes what engineers mean and then adds the walker's view; nothing is scornful. *Cure:* match the choice to the author's hedges and evaluative words, and place it on the scale of intensity in [Lesson 2](chapter:5).

**11. Role confusion (ROLE).** *Tell:* a choice that says what a line states, not what it does. *Common in:* function questions. *Example:* "The author mentions the railings in order to describe their thickness." The railings are there as evidence that the older bridge is over-built and dull to cross. *Cure:* name the job in two words (supports, concedes, contrasts, qualifies) before you read the choices.

**12. Wrong direction (DIR).** *Tell:* a choice that strengthens when you were asked to weaken, or the reverse; or one that supports "except" when asked for "least." *Common in:* strengthen, weaken, assumption. *Example:* asked for a finding that would *weaken* the claim that walkers care about the feel of a crossing, a choice reports that most walkers say they choose routes for the way a crossing feels. That strengthens it. *Cure:* reread the stem's verb before you read the choices.

**13. Outside-knowledge lure (LURE).** *Tell:* a statement that may be true in life but that the passage never supports. *Common in:* inference, application, new information. *Example:* "Suspension bridges are generally stronger than beam bridges." It may sound like engineering sense; nothing here says it. *Cure:* use only the text; if you cannot find a line, the choice goes.

Praxis Tip: Write the abbreviations (EXT, REV, HALF, TRUE, FAM, OUT, INF, MIS, SCOPE, TONE, ROLE, DIR, LURE) once on an index card. They are only useful if you can write them faster than you can feel tempted.

**Part 3: Which traps hide where**

The table below shows the traps most often found next to each kind of question. It is a pointer for practice, not a law; any trap can appear anywhere.

| Question type | Traps to expect | First move |
|---|---|---|
| Main idea | SCOPE, EXT, HALF, MIS | Say the Point first |
| Detail | REV, FAM, TRUE | Find the line |
| Function | ROLE, TRUE, REV | Name the job |
| Tone or attitude | TONE, MIS, EXT | Match the hedges |
| Inference | INF, EXT, OUT, LURE | Defend it with a line |
| Application | OUT, LURE, FAM | State the principle |
| Strengthen or weaken | DIR, TRUE, OUT | Restate the claim |
| Assumption | DIR, TRUE, HALF | Apply the negation test |

Praxis Tip: When you have a miss, find the trap in the right-hand column that matches your wrong choice. If none does, add a new row to your own table, because your personal traps are the ones that matter most.

**Part 4: A short drill with the catalog**

Use the bridge passage above for these. Answer in your notebook, naming the trap in each case.

1. A main-idea question offers: "The newer footbridge is more successful than the older bridge." The passage presents the footbridge as graceful but flawed. Which trap?
   A. Reversal
   B. Misattributed view
   C. Unsupported inference
   D. Role confusion

2. A choice reads: "Engineers believe that a bridge succeeds when it stands, and for that reason they ignore walkers entirely." Which trap?
   A. Half-right
   B. Out of scope
   C. Wrong direction
   D. Outside-knowledge lure

3. A tone question offers: "The author is bitterly disappointed in modern design." Which trap?
   A. Familiar phrase
   B. Tone mismatch
   C. True but not asked
   D. Wrong scope

4. A weakener question asks which finding would most weaken the claim that walkers care about the feel of a crossing. Which choice commits the wrong-direction trap?
   A. Most walkers report that they pick routes by how a crossing feels
   B. Walkers use both bridges in nearly equal numbers
   C. Most walkers cannot say which bridge they used yesterday
   D. Walkers report no difference in how the two bridges feel

---

**Stop here.** Name the trap in each case before you read the answers.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** The passage says the footbridge is graceful and flawed; calling it "more successful" goes beyond the text, which does not rank the bridges. (A would flip a stated claim, which this does not; B would give the author a reported view, and no one else holds this one; D concerns the job of a line in a function question.)
2. **A.** The first clause is faithful (engineers usually mean it stands) and the second clause adds a claim that engineers "ignore walkers entirely," which the passage does not say. (B is wrong because walkers are discussed; C applies to strengthen and weaken questions; D is wrong because the choice has no outside fact in it.)
3. **B.** "Bitterly disappointed" is hotter than the measured, constructive tone of the passage. (A applies to recombined phrases; C to a choice stated in the passage but not asked about; D to a choice that is too narrow or too broad in a main-idea question.)
4. **A.** The finding strengthens the claim, so it is the wrong direction for a weakener question. (B, C and D would each weaken the claim to some degree, so none commits the trap.)

**Part 5: Putting it to use**

For the first weeks of practice, label every wrong choice you considered, not only the one you picked. After about ten passages you will see that three or four of the thirteen traps account for most of your near misses. Make index cards for those, with your own example from a practice set on the back. When you review an exam ([Strategy: Reviewing a CARS Practice Exam](chapter:20)), use the same names in your cause tags so that your error log and this catalog speak the same language.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: The commonest trap is not the one that looks most like a trap. It is the one that looks like a perfectly reasonable summary.

Praxis Tip: If a choice contains a word you never saw in the passage and cannot define from the passage, ask whether the writer is trying to sell you an outside fact.

Praxis Tip: When a question has an "except" or "least," rewrite the stem in your own words as a positive instruction ("find the three that the author says; the leftover is the answer"). Half the wrong answers in these questions are reversals of the stem, not of the passage.

Praxis Tip: Keep one page of your notebook for traps of your own invention. The examiner has not read it, which is the main reason it is useful.

Praxis Tip: A choice that is true of the passage as a whole may still be wrong for a question about one paragraph. Always check which unit the stem is asking about.
