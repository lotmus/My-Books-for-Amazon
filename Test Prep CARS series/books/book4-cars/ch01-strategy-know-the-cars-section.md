# Strategy: Know the CARS Section

**What you will be able to do**

- Describe the format of the Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills (CARS) section, and tell which facts you must confirm with the exam owner.
- State what the section tests, and just as important, what it does not test.
- Recognize the kinds of passages you will meet and the three families of questions that follow them.
- Use this ebook properly: scratch paper, timing, answer keys and the order in which to work.

**Why it matters**

Most of the MCAT is a test of what you know. CARS is a test of what you can do with a page of unfamiliar prose. That difference changes how you prepare. You cannot cram a passage on the history of architectural criticism, because you will not see that passage until test day, and you do not need to. What you need is a set of reading habits and reasoning habits that work on any passage, plus enough practice that they run without effort when the clock is on.

Many students treat CARS as the section that cannot be studied for. That belief is expensive. The section rewards a specific, trainable method, and this book teaches it. Your reward for taking it seriously is not only a better score, but also a section that feels like a manageable puzzle rather than a fog.

Praxis Tip: Do not skip the orientation chapters because "reading is something I already do." So is walking, and yet people take lessons in running.

**A note on using this ebook**

You are reading on a device, and a device does not let you write in it. Every exercise in this book assumes you have scratch paper or a notebook beside you. You will use it for three jobs:

1. **Passage maps.** A short outline of each passage, built as you read ([Strategy: The Active Reading Method](chapter:2) shows how).
2. **Answers before you look.** Write the letter of every answer before you open the key. Writing it commits you; looking at the key first only produces the feeling of having known it.
3. **Error log entries.** One line for every miss, naming the reason you missed it (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9 gives the template; the log itself lives in your notebook, not here).

Every set of questions in this book ends with a line that reads **Stop here.** Answers and explanations appear only after that line. Do not read past it until you have answered everything. If you read an ebook on a device that lets you jump to the end, resist the urge; the explanations are far less useful once you have seen them.

Praxis Tip: Keep a pencil and a pad of scratch paper permanently next to your reading device. If it is not within reach, it will not be used, which is the same as not owning it.

**What is tested**

The section tests **reading comprehension and reasoning applied to a text that you have never seen.** Concretely, you must be able to do the following, and the rest of this book is built around them:

- Identify what a passage is mainly about and what the author is trying to do with it.
- Follow how the argument is built: which statements are claims, which are support, which are objections the author raises or answers.
- Distinguish the author's own view from views the author reports, quotes or mocks.
- Judge the author's attitude from word choice and emphasis.
- Draw conclusions that the text supports without stating, and refuse conclusions that it does not support.
- Apply the author's reasoning to a situation the passage never mentions, and decide how new information would help or hurt an argument.

**What is not tested**

The most important sentence in this chapter is this one: **you are not expected to bring outside knowledge to the section.** [VERIFY] The passage supplies what you need. A question may ask about an unfamiliar thinker, a period you never studied, or a term you have never met, and the right answer is always findable in, or soundly reasoned from, the words on the page.

This has a practical consequence that surprises strong students. If you happen to know a lot about the topic, **that knowledge is a hazard rather than an asset.** A choice can be true in the real world, supported by everything you learned in a seminar, and still be wrong, because the passage does not say it or imply it. The question is never "Is this true?" It is "Does this passage support it?" or "Would the author agree?"

You are also not tested on vocabulary lists, grammar rules, or the memorized names of rhetorical devices. You will see a word you do not know now and then. Context usually reveals enough of its meaning to answer the question, and when it does not, the question did not need the word.

**Worked Example 1: Is this answer allowed?**

Read this short passage and the question that follows. Use only the text.

*Passage.* The town council defended its decision to move the market from the harbor to the hilltop by pointing to flooding. Merchants countered that the harbor was where the customers were. The council's own survey, however, showed that most shoppers already arrived by the hill road, and that harbor floods had closed the market on only three days in the past decade. The council's defense, in other words, rested on a danger it had barely documented.

*Question.* The author's discussion of the survey most strongly suggests that the council's decision was
A. supported mainly by merchants' complaints
B. weakly justified by the council's own evidence
C. necessary because the harbor was unsafe
D. opposed by most shoppers

*Working.* The author reports that the survey showed floods closed the market on only three days in ten years, and calls the danger "barely documented." That makes B the answer the text supports. Choice A reverses things: the merchants objected. Choice C repeats the council's claim, which the author is undermining. Choice D is a stretch; the survey says shoppers already used the hill road, which does not show opposition. Notice that nothing here required knowledge of markets, harbors or town government. If you caught yourself thinking "councils usually have good reasons," you were reaching outside the page, and that thought should be dropped.

Praxis Tip: When a choice feels obviously true but you cannot point to a sentence that supports it, treat that feeling as a warning light. Real-world truth is not a credential on this section.

**Passage types**

The passages come from the humanities and the social sciences. [VERIFY] The two groups behave differently, and Lessons 7 and 8 treat each in detail.

**Humanities passages** discuss art, literature, music, philosophy, ethics, architecture and the history of ideas. They are often interpretive: the author argues for a way of seeing a work, a movement or a question. Expect figurative language, a distinct authorial voice, and reasoning that moves by example and contrast rather than by data. The tone may be admiring, sardonic, nostalgic or exasperated.

**Social-science passages** discuss economics, sociology, anthropology, political thought, education and linguistics. They are often explanatory or evaluative: the author describes a model, tests it against evidence, and says where it falls short. Expect claims about how groups behave, mentions of studies or cases, and a careful tone that qualifies its conclusions.

Within either group, passages also differ in **purpose**. Some mainly explain a topic. Some argue a position. Some reflect, building toward an insight rather than a thesis. Some mainly review and criticize other people's views. Naming the purpose within the first paragraph or so tells you what to expect from the rest.

Praxis Tip: Do not choose a favorite subject and rush toward it. Your job is to read well on whatever arrives, including the passage about nineteenth-century opera librettos you were secretly dreading.

**The three skill families**

The questions are designed to measure three families of skill. [VERIFY] The shares below are my understanding of the public description; confirm them, and treat them as approximate.

| Skill family | Short description | Approx. share |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations of comprehension | What the text says and does | About 30% [VERIFY] |
| Reasoning within the text | How its parts work together | About 30% [VERIFY] |
| Reasoning beyond the text | Applying, extending, testing | About 40% [VERIFY] |

**Foundations of comprehension.** These questions ask whether you understood the passage as written: the main idea, the point of a paragraph, the meaning of a term in context, what the author says about a particular matter. The answer is on the page, sometimes in restated form.

**Reasoning within the text.** These questions ask you to see how the passage fits together: why the author mentions an example, what role a paragraph plays, what a statement implies, what the author's attitude is, how two claims relate. The answer is not stated outright, but it is determined by the text.

**Reasoning beyond the text.** These questions ask you to take the passage's ideas somewhere new: apply the author's principle to a fresh case, judge which additional fact would strengthen or weaken the argument, identify an analogy to the author's reasoning, or predict what the author would say about a related issue. The passage is the starting point, not the whole territory.

The three families are not three separate difficulty levels. A foundations question can be hard because the passage is dense, and a beyond-the-text question can be easy because the principle is clear. [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3) gives a way to identify the family of any question and the matching way to answer.

**How this book works**

The book has four parts.

- **Part 0 (Chapters 1 to 3)** orients you and teaches the two core procedures: mapping a passage while you read, and recognizing and answering the three question families.
- **Part 1 (Chapters 4 to 11)** is eight skill lessons. Each one teaches a skill, works through short examples with answers explained, and ends with seven checkpoint questions, an answer key after **Stop here.**, and a teach-back and review plan. Lessons 7 and 8 focus on the two kinds of passage.
- **Part 2 (Chapters 12 to 15)** is four practice sets of full passages with detailed explanations of why each wrong choice is wrong.
- **Part 3 (Chapters 16 to 22)** covers pacing, elimination and trap answers, and two full-length CARS exams, each split into two parts so that you can take it in two sittings, along with a chapter on reviewing your results.

The appendices (a source ledger, passage-mapping templates and a trap catalog, and a glossary) are for reference. Every passage in this book was written for it. None is taken or adapted from a published source, and none depends on knowledge outside the page.

A sensible order is to read Part 0 in one weekend, work one lesson per sitting through Part 1, and begin Part 2 as soon as you have finished Lesson 3 if you need more practice earlier. Mixed practice beats blocked practice (Core Test Skills, Chapter 8), so once you have started the practice sets, alternate humanities and social-science passages instead of doing all of one kind in a row.

Praxis Tip: Practice CARS almost daily and briefly. One passage a day, carefully reviewed, builds more than a Saturday marathon followed by a week of avoidance.

**Common Mistakes**

- Studying CARS as if it were a content section and hunting for "what to memorize."
- Letting real-world knowledge pick the answer when the passage says something different.
- Reading the questions first and the passage as a mere hunting ground, which tends to produce shallow understanding.
- Treating the three skill families as topics to study separately rather than as ways of asking about one passage.
- Never reviewing: a passage not reviewed is a passage mostly wasted.
- Relying on rumors about the format instead of confirming it with the exam owner.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Expect to find a few passages dull. Dull is a feeling about the passage, not a fact about the questions, which are as sharp as ever.

Praxis Tip: Practice at the real time limit early, even if you score badly. You need to know what the pace feels like, and the sooner the unpleasant surprise comes, the cheaper it is.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. A student who has studied musicology for years meets a passage about a composer. One answer choice states a well-known fact about the composer's later life that the passage never mentions. The best response is to
   A. choose it, because a true statement is always safe
   B. avoid it unless the passage supports it, because the section tests the text rather than outside knowledge
   C. choose it, because expert students are expected to use their background
   D. ignore the passage and answer from memory

2. The passage says a guild rejected a new loom because it could be used without years of training. A question asks, "According to the author, why did the guild reject the loom?" This question mainly tests
   A. reasoning beyond the text
   B. reasoning within the text, because the answer is unstated
   C. outside knowledge about guilds
   D. foundations of comprehension

3. A question asks, "Which of the following new findings, if true, would most weaken the author's argument?" This question belongs mainly to the family of
   A. reasoning beyond the text
   B. foundations of comprehension
   C. vocabulary knowledge
   D. reasoning within the text

4. Which use of this ebook is best?
   A. Read each passage on the device and answer the questions in your head
   B. Read the answer key first to learn the pattern, then do the questions
   C. Work a passage on scratch paper, write every answer, then read the key after the stop line
   D. Skip the questions and read only the explanations

5. Why is a passage on an unfamiliar topic not, in principle, a disadvantage?
   A. The questions are meant to be answered from the passage, so the needed information is supplied
   B. Unfamiliar topics are always easier than familiar ones
   C. The passages are always about topics every adult knows
   D. Questions about unfamiliar topics are scored more leniently

6. Based on the format as understood in this chapter [VERIFY], about how much time is there for each passage and its questions?
   A. Five minutes
   B. Twenty minutes
   C. Fifteen minutes
   D. Ten minutes

7. Which task is an example of reasoning beyond the text?
   A. Stating the passage's main idea in your own words
   B. Deciding how the author's stated principle would apply to a new case the passage never mentions
   C. Finding the paragraph in which the author first mentions a term
   D. Defining a word from its use in a sentence

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** The section is built so that the passage supplies what is needed, and a choice must be supported by it. (A treats real-world truth as sufficient, which it is not; C invents an expectation that the section does not have; D abandons the text altogether.)
2. **D.** A question about what the author says, and why in the author's own account, asks for comprehension of a stated point. The word "because" in the passage makes the reason explicit. (A is wrong because nothing new is being applied; B is wrong because the reason is stated, not merely implied; C is wrong because no outside knowledge is needed.)
3. **A.** Judging how new information affects the argument takes the passage outward into a hypothetical. (B asks only what the text says; C is not a skill family; D concerns how the existing parts fit, not new evidence.)
4. **C.** Writing the answers commits you to them, and the key is useful only after that. (A gives no commitment and no record; B turns practice into reading answers; D skips the retrieval that does the learning.)
5. **A.** The design means the passage carries the information. (B overstates, since an unfamiliar topic can still be hard; C is false, because the topics can be unfamiliar; D invents a scoring policy that does not exist.)
6. **D.** Ninety minutes divided among about nine passages gives about ten minutes each. (A would leave too little time to read; B and C each overspend and would run the clock out well before the last passage.)
7. **B.** Applying the author's principle to a new case goes beyond the passage. (A is comprehension; C is a locating task within the text; D is a context-based vocabulary task.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, name the slip behind each miss (outside knowledge, wrong family, misread time) and log it in your error log (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9).

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "CARS is, as I understand it, about 53 questions in 90 minutes over about nine passages from the humanities and social sciences; it tests comprehension and reasoning from the text alone, with no outside knowledge required, in three families: foundations of comprehension, reasoning within the text, and reasoning beyond the text; and I will confirm every format fact with the exam owner."

*Review schedule:* revisit this chapter after 1 day (redo Worked Example 1 without looking), after 3 days (answer the checkpoint questions from a blank page), after 7 days (sort five questions from any later set into the three families), and after 21 days (explain the section to someone who has never seen it).

*Interleave:* once you start the lessons, put a short family-sorting exercise at the start of each session: take three question stems from your last practice and name the family of each.

Praxis Tip: At the end of the week, write on a card the three families and one sentence for each. Pin it where you study; it is the cheapest piece of equipment in this course.
