# Lesson 2: Tone, Attitude and Author's Purpose

**What you will be able to do**

- Distinguish tone, attitude and purpose, and say which one a question is asking about.
- Find the evidence for an author's attitude in word choice, qualifiers, concessions and contrasts.
- Calibrate your answer to the right intensity (neither too mild nor too strong), using the passage's own wording.
- Separate the author's attitude from the attitudes of people the author reports, and recognize irony.
- Name an author's purpose with a precise verb, such as argue, explain, caution, reconcile or illustrate.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Tone and purpose questions sit in the foundations-of-comprehension and reasoning-within-the-text families described in [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3). They are often the questions candidates feel are subjective, and so guess at. They are not subjective. The author left evidence on the page, and the correct choice is the one whose strength and direction match that evidence. The CARS section is understood to draw its passages from the humanities and social sciences, where authors are rarely neutral for long [VERIFY: confirm current passage sources on the exam owner's free format page]. Learning to read attitude is therefore not a luxury. It is a repeatable method.

This lesson builds on [Lesson 1](chapter:4). Once you know what a passage argues, you ask how the author feels about it, and why the author wrote it.

Praxis Tip: Attitude is evidence-based, not mind-reading. If you cannot point to the words that prove your answer, you are guessing, however confident you feel. Confidence, as every committee knows, is not evidence.

**Concept Review**

**Three related ideas.**

- **Attitude** is the author's evaluation of a subject, a person, a view or an event: approving, doubtful, admiring, critical, indifferent.
- **Tone** is how that attitude sounds on the page: measured, wry, urgent, detached, ironic. In practice, exam questions use the two words almost interchangeably.
- **Purpose** is what the author is trying to do: to inform, to argue, to caution, to correct, to illustrate, to reconcile two views, to defend a position against criticism.

A single passage has one overall attitude toward its main subject, but it may report other attitudes belonging to people the author mentions. Both appear in questions, so keep track of whose attitude is whose ([Lesson 3](chapter:6) develops this).

**Where attitude hides.**

1. **Connotation.** Words with the same denotation can carry different feelings: *thrifty* and *stingy*; *determined* and *stubborn*; *experiment* and *meddling*. The word the author picks is a vote.
2. **Evaluative words.** Adjectives and adverbs that judge: *shrewd, naive, sadly, regrettably, to its credit, remarkably.*
3. **Qualifiers and hedges.** *Perhaps, may, seems, largely, in part, at best.* Hedges signal caution or limited commitment. Their absence signals strong commitment.
4. **Concession and contrast.** *Admittedly, granted, of course... but.* The author concedes something to be fair, then asserts the real point. The attitude lives after the "but."
5. **Scare quotes and sarcasm.** Quotation marks around a word often mean "so-called" and signal doubt. A word that does not fit its context (praise applied to a failure) signals irony.
6. **Rhetorical questions.** A question the author does not intend to have answered usually signals the opposite of what it asks.
7. **What the author chooses to include.** An example that makes a view look foolish is an attitude in itself.

Praxis Tip: Read the adjectives and adverbs twice. The nouns tell you what is being discussed; the modifiers tell you how the author feels about it.

**A scale of intensity.** Attitudes come in strengths. Here is a rough ladder for common tone words:

| Direction | Mild | Moderate | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Favorable | appreciative, approving | admiring, sympathetic | enthusiastic, reverent |
| Unfavorable | doubtful, wary | critical, skeptical | scornful, dismissive |
| Neutral | objective, descriptive | analytical, balanced | detached |
| Mixed | qualified, tempered | ambivalent | conflicted |

The scale matters because wrong answers are frequently the right direction at the wrong strength. If the author writes "the proposal has merit but is incomplete," *critical* may be a bit hot, *scornful* is wrong, *qualified approval* fits. As a general pattern (not a rule you can rely on blindly), authors in humanities and social-science passages are measured rather than extreme, and the best answer is usually moderate and well supported [VERIFY: this is a general observation about published practice, not an official statement].

Praxis Tip: Pick the direction first (favorable, unfavorable, mixed, neutral), then the strength. Two steps are easier than one big guess among eight adjectives.

**Neutral is not the same as mixed.** A neutral author reports without evaluating. A mixed (ambivalent or qualified) author evaluates, but in two directions: some praise, some criticism. If a passage says an idea is "useful in some respects and misleading in others," the attitude is mixed, not neutral. Choosing "neutral" for a mixed passage is a common error.

**Irony.** Irony says one thing and means another, or describes an outcome as the reverse of what was intended. Signals: praise that cannot be sincere given the facts that follow; a "success" described with details of failure; a contrast between what was announced and what happened. The author's attitude toward the subject is then critical, even though the surface words sound complimentary. You do not need to find the passage funny; irony in an exam passage is usually dry and quiet.

**Reported attitude versus the author's attitude.** When the author writes "Some critics dismiss the novel as slight," the word *dismiss* belongs to the critics. The author's own view is expressed in the next sentence, if at all. Ask: does the author endorse the view, reject it, or merely report it? Ask the same question about every "some argue," "it is often said" and "many assume."

**Purpose: choosing the verb.** Match the verb to what the author does:

- **Explain or describe:** the passage lays out how something works or what happened, with no pivot against another view.
- **Argue or defend:** there is a thesis, evidence, and often an objection answered.
- **Challenge, correct or question:** there is a common view and the author shows it is incomplete or mistaken.
- **Caution or warn:** the author urges care about an inference, a policy or an assumption.
- **Illustrate:** a case is used to show a principle.
- **Reconcile or synthesize:** two views are shown to be compatible, or a middle position is built.
- **Evaluate or assess:** the author weighs strengths and weaknesses.

The verb must match the passage's whole shape. A single sentence of criticism does not turn an explanation into an attack.

Praxis Tip: When a purpose question appears, first say what the author does in the final paragraph. Then check the answer choice's verb against that. A choice with the right content and the wrong verb ("to complain" for "to explain") is a trap.

**Worked Example 1: Marking words that show attitude (skill: connotation and qualification)**

*Passage.* The late novels of Ilse Varden are often called difficult, and so they are, but the word is used as though it were a verdict. Varden asks readers to hold a sentence open for half a page before it resolves, and she withholds the names of her characters until the reader has formed a loyalty to them. This is a demanding bargain. Yet the reward is considerable: scenes of ordinary life gain an attention that easier books cannot give them. One can only wish more novelists risked as much.

*Marking.* "Often called difficult... but the word is used as though it were a verdict" (the author resists a dismissal). "Demanding bargain" (neutral to slightly cautionary). "Yet the reward is considerable" (favorable). "One can only wish more novelists risked as much" (strong approval, but stated as a wish).

*Direction:* favorable, with an acknowledged cost. *Strength:* moderate to strong. *Best label:* admiring, with candor about the difficulty. "Enthusiastic" overshoots, because the author concedes the books are demanding. "Neutral" ignores the evaluative language. "Critical" reverses the direction.

**Worked Example 2: Irony (skill: praise that cannot be sincere)**

*Passage.* The reform was announced as a revolution in how the school would assess its students. In place of a grade, every pupil would receive a written comment. Within a year the comments had been standardized into a menu of eleven phrases, each corresponding exactly to a former grade. The revolution, it seems, had succeeded in changing the vocabulary while sparing everything else.

*Question.* The author's attitude toward the reform is best described as

A. enthusiastic
B. ironic and critical
C. neutral and informative
D. nostalgic

*Reasoning.* The words "revolution" and "succeeded" are praise, but the facts that follow undercut them: the new comments map exactly onto the old grades. A claim of success contradicted by the evidence is irony. The direction is unfavorable (the reform changed nothing), the strength moderate and dry. **B**. **A** takes the surface praise at face value. **C** ignores "it seems" and the pointed last sentence. **D** invents a longing for the past that the passage never mentions.

**Worked Example 3: Attitude toward a reported view (skill: sympathetic but qualified)**

*Passage.* Proponents of "slow reading" urge us to read fewer books, more deeply, with the phone in another room. Their instinct is sound: a mind that is constantly interrupted cannot hold a long argument. But their claim that speed itself destroys comprehension is overstated. Skilled readers move quickly through familiar ground and slow down at difficult passages, which is a form of attention, not its absence. The movement is right about distraction and wrong about speed.

*Question.* The author's attitude toward the slow-reading movement is best described as

A. contemptuous
B. uncritically admiring
C. sympathetic but qualified
D. indifferent

*Reasoning.* Direction: mixed, leaning favorable ("sound," "right about distraction"). The qualifier ("overstated," "wrong about speed") limits the agreement. Strength: moderate. **C**. **A** is the wrong direction and far too strong. **B** ignores the criticism. **D** would require no evaluation at all, but the author evaluates in nearly every sentence.

**Worked Example 4: Naming the purpose (skill: verbs)**

Choose the best purpose verb for each short passage.

(i) "A glacier moves because its own weight forces the ice downhill. Ice at the bottom flows slowly under the pressure of ice above, and meltwater at the base lets the whole mass slide." Verb: **explain**. There is a mechanism and no opposing view.

(ii) "It is often assumed that a longer sentence is a harder one. But difficulty comes from the number of ideas packed into a sentence, not from its length: a long list of simple items is easy, and a short proposition about an abstract relation is not." Verb: **correct** (or challenge). A common assumption is addressed and revised.

(iii) "The council's plan treats traffic and housing as separate questions. They are not. A housing development that is placed far from jobs creates traffic, and a road that is placed through the center of a neighborhood destroys housing. Any sensible plan will take both at once." Verb: **argue** (a thesis with reasons and a prescription).

Praxis Tip: If a purpose choice contains a verb you would not use to describe your own sentences ("lament," "ridicule," "extol"), it is usually too strong for this kind of passage.

**Worked Example 5: Calibrating intensity on a quiet passage (skill: neutral versus critical)**

*Passage.* The Treaty of the Salt Roads, concluded after nine months of talks, fixed the toll on caravans at one tenth of the cargo and obliged each signatory to keep its stretch of road in repair. Within a decade the northern signatories had let their stretch decay, and traders began to avoid it. The treaty's collapse is sometimes laid at the door of the northern rulers' neglect; it may equally reflect the fact that one tenth was a toll few merchants could afford.

*Question.* Which best describes the author's tone?

A. Accusatory
B. Analytical and even-handed
C. Nostalgic
D. Enthusiastic

*Reasoning.* The passage reports events in plain language. Where it offers explanations, it offers two ("sometimes laid at the door of... it may equally reflect"), which is hedged and balanced. There is no loaded word. Direction: neutral. Strength: moderate (analytical, because it weighs causes). **B**. **A** is the wrong direction; the author declines to blame the northern rulers alone. **C** and **D** add feelings that no word supports.

Notice that "neglect" and "decay" are negative words, but they describe what happened to the road, not the author's attitude toward anyone. Do not mistake negative subject matter for a negative author.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Read for the author's own voice. In "Critics have called the film shallow," the author has not yet said it is. Verbs of report (*claim, hold, maintain, say*) separate the speaker from the claim.

Praxis Tip: A single loaded word is not an attitude, any more than one swallow is a summer. Look for a pattern across the passage before committing.

Praxis Tip: If every answer choice seems too strong, the answer is the one with the hedge ("qualified," "measured," "cautious").

Praxis Tip: Quotation marks around an ordinary word are often an eyebrow raised in print. Ask whether the author would say the word without them.

Praxis Tip: Do not choose "neutral" merely because the prose is calm. A calm voice can deliver a firm verdict, and the quieter the voice, the more careful you need to be with the evidence.

Praxis Tip: For "primary purpose" questions, rehearse a pair of verbs before looking at the choices: what does the author do, and what does the author *not* do? Rule out the verbs that fit one paragraph only.

**Common Mistakes**

- Treating the attitude of a person the author reports as the author's own.
- Choosing the right direction at the wrong strength (scornful for skeptical, enthusiastic for approving).
- Calling a mixed passage neutral.
- Mistaking a negative subject (war, decline, failure) for a negative author.
- Taking surface praise in an ironic passage literally.
- Choosing a purpose verb that fits one paragraph but not the whole passage.
- Importing a feeling (nostalgia, anger, alarm) that no word of the passage supports.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. *Passage.* The architect's early houses were praised for their honesty. Her later public buildings, by contrast, are described by admirers as "confident," which is a polite way of saying that they do not trouble to explain themselves. A visitor must work out where the entrance is. It is hard to say whether this is courage or carelessness, and the architect has never said.

   The author's attitude toward the later buildings is best described as
   A. mixed, with some doubt about their merit
   B. openly contemptuous
   C. uncritically admiring
   D. indifferent and purely descriptive

2. *Passage.* The company's new open-plan floor was described in its brochure as a "space for creative collaboration." On the first day, forty employees found that the only quiet place in the building was the stairwell, and by the end of the week the stairwell had a queue.

   The phrase "space for creative collaboration" is most likely placed in quotation marks in order to
   A. show that the author fully endorses the company's description
   B. indicate that the phrase is a direct quotation from an employee
   C. express the author's doubt that the description matches reality
   D. emphasize that creative collaboration is generally impossible in offices

3. *Passage.* Some historians dismiss folk songs as the raw material of real music, valuable only until a trained composer arrives. This view is held with great confidence and very little evidence. A good folk song has survived hundreds of performances by amateurs who changed what did not work, which is a kind of editing few composers can match.

   The author's attitude toward the historians' view is best described as
   A. approving
   B. skeptical
   C. neutral
   D. resigned

4. *Passage.* Can a city be said to have a character? Residents insist that it can, and visitors seem to agree. But the evidence is disappointingly thin. The people who describe a city as "friendly" or "hurried" have, almost without exception, met only a few hundred of its citizens, and those few were mostly people who serve visitors. A city's character, on this evidence, is closer to a reputation than to a fact.

   The primary purpose of the passage is to
   A. demonstrate that every city has a distinct and measurable character
   B. celebrate the friendliness of city residents toward visitors
   C. describe how visitors collect their impressions of a city
   D. question the evidence for a common belief

5. *Passage.* A museum director announced that visitors would now be welcomed more warmly than ever, because the number of staff on the gallery floor had been cut by half, so that nothing need come between the visitor and the art. The critic who reported the announcement added only: "A welcome, evidently, is measured by the absence of welcomers."

   The critic's closing remark is best characterized as
   A. a sincere endorsement of the director's reasoning
   B. a neutral summary of the policy and its stated reasoning
   C. an angry denunciation of the museum's staff
   D. ironic, showing the policy defeats its own aim

6. *Passage.* Critics have called the festival a failure, because attendance fell for three years. This ignores what the festival was founded to do, which was to give local musicians a stage, not to fill a stadium. By that standard, the festival has done well, though it would be foolish to pretend that a smaller audience costs nothing.

   Which statement best describes the author's attitude toward the festival?
   A. Enthusiastic and without reservation
   B. Approving, but with an acknowledged cost
   C. Disappointed that it did not fill a stadium
   D. Neutral, because the author reports facts about attendance

7. *Passage.* The new tax is estimated to raise a modest sum, and its supporters present this as proof of fairness: everyone pays a little. Its opponents say the same fact proves it is pointless. Both sides assume that the size of the sum settles the question. It does not. A tax can raise little and still change behavior, and a tax can raise a great deal and change nothing.

   The author's attitude toward the dispute between supporters and opponents is best described as
   A. critical of both sides for sharing a flawed assumption
   B. sympathetic to the supporters but doubtful of the opponents
   C. neutral, merely reporting the two positions
   D. entirely indifferent to the outcome of the tax

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** The author reports admiration, hints at fault, and declines to decide, which is a qualified, doubtful, mixed attitude. (B is stronger than the passage supports; the author says it is hard to say which; C ignores the pointed remark that admirers use "confident" as a polite word; D overlooks the evaluative language ("a polite way of saying," "carelessness").)
2. **C.** The quotation marks plus the stairwell queue create irony; the author keeps a distance from the brochure's claim. (A reverses the function of the quotation marks, which here signal distance; B is unsupported; the passage attributes the phrase to the brochure; D is too extreme and goes beyond the passage's single example.)
3. **B.** The author rejects the view with a measured but pointed remark about confidence and evidence; skeptical matches in direction and strength. (A reverses the direction; the author says the view has "very little evidence"; C ignores the evaluative comment on the view's lack of evidence; D imports a feeling of acceptance that no word supports.)
4. **D.** The author raises a question, examines the evidence for a common belief and concludes it is weak. (A reverses the author's doubt and is too extreme; B borrows a word from the passage and misses its critical purpose; C is a detail used as evidence, not the author's purpose.)
5. **D.** Defining a welcome by the absence of welcomers exposes the contradiction in the director's claim; the tone is dry and critical. (A reverses the remark, which mocks the reasoning; B overlooks the evaluative edge of "evidently"; C is the wrong target and too strong, since the staff were the ones cut.)
6. **B.** The author rejects the critics' standard, judges the festival a success by its own standard and concedes a cost, which is qualified approval. (A overshoots the strength, since the author concedes a smaller audience costs something; C attributes the critics' standard to the author; D overlooks the evaluative claim that the festival "has done well".)
7. **A.** Both sides are said to "assume" that the size settles the question, and the author states that it does not. The criticism is measured and even-handed. (B is unsupported; the author treats both sides alike; C ignores the pointed verdict "It does not"; D goes beyond the passage, which shows interest in the reasoning, not in the outcome.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, for every miss write down the word or phrase you should have used as evidence, and whether your error was in direction, strength or voice. These three labels make a useful column in your error log (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9).
