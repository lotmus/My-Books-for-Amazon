**Passage 1 (questions 1-6)**

Planners often defend a major project, such as an expressway cut through an old district, by appeal to a compensation test. The test asks whether the people who gain from the project gain enough that they could pay the people who lose and still come out ahead. If so, the project is declared an improvement, even if no payment is ever made. The test has a respectable pedigree and an obvious appeal, since it turns a quarrel about values into a sum.

The flaw is easy to state: the test asks whether compensation could be paid, not whether it will be. A project that passes the test but leaves its losers uncompensated has made some people richer and others poorer, and the sum conceals the transfer. Defenders reply that over many projects the losses and gains even out, since the household flattened by one road will profit from another. This is an empirical claim, and it is rarely checked. There is reason to doubt it. The losers of such projects tend to be the same households each time, those with little money and nowhere else to go, because land in their districts is the cheapest to acquire. The gainers, meanwhile, tend to be the same households each time as well, because they are the ones who live near the new road and own the land beside it.

A second difficulty concerns what counts as a loss. Compensation is calculated at market value, but market value measures what a home would fetch from a stranger, not what it is worth to the person who lives in it. A family of forty years' standing has a network of neighbors, a school within walking distance, a shopkeeper who extends credit. None of this appears in the price. When such a family is paid the price and moved, it receives the full value of the asset and loses most of what made the asset valuable to it. Planners may say that these losses cannot be measured. But a loss that is hard to measure is not thereby zero, and to treat it as zero is to count what is easy to count as though it were all that matters.

A planner might fairly object that a rule requiring unanimous consent would end all building, since any project will have someone who prefers things as they are. The objection is correct, and it shows that the alternative to the compensation test cannot be a veto. It does not follow that the test should be accepted as it stands. A more defensible rule would require that compensation actually be paid, and paid at a level that includes the cost of relocation and the loss of community ties: the standard of what the displaced would accept, not what a stranger would pay. Such a rule would make some projects too costly to build. That is not a defect of the rule but the rule working as intended, since a project whose gains cannot cover its true costs was never an improvement. The projects that survive it would be better projects.

**Passage 2 (questions 7-11)**

A familiar complaint holds that each new generation of speakers is lazy: they swallow final consonants, slur vowels, and clip words that their elders pronounced in full. The complaint rests on an explanation as much as on an observation. The observation is that pronunciation changes; the explanation is that it changes because people cannot be bothered. The first is true. The second is much less convincing than it appears.

Consider how laziness would have to work. A lazy speaker should economize wherever effort can be saved, and should do so unsystematically, taking whatever shortcut is nearest. Sound change does not behave that way. When a community begins to drop a final sound, it typically drops it in every word where that sound occurs in that position, and only there. The same sound is pronounced with care at the start of words, and in other stretches of the same community's history, harder sounds have been added where the older speech lacked them. Laziness predicts scattered shortcuts; what is observed is a rule applied across the whole vocabulary. A rule is a form of discipline, and speakers who follow one are doing something other than slacking.

A defender of the laziness view might reply that regularity is compatible with laziness: a single easier habit, once adopted, would naturally apply everywhere it can. This is a fair point, and it forces the critic to concede that the pattern alone does not refute the view. But the reply changes what the view says. If laziness merely names whatever habit happens to arise, it explains nothing about why that habit arose and not another. The same speakers who are charged with dropping a sound in one century will, in another, begin pronouncing a sound their ancestors omitted, and the charge will have to be dropped or reversed. A theory that is satisfied by change in either direction predicts nothing.

A better candidate for explanation looks to how children learn. Children do not receive the older generation's rules; they infer rules from what they hear, and a slight shift in how often a sound is clipped in casual speech can lead a child to infer a slightly different rule. Nothing in this process requires anyone to be careless. A child who infers a different rule is being exactly as careful as her parents were, with slightly different evidence. The result will look to the older generation like decay, because it differs from what they learned, but a difference is not a loss. The older speaker's own pronunciation is, after all, the residue of earlier changes that were once met with the same complaint.

This account is not complete. It does not say why one shift in casual speech is picked up by children and another is not, and until that is explained the question of why languages change in the particular ways they do remains partly open. It has the merit, at least, of not explaining everything by a fault that no one can observe.

**Passage 3 (questions 12-17)**

Free services are not free. The newspaper that costs nothing to read online, the video platform with no subscription, the map that asks for nothing are all paid for, and the currency is attention. This is usually put as a slogan, that if you are not paying, you are the product, but the slogan is less useful than the economics beneath it. Attention differs from money in one respect that matters: there is a fixed amount of it. A person can earn more money, but no one has more than a day's waking hours to spend. Where a resource is fixed and many parties compete to claim it, competition does not make the resource more plentiful; it raises the cost of winning a share.

Some economists conclude that the market for attention is a market like any other, and that people who spend attention on trivial things are revealing what they prefer. The conclusion is too quick. In ordinary markets a buyer weighs a price against the value of what is received. Attention is spent differently: it is taken, through design choices such as notifications, endless feeds and automatic playing of the next video, that lower the cost of continuing until stopping, not continuing, is the act that requires a decision. A buyer who must make a decision to stop each purchase is not in the situation that the argument from revealed preference imagines.

Defenders have a serious reply: every product is designed to be attractive, and no one calls a well-made chair manipulative. The difference, however, is not that attractive design exists but what the design is attractive for. A chair succeeds when sitting in it is comfortable and the sitter is later glad to have bought it. A feed succeeds when the user stays on it, whether or not she is later glad. The first measures success by the buyer's satisfaction, the second by the buyer's time. Where the two come apart, the designer's incentive is to prefer the time.

The wider cost is less visible. When attention is the scarce resource, a growing supply of things to look at does not enrich people; it sharpens the competition for the one thing they cannot make more of. Some of the cost falls on people who never chose the service at all: the family at dinner, each member elsewhere, pays for what each member's screen collects.

The remedy need not be prohibition. Designers could be required to disclose how much time their products take, and stopping could be made the easy default rather than the hard decision, in the way that a well-run shop does not hide its door. None of this shows that attention-funded services are on balance harmful, and many users are well served by them. It shows only that the inference from "people keep using it" to "people want it" is weaker than it is usually taken to be, and that a market in which the seller sets the cost of leaving is not a market in which use can be read as consent.

**Answers and explanations**

1. **C.** The passage argues that the test says only that compensation could be paid, and proposes a rule that it actually be paid at a level that includes true losses. [Foundations] (A is too extreme: the author does not reject the test outright or say all displacement is harm, and paragraph 4 accepts that a veto is no alternative; B is unsupported: no motive of avoiding consultation is attributed to planners; D reverses the claim, since the passage says the sum conceals a transfer from losers and gives no support for a majority being made richer.)
2. **A.** Paragraph 3 says that market value 'measures what a home would fetch from a stranger, not what it is worth to the person who lives in it.' [Foundations] (B is unsupported: no one is said to set the figure low on purpose; C reverses the point, since the family is said to lose value, not to be overpaid; D is true-sounding but out of scope: replacement cost is never discussed.)
3. **D.** Paragraph 2 calls it 'an empirical claim' that 'is rarely checked,' and then gives reason to doubt it. [Within] (A reverses the author's position; B is too extreme: the author calls the claim unchecked, not deceitful; C misreads the passage, which treats the claim as a reply to the flaw and answers it.)
4. **B.** The doubt rests on the statement that the losers and the gainers tend to be the same households each time, which requires that losers do not also gain later. [Within] (A is true-sounding but not required, since the argument concerns who bears losses, not how they feel about payments; C goes beyond the text, because the argument needs only a tendency, not foresight; D concerns the rule discussed in paragraph 4, not this doubt.)
5. **D.** If many displaced households later became main beneficiaries, the claim that losses and gains even out gains support. [Beyond] (A strengthens the doubt by explaining why the poor are displaced repeatedly; B concerns whether payments cover losses, not whether losses and gains even out across projects; C is irrelevant: planners' support for a rule says nothing about who gains or loses.)
6. **A.** The author's point is that a payment equal to market price omits the value that the thing had for its particular holder, which is the family's objection to the appraised price of the clock. [Beyond] (B reverses the structure, since the farmer is paid more rather than less than his loss; C is the unanimous-consent veto that paragraph 4 discusses and rejects; D parallels the paragraph 2 point about whether payment will be made, not the paragraph 3 point about what counts as a loss.)
7. **B.** The passage criticizes the laziness explanation in paragraphs 2 and 3, and then proposes the child-learning account in paragraph 4. [Foundations] (A is the view the author argues against; C is too extreme: the author says the account is incomplete and that the question 'remains partly open'; D contradicts the statement that 'a difference is not a loss.')
8. **C.** The sentence follows the observation that laziness 'merely names whatever habit happens to arise,' and so objects to the reply by saying the view then explains nothing in particular. [Within] (A reverses the paragraph, which grants a fair point and then argues that the reply changes the view; B is unsupported: the passage gives no figures about how common each direction is; D misplaces the paragraph, since the child-learning account begins in the next paragraph.)
9. **A.** If parents' casual habits are carried into children's rules, the account of children inferring rules from what they hear gains support. [Beyond] (B reports the complaint the passage is examining, which is not evidence for any explanation; C concerns adult speakers moving to new places, a different process from the one in paragraph 4; D repeats the regularity from paragraph 2, which supports the case against laziness but says nothing about children.)
10. **D.** The paragraph concludes that laziness predicts scattered shortcuts and that a regular rule is observed, which requires the premise that laziness would not produce a regular rule. [Within] (A is out of scope: where change begins is not discussed in the second paragraph; B is not needed, and the passage suggests speakers do follow rules; C is not required: the paragraph does not claim that elders pronounce every sound with full care.)
11. **C.** The passage says that the same speakers charged with dropping a sound may later add one, and that a theory satisfied by change in either direction predicts nothing. [Beyond] (A treats any change as confirmation, which is the very move the author criticizes; B reverses the passage, which notes that harder sounds have been added; D is too extreme: the author argues that the view is empty, not that a single case refutes it.)
12. **B.** The second and final paragraphs state that continued use is weaker evidence of wanting than is usually supposed, because the design makes stopping the act that needs a decision. [Foundations] (A is too extreme: the final paragraph says that the argument does not show the services to be harmful on balance and that many users are well served; C contradicts the first paragraph, which says that attention differs from money in being fixed; D contradicts paragraph 3, which distinguishes feeds from chairs.)
13. **D.** Paragraph 3 says a chair succeeds when the sitter is later glad, and a feed succeeds when the user stays. [Foundations] (A reverses the text, which says every product is designed to be attractive; B is true of the service's price but is not the distinction drawn in paragraph 3; C is unsupported: nothing is said about makers of chairs being unable to learn whether buyers are satisfied.)
14. **A.** If users decline an easy way to stop and behave as before, their use looks like preference rather than the effect of costly stopping. [Beyond] (B strengthens the author's claim by suggesting that use goes beyond what users want; C also strengthens it, since the design change raised use; D is consistent with the author's claim about incentives and does not bear on whether users want to continue.)
15. **C.** The designers prefer the user's time to the user's satisfaction; the casino owner measures success by hours played and arranges the floor to keep players there. [Beyond] (A measures success by satisfaction, like the chair maker; B also measures success by customer satisfaction; D is an incentive to attract customers by a loss-leading price, a different matter from preferring time to satisfaction.)
16. **B.** The reply says a chair succeeds when the sitter is later glad and a feed succeeds when the user stays 'whether or not she is later glad,' which treats later gladness as the fairer test. [Within] (A is not required; the reply turns on what the design is attractive for, not on how attractive it is; C is out of scope: the author makes no claim about designers' economic knowledge; D is too extreme and is not needed by the comparison.)
17. **A.** The last paragraph says that the argument shows 'only' that the inference from use to wanting is weak, and so confines the claim to a rebuttal. [Within] (B reverses the paragraph, which restates the criticism that stopping is made hard; C goes beyond the text, which proposes disclosure and defaults as a remedy; D is true as a report of the qualification but too strong in its conclusion, since the author does not argue that users should be left to decide.)

**A model passage map (Passage 1)**

One way to map the passage on scratch paper, described in words. Paragraph 1: the claim under examination, the compensation test, which declares a project an improvement if winners could pay losers. Note the neutral tone and the phrase "even if no payment is ever made," which sets up the flaw. Paragraph 2: the first objection (could, not will), then the defenders' reply (losses and gains even out), then the author's counter-reply (the same households lose each time and the same gain). Star the sentence "This is an empirical claim, and it is rarely checked," because it shows the author attacking the support for the reply rather than its logic. Paragraph 3: a second, separate objection, about what counts as a loss; the key contrast is market value to a stranger versus value to the resident, and the sentence "a loss that is hard to measure is not thereby zero" is the author's principle. Paragraph 4: a fair objection (a veto would end all building), a concession, and then the author's positive proposal: pay actually, and pay for true losses; the closing sentence turns the objection about cost into a virtue. Beside the map, write the tone in three words ("measured, critical, constructive"). With this map, Question 1 comes from paragraphs 2 to 4 together, Question 2 from paragraph 3, Questions 3 to 5 from paragraph 2, and Question 6 from the principle in paragraph 3 applied to a new case.

Praxis Tip: For an assumption question, try the negation test. Take the choice you like and ask what would happen to the argument if the choice were false. If the argument collapses, you have found the assumption; if it carries on unharmed, you have found a pleasant remark.

Praxis Tip: When asked to weaken a claim, find the claim's own support first. In Question 5, the author's doubt rested on the same households losing every time. A finding that attacks that support, rather than the conclusion, is the strongest weakener, and a finding that merely agrees with the conclusion's opposite side is usually a trap.

Praxis Tip: In analogy questions, strip both situations to a skeleton ("a payment equal to the market price, ignoring value to the holder") and compare skeletons. The surface details, clocks or casinos, are decoration, and wrong choices are often built from decoration that matches the passage's topic while the skeleton does not.

Praxis Tip: A concession is not a defeat. When an author says "the objection is correct," as in the last paragraph of Passage 1, look for the "but" or "however" that follows. The conclusion is almost always on the far side of it, and choices that echo only the concession are written for the reader who stopped early.

Praxis Tip: If you are choosing between two options that both look true, ask which one the author would be prepared to say aloud in a lecture. Options with "always," "never," "all," or "merely" are usually claiming more than any careful author will sign for.
