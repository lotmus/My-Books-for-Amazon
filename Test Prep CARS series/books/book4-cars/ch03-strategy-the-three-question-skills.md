# Strategy: The Three Question Skills

**What you will be able to do**

- Identify the family of any CARS question from its stem.
- Answer a foundations question by locating and restating what the passage says.
- Answer a reasoning-within-the-text question by finding what the text requires, and nothing more.
- Answer a reasoning-beyond-the-text question by extracting the principle and testing each choice against it.
- Explain why each wrong choice is wrong, using a small set of named flaws.

**Why it matters**

Every CARS question asks one of three things: what does the text say, how does the text work, or what follows if we carry the text somewhere new. [VERIFY] The exam owner is understood to describe these as foundations of comprehension, reasoning within the text, and reasoning beyond the text, at roughly 30, 30 and 40 percent of the questions. Which of the three you face changes where you look and how far you may reach.

Most students use one mental move for every question: reread the passage and pick whatever sounds right. That works for some foundations questions and fails on the rest. Beyond-the-text questions, in particular, are not answered by rereading; they are answered by understanding the passage's principle well enough to use it. This chapter gives you three procedures instead of one.

Praxis Tip: Before you hunt for an answer, spend two seconds naming the question's family. It is the cheapest sorting you will do all day.

**Telling the families apart**

The stem of a question is the best clue. These cues are guides, not laws, so read the stem for what it truly asks.

| Family | Typical stem cues | Where the answer lives |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations | "According to the passage," "the main idea," "the term means" | Stated in the text |
| Within the text | "implies," "the author's attitude," "in order to" | Required by the text |
| Beyond the text | "would most likely," "if true," "most similar" | The text's principle, applied |

A useful test is to ask: if I removed the passage and substituted a different one, could this question be asked about it? "What is the main idea?" can. "Which new finding would weaken the author's argument?" can too, but it needs an understanding of an argument that foundations questions only skim.

**Family 1: Foundations of comprehension**

These questions check that you understood what the passage says. Typical types:

- **Main idea or primary purpose:** the whole passage, summarized in one claim.
- **Detail:** a fact or position stated in the passage.
- **Meaning in context:** what a word or phrase means as the author uses it.
- **Paragraph point:** what one paragraph says.

**How to answer.** Predict before you read the choices. For a main-idea question, use your frame (topic and point). For a detail question, find the sentence in the passage, using your map to go to the right paragraph, and put it in your own words. Then choose the option that restates it. Options for foundations questions are usually close paraphrases of the text, so the right answer may look unfamiliar because the wording changes, and a wrong answer may look familiar because the words are repeated.

**Worked Example 1: A foundations question**

*Passage.* For most of the nineteenth century, the town's lending library charged no fee, but it admitted only members of the founding families. Reformers argued that this policy turned a public good into a private club. The trustees replied that the founders' endowment, not the town's taxes, paid for the books, and that the founders therefore had the right to set the terms. In 1902, a gift from a local mill owner allowed the trustees to open the shelves to everyone, and membership of a founding family ceased to matter. Critics have since noted that the trustees admitted no fault, describing the change as an expansion of generosity.

*Question 1.* According to the passage, the trustees defended the restricted policy by arguing that
A. the library charged no fee and so needed no further justification
B. the founders' endowment, rather than taxes, paid for the books
C. the mill owner had demanded it as a condition of his gift
D. most townspeople had no wish to use the library

*Working.* The map would say: paragraph about the dispute; trustees reply = endowment paid, so founders set terms. The detail question points straight at it, and B restates it. Choice A mentions the fee, which is in the text but is not the trustees' defense. Choice C is invented: the gift came later and opened the library. Choice D is also unsupported. **Answer: B.**

*Question 2.* Which of the following best states the main idea of the passage?
A. A library was opened to the public after reformers defeated a wealthy donor.
B. A mill owner's generosity was the greatest benefit the town ever received.
C. A library's restricted admission, defended on grounds of ownership, ended in 1902 without any admission of fault by the trustees.
D. Libraries funded by taxes are always better run than libraries funded by gifts.

*Working.* The frame is: restricted library, defended by the trustees, opened by a gift, the trustees admitting no fault. C captures all of it. Choice A reverses the cause, because the mill owner's gift opened the library and did not oppose the reformers. Choice B inflates one detail into a superlative. Choice D brings in a comparison that the passage never makes. **Answer: C.**

Praxis Tip: For a foundations question, the passage should be able to testify for your answer in a sentence or two. If it cannot, you are probably in a different family, or you are wrong.

**Family 2: Reasoning within the text**

These questions ask how the passage works and what it requires without saying. Typical types:

- **Inference:** what must be true, or is very likely true, given what is stated.
- **Author's attitude or tone:** the author's stance toward a subject.
- **Function:** why a detail, example or paragraph appears.
- **Relationship:** how one claim bears on another.

**How to answer.** Treat the text as evidence and ask what it forces. The right answer for an inference question is something the passage supports closely, typically a modest step beyond a stated fact. The wrong answers tend to be too strong (turning "some" into "all"), out of scope (raising something the passage never touches) or merely possible. For function questions, say what the part does for the argument, using your paragraph jobs: "grants the opposition a point," "offers a case," "sets up the problem." For attitude questions, collect the evaluative words and the signal words, and ask where they point.

**Worked Example 2: Reasoning within the text**

*Passage.* Defenders of standardized spelling often say it exists to help readers. There is something in this, since a reader who meets the same word spelled five ways must pause to decide whether it is the same word. Yet spelling reform has sometimes been resisted not by readers but by printers and schoolmasters, whose livelihoods rested on the existing conventions. The pattern suggests that what is defended as a convenience for readers may also be a defense of those who teach and publish the conventions. That is no argument for chaos; it is a reminder to ask who benefits when a rule is called natural.

*Question 1.* The author mentions the reader who meets a word spelled five ways in order to
A. show that spelling reform is impossible
B. grant a genuine benefit of standardization before questioning how it is defended
C. argue that readers are the only people who matter in spelling
D. prove that printers and schoolmasters were always right

*Working.* Map: paragraph = concession, then turn ("Yet"). The sentence beginning "There is something in this" is a concession, and the example supports it. That is B. Choice A claims impossibility, which no sentence supports and the last sentence rules out ("no argument for chaos"). Choice C and D go in the opposite direction from the author's skepticism. **Answer: B.**

*Question 2.* The passage most strongly implies that printers and schoolmasters who resisted reform
A. had a stake in the existing conventions beyond readers' convenience
B. were wrong in every case
C. wanted to abolish spelling altogether
D. were unpopular with readers

*Working.* The text says their livelihoods rested on the conventions. That supports A: they had a personal interest in the existing rules. Choice B is too extreme; the author says only that the pattern raises a question. Choice C is out of scope. Choice D is not supported either; popularity is never mentioned. **Answer: A.**

Praxis Tip: An inference question rewards the smallest step that the text allows. If your answer needs two extra assumptions, it has wandered off.

**Family 3: Reasoning beyond the text**

These questions take the passage as a starting point. Typical types:

- **Application:** which new situation the author's principle fits.
- **Strengthen or weaken:** which new fact helps or hurts the argument.
- **Analogy:** which different situation has the same structure as the author's reasoning.
- **Prediction:** what the author would say about a related matter.
- **New information:** how the passage's claims change if a fact is added.

**How to answer.** Do not hunt for the answer in the passage; it is not there. Instead:

1. **Extract the principle or the argument.** State in one sentence what the author claims and why.
2. **Hold it up against each choice.** Does the choice apply the principle, support the argument's weak point, or have the same structure?
3. **Prefer the choice that engages the reasoning, not merely the topic.** A wrong choice often mentions the same subject without touching the argument.

For strengthen and weaken questions, find the argument's key assumption first ([Lesson 6](chapter:9) is devoted to this). To weaken, attack the link between evidence and conclusion. To strengthen, protect that link. A fact that fits the passage's topic but does not touch the link does neither.

**Worked Example 3: Reasoning beyond the text**

*Passage.* A common view holds that cheap airfare has made people more adventurous. It may have done the opposite. When a trip to another country takes a year of saving and planning, travelers prepare, learn something of the language and arrive curious. When the same trip costs a weekend's wages and is booked in an evening, it can be taken without preparation, and many travelers arrive as tourists in the narrow sense, seeing the sights listed in the guide and leaving. The ease of travel, in short, may reduce the effort that once made a trip a form of learning.

*Principle.* Removing the effort that once forced engagement can reduce engagement.

*Question 1.* The author's reasoning would most likely apply to which of the following?
A. Because a cheaper train fare lets more people visit relatives, family bonds will strengthen
B. Because airlines compete, flights will become safer
C. Because books are better than films, reading should replace viewing
D. Because any recording can now be played instantly and free, listeners may attend less closely to each one than they did when each cost a purchase

*Working.* The principle is about effort and engagement. Choice D transfers it exactly: the effort that once forced attention (buying a recording) has gone, and attention may drop. Choice A is about family bonds and cheaper access making things better, the reverse direction. Choice C compares two media, which the passage does not. Choice B is about competition and safety, a different topic altogether. **Answer: D.**

*Question 2.* Which finding would most weaken the author's argument?
A. Travelers who book cheap flights in an evening are as likely to study their destinations beforehand as those who save for a year
B. Many travelers on cheap flights visit only the sights listed in the guide
C. The price of flights fell by half over the last decade
D. Guidebook sales have risen in recent years

*Working.* The argument's link is that low effort leads to unprepared, shallow travel. A, which shows that low-cost travelers prepare as much, severs that link. Choice B supports the author. Choice C is background that the author already accepts. Choice D is ambiguous at best: rising guidebook sales could mean more preparation, but equally more reliance on listed sights, so it does not clearly weaken. **Answer: A.**

Praxis Tip: In a weaken question, read each option and ask "does this make the conclusion less likely?" If the answer is "it is just about the same topic," cross it out.

**One routine for all three**

Whatever the family, the same four steps help:

1. **Read the stem alone and name the family.**
2. **Predict the answer, or the principle, before reading the choices.**
3. **Check each choice against the text and cross out by reason**, not by feeling. The reasons you will use most are: too extreme, out of scope, reverses the claim, true but not asked, uses text language but changes meaning, and unsupported inference. [Strategy: When You Are Stuck](chapter:17) catalogs these.
4. **Choose the best of what remains.** On this section, "best" is the choice the passage supports most directly, not the choice that is most interesting.

If two choices both seem right, ask which one the passage would defend. The other is usually claiming a little more than the text can back, or addressing a slightly different question.

**Common Mistakes**

- Using one method for all questions, usually rereading and matching words.
- Choosing an answer because it repeats words from the passage.
- Answering a beyond-the-text question with the passage's topic instead of its principle.
- Choosing a true statement that the passage does not support.
- Stretching an inference with extra assumptions.
- Confusing "the author mentions" with "the author endorses."
- Forgetting to check whether a choice is too strong ("always," "never," "only").

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Extreme words (all, never, only, entirely) are not always wrong, but they must be earned by the text. Make them show their papers.

Praxis Tip: When an answer is close to your prediction but not quite, ask what exactly differs. The difference is usually why it is wrong.

Praxis Tip: If you can state why three choices are wrong, you are allowed to be relieved about the fourth.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. Which stem is a foundations-of-comprehension question?
   A. "According to the passage, which factor most influenced the council's decision?"
   B. "Which of the following findings, if true, would most weaken the author's argument?"
   C. "The author would most likely agree that a similar policy in another city would"
   D. "The author's reasoning is most similar to that of someone who"

2. *Passage.* The theater's program praised the new lighting system at length. It said nothing about why the old lamps had been retired, though two actors had been burned by them the previous season.

The passage most strongly suggests that the program's authors
   A. believed the new system was unsafe
   B. thought the old lamps had never been used
   C. preferred not to draw attention to the reason for the change
   D. were unaware of the earlier accidents

3. *Passage.* A fast reader and a slow reader may finish the same novel, but they have not read the same book. The fast reader gets the plot; the slow reader also gets the sentences, the hesitations and the places where the author declined to say what happened. Speed is a fine skill, but it is not the only way to be a good reader.

The main idea of the passage is that
   A. slow readers always understand novels better than fast readers do, whatever the novel
   B. speed matters, but slow reading can capture what fast reading misses
   C. novels should be written with shorter sentences
   D. plot is the least important feature of a novel

4. *Passage.* Teachers who correct every error as it happens often produce students who stop speaking. Students gain fluency when they are allowed to finish a thought, with corrections saved for afterward.

The principle in the passage would most likely support which practice?
   A. A teacher who repeats each sentence a student says aloud
   B. A teacher who forbids students from speaking
   C. A teacher who corrects only written work and never speech
   D. A music coach who lets a student finish a piece, then comments

5. *Passage.* The city credits its new bus lanes for the drop in average commute times since they opened.

Which finding would most weaken the city's claim?
   A. Bus ridership rose after the lanes opened
   B. The lanes cost less than predicted
   C. A large employer left the city the month the lanes opened, removing thousands of daily commuters
   D. Commuters say they like the lanes

6. *Passage.* The prize committee called the novel "brave." Brave is what we call books we have not finished.

The tone of the second sentence is best described as
   A. warmly approving of the committee
   B. wryly skeptical of the praise
   C. earnestly confused
   D. neutral and informative

7. *Passage.* A translator who renders every idiom literally is like a map-maker who draws every pebble: faithful to detail, useless for travel.

Which person's mistake is most similar to the one the author describes?
   A. A chef who follows a recipe exactly as written
   B. A runner who trains daily for a long race
   C. A teacher who gives short tests every week
   D. A guide who lists every fact and never says what to see

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** The stem asks what the passage says. (B is a weaken question, which goes beyond the text; C asks for the author's likely view of a new case; D asks for an analogy.)
2. **C.** The program praised the new system at length yet omitted the reason for the change, which suggests an avoidance. (A has no support, since the program praised the system; B contradicts the burns; D is unsupported, and it is unlikely that the authors forgot something so recent.)
3. **B.** The passage credits speed as a skill and then claims slow reading captures more. (A overstates with "always"; C and D each bring in a point the passage does not make.)
4. **D.** Letting someone finish and then giving corrections is the stated principle, applied to a different field. (A concerns repetition, not delayed correction; B is far beyond the text; C changes the subject to written work.)
5. **C.** If the commuters themselves left, the drop in commute time has another cause. (A would, if anything, support the claim; B concerns cost, not effect; D concerns opinions, not commute times.)
6. **B.** The author implies that "brave" is praise for something not read, so the praise is mocked. (A reverses this; C misreads dry wit as confusion; D ignores the evaluative edge.)
7. **D.** The fault is faithfulness to detail without any judgment about what matters, as in this guide. (A and B are not faults of the same kind; C is unrelated to the structure of the mistake.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, name the slip behind each miss (wrong family, reached too far, matched words instead of meaning) and log it in your error log (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9).

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "Foundations questions ask what the passage says, so I find the sentence and restate it; within-the-text questions ask what the passage requires, so I take the smallest supported step; beyond-the-text questions ask me to apply the passage's principle or test its argument, so I extract the principle first and then check each choice against it."

*Review schedule:* revisit this chapter after 1 day (redo Worked Example 3 without looking), after 3 days (checkpoint questions from a blank page), after 7 days (sort ten question stems from a later practice set into the three families), and after 21 days (explain, with your own example, how a weaken question differs from an inference question).

*Interleave:* once you begin the lessons, finish every set by labeling the family of each question you missed. If most of your misses cluster in one family, spend your next sessions on the matching lesson ([Lesson 4](chapter:7) on inference or [Lesson 5](chapter:8) on application, for example).

Praxis Tip: Keep a tally in your notebook of misses by family, not only by passage. A pattern you can see is a pattern you can fix.
