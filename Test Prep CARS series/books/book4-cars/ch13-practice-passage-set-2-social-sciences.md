**Passage 1 (questions 1-6)**

In many towns, shops that sell the same goods stand side by side: a street of furniture dealers, a row of shoe sellers, a block of electronics stalls. A newcomer might suppose that rivals would do better to spread out, each claiming a neighborhood and enjoying a local monopoly. Economists who have studied the matter offer a simple model that explains why they do not. Imagine a long beach with customers spread evenly along it and two vendors selling identical ice cream at identical prices. Customers walk to the nearer vendor. If the vendors begin at the quarter points of the beach, each serves half of it, and either can gain by moving toward the middle: every step toward the other's position takes some customers away at no cost. The only position from which neither can gain further is the center, where the two vendors stand back to back.

The model is deliberately unrealistic, and its authors say so. Customers in real towns do not walk to the nearest seller regardless of price; vendors differ in quality; and no actual beach is a perfectly straight line. Yet the model survives because its central mechanism is robust: when the main thing a seller can change is its location, and customers choose mostly on convenience, each seller is drawn toward the others. The result is clustering that no one planned and that, in the model, leaves customers worse off than they could be, since the average walk is longer than if the vendors had stood at the quarter points.

Later researchers raised an objection that complicates this picture. When the model is extended so that sellers compete on price as well as location, the pull toward the center weakens or disappears, because two sellers standing side by side with identical goods will cut prices until profits vanish. Sellers who foresee this have reason to keep apart. The prediction, then, depends on what the sellers can vary. This is not a defect so much as a lesson in how to read a model: its conclusions are conditional on what has been allowed to change.

A different line of evidence comes from the customers' side. Shoppers who are unsure what they want, such as someone furnishing a first apartment, prefer to visit a street where many dealers are gathered, since one trip lets them compare. Dealers who cluster thus attract more total customers than they would scattered, and that gain may outweigh the loss from sharing the crowd. On this view clustering is not merely a trap into which rivals fall; it can be an arrangement that serves buyers and sellers both. Nothing in the original model rules this out, but nothing in it predicts it either.

The reasonable conclusion is that neither explanation is a complete account. The beach model isolates one force and shows how far it can go. The comparison-shopping argument introduces another. Which force dominates in a given town is an empirical question, and one that can be answered only by looking at what the sellers there actually compete over.

**Passage 2 (questions 7-12)**

When I first lived in the fishing village I will call Halden Cove, I took the custom of the shared catch to be a form of insurance. After each day's fishing, the crews laid out a portion of their fish on a long plank by the harbor, and any household could take what it needed. The arrangement seemed to fit a standard account: in a trade where luck is uneven and a bad week can ruin a family, those who give today buy protection against tomorrow's failure. I wrote this in my notes during the first season, and for a while nothing contradicted it.

The trouble began with the details. Insurance should be sensitive to need and to risk, but the plank was not. The most prosperous household, owner of two boats, took fish as freely as the widow in the lowest house, and no one remarked on it. Crews that had had a poor day still laid out their share, even when they themselves went short. And when a visitor from another village, a stranger who would never return, took fish from the plank, he was received with no more hesitation than a neighbor. A calculation of future benefit cannot explain generosity toward someone who will never be in a position to repay it.

I came to think the plank was doing different work. Villagers rarely described it in terms of help. They said that the plank was "where the sea is shared," and that a crew which withheld its portion was "keeping the sea to itself." The offense was not stinginess but a kind of claim: the withholder implied that the fish belonged to the crew by right of skill, rather than to the water that had produced them. By giving, a crew declared that its success was a gift it had received, and so owed no one an account. This also explained why prosperous households took fish without shame: to refuse would have been to imply that their wealth was something other than good fortune.

I do not wish to discard the insurance account entirely. Hardship in Halden Cove is real, and I met families who were carried through bad winters by the plank. The point is that insurance describes one consequence of the custom rather than its meaning. A custom can have useful effects without being practiced for their sake, as a village may benefit from a festival without having held it in order to benefit. Observers who explain every institution by its usefulness risk mistaking the effect they can measure for the reason that the participants hold.

There is a final difficulty in my own position. The villagers' account, the sea as the common source, is the kind of explanation that insiders give, and insiders do not always know why they act as they do. I have no way of proving that "where the sea is shared" is more than a flattering story told afterward. What I can say is that it predicts the behaviors the insurance account could not, and that it was offered to me unprompted, in the villagers' own words, by people who had no reason to expect I would value it.

**Passage 3 (questions 13-17)**

A school that is told it will be judged by its students' test scores will, sooner or later, begin to teach the test. This observation is usually offered as a complaint, but it is better understood as a prediction that anyone who designs a measure should make before, rather than after, the measure is adopted. People respond to what is counted. A measure that began as a window onto the quality of teaching becomes, once consequences are attached, a target to be hit, and a target can be hit in ways that have nothing to do with the thing it was meant to reveal.

The defenders of test-based accountability have a serious reply. Without common measures, they say, failing schools can hide behind vague praise, and the children with the least influential parents are the ones who suffer from the silence. Averages, however imperfect, make visible what impressions conceal. This argument has force, and it should not be dismissed as the self-interest of teachers who would rather not be evaluated. The question is not whether schools should be measured but what happens when the measure carries consequences.

What tends to happen is a narrowing. Subjects that appear on the test receive more time; subjects that do not, such as art, history and extended writing, receive less. Within tested subjects, instruction drifts toward the forms of question that the test favors. Teachers may concentrate on students just below the passing line, since moving them changes the school's figure, while students far above or far below receive less attention. None of this requires bad faith. Each decision is reasonable given the incentives, and the sum of reasonable decisions is a school optimized for its figure rather than for its pupils.

An ironic result follows. The more weight a measure bears, the less reliable it becomes as a measure. Scores rise, and the rise is celebrated, but if the rise reflects practice at the test's particular format rather than broader learning, it provides no evidence that the children know more. Officials who see only the scores cannot tell the difference, and the pleasant numbers may delay recognition that nothing has improved.

The solution is not to abandon measurement, which would return us to impressions, nor to hope for a test so comprehensive that it cannot be gamed, which would be a test as long as schooling itself. A more modest course is to treat any single figure as a question rather than a verdict: to attach to it other kinds of evidence, such as samples of student work, visits by informed observers, and the testimony of those who later teach the same pupils, and to attach consequences to the whole picture rather than to the number. Such an arrangement is slower and less tidy than a ranking, and it will never produce a headline. It also has the advantage of being harder to fool, because to fool it a school would have to actually improve.

**Answers and explanations**

[Foundations] (B reverses the passage, which says clustering can serve buyers and sellers both; C is too extreme: the passage says the model survives because its central mechanism is robust; D goes beyond the text, which gives benefit to comparison shoppers but makes no claim that prices fall.)
2. **D.** The first paragraph states that either vendor 'can gain by moving toward the middle' and that no further gain exists once both are there. [Foundations] (A is unsupported: customers in the model simply walk to the nearer vendor; B is not stated: the passage describes rivals, and no agreement is mentioned; C reverses the text, which says the average walk is longer at the center than at the quarter points.)
3. **B.** Paragraph 3 gives the reason directly, in the sentence containing 'because two sellers standing side by side.' [Within] (A is not stated: the passage does not discuss customers refusing to walk; C uses the idea of quality from paragraph 2 but gives it a role the passage does not assign to it here; D is unsupported: crowding is never given as a reason in the passage.)

**A model passage map (Passage 3)**

One way to map the passage on scratch paper, described in words. Paragraph 1: the thesis. When a measure carries consequences, people hit the target in ways unrelated to what the measure was meant to reveal. Mark the line "people respond to what is counted" as the central principle. Paragraph 2: the objection, given fairly: without common measures, failing schools hide behind vague praise, and the least influential families suffer. Paragraph 4: the irony. The heavier the weight a measure bears, the less it can be trusted, and officials cannot tell a rise from real learning. Paragraph 5: the author's proposal. Keep measurement but treat any figure as a question, attach other evidence, and tie consequences to the whole picture; the last sentence is a wry point that a system that cannot be fooled except by genuine improvement is the goal. Praxis Tip: For application questions, restate the passage's principle in one line on scratch paper before you read the choices ("when a number carries consequences, the number gets moved"). Then ask of each choice, "Is this the principle at work?" Choices that merely mention the topic fall away.

Praxis Tip: In an EXCEPT question, mark each choice with a plus (the passage says this) or a minus (it does not) and pick the odd one out. It is more reliable than keeping the negative in your head, where it tends to slip out during the third choice.

Praxis Tip: For strengthen and weaken questions, notice that two of the wrong choices usually push in the opposite direction or push in no direction at all. If you can say "this would strengthen it" or "this has nothing to do with it" about a choice, you have named a trap and need not pick it.

Praxis Tip: A fieldwork account written in the first person, like Passage 2, is still an argument. Separate what the author observed, what the author inferred, and what the author concedes; the questions usually live in the gaps between the three.
