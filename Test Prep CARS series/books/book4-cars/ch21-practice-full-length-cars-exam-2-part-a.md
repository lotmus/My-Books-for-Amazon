**Passage 1 (questions 1-6)**

Schools teach by telling. A teacher explains a procedure, students practice it on exercises, and a test later confirms whether the explanation took. Apprenticeship works the other way around. The novice is set beside a practitioner, given a small task that is genuinely part of the work, and corrected as the work proceeds. Explanation, when it comes, arrives after the need for it has been felt. Defenders of apprenticeship argue that this order is not a historical leftover but a better match for the way skill is actually acquired.

Their central claim concerns what cannot be said. A master joiner knows how much pressure a plane needs on a given grain, but she cannot state it as a rule; she can only show it, and the learner can only catch it by repeating the motion while the master watches. Such knowledge is called tacit, and the defenders hold that a great deal of expertise, in the trades, in clinical medicine and in cooking, is mostly tacit. If so, a curriculum made of statements will always miss the part of the skill that matters most.

A second argument concerns motivation. Apprentices sweep floors and carry timber, but they do so in a place where the purpose of every task is visible and where a finished piece of work is the reward. Classroom exercises, by contrast, are often finished for no one. The student who solves the fortieth problem on a worksheet is not building anything, and knows it.

Critics have raised two objections. The first is that apprenticeship is slow and depends heavily on the luck of the master. A gifted craftsman may be a poor teacher, and a poor one may keep novices at drudgery for years, since cheap labor is useful. The second is that apprenticeship teaches the practice as it is, not as it might be: the apprentice absorbs the master's habits, mistakes included, and has little occasion to ask why. Classroom instruction, whatever its faults, at least lays out reasons that can be examined and rejected.

Neither objection, the defenders reply, is fatal. Guilds historically met the first by limiting the number of novices a master could take and by inspecting the finished work; modern programs pair training on the job with periods of study away from it, which meets the second. In practice, the most successful programs appear to be hybrids. Whether this vindicates apprenticeship or merely concedes that schooling is indispensable is a question the two camps answer differently, and the evidence so far does not settle it, since the programs that report the best outcomes also tend to select their entrants with great care.

**Passage 2 (questions 7-11)**

Puppetry is usually mentioned in the arts pages only at Christmas, and then in the tone reserved for a nephew's recital. It is assumed to be theater for children, or theater for those who cannot afford actors: a minor art in the sense that a minor key is supposed to be a lesser key. This assumption deserves to be challenged, not because puppetry is secretly the equal of every other art, but because it does something that no other theatrical form does, and the thing it does is not childish.

Consider the oddity of the situation. A puppeteer stands in plain view, or in dark clothes that the audience has agreed not to see, and moves a figure of wood and cloth. Every spectator knows that the figure is lifeless. And yet within a minute or two the spectator is watching the figure rather than the hands, and a tilt of its head reads as doubt. Theater has always depended on the audience's willing suspension of disbelief, but in most theater the disbelief is only lightly suspended: the actor is alive, and the audience's work is to treat a living person as someone else. With puppets the work is far greater, for the audience must treat a dead object as alive, and it does so knowingly. The pleasure of puppetry lies in that complicity. We are not fooled; we are collaborating.

Because the puppet is limited, collaboration is exactly what it demands. A puppet cannot frown, and its face is fixed. It can lower its head, hesitate at a doorway, turn away at the wrong moment. Gestures that a human actor might perform in passing, and that a viewer might overlook, become the whole of the performance, and a good puppeteer selects each one with the economy of a poet. Spectators who have watched a puppet express grief by doing nothing more than setting down a cup are seldom inclined to call it a lesser art.

Some will say that the argument proves too much, since every limitation can be recast as a virtue. The reply is that a limitation becomes a virtue only when the art makes use of it, and many puppet shows do not. A show in which the figures chatter and bounce in imitation of cartoons gives up the form's strength and earns the condescension it receives. But the same is true of any art; the existence of bad sonnets has not made poetry a trivial pursuit.

The point is not that puppetry should be crowned. It is that an art can be small in scale, modest in reputation and exacting in its demands, and that these facts are compatible. The critic who has seen a figure of felt and string make an audience of adults fall silent has encountered something that deserves a better word than "quaint."

**Passage 3 (questions 12-17)**

Textbook accounts of gift exchange tend to treat it as barter in slow motion. A gives B a mat; some months later B gives A something of equal worth; the books balance, and the delay is merely the sign of a society without markets, in which a swap cannot be completed in a single afternoon. The invented case of the Tolmai, a farming people who exchange woven mats at births, marriages and funerals, shows why this account fails.

Among the Tolmai, the return of a gift is governed by two rules that appear to contradict the barter idea. The first is that a return made too soon is an insult. A family that answers a wedding mat with a mat the following week is said to be "closing the door," for it signals that it wishes to owe nothing and so to have nothing further to do with the giver. The second is that a return of precisely equal value is almost as bad. The proper return is a little more, or a little less, or in a different form, such as labor at harvest. A good exchange, in the Tolmai view, is one that cannot be added up.

The point of these rules becomes clear if we ask what the exchange is for. If the aim were to transfer goods, the quickest and most exact return would be best. But the Tolmai are not transferring goods; they are maintaining a relation. A debt that is outstanding is a reason for two families to remain in contact, to attend one another's funerals, to remember. Settling the debt exactly ends the relation, just as paying off a lease ends the tenancy. The gift is not a loan to be repaid but an opening move in a conversation, and the delay and the imbalance are what keep the conversation going.

It would be easy to turn this into a romance of the premodern, in which gift-givers are warm and market people cold. The Tolmai case does not support that. Tolmai families keep careful mental records of who has given what, and quarrels over gifts thought too small are frequent and bitter. The difference lies not in warmth but in aim. A buyer wants the account to end; a Tolmai giver wants it kept open.

The recent history of the islands offers a kind of test. Since a road reached the coast, mats have been sold to visitors for cash, and some villagers now speak of a gift mat as "worth" a certain number of coins. Older Tolmai complain that such talk spoils the exchange, and the complaint is just what the analysis predicts: once a gift can be priced, the return can be exact, and an exact return can end the relation.

**Passage 4 (questions 18-23)**

The phrase "what counts" is useful because it is ambiguous. To count something is to include it in a tally; to count, in the other sense, is to matter. The ambiguity is no accident. A figure that is published every quarter, reported on the evening news and used to judge governments quickly acquires the authority of the second sense from the first. What is in the total is thought to matter, and what is left out is quietly assumed to have been small.

Consider the standard measure of a nation's yearly output, which sums the value of goods and services that are bought and sold. Its designers never claimed that it measured well-being, and its sober defenders still do not. But the figure has a well-known blind spot. Unpaid work at home, such as cooking, cleaning, and the care of children and elderly relatives, is not sold, and so does not appear. An old joke observes that a man who marries his housekeeper reduces the national income, since the same work is now done without a wage. The joke is a good one because the absurdity is real: the work has not changed, only the way it is paid for, and yet the total declines.

It might be proposed that the remedy is simply to count more: to assign wages to unpaid work and add them in. There is something to this, and some statisticians now publish such supplementary estimates. But the remedy has its own difficulty. To price an hour of caring for a sick parent at the going rate for a hired nurse is to assert that the two hours are interchangeable, and many of the people doing the caring would deny it. The act of counting does not merely record the activity; it redescribes it in the terms of the market, and something of what it was is lost in translation.

The conclusion is not that measurement should be abandoned. Without figures we would be at the mercy of anecdote, and the unpaid carer is better served by a number, even an imperfect one, than by a silence. The conclusion is rather that a measure should be read with its omissions in mind. Every instrument is built to detect something, and what it detects is exactly what it has been tuned to; the rest is not zero but unrecorded. An honest statistician and an honest reader should ask of any figure not only what is in it, but what would have had to be true for something to be in it, and who decided.

**Passage 5 (questions 24-29)**

Strangers who will never meet again buy used cameras, rent spare rooms and hire plumbers from one another every day, and a surprising fraction of these transactions end without incident. Early observers of online markets treated this as a puzzle. Trust, on the traditional view, grows out of repeated dealings or shared membership in a community, and the online stranger offers neither. Yet sellers' star ratings seemed to do the work: a seller with hundreds of positive reviews was trusted, and a seller with none was not. Reputation, it was said, had replaced acquaintance.

That account is incomplete, and the first sign is the ratings themselves. On most platforms the average rating is extraordinarily high; in a typical sample, nearly all sellers fall between 4.6 and 5.0 stars. A scale that nearly everyone tops cannot distinguish the careful seller from the merely adequate. The inflation has a simple social cause. A buyer who leaves an honest three-star review risks a retaliatory review in return, and she may also feel uncomfortable about damaging the livelihood of someone with whom she has just exchanged friendly messages. Praise costs nothing, and criticism has a price. Ratings therefore function less as measurements than as courtesies, like the "fine, thanks" that answers "How are you?"

If ratings are courtesy, something else must be supporting trust. A more plausible candidate is the platform. Buyers do not, in the end, rely on the seller's reputation but on the existence of a procedure: payment held until the goods arrive, a refund for non-delivery, a mediator for disputes. The platform's guarantee turns an exchange between strangers into a transaction within an institution, and institutional trust is of a different kind from personal trust. One trusts a bank not because one knows the teller but because one expects the bank's rules to be enforced. The star rating, on this view, is decoration on a structure that bears its weight elsewhere.

This reading explains an otherwise odd observation: buyers report that they rely heavily on the text of reviews, especially the rare negative ones, and on small signals such as whether a seller answers questions promptly. These are not measurements of reputation but cues of attentiveness, the kind of evidence people have always used to judge strangers across a counter. The online market has not invented a new form of trust; it has rebuilt the old one around a new screen.

A caution is in order. The institutional account is not a claim that reputation is irrelevant. The guarantees are costly for the platform, which will back sales generously in categories where fraud is rare and cautiously where it is common, so that sellers' histories may matter more in precisely those categories where the platform cannot afford to stand behind every sale. The two sources of trust are better seen as partners than rivals, with the platform supplying a floor and the seller's conduct determining what is built above it.
