# Lesson 4: Inference and Implication

**What you will be able to do**

- Separate what a passage states, what it implies, what it merely allows, and what it contradicts.
- Judge how strong an inference is by asking whether the passage could be true while the inference is false.
- Read qualifiers (some, most, often, may, usually) as part of the evidence rather than as decoration.
- Draw implications from word choice, tone and the arrangement of examples, not only from explicit claims.
- Apply conditional logic (see Core Test Skills, Chapter 16) to claims inside a passage without over-reading it.
- Recognize the six standard ways an inference answer goes wrong.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Many questions in the Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills section ask what a passage "implies," "suggests," "most strongly supports," or "would most likely agree with." These belong to the second skill family, reasoning within the text. The exam owner's public description is understood to give this family roughly 30 percent of the questions [VERIFY], and the family leans heavily on inference. Inference questions are also where careful readers lose the most points to confident ones: the wrong answers are usually sensible, true-sounding statements that the passage simply does not support.

This lesson builds on [Lesson 1](chapter:4) (main idea and structure), [Lesson 2](chapter:5) (tone and purpose) and [Lesson 3](chapter:6) (the anatomy of an argument). It uses the mapping habit from [The Active Reading Method](chapter:2) and the question families from [The Three Question Skills](chapter:3). No outside knowledge is needed for any question here. If you find yourself thinking "well, in real life...," stop: the passage is the only evidence.

Praxis Tip: An inference answer is not the answer you find most interesting, most plausible or most true in the world. It is the answer you could defend by pointing at the page.

**Concept Review**

**What an inference is.** An inference is a claim you accept because other claims, already accepted, make it very likely or certain. In the CARS section, the "already accepted" claims are the ones in the passage. So a good inference answer has a traceable source: you can say "this follows from the sentence about X together with the sentence about Y."

It helps to think in four grades of connection between a passage and a statement:

| Grade | Relation to the passage | Typical wording |
|---|---|---|
| Stated | Said outright | "the author says" |
| Implied | Follows from what is said | "suggests," "implies" |
| Possible | Not ruled out, not supported | "could be true" |
| Contradicted | Conflicts with the passage | "is inconsistent with" |

Correct inference answers sit in the first two grades. The tempting wrong answers almost always sit in the third.

**The defense test.** For each option, ask: could the passage be entirely true while this option is false? If yes, the option is not a safe inference. This does not mean the correct answer must be certain; "most strongly supported" questions allow an answer that is very probable. It means the correct answer should be the one that is hardest to deny, and the wrong answers should each have an easy way out.

**Qualifiers carry weight.** Compare these three sentences from a hypothetical passage.

- "Critics were unimpressed." (all critics, or critics in general)
- "Some critics were unimpressed." (at least one, perhaps many, perhaps not most)
- "Most critics were unimpressed." (more than half)

Each supports a different set of inferences. From the second you may not conclude that critics as a group disliked the work. A large share of wrong answers change a qualifier: *some* becomes *all*, *often* becomes *always*, *may* becomes *does*, *is one cause* becomes *is the cause*. When you read a passage, circle quantity and strength words on your scratch paper map; when you read an option, check whether it has quietly upgraded one.

**Stated, implied and assumed.** These are related but different.

- *Stated:* the sentence says it.
- *Implied:* the sentence, together with others, leaves only one sensible reading. If a passage says "the second sculptor finished the statue after the first one died," it implies that the first sculptor did not finish it, even though it never says so.
- *Assumed:* the author needs it to be true for the argument to work but may never say or imply it. Assumptions are the business of [Lesson 6](chapter:9). The difference matters: an inference is something that follows *from* the passage; an assumption is something the passage *depends on*.

**Inference from word choice and tone.** Authors imply things through diction. "The ministry's celebrated 'simplification'" does not claim the ministry failed, but the scare quotes and the word "celebrated" signal doubt. When an option asks about the author's attitude, use [Lesson 2](chapter:5): track evaluative adjectives, quotation marks, verbs of reporting ("claims," "admits," "insists") and the way concessions are phrased. Inferences about attitude should stay modest: from "evident pride" you may infer the office is pleased with itself; you may not infer that it is lying.

**Inference from structure.** Where an author places an example matters. An example offered after "however" usually undermines the preceding view. An example offered "for instance" supports the claim that precedes it. The question "why does the author mention the wedding in paragraph two?" is an inference about purpose, and it is answered by finding the claim the wedding serves.

**Inference with conditionals.** When a passage contains an "if...then," "only if," "unless," or "no X without Y," translate it before you evaluate options (Core Test Skills, Chapter 16, covers the contrapositive, converse and inverse). Only two moves are safe: from "if P then Q" and P you may conclude Q, and from "if P then Q" and not-Q you may conclude not-P. The common wrong moves are affirming the consequent (Q, therefore P) and denying the antecedent (not-P, therefore not-Q). Passages in this section state conditionals in ordinary prose, so look for words like *requires*, *only when*, *as long as*, *never without*.

**Six ways an inference answer goes wrong.** Each wrong option in the questions below is built from one of these, and the explanations name which. The full catalog is in [Elimination and Trap Answers](chapter:17).

| Trap | What it does |
|---|---|
| Too extreme | Upgrades *some* to *all* or *may* to *must* |
| Out of scope | Talks about something the passage never addresses |
| Reversed | Flips the direction of a relationship |
| Unsupported | Plausible, but needs an extra assumption |
| True but not asked | Accurate, yet not what the question wants |
| Language lift | Reuses the passage's words with a changed meaning |

**A five-step routine for inference questions.**

1. Read the question stem and note the strength word: *must*, *most strongly*, *suggests*, *could*.
2. Return to the part of your map that the stem points to. If it points nowhere, the question concerns the passage as a whole.
3. Predict, in your own words, what the passage supports. Keep the prediction loose; you are predicting a direction, not a sentence.
4. Test each option with the defense test, asking whether you can point to the line.
5. Choose the option with the best support and the fewest extra assumptions, then check it against the stem once more.

Praxis Tip: When two options both look supportable, choose the one that asks you to assume less. Inference questions reward the plainer answer.

**Worked Example 1: Implied versus stated (skill: reading a detail for what it implies)**

> The conservatory's new concert hall was praised by every critic who reviewed it for its clarity: each note, they wrote, arrived at the back row exactly as it left the stage. The older players were less delighted. A violist who had spent three decades in the old, reverberant hall said that she could now hear every flaw in her section's playing and that, for the first time, the audience could too. She did not ask to return to the old hall. She asked for more rehearsal time.

*Question.* Which of the following is best supported by the passage?

A. The old hall's reverberation had helped to blur small imperfections in the orchestra's playing.
B. The violist regrets the orchestra's move to the new hall.
C. The orchestra plays worse in the new hall than it did in the old one.
D. The critics disliked the old hall.

*Reasoning.* The violist's remark has an implied half: if she can now hear flaws she could not hear before, and the audience can too, then something about the old hall had masked them. The only feature the passage attaches to the old hall is reverberation, so A follows. B is tempting because she is "less delighted," but she "did not ask to return," so regret is unsupported. C reverses the point: the flaws were presumably there before and are only now exposed, not newly created. D is out of scope: the critics are praised for their comments on the new hall and nothing is said about their view of the old one.

*Skill used:* treating a speaker's comment as evidence about a situation the passage never describes directly.

Praxis Tip: When an author reports someone's reaction, ask what situation would make that reaction sensible. The answer is often the inference.

**Worked Example 2: Surviving and failing (skill: refusing the converse)**

> Most of the town's bakeries closed during the decade after the supermarket opened, although several did not. The surviving bakeries had one thing in common: each sold at least one kind of bread that the supermarket did not.

*Question.* Which of the following must be true?

A. At least one bakery that sold bread unavailable at the supermarket was still open at the end of the decade.
B. Every bakery that closed had sold only bread that the supermarket also sold.
C. Selling bread unavailable at the supermarket guaranteed that a bakery would survive.
D. The supermarket's opening caused most of the closures.

*Reasoning.* The passage says several bakeries survived and each sold a distinctive bread, so A must be true: it simply restates a necessary consequence. B moves the wrong way. "Every survivor was distinctive" tells you what survivors had in common; it does not tell you that every non-survivor lacked distinctiveness (the inverse of the passage's pattern). C is the converse: it treats distinctiveness as sufficient for survival, but the passage only presents it as shared by survivors, which is closer to a necessary condition. D is a causal claim; the passage reports timing and a pattern, not a cause.

*Skill used:* keeping necessary and sufficient apart, and noticing that "after" is not "because."

Praxis Tip: When a passage says every member of a group had a feature, you may infer something about the group. You may not infer that everyone with the feature belongs to the group.

**Worked Example 3: Implication from tone (skill: scare quotes and evaluative words)**

> The ministry's celebrated "simplification" of the school curriculum removed forty pages of regulations and added sixty pages of guidance on how to interpret what remained. Teachers, who had hoped for fewer documents, now receive more of them.

*Question.* The author's use of the word "simplification" in quotation marks most strongly suggests that the author

A. believes the ministry acted dishonestly.
B. thinks the curriculum was too short.
C. doubts that the change made the curriculum simpler.
D. would prefer more regulations to fewer.

*Reasoning.* The scare quotes plus the arithmetic (forty pages removed, sixty added) point to one modest conclusion: the author doubts the result deserves the name. That is C. A is too extreme: nothing in the passage accuses anyone of intent to deceive. B is out of scope: the passage is about the number of documents, not the curriculum's length. D reverses the author's sympathy with teachers who "hoped for fewer documents."

*Skill used:* reading a quotation mark as evidence, and stopping at the strength the evidence supports.

**Worked Example 4: Ranking strength (skill: "most strongly supported")**

> In the island's oral tradition, a storyteller who changed the ending of a well-known tale was never scolded. But the audience would fall silent for a moment, and the following week the storyteller would usually have fewer listeners.

*Question.* Which of the following is most strongly supported?

A. Storytellers who changed endings were formally punished.
B. The audience expected to hear familiar endings and let a departure be known informally.
C. A storyteller who changed an ending never regained an audience.
D. Tale endings were the same on every island.

*Reasoning.* Rank the options by how much you must add to the passage. B adds almost nothing: silence and shrinking attendance are an informal way of signaling displeasure with a departure from expectation. A reverses the text ("never scolded"). C is too extreme: "usually have fewer listeners" does not say that listeners never returned. D is out of scope: the passage concerns one island.

*Skill used:* ranking candidates by the number of extra assumptions each needs.

**Worked Example 5: A conditional inside a passage (skill: logic without over-reading)**

> The gallery's acquisition policy is plain: no work enters the permanent collection unless the full acquisitions committee has approved it. The harbor painting entered the permanent collection last year. Several committee members have since said privately that they were lukewarm about it.

*Question.* Which of the following can be inferred?

A. The full committee approved the harbor painting.
B. The committee members who were lukewarm voted against the painting.
C. The harbor painting is one of the gallery's finest works.
D. The policy was broken, because some members were lukewarm.

*Reasoning.* Translate: if a work is in the collection, then the full committee approved it. The painting is in the collection, so the full committee approved it: A. B contradicts A (an approving committee did not contain a vote against) and invents a vote. C is out of scope; the policy says nothing about quality. D confuses private feeling with formal approval; lukewarm members may still have approved.

*Skill used:* applying "if in, then approved" in the one direction that is valid.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Underline every qualifier in the stem and in the option you are tempted to choose. If the option's qualifier is stronger than the passage's, it is wrong.

Praxis Tip: "Suggests" and "implies" ask for a short step beyond the text. "Must be true" asks for a logical necessity. Do not use a necessity standard on a "suggests" question, or a suggestion standard on a "must" question.

Praxis Tip: If you need a sentence beginning "unless we also assume..." to justify an option, that option is not a good inference. The extra assumption is the giveaway.

Praxis Tip: Check the verb. "Studies find that" and "the author believes that" and "critics propose that" belong to different speakers, and options often swap them. Passages here are deliberately careful about who says what.

Praxis Tip: Do not penalize an answer for being dull. The correct inference is often a modest restatement of a consequence; the exciting options are often the traps.

Praxis Tip: Time spent on a hard inference question is often better spent returning to the passage than staring at the options. Inference lives in the text, not in your head.

**Common Mistakes**

- Choosing an option because it is true in the real world.
- Letting a qualifier slide: *some* read as *most*, *may* read as *does*.
- Treating the author's description of someone else's view as the author's own.
- Reading a correlation or sequence in the passage as a cause.
- Confusing "the passage does not rule it out" with "the passage supports it."
- Treating a tone word as proof of intent or motive.
- Rejecting a correct answer because it feels too obvious.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. For two centuries the guild of glaze-makers in the river town kept its recipes in a locked chest, and each master taught them to only one apprentice, usually a son or a niece. When the guild dissolved, the chest was found to hold not recipes but a set of small clay tiles, each bearing a color and a string of tally marks. Historians have suggested that the tallies recorded quantities of ingredients. If so, the guild's secrecy was not merely a matter of hiding a list: a stranger who found the tiles could not have used them without years of training in how a tally corresponded to a measure.

   The passage most strongly implies that the tiles

   A. proved that the guild's glazes were better than those of rival towns
   B. would have been of little use without training by a master
   C. were shown by historians to record exact ingredient quantities
   D. were always passed to a relative of the master

2. The planning office has announced, with evident pride, that the new riverside promenade will be "fully accessible." The plans show wide ramps, tactile paving and fifty benches. They also show that the only entrance from the old town is a flight of forty-two steps, and the office's reply to questions about this has been that stairs are "a heritage feature."

   The author's choice to quote the phrase "fully accessible" most nearly suggests that the author regards the claim as

   A. an accurate summary of a carefully and thoughtfully designed plan
   B. a deliberate lie told in order to mislead the public
   C. unrelated to the promenade's actual design
   D. undercut by a feature the planners overlook

3. Surveys of the town's three libraries found that most borrowers over sixty visit at least once a month, while fewer than a fifth of borrowers under twenty-five do. The surveys did not ask why. The head librarian suspects that younger residents read on screens, but she concedes that the surveys cannot show this, since they counted only borrowers, not readers.

   Which of the following conclusions is best supported by the passage?

   A. The surveys do not show that younger residents read less.
   B. Younger residents read on screens in place of borrowing books.
   C. Older residents read more than younger residents.
   D. The surveys were conducted at a single library.

4. A cooperative's charter states that no member may sell shares to an outsider unless the board has first offered them to the existing members and none has accepted. Last spring, a member sold shares to an outsider.

   Which of the following must be true?

   A. The board offered the shares to existing members, and none accepted.
   B. The sale broke the charter, and the board knew it.
   C. Either the offer was made and refused, or the charter was broken.
   D. The existing members declined because the price was too high.

5. Some readers believe a translation succeeds when it disappears, when the reader forgets that the words were once in another language. But a translation that leaves no trace of its origin has, in a sense, hidden the one fact that makes it a translation. A better ideal might be a text that is fluent without being seamless: one whose occasional strangeness reminds the reader that someone, somewhere, once said these things differently.

   Which of the following would the author most likely agree with?

   A. Occasional strangeness in a translation can be a virtue.
   B. A translation should be so awkward that readers constantly notice it.
   C. Fluent translation is impossible in practice.
   D. Most readers prefer translations that disappear.

6. In the village archive, the 1890s account books list the schoolmaster's salary beside the salary of the church bell-ringer. The bell-ringer was paid more. No note explains the difference, and the surviving minutes of the parish council never mention it. Whether it troubled anyone at the time is unknown, but it is notable that the schoolmaster's salary was raised only after the bell-ringer retired.

   Which of the following is best supported by the passage?

   A. The parish council deliberately underpaid the schoolmaster out of disrespect.
   B. The bell-ringer's work was considered more skilled than the schoolmaster's.
   C. The schoolmaster complained to the parish council about his salary.
   D. The records allow a link between the retirement and the raise but do not prove one.

7. The composer wrote the quartet during the winter in which she lost her hearing in one ear. Early listeners noticed that the second violin, normally a supporting voice, carries the melody in the slow movement. One critic proposed that the composer, hearing the instruments unevenly, had begun to favor the voice seated nearest her good ear. The composer's letters contain no reference to the matter. In her later quartets the second violin returns to its usual role.

   Each of the following can be inferred from the passage EXCEPT:

   A. The critic's explanation has not been confirmed by the composer's own letters.
   B. The composer revised the slow movement after the critic's proposal.
   C. The second violin's melodic role in the slow movement was not repeated in her later quartets.
   D. The unusual treatment of the second violin was noticed early.

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **B.** The final sentence says a stranger could not have used the tiles without training, and that is conditional only on the historians' suggestion. (A is out of scope; the passage never compares glazes; C is too extreme; historians only "suggested" it, and the passage adds "if so"; D is too extreme; the passage says "usually" and concerns apprentices, not the tiles.)
2. **D.** Quotation marks plus the contrasting detail (forty-two steps as the only entrance) signal doubt about the label, not an accusation of dishonesty. (A reverses the sense of the passage; the steps and the office's reply are offered as a problem; B is too extreme; the passage reports pride and a poor reply, not intent to deceive; C is out of scope; the ramps and benches are part of the design the claim describes.)
3. **A.** The surveys counted borrowers, and the librarian herself says they cannot show reading habits, so no conclusion about reading follows. (B is unsupported; this is the librarian's suspicion, which she concedes the surveys cannot show; C is unsupported; the surveys measured borrowing, and reading is not the same thing; D reverses the text, which refers to the town's three libraries.)
4. **C.** The charter says a sale is permitted only if the offer step happened, so a sale means either the step happened or the rule was violated; the passage does not say which. (A is unsupported; it assumes the charter was followed, which the passage does not tell us; B is unsupported; it assumes the charter was not followed, which the passage does not tell us; D is out of scope; the passage gives no reason for any decision.)
5. **A.** The author proposes fluency with occasional visible strangeness as a "better" ideal than seamlessness. (B is too extreme; the author wants "occasional" strangeness, not constant awkwardness; C reverses the text; the ideal is fluent "without being seamless"; D is out of scope; the passage reports what "some readers believe," not what most prefer.)
6. **D.** The passage notes the sequence of events and says nothing about cause, so the careful reading is that a link is possible but unproven. (A is unsupported; no motive is recorded; B is unsupported; the passage says no note explains the difference; C is unsupported; the passage says it is unknown whether anyone was troubled.)
7. **B.** Nothing in the passage says the composer revised anything or read the critic; the other options each rest on a sentence in the passage. (A can be inferred, since the letters "contain no reference to the matter"; C can be inferred, since the violin "returns to its usual role"; D can be inferred, since "early listeners noticed" it.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, name the trap on every option you rejected (extreme, out of scope, reversed, unsupported, true but not asked, language lift) and record any trap you fell for in your error log (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9).
