# Lesson 3: The Anatomy of an Argument

**What you will be able to do**

- Break a passage's argument into claims, evidence, assumptions and conclusions, and tell which is which.
- Separate the main conclusion from intermediate steps, and an argument from mere description.
- Spot an unstated assumption with the negation test.
- Identify and classify counterarguments (objection, concession, rebuttal, refutation) and say what each does for the author.
- Distinguish the author's own view from views the author only reports, and answer "would the author agree?" questions correctly.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Most CARS passages are arguments in disguise, and most questions beyond main idea ask about some part of the argument: what supports a claim, what the author assumes, how an objection is handled, what would weaken the reasoning. The skills of reasoning within the text and reasoning beyond the text, described in [Strategy: The Three Question Skills](chapter:3) (approximate shares of 30 and 40 percent of the section [VERIFY]), rest on this anatomy. This lesson applies the logic basics from Core Test Skills, Chapter 16 to flowing prose, where premises do not arrive in neat numbered rows and the author's opponents are often quoted inside her own paragraphs.

The skill that costs the most points is also the simplest to describe: keeping track of **who is speaking**. An author who writes "Some argue that wealth brings happiness" has not claimed that wealth brings happiness. Every wrong answer that treats a reported view as the author's is a point thrown away.

Praxis Tip: Before you answer any question about an argument, ask who holds the view in the sentence you are looking at: the author, a named person, "some critics," or nobody in particular. Four seconds on this question save a surprising amount of regret.

**Concept Review**

**The parts of an argument.**

- **Claim:** a statement offered as true. Claims come in kinds: *descriptive* ("attendance fell"), *interpretive* ("the fall reflects boredom"), *evaluative* ("the festival succeeded") and *prescriptive* ("the council should fund it").
- **Evidence:** what is offered in support: examples, cases, statistics, testimony, comparisons, analogies and appeals to authority. Evidence answers the question, "Why should I believe that?"
- **Conclusion:** the claim the argument is trying to establish. The **main conclusion** is the one every other part serves. An **intermediate conclusion** is established on the way and then used as a premise.
- **Assumption:** an unstated claim the argument needs. It bridges the gap between the evidence and the conclusion.
- **Counterargument:** a consideration that goes against the conclusion, whether from the author or from someone else.

**Argument or description?** Not every passage argues. A passage that narrates how something developed, or explains how a mechanism works, may contain no conclusion at all in the sense used here. Test: is there a claim that a reasonable person could resist, and does the text try to move you toward it? If yes, it is an argument. If the text only says what happened or how it works, it is an explanation, and "what is the author's conclusion?" is the wrong question.

**Finding the conclusion.** The conclusion is not always the last sentence, and is often stated near the beginning and then defended. Look for:

- indicator words: *therefore, thus, so, hence, it follows that, in sum*;
- prescriptions (*should, ought, must*) and evaluations (*better, worse, mistaken*);
- the claim that every other sentence supports when you ask, of each sentence, "Is this here to support something else?"

**Mapping an argument in prose.** On scratch paper, write a chain of short labels: Claim; Because (evidence); Assuming (the gap); Objection (who says?); Reply. This five-label layout works for most CARS arguments. Fill in what you can on the first read and complete the rest only when a question needs it.

Praxis Tip: Make the map small enough to hold in your head. A map with fifteen boxes is a transcript.

**Types of evidence, and what each can and cannot do.**

| Type | Can show | Cannot show |
|---|---|---|
| Single example | That something is possible | That it is typical |
| Many cases | A pattern or tendency | A cause, without more |
| Statistic | The size or frequency of something | Why it occurs |
| Analogy | That a principle may carry over | That the two cases are alike in the way that matters |
| Expert testimony | That a specialist believes it | That it is true |

Always match the evidence to the strength of the claim. If an author claims that something is **always** so and offers a single example, the claim outruns the evidence. If the claim is only that something **can** happen, a single example suffices.

**Assumptions and the negation test.** An assumption is something the author needs without having said. To check a candidate, **negate it** and see if the argument falls apart. Take: "Attendance at the museum doubled after admission became free, so free admission brought in people who would not otherwise have come." Candidate assumption: "The doubling was not mainly existing visitors coming more often." Negate it: "The doubling was mainly existing visitors coming more often." Now the conclusion no longer follows. So the candidate is a genuine assumption. A statement that, when negated, leaves the argument intact is merely compatible with it, not required.

Two cautions. First, an assumption is not the same as a premise the author states. Second, an assumption is not simply anything that would be nice to know. It must be needed.

**Counterarguments and how authors handle them.** An author who raises an objection can do one of four things. The names matter less than the recognition:

| Move | What the author does | Typical signal |
|---|---|---|
| Concession | Grants a point, but keeps the conclusion | admittedly, granted, to be sure |
| Rebuttal | Answers the objection with a reason | but, yet, however, even so |
| Refutation | Shows the objection to be mistaken or based on a false premise | this ignores, this overlooks |
| Dismissal | Sets aside the objection as irrelevant | beside the point, no matter |

Concessions strengthen an argument because they make the author look fair, and they are often where wrong answers hide: a concession is the author's grant, not the author's conclusion. If the author says "The charge is not baseless," the charge is not endorsed in full.

**Whose view is it? A table of voices.**

| Phrase | Whose view? | The author's own? |
|---|---|---|
| "Some critics hold that..." | The critics | Not yet; look for a reply |
| "It is often said that..." | A common view | Usually about to be qualified |
| "Proponents argue that..." | Proponents | Not unless the author agrees |
| "In fact" / "the truth is" / "I will argue" | The author | Yes |
| "Surely we must admit that..." | Ambiguous | Read the next sentence |
| "So-called experts" | The author mocks them | The author's attitude, not the experts' |

Rules of thumb: a view is the author's if she states it in her own voice, endorses it ("this is right"), or relies on it in her reasoning. A view is only reported if she attributes it, and it stays reported until she signals agreement.

Praxis Tip: When a question asks what the author "would agree with," treat reported views as suspects. Find a sentence in which the author herself endorses the idea, or an idea that the author's reasoning depends on.

**Question types in this family.**

- **Conclusion / main claim:** "The author's central claim is..."
- **Role of a sentence:** "The author's statement about the fountain bench serves to..." (evidence, concession, example, objection, reply).
- **Author's view versus reported view:** "Which of these would the author most likely endorse?"
- **Assumption:** "The argument assumes that..." or "The author's reasoning depends on which of the following?"
- **Weaken/strengthen:** "Which finding would most weaken the argument?" ([Lesson 6](chapter:9) treats these in depth.)
- **Evidence assessment:** "Which of these, if true, would be the best support for the claim in paragraph 2?"

Praxis Tip: For role-of-a-sentence questions, label each nearby sentence with a verb (states, supports, concedes, objects, replies). The right answer will usually be one of these verbs in disguise.

**Worked Example 1: Parsing a full argument (skill: claim, evidence, assumption)**

*Passage.* Defenders of daily homework in primary school say that it builds the habit of study. The habit may be real, but the evidence offered for it is thin. Children who do more homework tend to perform better; yet children whose families support schoolwork both do more homework and perform better, so the correlation shows nothing about what homework itself does. What homework certainly does is consume evening hours that children could spend in unstructured play, which develops skills (negotiation, invention) that no worksheet teaches. Until homework's benefits are shown apart from family background, schools should assign far less of it.

*Map.*

- Reported view: homework builds the habit of study (the defenders').
- Concession: "The habit may be real."
- Objection to the evidence: the correlation is explained by family background.
- Evidence for a cost: homework displaces play, which teaches skills.
- Main conclusion (last sentence, prescriptive): schools should assign far less homework until benefits are shown independently.
- Assumptions: that the skills learned in play are valuable; that the displaced time would in fact be spent in play; that a benefit not yet shown is not worth the cost.

*Reading it.* The first sentence states the defenders' view, not the author's. The author's conclusion is the prescriptive final sentence. "May be real" is a concession. The evidence for the author's own case is the cost of displaced play.

**Worked Example 2: Author's view versus views reported (skill: tracking voices)**

*Passage.* Some argue that tourism cheapens local traditions by turning them into performances. Others reply that traditions have always been performances: festivals exist to be watched. The first group is right about something: when a festival is rescheduled to suit a tour operator, a decision has passed from the community to outsiders. The second group is right about something else: nothing in the performance itself betrays the tradition. What matters, then, is not whether a ritual is watched but who decides when and how it is held.

*Question.* With which statement would the author most likely agree?

A. Tourism necessarily cheapens a tradition by making it a performance.
B. A tradition is not harmed merely because outsiders watch it.
C. Festivals should never be rescheduled.
D. Tour operators are the main cause of cultural decline.

*Reasoning.* Voices: "some argue" (group 1), "others reply" (group 2), and then the author's own sorting: each group "is right about something." The author's conclusion is the last sentence: control matters, not observation. **B** follows from that: being watched is not what harms a tradition. **A** is group 1's view, and the author denies it ("right about something," not about everything). **C** is too extreme; the passage objects to a rescheduling that passes control to outsiders, not to every rescheduling. **D** is a stronger claim about cultural decline in general than the text provides.

**Worked Example 3: Finding the assumption by negation (skill: the missing bridge)**

*Argument.* Attendance at the city museum doubled in the year after admission became free. The director concludes that free admission drew in people who had never visited before.

*Candidate assumptions.*

(i) Most of the new attendance came from people who had not visited before. Negated: most of the new attendance came from people who had visited before. The conclusion no longer follows. (i) is **required**.

(ii) The museum's exhibits are good. Negated: the exhibits are poor. Attendance still doubled, and the conclusion (about who attended) is unaffected. (ii) is **not required**.

(iii) Attendance would not have doubled anyway for some unrelated reason. Negated: it would have doubled anyway. The inference that *free admission* caused the draw fails. (iii) is **required** for the causal claim.

Note that (i) and (iii) answer different parts of the argument: who came, and why.

**Worked Example 4: Labeling counterargument moves (skill: concession, rebuttal, refutation)**

*Passage.* (1) It will be objected that handwritten letters are slow, and that slowness is a cost. (2) Granted. (3) But the objection assumes that speed of delivery is what a letter is for. (4) A letter is composed, not merely sent: the writer has time to decide what she means, and the reader has a record to return to. (5) Whatever is lost in haste is gained in thought.

*Labels.* (1) Objection (attributed to "it will be objected"). (2) Concession: the author grants that letters are slow. (3) Refutation: the objection rests on a false premise about purpose. (4) Evidence for the author's view of the purpose. (5) Conclusion, with a trade-off stated as a gain.

*Why it matters.* A question that asks "The author's statement in sentence (2) serves to..." has the answer "concede a point to the objection, without abandoning the position." A question that asks whether the author thinks letters are slow has the answer yes. A question that asks whether the author regards slowness as a serious cost has the answer no. The concession grants a fact, not a verdict.

**Worked Example 5: Matching evidence to the claim (skill: evidence assessment)**

*Passage.* It is sometimes claimed that bilingual children are more creative. The claim usually rests on a single study, conducted in one school, of forty children. That study found bilingual children scored higher on a creativity test. A reasonable reader may grant the result and still withhold the conclusion, for two reasons: forty children in one school may differ from the rest in many ways besides language, and a test is a narrow measure of something as broad as creativity.

*Question.* What is the main weakness the author finds in the evidence for the claim?

A. The study's result was negative.
B. A small sample from a single school and a narrow test cannot support a general claim.
C. Bilingual children have no advantages of any kind.
D. The researchers were dishonest.

*Reasoning.* The claim is broad ("bilingual children are more creative"); the evidence is a single study, small, local, and measured by one test. The mismatch of breadth and evidence is the weakness. **B**. **A** reverses the finding, which the author grants. **C** is far too extreme and not claimed. **D** has no textual support: the author questions the conclusion, not the researchers' honesty. Notice that the author does not say the claim is false, only that the evidence is insufficient: a classic "withhold the conclusion" move.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: A concession is not a defeat. When an author writes "granted," she is spending a small point to buy a large one. Do not let the small point sit in the answer you choose.

Praxis Tip: To separate evidence from conclusion, ask "Is this sentence something the author needs me to believe, or something she is giving me a reason to believe?" The first is the conclusion; the second is evidence.

Praxis Tip: Distrust any answer choice that treats an objection as the author's position. If the choice repeats a phrase beginning "Some argue," check whether the author signs it.

Praxis Tip: "Would the author agree?" questions have a safe harbor: choices that follow from the author's own explicit statements. Choices that merely sound sensible need a source.

Praxis Tip: Assumptions are usually quiet and boring. A required assumption often sounds like "nothing else explains the result," "the example is typical" or "the two cases are alike in the way that matters." Look for the bridge, not the flourish.

Praxis Tip: When you suspect an assumption, run the negation test on your scratch paper. It takes ten seconds and removes any guesswork about whether the candidate is actually necessary.

**Common Mistakes**

- Attributing a reported view ("some critics say") to the author.
- Treating a concession as the conclusion, or stopping at the concession.
- Calling a premise an assumption (it is stated) or calling a nice-to-know claim an assumption (it is not needed).
- Looking for a conclusion in a passage that only explains or describes.
- Confusing the strength of the evidence with the strength of the claim; a modest claim can survive thin evidence, a sweeping one cannot.
- Choosing a choice that is true in the world but unsupported by the passage.
- Missing an intermediate conclusion and treating it as the main conclusion.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. *Passage.* A common remedy for traffic congestion is to build more roads. But new roads attract new drivers, so within a few years the roads are as crowded as before. A road is built to relieve pressure, and it ends by creating it. Cities should therefore invest in public transit.

   The author's main conclusion is that
   A. new roads attract new drivers
   B. congestion is the most serious problem facing cities
   C. building roads has never relieved congestion at any time
   D. cities should invest in public transit

2. *Passage.* Some scholars hold that medieval guilds were purely economic bodies, concerned only with prices and standards. The records of one town tell a different story: its guild paid for burials, supported widows and organized an annual procession. A body that did all this was plainly a community as well as a trade association.

   The author mentions the guild's burials, widows and procession primarily as
   A. support for the scholars' claim about prices
   B. evidence against the scholars' claim
   C. an objection that the author goes on to dismiss
   D. a concession that guilds sometimes acted outside trade

3. *Passage.* It is often said that a national anthem unites a people. There may be something in this, but the evidence usually cited is a crowd singing together, and a crowd singing together proves only that people can follow a tune. Whether the singers agree about anything else is exactly what the singing does not show. If anthems unite, they do so by giving people a shared occasion, not by giving them shared beliefs.

   Which of the following would the author most likely endorse?
   A. A national anthem unites a people by giving them shared beliefs.
   B. Crowds who sing together are rarely sincere.
   C. Singing together does not show agreement.
   D. The idea that anthems unite is entirely mistaken.

4. *Passage.* The theater's attendance rose after it began showing older films on weeknights. The manager concludes that the weeknight screenings of older films are what brought new viewers in.

   Which of the following is an assumption on which the manager's conclusion depends?
   A. Nothing else accounts for the rise in attendance.
   B. Older films are, on the whole, better than most recent releases.
   C. Weeknight audiences are larger than weekend audiences.
   D. The theater had no viewers on weeknights before the change.

5. *Passage.* A town council banned cars from its central square and reports that shop sales in the square rose by a fifth the following year. It concludes that traffic restrictions are good for shops.

   Which finding would most weaken the council's conclusion?
   A. Unrestricted neighboring shops saw similar sales growth.
   B. Most shoppers in the square already arrived on foot before the ban took effect.
   C. Some shopkeepers in the square opposed the ban before it was introduced.
   D. The square is now more pleasant for pedestrians than it was.

6. *Passage.* Skeptics say that the famous letter is a forgery, because its paper was made some years after its supposed date. Admittedly, the paper is later. But the letter's owner, a careful archivist, explains that the original was copied onto fresh paper when it began to crumble, and that the copy was made by the writer's own hand. If so, the objection loses its force.

   The author's sentence beginning "Admittedly" serves to
   A. introduce the author's main conclusion about the forgery
   B. refute the skeptics by showing the paper is earlier than they claim
   C. grant a point without giving up the position
   D. show that the archivist is not a reliable witness

7. *Passage.* Some philosophers argue that we owe nothing to people we will never meet. Others reply that moral concern must extend to the farthest stranger. Both positions strike me as too tidy. A person's obligations are strongest where her actions have the most effect, and they weaken, but do not vanish, with distance.

   Which of the following best expresses the author's own view?
   A. We owe nothing to people we will never meet.
   B. Obligations weaken with distance but persist.
   C. Moral concern must extend equally to the farthest stranger.
   D. Philosophers have not seriously considered the question of distance.

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **D.** The word "therefore" and the prescriptive "should" mark the last sentence as the conclusion; the earlier claims lead up to it. (A is an intermediate claim offered as support, not the main conclusion; B overstates the passage, which never ranks problems; C is too extreme; the passage says roads bring crowding "within a few years," not that they never help.)
2. **B.** The records are introduced with "a different story" and followed by the author's inference that guilds were communities too. (A reverses the function of the examples; C mislabels the author's own evidence as an objection; D treats the examples as a grant to the other side, when the author uses them to reject it.)
3. **C.** The author's own claim is that singing together shows a shared occasion, not agreement; the common view is only reported. (A reverses the author's final sentence; B goes beyond the passage, which does not question their sincerity; D is too extreme given the concession "there may be something in this" and the author's alternative account.)
4. **A.** Negate the correct choice (something else did change) and the causal conclusion about the screenings no longer follows. (B is not required, since the conclusion concerns attendance, not quality; C is not needed to link the screenings to the rise; D is too strong, since the argument works even if some viewers already attended.)
5. **A.** If unrestricted areas also saw similar growth, then the ban was probably not what raised sales, which attacks the causal assumption. (B does not damage the conclusion and may even be compatible with it; C concerns early opinion, not the effect on sales; D leaves the causal claim about sales untouched.)
6. **C.** "Admittedly... But" is the concession pattern: the fact is conceded and the objection is then answered. (A mistakes a concession for the conclusion; B reverses the content; the author grants that the paper is later; D has no textual support; the archivist's explanation is treated as a serious reply.)
7. **B.** The author rejects both reported positions and gives her own in the last sentence, which is in her own voice. (A is the first philosophers' position, which the author calls too tidy; C is the second position, which the author also rejects as too tidy; D is unsupported; the passage reports that philosophers have taken positions.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, for each miss write whose view you attributed to the author, or which part of the argument you mistook for another. Add the pattern to your error log (Core Test Skills, Chapter 9).

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "An argument is a conclusion supported by evidence and bridged by assumptions; I find the conclusion by asking what everything else serves, I find assumptions by negating candidates, I recognize an objection and the author's concession, rebuttal or refutation of it, and I never credit the author with a view she only reports."

*Interleave:* alternate an argument-mapping task with a tone question from [Lesson 2](chapter:5) and an inference question from [Lesson 4](chapter:7). When you are ready for strengthen and weaken questions, go to [Lesson 6](chapter:9).

Praxis Tip: End each study session by choosing one argument from your day (a news item, a conversation, a text from a relative with firm views) and naming its conclusion, one piece of evidence and one assumption. If you can, offer the person a gracious concession as well. It is the quickest known way to win an argument without anyone noticing.
