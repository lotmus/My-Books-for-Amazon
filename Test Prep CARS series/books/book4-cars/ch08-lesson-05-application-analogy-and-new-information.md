# Lesson 5: Application, Analogy and New Information

**What you will be able to do**

- Extract the underlying principle from a passage and apply it to a new situation.
- Match analogies by relationship and structure, not by surface subject matter.
- Judge how a piece of new information affects a passage's claim: supports, undermines or leaves it alone.
- Predict how an author would respond to a hypothetical case.
- Spot the standard traps in questions that ask you to move beyond the text.

**Why it matters for the exam**

The third skill family in the CARS section, reasoning beyond the text, is understood to carry the largest share of questions, roughly 40 percent [VERIFY]. These questions hand you a new scenario, a new fact, or a parallel case and ask what follows. They are not harder because they need outside knowledge (they do not); they are harder because the passage does not tell you where to look. You must carry an idea out of the passage and test it elsewhere.

[Lesson 4](chapter:7) taught you to stay inside the text. This lesson teaches you to leave it with a passport: the principle you extract is the passport, and the question tells you where to travel. [Lesson 6](chapter:9) deals with the most formal beyond-the-text questions (strengthen, weaken and assumption), and uses the new-information skill developed here.

Praxis Tip: A question that starts "Suppose..." or "Which of the following situations..." is asking whether you understood the passage's idea, not its wording. Answer with the idea.

**Concept Review**

**Three question shapes.**

| Shape | The question asks | Typical stem |
|---|---|---|
| Application | Which new case fits the passage's principle? | "Which situation best illustrates the author's point?" |
| Analogy | Which parallel has the same structure? | "Which is most analogous to...?" |
| New information | How does a new fact bear on the claim? | "Which finding, if true, would most...?" |

All three share one skill: **abstraction**, which means stating the passage's idea at a level where it has no subject matter. "The author argues that a public square is defined by what it permits" becomes "something is defined by the range of things it allows, not by what happens in it." Once the idea is abstract, you can recognize it in a bus station, a poem or a legal code.

**The abstraction ladder.** Take a claim and climb three rungs on scratch paper.

1. *As written:* "The 1820 charter gave every villager the right to graze animals on the common."
2. *With the subject removed:* "A right granted to everyone worked because of conditions no one stated."
3. *As a principle:* "Unstated conditions can make an unlimited right harmless, and changing those conditions can make it harmful."

Rung 3 is what you carry into the answer options. Rung 1 is what the wrong options borrow.

**Application questions.** The passage gives a principle; each option offers a case. Check three things.

- *Relationship:* does the case contain the same relationship between its parts, with the same direction?
- *Conditions:* does the case meet the conditions the author attached (for instance "only when...," "in so far as...")?
- *Scope:* does the case stay within what the principle covers, or does it stretch it?

A case can borrow the passage's vocabulary and still fail all three. A case can use entirely different vocabulary and pass. Choose by the relationship.

**Analogy questions.** An analogy compares two relationships, not two things. "A is to B as C is to D" means the way A acts on B is the way C acts on D. When you map the passage's example, name each part by its *role*: the one who guides, the thing guided, the end state. Then name the roles in each option. The right option fills every role; a wrong option fills some roles and fails others, or fills them in reverse order. Two traps recur.

- *Surface match:* the option shares a subject (a river, a ship) with the passage's example but not its relationship.
- *Partial match:* the option mirrors one feature of the example and ignores the one that matters.

**New-information questions.** Treat the passage's argument as a bridge from evidence to conclusion (see [Lesson 3](chapter:6)). New information can do four things.

| Effect | What it does to the bridge |
|---|---|
| Supports | Strengthens the link or removes a rival explanation |
| Undermines | Cuts the link, offers a rival explanation or damages the evidence |
| Is consistent but irrelevant | Fits the passage but leaves the bridge as it is |
| Is irrelevant | Concerns a topic the argument does not use |

Decide the direction before you read the options, and decide the target: the evidence, the link or the conclusion. A fact that merely repeats the evidence does not strengthen the argument; a fact that merely agrees with the conclusion does not either, because it does not touch the link between them.

**Extension questions.** Some stems ask how the author would react to a hypothetical: "Which of the following would the author most likely predict?" Use the author's principle as a rule for generating predictions. If the author argues that nostalgia flatters the past, predict flattering distortion, not accuracy and not blank memory. Stay with the direction and strength the author gave. An author who said "often" will not be committed to "always."

**Putting limits on a principle.** Authors often state a principle with its limits attached: "a custom deserves respect except where it causes serious harm." An application option that ignores the exception, or uses it to cancel the principle, fails. Underline limiting words during your first read.

**A routine for beyond-the-text questions.**

1. Read the stem and name the shape (application, analogy, new information, extension).
2. State the passage's relevant idea in one abstract sentence.
3. For analogy, list the roles; for new information, list the bridge (evidence, link, conclusion).
4. Predict what a good answer would do, not what it would say.
5. Test each option: does it fit the abstract sentence and the roles? Eliminate by naming the failure (surface match, wrong direction, ignores a condition, goes beyond the scope).

Praxis Tip: Do the abstraction before you read the options. After you see four concrete cases, the surface details start to look like evidence.

**Worked Example 1: Application of a principle (skill: abstraction)**

> A good public square, the architect argues, is defined not by what happens in it but by what it permits to happen: a place where a street musician, a protest and a picnic can all occur without anyone having to ask permission. A square designed for one grand purpose, such as a parade, tends to stand empty on the other three hundred days of the year, while a square designed only to be open fills up with purposes its designer never imagined.

*Question.* Which of the following spaces best reflects the architect's ideal?

A. A plaza with fixed seats facing a stage and a posted schedule of approved events
B. An unroofed paved area with no assigned use and no admission requirement
C. A plaza whose single fountain draws large crowds to the same spot every day
D. A courtyard in which each use must be booked at an office a week in advance

*Reasoning.* Abstract the idea: the best space is open-ended, allowing uses without permission. B fits both parts, no assigned use and no admission. A fixes the uses and so fails the "without anyone having to ask" test. C is popular, but its popularity depends on a single use; it fits the architect's complaint better than the ideal. D explicitly requires permission. Notice that C is the tempting trap: it has a feature the passage seems to value (a full square) but not the *reason* it values it.

*Skill used:* matching the principle, not the outcome, to the case.

Praxis Tip: When a case succeeds on a feature the author mentions only in passing, and fails on the principle the author argues, it is a wrong option.

**Worked Example 2: Analogy by roles (skill: mapping a relationship)**

> A good teacher, in the headmaster's view, is a scaffold and not a crutch. A scaffold is built to be taken down once the building can stand by itself; a crutch is something the walker comes to rely on, and the longer it is used the less the walker's own leg can do.

*Question.* Which of the following relationships is most analogous to the one the headmaster approves?

A. Training wheels that are removed once a child can balance a bicycle
B. A map that a traveler checks at every junction on every journey
C. A lighthouse that guides ships into the same harbor each night
D. A commentary that explains a poem so fully that the reader never rereads it

*Reasoning.* Roles: a helper, a person helped, and an end state in which the person no longer needs the helper. In A the wheels support, then are removed when balance is learned. B and C describe continuing dependence, the crutch pattern. D describes a helper that replaces the reader's effort, which is also the crutch pattern. Only A has the helper working toward its own removal.

*Skill used:* naming roles and checking the end state.

**Worked Example 3: New information that weakens (skill: finding the bridge)**

> Some historians claim that the fishing village's decline began when its harbor silted up, because the village records show catches falling in the years after the silting.

*Question.* Which of the following findings, if true, would most weaken the historians' claim?

A. The silting of the harbor was gradual and took thirty years.
B. Newly discovered records show that catches had been falling for twenty years before the harbor silted up.
C. Neighboring villages were not affected by silting.
D. The village's records were written by the harbor master.

*Reasoning.* Bridge: evidence (catches fell after silting) leads to conclusion (silting began the decline). The link requires that the decline did not start earlier. B removes that: if the fall began before the silting, the silting cannot have begun it. A is consistent with the claim and says nothing about when the decline started. C is irrelevant to the village's own timeline. D raises a vague doubt about the records but gives no reason to think they are wrong, so it does not touch the link.

*Skill used:* aiming new information at the link, not the topic.

**Worked Example 4: Predicting an author's response (skill: extension)**

> Proverbs survive, the author argues, not because they are true but because they are flexible. "Look before you leap" and "He who hesitates is lost" contradict each other, yet both are in constant use, because each fits a different moment and neither is ever required to fit them all.

*Question.* Which of the following would the author most likely expect to find in a community with a rich stock of proverbs?

A. Two sayings about spending money that advise opposite courses, both quoted regularly
B. A single saying about each topic, each applied in every case
C. The disappearance of sayings that contradict other sayings
D. Proverbs that are tested against evidence and dropped when they fail

*Reasoning.* Principle: survival depends on adaptable use, not on consistency or truth. A is a direct application, with two opposed sayings both alive. B demands a universal fit, which the author denies. C reverses the claim: contradiction does not remove a saying. D makes truth the test of survival, which is precisely what the author rejects.

*Skill used:* generating a prediction from the author's rule and checking direction.

**Worked Example 5: Surface match versus structure (skill: analogy under temptation)**

> A language, a linguist writes, resembles a river: it stays the same only by changing. The water that fills the channel today is never the water of yesterday, yet we call the river by the same name, and no one thinks the name a mistake.

*Question.* Which of the following is most analogous to the linguist's view?

A. A ship whose planks are replaced one at a time until none of the originals remains, though it is still called by its original name
B. A mountain whose height stays the same while visitors come and go
C. A river whose floodwaters have damaged a village
D. A lake that dries up in summer and refills each winter

*Reasoning.* Roles: a whole that keeps its identity while its parts are replaced. A has that: parts replaced, identity retained. B shares movement (visitors come and go) but it is the *mountain* that does not change, which is the reverse of the claim. C shares the river but not the relationship; it is a surface match. D concerns change and return of the whole lake, not identity through replacement of parts.

*Skill used:* resisting a surface match, and checking that the direction of change is the right one.

Praxis Tip: The option that repeats the passage's central noun (river, ship, garden) is usually the surface match. Look hardest at the options that do not.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: For an analogy, write the roles as three or four words on scratch paper: "helper, helped, removed when ready." You will compare options against those words and not against your first impression.

Praxis Tip: For new information, ask "which part of the argument does this touch?" If you cannot answer, the option is probably irrelevant.

Praxis Tip: Beware of the option that sounds wise but could have been written without reading the passage. Correct beyond-the-text answers depend on the passage's particular idea.

Praxis Tip: In application questions, check the exceptions the author mentioned. An option may fit the principle perfectly and still fall in the exception.

Praxis Tip: "Most analogous" means best available, not perfect. Pick the option whose structure fits best and do not eliminate the best fit because of a minor surface mismatch.

Praxis Tip: If the new information fits the author's view equally well whichever way the facts turn out, it is probably irrelevant.

**Common Mistakes**

- Choosing the option that shares the passage's subject rather than its structure.
- Choosing a new fact that agrees with the conclusion but does not touch the link.
- Ignoring a condition or exception attached to the principle.
- Applying the principle with more strength than the author gave it.
- Reversing the direction of a relationship in an analogy.
- Looking for a perfect match and rejecting the best one.
- Importing outside knowledge about the new case instead of using only the passage's idea.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. A town's founding charter, the essayist notes, gave every resident the right to graze animals on the common, and for two centuries this worked, because the common was large and the herds were small. The right was never meant as a promise that the grass could not run out; it presupposed a balance that nobody wrote down. The essayist's point is that rights are often stated absolutely but depend silently on conditions that make them harmless. When the conditions change, insisting on the letter of the right may destroy the very thing it was meant to protect.

   Which of the following situations best illustrates the essayist's point?

   A. A tenant is refused a privilege because the lease never mentioned it, though the landlord could have granted it.
   B. A council withdraws a right once residents have come to dislike it and no longer use it.
   C. A lakeside village lets every household fish freely, harmless when few fished but ruinous now that many do.
   D. A clearly stated rule continues to work only because it is enforced strictly and without exception.

2. The best critics, in the reviewer's view, behave like cartographers. They do not improve the land they chart, and they are not admired for the beauty of their drawing. They are judged by one thing: whether a traveler who follows the map arrives where it says. A critic who praises what a reader cannot find, or who warns against what the reader finds delightful, has made a bad map, however fine the prose.

   Which of the following is most analogous to the reviewer's ideal critic?

   A. A restaurant guide, judged by whether diners who follow it find what it describes
   B. A chef who improves the quality of the ingredients before he starts cooking
   C. A gardener who reshapes a landscape to make it more beautiful
   D. A mapmaker admired chiefly for the elegance of the drawings

3. Educators in the district credit the new daily music program for a rise in math scores. Scores rose 8 percent in the two years after it began, and the rise was largest in the schools that gave students the most music time. The district concluded that music practice strengthens the habits of pattern recognition on which mathematics depends.

   Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the district's conclusion?

   A. Music teachers in the district are paid less than the district's math teachers are.
   B. A new math curriculum began in those same two years, first where music time was highest.
   C. Pattern recognition is also used when students learn to read musical notation.
   D. Students who left the music program part-way through saw their math scores fall.

4. A curator proposes that a set of unsigned landscape paintings came from one workshop. All of them, she notes, use an unusual pigment mixture, and all set the horizon slightly above the midline of the canvas.

   Which of the following findings, if true, would most strengthen the curator's proposal?

   A. The same pigment mixture appears in paintings from several rival workshops in other regions.
   B. Landscape painting was a popular genre during the period in which these paintings were made.
   C. The curator has written widely about workshop practice and the history of pigments.
   D. A surviving ledger from one workshop records teaching that pigment mix and high horizons.

5. Nostalgia, the critic argues, is less a memory than a revision. The mind keeps the warmth of an old afternoon and quietly discards the boredom that surrounded it, so that the past arrives already flattered. This is not dishonesty; the editing is done before we are aware of it. But it explains why people who describe their schooldays as the happiest of their lives were often, according to their own diaries from the time, restless and impatient to leave.

   Which of the following would the critic most likely predict about a reunion of former classmates?

   A. Their recollections will match their old diaries closely, entry by entry.
   B. They will remember the unpleasant parts of school most vividly of all.
   C. Many will recall the cafeteria more fondly than they felt then.
   D. They will remember nothing of their schooldays.

6. A translator's note in a poetry collection argues that rhyme matters less in translation than rhythm. Rhyme, she says, depends on accidents of the sounds of one particular language and rarely survives the crossing, whereas rhythm follows the pace of feeling, which is much the same in every language.

   Applying the same reasoning, which of the following would the translator most likely endorse in adapting a comic play into another language?

   A. Keeping every pun intact even if the scenes' timing must change
   B. Replacing the puns if needed, while keeping the timing of each embarrassment
   C. Leaving both puns and timing exactly as written, whatever the cost to the audience
   D. Rewriting the timing to match local habits while preserving the puns

7. A proofreader, in the essayist's view, succeeds precisely when no one notices. The better the work, the less visible it is, and so the profession is rewarded mostly by the absence of complaint. No one writes to a publisher to say that a book contained no typographical errors.

   Which of the following is most analogous to the proofreader's situation as the essayist describes it?

   A. A plumber whose work is confirmed only by the silence of the pipes
   B. A conductor praised for the visible energy of her sweeping gestures
   C. A critic whose reviews are widely quoted in advertisements
   D. An architect famous for the ornate towers of his buildings

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** The lake example has an absolute right, an unstated condition (few users) and a change in conditions that makes insisting on the right self-defeating. (A is out of scope; the essayist's point concerns a right that exists and becomes harmful; B fails the point, since the right becomes harmful through changed conditions, not through dislike; D reverses the point, which concerns unstated conditions rather than enforcement.)
2. **A.** The guide's role is to describe accurately for a follower and to be judged by the follower's experience, which maps the critic, reader and work. (B has the wrong relationship, since the critic is said not to improve what is charted; C reverses the relationship, since the critic does not alter the land; D fails on the one criterion the reviewer rejects, namely beauty of drawing.)
3. **B.** A new math curriculum introduced where music time was highest gives a rival explanation for the larger rise there. (A is irrelevant; salaries do not bear on whether music practice affects math scores; C is consistent with the conclusion but does not touch its link; D would strengthen the conclusion, not weaken it.)
4. **D.** A record linking one workshop to both features supports the claim that the shared features point to a single source. (A would weaken the proposal, since the pigment would then not identify one workshop; B is irrelevant; popularity does not tie the paintings to one workshop; C is irrelevant; her reputation is not evidence about the paintings.)
5. **C.** The critic's principle is that memory flatters the past, so fonder recollection than the original feeling is the direct prediction. (A reverses the critic's claim, which is that recollection departs from the record; B goes against the critic's idea that the past is flattered; D is too extreme; the critic describes revised memory, not the absence of memory.)
6. **B.** The play's puns correspond to rhyme (tied to one language) and the timing of embarrassment to rhythm (shared across languages), so the translator would give up the former first. (A reverses the priorities, since it protects the language-bound feature at the expense of the universal one; C ignores the translator's point that the language-bound feature rarely survives; D reverses the translator's ordering of what matters.)
7. **A.** Both the plumber and the proofreader do work whose success is shown by nothing going wrong, so good work earns silence. (B is a mismatch, since the proofreader's best work is the least visible; C is a mismatch, since the proofreader earns no public notice for good work; D is a mismatch, since the proofreader is noticed only through errors, not display.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, rewrite your abstract sentence or role list for any question you missed. If it was wrong, the mistake was upstream of the options.

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

*Say this aloud in one breath:* "For application, analogy and new-information questions I first state the passage's idea with the subject matter removed, then I test each option against that idea, choosing the structure that matches and rejecting surface matches, reversals, ignored conditions and facts that do not touch the argument's link."

*Review schedule:* revisit this lesson after 1 day (redo Worked Examples 2 and 3 without looking), after 3 days (the checkpoint questions from scratch), after 7 days (a mixed set that includes [Lesson 4](chapter:7) inference questions), and after 21 days (explain the abstraction ladder aloud).

*Interleave:* alternate an application passage with a tone passage and a structure passage. Then continue to [Lesson 6](chapter:9), which formalizes strengthen, weaken and assumption questions.

Praxis Tip: End each study session by choosing one idea from anything you read that day and stating it at the abstract level. Then invent one case that fits it and one surface match that fails it. Few skills are better exercise.
