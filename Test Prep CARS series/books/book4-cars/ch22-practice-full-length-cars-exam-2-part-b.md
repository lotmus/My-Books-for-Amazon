**Passage 1 (questions 1-6)**

Economists asked to value an old building face an awkward fact: nobody sells it. Market prices exist for the land and for the bricks, but the thing that the preservationist cares about, the continuity of a place that generations have recognized, is not traded anywhere. Two methods have grown up to fill the gap. The first, contingent valuation, asks residents how much they would be willing to pay to keep the building. The second, hedonic pricing, examines what buyers actually pay for homes near protected buildings and infers the premium that proximity commands. The first has the virtue of reaching people who never buy anything nearby; the second has the virtue of observing money actually spent.

Each method also errs in a predictable direction. Stated willingness to pay is cheap talk: respondents who face no bill may overstate, or may answer with a general sympathy for old things rather than with a figure they would honor. Hedonic studies avoid that problem, but they capture only the benefits enjoyed privately by neighbors. They miss the visitor who admires a facade for an afternoon, and the descendant who will never see it but is glad that it stands. One method therefore tends to overshoot and the other to fall short, and the honest report of either is a number with a direction of error attached.

A deeper objection holds that even perfect measurement would answer the wrong question. Preservation is often defended not as a good that people happen to want but as a duty to those who come after, and a duty does not vary with the result of a survey. If that is so, pricing heritage invites a comparison, a mill is worth less than a car park, that the idea of duty was meant to forbid. Defenders of valuation reply that resources are limited, and that a city that declines to put a figure on its buildings does not thereby escape trade-offs. It makes them silently, usually in favor of whoever speaks most forcefully at the planning meeting. A refusal to count is not a refusal to choose.

Both positions have something right, and a modest use of the figures can respect both. The numbers should be treated as a floor rather than a verdict. If even the narrow estimate of benefits, the one built from nothing but what neighbors will pay, exceeds the cost of preserving a building, the case is settled without any appeal to duty, and the duty-minded and the cost-minded can agree. If the estimate falls short, the question stays open, and the argument must then be conducted in other terms: terms of obligation, memory and identity, which the figures never claimed to capture. This asks of economics only what economics can do. It does not ask the discipline to say what a place is worth to those who will inherit it, a question on which its practitioners have no special authority, and on which its more confident members would do well to be quiet.

**Passage 2 (questions 7-12)**

The thought that the meaning of a word is the thing it stands for has the attraction of all simple pictures: it is easy to hold and hard to apply. It serves for proper names well enough, though even there it falters, since a description such as "the author of the report" may fail to pick out anyone and yet remain perfectly meaningful. It serves poorly for the small words, such as "if" and "but", for which there is no object to point at. A rival view holds that meaning is use: to know what a word means is to know what may be done with it, in which circumstances it is fittingly uttered, and what follows from its utterance. "Ouch" is not the name of a pain but part of what people do when in pain, and to learn it is to learn a practice, not to attach a label.

The use theorist is exposed to an objection more serious than it first appears. Uses vary. If meaning simply were use, then each deviant occasion, an irony, a joke, a lie, would alter the meaning of the word employed, and nothing would remain for the irony to be ironic about. Irony depends on the hearer's grasp of what the word ordinarily does, which the speaker then turns against itself. The theorist replies by distinguishing the practice from its occasions: meaning lies in the standing pattern of correct use, against which particular uses count as ordinary or as deviant. But the reply has a price. The notion of correct use now bears the weight that the notion of meaning bore before, and one may ask what makes a use correct, if not that it fits the meaning.

The defender answers that the circle is not vicious. Correctness is not conferred by a prior meaning; it is constituted by the agreement of a community in what it will accept and what it will correct. A child who says the wrong thing is corrected, and the correcting is what settles the usage. Meaning does not precede practice as its cause; it is abstracted from the practice as its pattern. This reply has the advantage of making meaning public. A private language, whose words would refer to sensations accessible to one speaker alone, would have no one to correct the speaker, and so no distinction between using a word rightly and merely thinking that one did. What cannot be corrected cannot be a rule.

It would nonetheless be hasty to conclude that the referential picture has nothing to teach. That "Everest" is used as it is depends in part on the fact that there is a mountain, and the use theorist can acknowledge this only by admitting that some practices are shaped by the world, which makes the world's contribution part of the explanation of meaning after all. The two views are better seen as answers to different questions. Reference says what connects certain words to things; use says what makes a noise a word at all. Neither alone says what meaning is, and each states what the other leaves out. A theory that wished to dispense with either would have to explain, without it, how a community's agreement could be about anything in particular, or how an object could ever be the object of a word.

**Passage 3 (questions 13-18)**

It is commonly said that ritual belongs to religion, and that as religion recedes from public life, ritual recedes with it. My fieldwork in a mid-sized logistics company, a workplace that would describe itself as thoroughly secular, suggests otherwise. On the last Friday of each month the employees stop work at three in the afternoon, gather in the loading bay, and read aloud the name of every package lost that month, one by one, before a manager says, "We go on." No policy requires the ceremony. When I asked what it was for, I was told that it was "just what we do," and then, a moment later, that it "keeps us honest."

Three features struck me. The first is repetition: the same wording, the same place, the same order. The second is a boundary. For ten minutes the ordinary rules lapse; telephones are put away, nobody argues over blame, and a junior clerk may speak before a director. The third is that the ceremony makes explicit something otherwise left unsaid, namely that errors are shared and survivable. A memo to the same effect would be skimmed. Said aloud, in company, it is felt.

Colleagues who read my notes objected that this is mere habit, and that to call it ritual is to dress a routine in borrowed solemnity. The distinction matters, and it can be tested. A habit is performed without attention to its meaning and would continue unchanged if the meaning were removed. A ritual is sensitive to its meaning. When the company merged two departments and a manager shortened the reading by skipping names, the staff objected within a week, not because time had been wasted but because the omission said that some lost packages did not count. Habits do not provoke objections of that kind.

I do not claim that every workplace practice is a rite, or that such practices can be manufactured. Consultants who have tried to install "team rituals" by directive have usually produced the opposite of what they intended: an event staged to create solidarity is received as an instruction, and the staff comply and sneer. The ceremony in the loading bay grew from one supervisor's gesture after a bad month and was adopted because people found in it something they needed. Its authority came from that history, which cannot be ordered from a catalogue.

What the case shows, I think, is not that secular life is secretly religious. It shows that the human need which ritual serves, to mark what matters and to act together upon it, does not depend on any particular belief. Where institutions no longer supply forms for that need, people improvise them, and they often do so without noticing that they have done it. The improvisation is easy to overlook precisely because it is not announced as sacred. It is announced as "just what we do," and the sociologist's task is to take the phrase seriously rather than to hear it as an apology.

**Passage 4 (questions 19-24)**

Every translator of a statute is asked to be faithful, and every translator knows the request is unclear. Faithful to what? To the words, which are all that the legislature actually enacted, or to the purpose that the words were chosen to serve? The question sounds like a quibble until one considers that, in a country with two official languages, both versions of a law are held to be equally authoritative. There the translator's choice is not a commentary on the law. It is part of the law.

The usual advice is to translate literally, on the ground that a translator has no authority to improve a statute. This sounds modest, and it is not. A word-for-word rendering of a term from one legal tradition may carry into the other language the connotations of the wrong institution. Suppose that a phrase meaning a duty of careful attention, in the first language, is rendered by a word that in the second suggests a duty of affection. A court reading the second version would be reading a different law. Literalism, in such a case, yields a faithful text and an unfaithful law, and the translator who claims to have made no choices has merely made them without admitting it.

The opposite counsel, to translate the purpose, fares no better. Purposes are inferred, and the inference belongs to the translator. A translator who decides what a law is for and writes accordingly has become a legislator of a minor and unelected kind. The two doctrines are usually presented as a dilemma, and the dilemma is real, but it is also overstated. In the great majority of provisions, the words and the purpose point the same way, and the translator's duty is plain: render the text accurately and move on. Difficulty is confined to a small number of provisions, such as those in which an institution exists on one side of the border and has no counterpart on the other.

For those provisions the honest course is neither silent literalism nor silent reinterpretation but disclosure. A note, available to the court and to the public, should record where the translator faced a choice and why the choice was made as it was. Officials tend to dislike this proposal, because a text that announces its uncertainties seems to invite litigation, and law is supposed to look settled. But the uncertainty exists whether or not it is announced. Concealment does not prevent disputes; it only ensures that they will be conducted without the information that would resolve them. There is an irony here that the profession may not enjoy. An occupation devoted to precision has arranged for its least precise moments to be kept off the page.

The ethic of the translator of law, then, is not fidelity, which cannot always be had, but candor about the places where fidelity runs out.

Part A ([Chapter 21](chapter:21)) and Part B together make the 53 questions of a full CARS section [VERIFY]. Praxis Tip: In an EXCEPT question, mark each choice with a plus (the passage says this) or a minus (it does not) and pick the odd one out. It is far more reliable than carrying the negative in your head, where it tends to slip out during the third choice.

Praxis Tip: For a strengthen or weaken question, say aloud which claim is under attack and which direction each choice pushes. A choice that is true, interesting and about the right topic can still push in no direction at all.

Praxis Tip: When an answer choice quotes the passage's own language, check what the quoted words are doing. Many wrong choices use the right vocabulary to say the wrong thing; the vocabulary is bait.

Praxis Tip: Treat the analogy as a structure to be matched, not a topic. In Question 11 the chess choice shares no subject with the passage, and it wins for that reason.

**Closing protocol: score, review, plan**

1. Score the exam in one pass and write the raw score on its own line. Do not interpret it yet.
2. For every miss and every correct answer marked low-confidence, write the passage number, the question type and the cause of the error.
3. Before reading any explanation twice, restate in a sentence why the right choice is right. Then read the explanation and compare.
4. Find the most frequent cause and the most frequent question type among your misses. Write one sentence naming each.
5. Add your Part A score, compare the total with your first exam, and write one sentence on what changed.
6. Choose one lesson from the map above and spend your next study session on it. Do not choose three.
7. Redo the passage you found hardest, untimed, with a full map, in a week.
