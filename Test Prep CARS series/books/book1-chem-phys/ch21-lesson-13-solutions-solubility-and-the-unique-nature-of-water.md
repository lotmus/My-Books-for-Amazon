# Lesson 13: Solutions, Solubility and the Unique Nature of Water

**What you will be able to do**

- Explain why water is a remarkable solvent and which of its properties matter for living systems.
- Calculate concentrations in several units and carry out dilutions.
- Use the solubility product (Ksp), the common-ion effect and complex-ion formation to predict solubility and precipitation.
- Predict colligative effects (freezing-point depression, boiling-point elevation, vapor-pressure lowering) and osmotic pressure.
- Apply osmosis and tonicity to cells.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Category 5A is about the unique nature of water and its solutions. Living systems are aqueous: every ion in a nerve, every metabolite in a cell and every drug in a bloodstream is a solute in water. The questions combine a little quantitative bookkeeping (molarity, Ksp) with a lot of qualitative reasoning (what happens to the solubility if I add this ion? which way does water move?). This lesson covers the solutions half of 5A; [Lesson 16](chapter:25) covers acids, bases, buffers and titration, the other half.

Praxis Tip: For any solution question, decide first what is the solute, what is the solvent, and which amounts are given as moles, as mass or as concentration. Convert everything to moles and liters before anything else.

**Concept Review**

**Water.** Water's properties follow from its bent shape, its polar O–H bonds, and its ability to form hydrogen bonds ([Lesson 11](chapter:19)).

- **High boiling point and heat of vaporization** compared with molecules of similar size, because of hydrogen bonding. Evaporating sweat cools the body for this reason.
- **High specific heat capacity**: water absorbs a lot of heat for a small temperature change, which stabilizes body and ocean temperatures.
- **Cohesion and adhesion**: molecules stick to each other and to other surfaces, giving surface tension and capillary action.
- **Density anomaly**: water is densest at about 4 °C, and ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats.
- **Excellent solvent for ionic and polar compounds**: the polar water molecules surround ions (**hydration**), with ion-dipole attractions. A hydrated proton is written H₃O⁺ (the **hydronium ion**).

**Common ions.** Learn the names, formulas and charges of the familiar ones: ammonium NH₄⁺, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, bicarbonate (hydrogen carbonate) HCO₃⁻, acetate CH₃COO⁻, and the monatomic ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻).

**Electrolytes.** A substance that produces ions in solution is an **electrolyte**. Strong electrolytes (soluble salts, strong acids and bases) dissociate completely; weak electrolytes (weak acids and bases) only partially.

**Concentration units.**

| Unit | Definition |
|---|---|
| Molarity (M) | moles of solute per liter of solution |
| Molality (m) | moles of solute per kilogram of solvent |
| Mass percent | (mass of solute / mass of solution) × 100 |
| Mole fraction (X) | moles of a component / total moles |
| Parts per million (ppm) | mg of solute per kg (about per liter, for dilute aqueous solutions) |

**Dilution.** Adding solvent changes the concentration but not the moles of solute: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂.

Praxis Tip: In dilutions the volumes can stay in milliliters as long as both are in the same unit, since the ratio is all that matters. Do not convert to liters unnecessarily.

**Solubility.** Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature. Rules of thumb: the solubility of most solid solutes increases with temperature, and the solubility of gases in liquids *decreases* with temperature (which is why warm soda goes flat). Henry's law says the solubility of a gas is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid.

**The solubility product, Ksp.** For a sparingly soluble ionic solid, the equilibrium between the solid and its dissolved ions has an equilibrium constant, the **solubility product**:

    AₓBᵧ(s) ⇌ x Aʸ⁺ + y Bˣ⁻,    Ksp = [Aʸ⁺]ˣ [Bˣ⁻]ʸ

The solid does not appear in the expression. The **molar solubility** s is the moles that dissolve per liter. For a 1:1 salt (such as AgCl), Ksp = s², so s = √Ksp. For a 1:2 salt (MX₂), Ksp = (s)(2s)² = 4s³.

**The reaction quotient and precipitation.** Compare the ion product Q (computed with the actual concentrations) with Ksp:

- Q < Ksp: unsaturated, more solid can dissolve; no precipitate forms.
- Q = Ksp: saturated, at equilibrium.
- Q > Ksp: supersaturated; a precipitate forms until Q drops to Ksp.

**The common-ion effect.** Adding an ion that is already part of the salt lowers its solubility, because the equilibrium shifts toward the solid (Le Chatelier's principle, covered in [Lesson 15](chapter:24)). It is used in laboratory separations.

**Complex ions and pH.** A metal ion can bind several ligands (molecules or ions with lone pairs) to form a **complex ion**, with a formation constant K_f. Complex formation removes the free metal ion from solution, so it *increases* the solubility of a salt (for example, AgCl dissolves in ammonia). The solubility of a salt whose anion is basic (such as a hydroxide, carbonate or phosphate) increases as the pH is lowered, because the added H⁺ removes the anion.

**Colligative properties.** Some properties depend on the *number* of dissolved particles, not on their identity:

- **Vapor-pressure lowering (Raoult's law):** P_solution = X_solvent × P°_solvent.
- **Boiling-point elevation:** ΔT_b = i K_b m.
- **Freezing-point depression:** ΔT_f = i K_f m.
- **Osmotic pressure:** Π = i M R T.

Here m is molality, M molarity, and **i** is the **van't Hoff factor**, the number of particles each formula unit produces in solution (ideally 1 for glucose, 2 for NaCl, 3 for CaCl₂). For water, K_f = 1.86 °C/m and K_b = 0.512 °C/m.

Praxis Tip: For colligative questions, count particles, not formula units. A 0.1 m CaCl₂ solution behaves like about 0.3 m of particles, so it freezes at the lowest temperature of the common choices.

**Osmosis and tonicity.** **Osmosis** is the net movement of solvent (water) across a semipermeable membrane toward the side with the higher solute concentration. The **osmotic pressure** is the pressure needed to stop it. For cells:

- A **hypotonic** solution has a lower solute concentration than the cell: water enters, and an animal cell swells and may burst.
- An **isotonic** solution has the same concentration: no net movement.
- A **hypertonic** solution has a higher solute concentration than the cell: water leaves, and the cell shrinks.

Physiological saline is isotonic with blood, roughly 0.9 percent NaCl, about 0.15 M (9 g/L ÷ 58.5 g/mol ≈ 0.154 M).

**Worked Example 1: Molarity and dilution (skill: moles first)**

(a) What is the molarity of a solution made by dissolving 5.85 g of NaCl (58.5 g/mol) in enough water to make 500 mL? (b) If 50 mL of a 2.0 M stock solution is diluted to 200 mL, what is the new concentration?

(a) Moles = 5.85 / 58.5 = 0.100 mol. Volume = 0.500 L. M = 0.100 / 0.500 = 0.200 M.

(b) M₂ = M₁V₁ / V₂ = (2.0)(50) / 200 = 0.50 M. The volume increased fourfold, so the concentration dropped fourfold ✓.

**Worked Example 2: Ksp and molar solubility (skill: setting up the expression)**

(a) The Ksp of AgCl is 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰. Find its molar solubility in pure water. (b) Find it in 0.10 M NaCl. (c) A salt MX₂ has Ksp = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹. Find its molar solubility.

(a) Ksp = s², so s = √(1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ M.

(b) In 0.10 M NaCl, [Cl⁻] ≈ 0.10 M (the contribution from AgCl is negligible). Ksp = s × 0.10, so s = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 0.10 = 1.8 × 10⁻⁹ M. The solubility fell by a factor of about 7,000: the common-ion effect.

(c) Ksp = (s)(2s)² = 4s³, so s³ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ / 4 = 8.0 × 10⁻¹², and s = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ M. Then [M²⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ M and [X⁻] = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ M.

*Check (c).* (2.0 × 10⁻⁴)(4.0 × 10⁻⁴)² = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 16 × 10⁻⁸ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ ✓.

**Worked Example 3: Will it precipitate? (skill: Q versus Ksp)**

Equal volumes of 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ M AgNO₃ and 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ M NaCl are mixed. Does AgCl precipitate? (Ksp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰.)

Mixing equal volumes halves each concentration: [Ag⁺] = [Cl⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M. Q = (1.0 × 10⁻⁵)(1.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰.

Q = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ < Ksp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰, so the solution is unsaturated and no precipitate forms. (The step that is most often forgotten is the dilution on mixing.)

**Worked Example 4: Freezing point and boiling point (skill: the van't Hoff factor)**

What is the freezing point of a solution of 1.0 mol NaCl in 1.0 kg of water? How does that compare with 1.0 mol of glucose in 1.0 kg of water? (K_f = 1.86 °C/m.)

NaCl: i = 2, m = 1.0, so ΔT_f = 2 × 1.86 × 1.0 = 3.72 °C. Freezing point ≈ −3.7 °C. Glucose: i = 1, ΔT_f = 1.86 °C, freezing point ≈ −1.9 °C.

*Reading it.* The same amount of NaCl lowers the freezing point twice as much because each formula unit supplies two particles. That is why salt is spread on icy roads.

**Worked Example 5: Osmotic pressure and cells (skill: Π = i M R T)**

What is the osmotic pressure of 0.15 M NaCl at body temperature (310 K)? What happens to a red blood cell placed in pure water?

Π = i M R T = 2 × 0.15 × 0.0821 × 310 = 7.6 atm.

A red blood cell in pure water is in a hypotonic environment: water moves into the cell, which swells and can burst. In a solution much more concentrated than the cell contents (hypertonic), water leaves and the cell shrinks.

**Worked Example 6: Vapor-pressure lowering (skill: Raoult's law)**

At 25 °C pure water has a vapor pressure of about 24 torr. What is the vapor pressure over a solution in which the mole fraction of water is 0.90?

P = X_water × P° = 0.90 × 24 = 21.6 torr, about 22 torr. The nonvolatile solute lowers the vapor pressure of the solvent, which is the basis of the boiling-point elevation.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: In Ksp problems, write the dissolution equation first, then the expression, then label s on each ion. Most mistakes come from skipping the first step.

Praxis Tip: To judge whether something will precipitate, make a one-line table: ion concentrations after mixing, Q, Ksp, comparison.

Praxis Tip: For osmosis, water moves toward the side with the higher solute concentration. Say "water follows the solute" and ignore the arrow-drawing for a moment.

Praxis Tip: When a question compares several solutions at the same molality, count the particles per formula unit. The solution with the most particles has the lowest freezing point, the highest boiling point and the largest osmotic pressure.

Praxis Tip: Two rules explain a lot of everyday gas facts. Gas solubility falls as temperature rises (a warm soda goes flat faster), and by Henry's law it rises with the gas's partial pressure (a sealed, pressurized soda stays fizzy).

**Common Mistakes**

- Forgetting the dilution caused by mixing two solutions.
- Using the Ksp expression for a 1:2 salt as if it were 1:1 (or forgetting to cube).
- Putting the solid in the Ksp expression.
- Ignoring the van't Hoff factor in colligative problems.
- Confusing molarity and molality.
- Reversing the direction of water movement in osmosis.

   A. the larger molar mass of water

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** M₁V₁ = M₂V₂, so M₂ = (4.0)(25.0) / 100 = 1.0 M. (B inverts the dilution factor; C multiplies by the dilution factor instead of dividing; D assumes dilution does not change the concentration.)
2. **B.** Ca₃(PO₄)₂(s) ⇌ 3 Ca²⁺ + 2 PO₄³⁻, so Ksp = [Ca²⁺]³ [PO₄³⁻]². The solid is omitted. (A swaps the exponents; C ignores the stoichiometric coefficients; D places the solid in the expression.)
3. **D.** The added Cl⁻ is a common ion. By Le Chatelier's principle, the equilibrium shifts toward solid AgCl, lowering [Ag⁺]. (A reverses the common-ion effect; B ignores the shift in the equilibrium; C overstates the effect: the solubility decreases but does not vanish.)
4. **C.** Freezing-point depression depends on the number of dissolved particles. CaCl₂ gives 3 per formula unit (i ≈ 3), the most of the choices. (A produces only 2 particles per formula unit; B does not dissociate, so it produces 1 particle; D is a weak acid and dissociates only slightly.)
5. **B.** A hypertonic solution has a higher solute concentration than the cell, so water moves out of the cell by osmosis. (A describes a hypotonic solution; C describes an isotonic solution; D misidentifies what crosses the membrane.)
6. **C.** Water's O–H groups form hydrogen bonds, which are far stronger than the intermolecular forces in H₂S. (A water is lighter than H₂S, so this reverses the facts; B water is polar; D does not explain the higher boiling point.)
7. **D.** Moles = 11.7 / 58.5 = 0.200 mol. Molarity = 0.200 / 2.0 = 0.10 M. (A forgets to divide by the volume of 2.0 L; B multiplies by the volume instead of dividing; C divides the mass by the volume without converting to moles.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, look at each wrong choice and name the slip (dilution on mixing, particles not counted, wrong exponent in Ksp). Log it in your error log ([Strategy: The Question Method](chapter:4)).
