# Strategy: The Question Method

**What you will be able to do**

- Apply the read, predict, eliminate, verify routine from the companion book to this section's passage sets and independent questions.
- Triage a section of 10 passage sets and 15 independent questions in three passes.
- Keep an error log with causes that fit chemistry and physics.

**Why it matters**

The general method is in [Core Test Skills, Chapters 9 to 12]: the error log, the question method, pacing with flag-and-move, and guessing and calibration. This chapter assumes you have read them (they are short) and adds what is specific here: how to read a science passage fast, how to decide which sets to do first, and which mistakes you are likely to log.

Praxis Tip: Do not reread the Core method in full each time. Keep four words on your scratch paper: read, predict, eliminate, verify. The details are in your hands by then.

**The two kinds of question**

Of the 59 questions, 15 are independent: a short stem with no passage. The other 44 belong to ten passage sets of 4 to 6 questions each. Independent questions are usually the cheapest points per minute; a passage set has a reading cost to pay before the first answer, which then spreads across its questions.

Even in a passage set, some questions need only a sentence of the passage (or none), and others need the passage's data or model. Learn to tell them apart quickly. If the question can be answered from your own knowledge, answer it before the passage has finished costing you time.

Praxis Tip: Read the question stems of a passage set *before* you read the passage when the passage is long or you are short on time. Knowing what is asked tells you what to look for. If the stems are mostly about the figure, start at the figure.

**Reading a science passage fast**

Spend one to two minutes on a typical passage. You are building a map, not memorizing.

1. **Title and first sentence:** what system or experiment is this?
2. **Variables and constants:** what is measured, what changed, what stayed the same?
3. **Models and equations:** note any formula the passage gives, along with what each symbol means. If the passage provides an equation, a question will often ask you to use it.
4. **Data:** read the table or graph labels: axes, units, scales (linear or log).
5. **Surprises:** a sentence beginning "however," a footnote about conditions, an odd result. These are where the questions hide.

Highlight sparingly. A passage that is half highlighted has told you nothing. Use highlighting for numbers, units and defined terms, if the interface offers it.

Praxis Tip: Write the passage's equation on your scratch page in your own symbols before you start the questions. Hunting back for it five times costs more than writing it once.

**Worked Example: A short passage, the four steps**

*Passage (original; the data are invented).* A student studies how quickly small steel spheres fall through a tall, narrow column of a viscous liquid, Fluid X. Each sphere is released at the surface and timed over a middle section of the column in which it falls at constant speed (its terminal speed, v_t). For small spheres at low speed, a standard model predicts

v_t = (2/9)(ρ_s − ρ_f) g r² / η

where r is the sphere's radius, ρ_s and ρ_f are the densities of the sphere and the fluid, g is the gravitational field strength, and η is the fluid's viscosity. The student repeats the experiment with three sphere sizes at the same temperature.

| Radius r (mm) | Terminal speed v_t (m/s) |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.020 |
| 2.0 | 0.080 |
| 3.0 | 0.180 |

*Map (ten seconds).* Spheres in a viscous fluid; terminal speed; equation has r² on top and η in the denominator; table shows r and v_t; same temperature.

**Question 1.** The data are most consistent with v_t being proportional to
A. r  B. r²  C. r³  D. 1/r

*Read.* Which power of r fits the table? *Predict.* Doubling r from 1 to 2 takes v_t from 0.020 to 0.080: a factor of 4. Tripling r gives 0.180 / 0.020 = 9. Both match r². *Eliminate.* A would give ×2 and ×3. C would give ×8 and ×27. D decreases. *Verify.* The equation in the passage has r². **B.**

**Question 2.** Fluid Y has a lower viscosity than Fluid X but the same density. A 2.0 mm steel sphere would fall in Fluid Y with a terminal speed that is
A. unchanged  B. lower  C. zero  D. higher

*Read.* Effect of viscosity on v_t. *Predict.* η is in the denominator of the equation, so lower η gives higher v_t. *Eliminate.* A ignores η. B has the direction reversed. C is silly. *Verify.* Think of honey versus water: the sphere falls faster in the runnier liquid. **D.**

**Question 3.** Which condition was most important to hold constant when comparing the three sphere sizes?
A. The temperature of the fluid
B. The color of the spheres
C. The order in which the spheres were released
D. The time of day

*Read.* A controlled variable. *Predict.* Whatever affects v_t other than r. The equation shows η, ρ_f and g; viscosity depends strongly on temperature. *Eliminate.* B, C and D do not enter the equation. *Verify.* The passage states the same temperature was used. **A.**

**Question 4.** At terminal speed, the net force on a sphere is
A. equal to its weight, directed downward
B. equal to the drag force, directed upward
C. zero
D. equal to the buoyant force, directed upward

*Read.* Net force at constant velocity. *Predict.* Constant velocity means zero net force ([Lesson 2](chapter:8)). *Eliminate.* A, B and D each name one force, not the net. *Verify.* The weight is balanced by drag plus buoyancy. **C.**

*Skill used:* applying the four steps to a data table and a model equation, and spending minutes on thinking rather than on rereading.

Praxis Tip: Question 4 needed no passage at all. If a question in a passage set can be answered from knowledge, do it first, then spend your time on the others.

**Triage: three passes**

With 59 questions in 95 minutes, you cannot give every set equal love. Use three passes.

*Pass 1 (about 60 to 65 minutes).* Go through the section in order. Answer every question you can answer in about a minute and a half. When a question will take longer, make your best guess, flag it, and move on. If a whole passage set looks like a time sink (a long mechanism, a calculation-heavy circuit, a topic you dread), skim the first two questions, answer what you can, flag the rest, and move on.

The Chapter 1 checkpoints (23, 46, 69, 90) describe one steady pass at about 1.5 minutes per question. Pass 1 here runs faster because you flag and skip; use the three-pass plan or the steady-pass checkpoints, not both at once.

*Pass 2 (about 20 to 25 minutes).* Return to flagged questions in order of how close they were. Questions where you narrowed to two choices come first.

*Pass 3 (the 5-minute buffer).* Check any answer where you had a specific reason for doubt, such as a misread qualifier. Resist rewriting answers on a feeling.

Praxis Tip: Guess before you flag ([Core Test Skills, Chapter 12]). There is no extra penalty for wrong answers, and a question you never return to still has a chance of being right.

**An error log with chemistry and physics causes**

The five general cause codes (content gap, misread, rushed, trap choice, calculation slip) still apply. Add a short note naming the sub-cause, which will help you pick fixes:

| General cause | C/P sub-causes to note |
|---|---|
| Content gap | Formula not recalled; trend not recalled; mechanism not recalled |
| Misread | Unit missed; sign or direction missed; graph axis missed |
| Calculation slip | Power of ten; unit conversion; square root; log |
| Trap choice | Reversed relationship; confused concentration with amount; confused rate with extent |
| Rushed | Skipped the diagram; skipped the estimate |

Praxis Tip: Add the lesson number to each log entry. After two weeks, a count by lesson tells you which chapter to reread.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Draw the diagram for every mechanics and circuit question, even a bad one. A sketch catches reversed directions that thought alone misses.

Praxis Tip: Estimate before calculating. If the answer choices differ by a factor of ten, an estimate settles it ([Strategy: The Quantitative Toolkit](chapter:5)).

Praxis Tip: Keep units in every line of a calculation. The unit that cancels wrongly is your alarm.

Praxis Tip: When a choice contains an absolute word ("always," "only," "never") in a science question, treat it as suspect until you have tested it with a counterexample.

**Common mistakes**

- Reading the whole passage word by word.
- Spending a passage-set's budget on a single stubborn question.
- Using the passage when the question can be answered from knowledge, and vice versa.
- Highlighting everything.
- Switching answers on a feeling.

**Do this today**

1. Copy "read, predict, eliminate, verify" onto your scratch-paper template.
2. Do ten diagnostic or practice questions using the four steps aloud.
3. Add the lesson number and C/P sub-cause to your error-log columns.
4. Plan your three passes in clock times, using the checkpoints from [Chapter 1](chapter:1).

**Recap**

Read, predict, eliminate, verify. Read passages for variables, equations and surprises; answer easy questions first; guess and flag the rest; and log mistakes by lesson and sub-cause.
