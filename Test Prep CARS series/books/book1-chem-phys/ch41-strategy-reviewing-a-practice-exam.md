# Strategy: Reviewing a Practice Exam

**What you will be able to do**

- Turn a full-length score into a diagnosis, not just a number.
- Sort every miss by cause: content gap, misread, rushed, trap choice or calculation slip.
- Spot false confidence and slow spots using your confidence notes and checkpoint times.
- Build a one-week repair plan, and decide whether to take Exam 2 right away or after repairs.

**Why it matters for the exam**

A practice exam is worth about as much as the review you do afterward. Two students can take the same exam and score the same; one learns three things from it and the other learns nothing. This chapter is the review routine, tuned to the Chemical and Physical section. It builds on Core Test Skills, "Review a Practice Test", and it assumes you kept an error log as in [Strategy: The Question Method](chapter:4).

You should have finished **Full-Length Exam 1 (Parts A and B)** before reading this. Do not open the answers for Exam 2 yet.

Praxis Tip: Review within 24 hours of the exam, while your memory of what you were thinking is fresh. After a week you will be reviewing the answer, not your reasoning.

**The review in five passes**

**Pass 1: Score and feelings (10 minutes).** Count correct answers for each part (out of 30) and for the whole (out of 60). Write down how you felt: confident, rushed, bored, shaky. This is data. Do not convert the number into a predicted real score. The practice exams in this book are original and not calibrated to any official scale; treat the number as a measure of your progress against yourself.

**Pass 2: Every wrong or guessed answer.** For each one, write in your notebook or error log:

- the question number and the topic tag (for example, 4C, "acids and bases");
- the lesson number it belongs to;
- the cause, from the list below;
- the fix, in one sentence;
- the date you will re-attempt it.

The causes:

| Cause | Meaning | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Content gap | You did not know or misremembered a fact or law | Re-read the lesson section; make flashcards |
| Misread | You answered a different question than the one asked | Underline the verb and the units in the stem, every time |
| Rushed | You knew it, but skipped a step | Slow the setup, not the arithmetic |
| Trap choice | You were drawn to a plausible distractor | Name the trap, then find the reason it is wrong |
| Calculation slip | Right method, wrong arithmetic or units | Estimate first, write units, check the order of magnitude |

*(The cause table is an image in the ebook, so you cannot edit it. Copy the five causes into your notebook as column headings.)*

**Pass 3: Timing.** Compare your actual checkpoints with the plan from [Strategy: Test-Day Plan, Pacing and Stress](chapter:38). Where did you gain time? Where did you lose it? Identify the single slowest passage and ask why: unfamiliar topic, awkward data, or poor triage.

**Pass 4: Confidence.** Place each question in one of four groups using your confidence ratings:

- *High confidence, correct:* fine.
- *Low confidence, correct:* lucky or fragile. Review these as if they were wrong.
- *Low confidence, wrong:* you knew you were unsure. This is a content gap and the lesson tells you what to do.
- *High confidence, wrong:* the most valuable group. You believed something false. Rewrite the idea correctly, in your own words.

**Pass 5: Patterns and a plan.** Count misses by topic and by cause. Pick the top two issues. Write one concrete action for each: a lesson to re-read, a set of 10 problems to redo, or a habit to practice.

Praxis Tip: Never review by only reading the answer explanation. Cover it, retry the question, and then compare. The effort of retrieving is what makes it stick.

**Worked Example: Reading an error summary**

Suppose after Exam 1 your counts are:

| Topic | Questions | Missed |
|---|---|---|
| Physics (Lessons 1-8) | 20 | 8 |
| General chemistry (9-17) | 24 | 6 |
| Organic chemistry (18-22) | 10 | 3 |
| Biochemistry (23-25) | 6 | 2 |

Misses by topic: physics 8/20 = 40%; general chemistry 6/24 = 25%; organic chemistry 3/10 = 30%; biochemistry 2/6 = 33%. The total is 19 misses out of 60 questions, so 41 correct. Physics is the weakest area both in the count (8 of 19 misses, about 42%) and in the rate. Say the causes for the 8 physics misses were three calculation slips, three content gaps (two on circuits, one on Doppler), and two misreads. Two actions follow: (1) re-read Lesson 5 and Lesson 6, then redo the Practice: Physics Mixed Set cold; (2) before every calculation on the next exam, write the units and a rough estimate, to cut the slips.

Notice what you do *not* conclude. You do not conclude "I am bad at physics." You conclude "two topics and one habit."

*Skill used:* separating count, rate and cause.

Praxis Tip: Rates beat counts. A topic with 20 questions and 8 misses is a bigger problem than one with 6 questions and 3 misses, even though the second is a 50% miss rate. Weight by how often the topic appears.

**Re-attempt rules**

1. Re-attempt each miss after three days, without looking at the explanation.
2. If you get it right, schedule one more attempt a week later.
3. If you get it wrong again, it is no longer a mistake; it is a topic. Reread the lesson section and make three flashcards.
4. After two successful re-attempts, retire the question.

**A one-week repair plan**

- **Day 1:** Passes 1 to 3 (score, misses, timing).
- **Day 2:** Passes 4 and 5. Write the two priorities and the plan.
- **Days 3 and 4:** Re-read the targeted lesson sections, and redo the targeted practice set cold.
- **Day 5:** Re-attempt Day-1 misses.
- **Day 6:** Light review: formula card, error log. Rest.
- **Day 7:** Take Exam 2 (Parts A and B) as a full-length simulation, with the clock.

If you are short on time, compress the plan, but keep the order: diagnose, repair, retest.

Praxis Tip: Do not take Exam 2 as soon as you finish this chapter. The point of two exams is a before-and-after. Repair first; then measure.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Keep a "wrong-answer wall" in your notebook: one line per false belief you caught ("I thought buffers resist all pH changes"), corrected. Reread it before the exam.

Praxis Tip: For every calculation slip, check whether the final answer was even the right size. An estimate would have caught most of them.

Praxis Tip: For every trap choice, write one sentence naming the trap ("this is the value before doubling"). Named traps are easier to avoid.

Praxis Tip: Compare the first half and second half of each part. If the second half is much worse, you have a stamina or pacing issue, not a knowledge issue.

**Common mistakes**

- Reviewing only the wrong answers, not the lucky ones.
- Reading explanations instead of retrying the question.
- Treating the practice score as a prediction.
- Fixing every problem at once instead of the top two.
- Taking the next exam before repairs are made.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. What are the five causes of a miss listed in this chapter?
2. Why is a high-confidence wrong answer more valuable to study than a low-confidence wrong answer?
3. In the worked example, why are physics misses a bigger priority than biochemistry misses even though biochemistry's rate (33%) is close to physics's (40%)?
4. What do you do with a correct answer you marked low confidence?
5. When should you re-attempt a missed question?
6. Why should you avoid predicting your real score from a practice exam in this book?
7. What is the goal of splitting the practice exams in two with a repair week between?

**Stop here.** Answer each question in your notebook before reading the answers.

**Answers and reasons**

1. Content gap, misread, rushed, trap choice, calculation slip.
2. It reveals a false belief held with certainty, which will repeat on test day. A low-confidence miss is a known weakness and is already on your list.
3. Physics has far more questions and misses (8 of 19 misses, from 20 questions), so fixing it gains more points; biochemistry has only 6 questions.
4. Review it as if it were wrong: it was probably luck or a fragile guess.
5. After about three days, without looking at the explanation, then again a week later if correct.
6. The practice exams are original and not calibrated to any official scale, so the number measures your progress against yourself only.
7. To measure the effect of your repairs: Exam 1 is the baseline, Exam 2 is the comparison.

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

Explain the five passes to an imaginary friend in two minutes. After two days, one week and three weeks, write the five causes of a miss from memory.
