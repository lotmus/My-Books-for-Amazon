# Lesson 20: Carboxylic Acids and Their Derivatives

**What you will be able to do**

- Explain the acidity of carboxylic acids and how substituents change it.
- Rank acid derivatives (acid chlorides, anhydrides, esters, amides) by reactivity, and explain the ranking.
- Predict the products of nucleophilic acyl substitution, esterification, hydrolysis, saponification and reduction.
- Describe decarboxylation and the special reactivity of β-lactams.
- Recognize the biological two-electron redox centers based on phenols and quinones.

**Why it matters for the exam**

If the carbonyl group is the star of organic chemistry, the carboxylic acid family is its supporting cast. Fats are esters. Proteins are chains of amides. Metabolic intermediates are carboxylic acids and their activated forms. Penicillin works because of a strained amide. The reactions all follow one pattern: a nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon, a tetrahedral intermediate forms, and a leaving group departs. If you can run that pattern and rank the leaving groups, you can answer most questions on the whole family without memorizing reactions one by one.

Praxis Tip: Learn the family as one mechanism with different leaving groups. The better the leaving group, the more reactive the derivative. That single idea predicts the entire reactivity ladder.

**Concept Review**

**Carboxylic acids.** The carboxyl group is –COOH. Names end in **-oic acid** (acetic acid, benzoic acid). Physical properties:

- Carboxylic acids form strong hydrogen bonds, even pairing into hydrogen-bonded **dimers**, so their boiling points are higher than those of alcohols of similar mass.
- Small acids mix with water; solubility falls as the carbon chain grows.
- They are **weak acids** (pKa about 4 to 5; acetic acid 4.76, benzoic acid 4.2), far more acidic than alcohols (16 to 18), because the conjugate base, the **carboxylate** ion, is stabilized by resonance: the negative charge is shared equally between the two oxygens.
- **Electron-withdrawing substituents** near the carboxyl group stabilize the carboxylate further and increase acidity (the inductive effect): chloroacetic acid (pKa 2.9) is stronger than acetic acid, and trichloroacetic acid (pKa about 0.7) is stronger still.
- Reaction with a base gives a **carboxylate salt**, which is ionic and usually water-soluble. Adding strong acid regenerates the neutral acid. This acid-base behavior is the basis of an extraction ([Lesson 22](chapter:32)).

**Acid derivatives.** Replacing the –OH of a carboxylic acid with another group gives the **acid derivatives**:

| Derivative | General formula | Leaving group |
|---|---|---|
| Acid chloride (acyl halide) | RCOCl | Cl⁻ |
| Anhydride | RCO–O–COR | RCOO⁻ |
| Ester | RCOOR' | R'O⁻ |
| Amide | RCONR'₂ | R'₂N⁻ |

A cyclic ester is a **lactone**; a cyclic amide is a **lactam**.

**Nucleophilic acyl substitution.** The common mechanism: (1) the nucleophile adds to the carbonyl carbon, breaking the C=O π bond and forming a **tetrahedral intermediate**; (2) the C=O reforms, expelling the leaving group. Net result: the leaving group is replaced by the nucleophile (substitution at the acyl carbon). It is an addition-elimination sequence, unlike SN2 at a saturated carbon.

**Relative reactivity.** The more stable (weaker base) the leaving group, and the less the carbonyl is stabilized by donation from the attached atom, the more reactive the derivative:

    acid chloride > anhydride > ester ≈ carboxylic acid > amide

Why the amide is the least reactive: the lone pair on nitrogen is delocalized into the carbonyl (resonance), which stabilizes the molecule and makes the carbonyl carbon less electrophilic, and the amide anion is a poor leaving group. Why the acid chloride is the most reactive: chloride is an excellent leaving group and chlorine is poor at donating electrons to the carbonyl. **Steric hindrance** also slows reaction at crowded carbonyl carbons.

A more reactive derivative can be converted into a less reactive one easily (an acid chloride reacts with an alcohol to give an ester, or with an amine to give an amide), but the reverse needs forcing conditions.

Praxis Tip: The ladder runs downhill easily and uphill with difficulty. If a question asks you to convert an amide into an acid chloride in one step, the answer is probably "not under mild conditions."

**Key reactions.**

- **Fischer esterification:** carboxylic acid + alcohol, with an acid catalyst, gives an ester + water. It is reversible; to increase the yield, use an excess of one reagent or remove water (Le Chatelier).
- **Ester hydrolysis:** in acid, the reverse of Fischer esterification (reversible). In base, **saponification**: the ester reacts with hydroxide to give a **carboxylate salt and an alcohol**. Saponification is effectively **irreversible**, because the carboxylate is a stable, unreactive anion. This is how soap is made from fats.
- **Transesterification:** an ester reacts with a different alcohol to swap the alkoxy group (used in biodiesel production).
- **Amide formation:** best done from an acid chloride or anhydride plus an amine. Mixing a carboxylic acid and an amine directly mostly gives an ammonium carboxylate salt.
- **Amide hydrolysis:** requires strong acid or base and heat; amides (including the peptide bonds of proteins) are quite stable in water, which is why proteins persist in cells and why digestion needs enzymes.
- **Reduction:** LiAlH₄ reduces carboxylic acids and esters to **primary alcohols**, and amides to **amines**. NaBH₄ does not normally reduce esters, acids or amides.
- **Decarboxylation:** loss of CO₂. Carboxylic acids resist it in general, but **β-keto acids** (a ketone two carbons from the acid) decarboxylate readily on gentle heating, because a six-membered cyclic transition state is available. Example: acetoacetic acid (3-oxobutanoic acid) → acetone + CO₂.
- **α-Substitution:** the α-carbon of an acid or its derivative can be functionalized through enol/enolate chemistry ([Lesson 19](chapter:29)).

**Strained rings: β-lactams.** A **β-lactam** is a four-membered cyclic amide. The ring strain prevents the usual resonance stabilization of the amide and makes the carbonyl unusually reactive. Penicillin and related antibiotics are β-lactams: the ring acylates (and thereby disables) an enzyme that bacteria need to build their cell walls. Bacteria can resist these drugs with enzymes (β-lactamases) that hydrolyze the ring.

**Phenols, quinones and biological redox.** **Hydroquinones** (dihydroxybenzenes, a kind of phenol) oxidize to **quinones**, and quinones reduce back to hydroquinones. This reversible two-electron, two-proton interconversion is the basis of **ubiquinone (coenzyme Q)**, a lipid-soluble electron carrier in the mitochondrial electron-transport chain (it can also pass through a one-electron semiquinone intermediate). Many biological redox centers work through such couples.

**Worked Example 1: Acidity ranking (skill: the inductive effect)**

Rank in order of increasing acidity: ethanol, acetic acid, chloroacetic acid, trichloroacetic acid.

Ethanol (pKa about 16) is the weakest acid. Acetic acid (4.76) is stronger because of the resonance-stabilized carboxylate. Chloroacetic acid (2.9) is stronger still: the electronegative chlorine pulls electron density and stabilizes the anion. Trichloroacetic acid (about 0.7) has three chlorines and is strongest.

Order of increasing acidity: ethanol < acetic acid < chloroacetic acid < trichloroacetic acid.

**Worked Example 2: Esterification and hydrolysis (skill: reversible versus irreversible)**

(a) Write the Fischer esterification of acetic acid with ethanol and say how to raise the yield. (b) What happens when ethyl acetate is treated with aqueous NaOH, then acidified?

(a) CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O (acid catalyst). Use a large excess of ethanol, or remove water as it forms.

(b) With NaOH, saponification occurs: CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃COO⁻Na⁺ + CH₃CH₂OH, irreversible. Acidifying with strong acid protonates the carboxylate and gives acetic acid.

**Worked Example 3: Reactivity ladder (skill: which conversions are easy)**

Which transformations are feasible under mild conditions? (a) acid chloride → ester; (b) ester → amide; (c) amide → ester; (d) anhydride → acid.

(a) Yes: a more reactive derivative to a less reactive one. (b) Yes, with an amine, but slowly compared with using an acid chloride (it is a downhill conversion in reactivity). (c) No: it goes uphill and amides are very unreactive; it needs harsh conditions. (d) Yes: anhydrides are hydrolyzed by water to two carboxylic acids.

**Worked Example 4: Reductions (skill: LiAlH₄ versus NaBH₄)**

What forms from (a) ethyl acetate + LiAlH₄ (then work-up), (b) acetamide + LiAlH₄, (c) acetic acid + LiAlH₄, (d) ethyl acetate + NaBH₄?

(a) Two molecules of ethanol (the carbonyl carbon is reduced to CH₂OH, which is ethanol, and the alkoxy part leaves as ethanol). (b) Ethylamine (the C=O becomes CH₂). (c) Ethanol. (d) No reaction under ordinary conditions.

**Worked Example 5: Decarboxylation (skill: β-keto acids)**

Why does acetoacetic acid (CH₃COCH₂COOH) lose CO₂ on warming while acetic acid does not?

The ketone carbonyl is β to the acid. In a six-membered cyclic transition state the carboxyl hydrogen transfers to the ketone oxygen as CO₂ leaves, producing an enol that tautomerizes to acetone. Acetic acid has no β-carbonyl to accept the electrons, so it does not decarboxylate under these conditions. In the body, acetoacetate (one of the ketone bodies) can decarboxylate to acetone (spontaneously, or catalyzed by an enzyme).

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: When you see a tetrahedral intermediate in a mechanism, ask which group leaves. The weakest base leaves best, which is almost always the one that makes the original carbonyl derivative more reactive.

Praxis Tip: For hydrolysis in base, remember the end product is a carboxylate salt, not the acid. You have to add acid to get the neutral acid.

Praxis Tip: For acidity, compare the stability of the conjugate base. Resonance beats induction, and induction beats nothing.

Praxis Tip: To remember the reduction products, count what happens to the carbonyl carbon: acids and esters lose the C=O to become CH₂OH (alcohols); amides become CH₂N (amines).

Praxis Tip: In any question about peptide bonds, remember they are amides: stable, planar and not easily hydrolyzed.

**Common Mistakes**

- Predicting that an amide is as reactive as an ester.
- Forgetting that saponification gives a carboxylate and an alcohol.
- Using NaBH₄ to reduce an ester or amide.
- Reversing the direction of the inductive effect on acidity.
- Writing an SN2-style mechanism for acyl substitution.
- Expecting simple carboxylic acids to decarboxylate readily.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. Which of the following is the most acidic?
   A. Acetic acid (CH₃COOH)
   B. Chloroacetic acid (ClCH₂COOH)
   C. Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH)
   D. Trichloroacetic acid (CCl₃COOH)

2. Which of the following is the most reactive toward nucleophilic acyl substitution?
   A. An amide
   B. An acid chloride
   C. An ester
   D. A carboxylic acid

3. Ethyl acetate is heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide. The organic products are
   A. sodium acetate and ethanol
   B. acetic acid and ethanol
   C. acetaldehyde and ethanol
   D. sodium ethoxide and acetic acid

4. Treatment of an amide with LiAlH₄, followed by an aqueous work-up, gives
   A. a carboxylic acid
   B. an alcohol
   C. an amine
   D. an aldehyde

5. Which change would increase the yield of ester in a Fischer esterification?
   A. Removing water as it forms
   B. Adding water to the mixture
   C. Removing the acid catalyst
   D. Lowering the concentration of the alcohol

6. Penicillin and related antibiotics are especially reactive acylating agents because they contain
   A. an acid chloride group
   B. a strained four-membered cyclic amide (β-lactam)
   C. a free carboxylic acid that is always decarboxylating
   D. a stabilized, unstrained amide

7. Gentle heating of 3-oxobutanoic acid (acetoacetic acid) produces
   A. acetic acid and formaldehyde
   B. butanoic acid and water
   C. acetone and carbon dioxide
   D. no reaction, because carboxylic acids do not decarboxylate

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **D.** Three electron-withdrawing chlorine atoms stabilize the carboxylate anion the most, so trichloroacetic acid has the lowest pKa. (A has no electron-withdrawing substituents; B has one chlorine, so it is less acidic than the trichloro compound; C is an alcohol, far less acidic than a carboxylic acid.)
2. **B.** Chloride is an excellent leaving group and chlorine does not donate electron density to the carbonyl, so an acid chloride is the most reactive derivative. (A has the poorest leaving group and the most resonance stabilization; C is less reactive than an anhydride or acid chloride; D is less reactive than an acid chloride.)
3. **A.** Saponification gives the carboxylate salt of the acid portion (sodium acetate) and the free alcohol (ethanol). (B would form in acid, not in base; C does not follow from ester hydrolysis; D reverses which fragment becomes the carboxylate.)
4. **C.** LiAlH₄ reduces the amide carbonyl to a CH₂ group, converting the amide into an amine. (A would require hydrolysis; B is the product from an ester or acid; D is not formed under these conditions.)
5. **A.** Esterification is an equilibrium; removing a product (water) shifts it toward the ester by Le Chatelier's principle. (B would shift the equilibrium back toward the acid and alcohol; C would slow the reaction, not shift the equilibrium; D would shift the equilibrium toward the reactants.)
6. **B.** The ring strain of the β-lactam prevents normal amide resonance stabilization and makes the carbonyl a reactive electrophile. (A penicillins do not contain acid chlorides; C does not explain acylation; D would be unreactive.)
7. **C.** A β-keto acid loses CO₂ through a cyclic transition state, giving an enol that tautomerizes to the ketone (acetone). (A does not follow from decarboxylation; B would require reduction; D β-keto acids are the exception.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, look at each wrong choice and name the slip (reactivity ladder reversed, reduction reagent, acidity direction). Log it in your error log ([Strategy: The Question Method](chapter:4)).
