# Practice: Full-Length Exam 1, Chemical and Physical Section, Part B

**Timing and conditions**

This is the second half of the first full-length exam for the Chemical and Physical Foundations section: 29 original questions, five passages (Questions 1 to 22) followed by seven independent questions (Questions 23 to 29). Allow 47 minutes, about 1.6 minutes per question, in one sitting. Use scratch paper only: no notes and no calculator. A periodic table may be consulted from the Appendix "Formula, Constant and Fact Sheet", but nothing else on that sheet is allowed until you review (on test day the real exam supplies a periodic table on screen). Use g = 10 m/s² unless a passage says otherwise.

Write your answer letters, with a confidence level (high, medium or low) for each, on scratch paper or in your notebook; an ebook cannot be written in. Check only when you have finished all 29.

Praxis Tip: Start the clock before you read the first passage, and note the time at Question 10 (target: about 16 minutes), at Question 20 (target: about 32 minutes) and at the end. A practice exam without a clock is a rehearsal for a different exam.

Praxis Tip: Skim each passage for its structure (what is measured, what is held constant, which table is which) before you read the first question. Then read each question closely and return to the passage only for what it asks.

Praxis Tip: Round aggressively. If an answer is "about 5", then 4.97 and 5.03 are the same answer for your purposes, and no honest MCAT item will separate them.

**Answers and explanations**

1. **B.** Magnification m = −dᵢ/d₀ = −120/24 = −5. The negative sign marks an inverted real image, which is the only kind that can be caught on a screen. [4A · Skill 4] (A the image on a screen is real, and a real image from a single lens is inverted; C inverts the ratio and uses d₀/dᵢ; D computes (dᵢ − d₀)/d₀ instead of dᵢ/d₀.)
2. **C.** Use 1/f = 1/d₀ + 1/dᵢ. From the first row, 1/60 + 1/30 = 3/60 = 1/20, so f = 20 cm. The third row confirms it: 1/24 + 1/120 = 6/120 = 1/20. [4D · Skill 2] (A takes the smallest recorded image distance to be the focal length; B averages the first row's two distances, 60 and 30; D adds the two distances in the first row.)
3. **D.** The lens must form a virtual image of a distant object (d₀ ≈ ∞) at the far point, 0.50 m in front of the lens, so dᵢ = −0.50 m and 1/f = 1/∞ + 1/(−0.50) = −2.0 m⁻¹. The power is −2.0 D, and a negative focal length is a diverging lens. [4D · Skill 2] (A the eye already converges light too strongly, and adding converging power worsens this; B reports the 0.50 m distance as the power instead of its reciprocal; C combines the wrong lens type with the reciprocal error.)
4. **B.** Light from a distant object arrives nearly parallel, and only a converging lens brings it to a real focus on a screen, at a distance equal to f. A diverging lens never forms a real image of a real object, so it cannot produce a spot on the screen. [4D · Skill 3] (A a small upright image is the signature of a diverging lens, so this reverses the test; C a converging lens is thicker at its center; D spreading light signals a diverging lens.)
5. **C.** Zinc has the more negative reduction potential, so it is oxidized (anode) and sends electrons through the wire to copper (cathode). Zn²⁺ accumulates around the anode, so anions from the bridge migrate toward it to keep that half-cell neutral. [4C · Skill 1] (A reverses the electron flow; zinc is the anode and is oxidized; B anions move toward the anode to balance the Zn²⁺ being produced, not toward the cathode; D electrons travel in the external wire, and ions carry charge in the bridge.)
6. **B.** Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 1/10⁻⁴ = 10⁴, so log Q = 4. E = 1.10 − (0.059/2)(4) = 1.10 − 0.12 = 0.98 V. Lower [Cu²⁺] makes the cell less favorable, so E falls below E°. [4C · Skill 2] (A gives log Q the wrong sign, so the potential rises as [Cu²⁺] falls; C uses n = 1 instead of n = 2; D uses the standard potential and ignores the concentration.)
7. **B.** Charge = 0.50 A × 19,300 s = 9,650 C, which is 9,650/96,500 = 0.10 mol of electrons. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu needs two electrons per atom, giving 0.050 mol Cu × 63.5 g/mol ≈ 3.2 g. [4C · Skill 2] (A assumes one electron per copper ion; C multiplies moles of electrons by 2 instead of dividing by 2; D divides by 2 a second time.)
8. **C.** A tenfold change in Q changes log Q by 1, so E changes by 0.059/n. A slope near 0.03 V means n = 2, matching the two electrons in Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. [4C · Skill 4] (A for n = 1 the slope is 0.059 V per decade, about double the observed value; B 0.059/3 is about 0.02 V; D 0.059/4 is about 0.015 V.)
9. **A.** To isolate the variable you suspect, hold it constant (here the temperature, with a water bath) while changing only [Cu²⁺]. If the unexplained drift disappears, temperature was responsible. The other designs change other variables or add new errors. [4C · Skill 3] (B keeps Q constant but does not control temperature, so it cannot isolate temperature as the cause, and it removes the concentration dependence the experiment is designed to measure; C this changes the chemistry and cannot isolate the effect of temperature; D drawing current lowers the measured potential and adds a new error.)
10. **A.** The IR band at 1715 cm⁻¹ with no O–H band indicates a ketone or aldehyde; there is no aldehyde proton near 9–10 ppm and the Tollens test is negative, so B is a ketone. The 3H triplet and 2H quartet are an ethyl group, and the 3H singlet near 2.14 ppm is a methyl on the carbonyl: CH₃CH₂C(=O)CH₃. [5D · Skill 4] (B an aldehyde would show an NMR signal near 9.7 ppm and a positive Tollens test; C an aldehyde would give a signal near 9.6 ppm, a 6H doublet and a positive Tollens test; D an alcohol would show a broad O–H band in the IR and alkene signals in the NMR.)
11. **C.** PCC oxidizes a secondary alcohol to a ketone, and B is 2-butanone, so A is the secondary alcohol that carries the same carbon skeleton, 2-butanol. Primary alcohols would give aldehydes, and a tertiary alcohol has no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon to lose. [5D · Skill 2] (A oxidation of a primary alcohol with PCC would give an aldehyde, butanal; B a primary alcohol would give an aldehyde; D a tertiary alcohol cannot be oxidized by PCC.)
12. **C.** Aldehydes are oxidized by Ag(NH₃)₂⁺ and give a silver mirror, whereas ketones are not oxidized. The other three measurements give the same result for these isomers. (This is the test the passage already reports: B's negative Tollens result is what excludes butanal.) [5D · Skill 3] (A both aldehydes and ketones give this precipitate; B both absorb near 1715 to 1730 cm⁻¹, too close to distinguish; D both isomers have the same molecular mass, 72.)
13. **B.** The carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar, and an achiral reagent adds hydride to either face with equal probability. The new stereocenter is formed as equal amounts of the two enantiomers, so the product is a racemic mixture with no net optical rotation. [4D · Skill 2] (A the achiral reagent attacks both faces of the planar carbonyl equally; C the achiral reagent attacks both faces of the planar carbonyl equally; D hydride adds to the carbonyl carbon, so the carbon skeleton and position of the OH are unchanged.)
14. **B.** Experiments 1 and 2 double [A] with [B] fixed and the rate doubles, so the reaction is first order in A. Experiments 1 and 3 triple [B] with [A] fixed and the rate rises by a factor of 9 = 3², so it is second order in B. Experiment 4 agrees: 2 × 9 = 18 times the rate of experiment 1. [4B · Skill 4] (A interchanges the orders; doubling [A] only doubles the rate; C assumes first order in both; tripling [B] raises the rate ninefold, not threefold; D doubling [A] only doubles the rate, so the order in A is one.)
15. **A.** From experiment 1: k = rate/([A][B]²) = (2.0 × 10⁻³ M/s)/(0.10 M × 0.010 M²) = 2.0 M⁻² s⁻¹. The reaction is third order overall, so k has units of M⁻² s⁻¹. [5E · Skill 2] (B assumes the rate law is rate = k[A][B]; C has the right number but units for a second-order reaction; D reports the initial rate of experiment 1 itself, which is not k.)
16. **D.** The Arrhenius equation gives ln k = ln A − (Eₐ/R)(1/T), so the slope of ln k against 1/T is −Eₐ/R. The slope is (3.7 − 0.7)/((3.13 − 3.33) × 10⁻³) = −15,000 K. Eₐ = 15,000 × 8.3 ≈ 1.25 × 10⁵ J/mol, or about 1.2 × 10² kJ/mol. [5E · Skill 4] (A reads the slope as an energy without multiplying by R; B divides the slope by R instead of multiplying; C doubles the correct value, as if the 1/T interval were 0.1 × 10⁻³ instead of 0.2 × 10⁻³.)
17. **B.** A catalyst offers an alternative mechanism with a lower-energy transition state. Lower Eₐ means a larger fraction of collisions are energetic enough, so k rises at constant T. Thermodynamic quantities such as ΔG, ΔH and K are unchanged. [5E · Skill 1] (A a catalyst does not change the temperature, which sets the average kinetic energy; C a catalyst does not change ΔG or the equilibrium constant; D a catalyst is regenerated and the order is a property of the mechanism.)
18. **D.** Subtract the 40 counts/s background: the net rates are 800, 400, 200 and 100 counts/s. The rate halves every 8 days, so the half-life is 8 days. [4A · Skill 4] (A takes half of the first interval; B uses the time for the net rate to fall to one quarter; C uses the whole time span of the table.)
19. **A.** A proper control removes only the variable of interest (the radioactive sample) and keeps everything else the same. The count rate with no sample present is the background to subtract from each raw reading. [4E · Skill 3] (B the sample is still radioactive and contributes counts at that time; C this changes the geometry and still includes the sample; D the last reading still includes counts from the remaining sample.)
20. **D.** In β⁻ decay a neutron becomes a proton, so Z rises from 53 to 54 (xenon) while the mass number stays at 131: ¹³¹I → ¹³¹Xe + e⁻ + antineutrino. [4A · Skill 1] (A decreasing Z by one is positron emission or electron capture, not β⁻ decay; B this is the product of α decay, not β⁻ decay; C β⁻ decay does not change the mass number.)
21. **A.** The peak at m/z = 12 + 3 + 127 = 142 is CH₃¹²⁷I and the peak at 146 is CH₃¹³¹I. Peak intensity is proportional to the amount, so the labeled fraction is 10/(90 + 10) = 10%. [4D · Skill 4] (B uses the intensity of the wrong peak; C divides 10 by 90 instead of by the total of 100; D uses the 4-unit mass difference, 4/142.)
22. **C.** After 16 days (two half-lives) one quarter of the ¹³¹I remains, so the 146 peak falls from 10 to 2.5. The 142 peak is unchanged at 90, giving 90 : 2.5 = 36 : 1. Decayed molecules no longer contribute at m/z = 146. [4E · Skill 2] (A uses one half-life (8 days) rather than two; B removes one quarter of the labeled molecules instead of leaving one quarter; D assumes the labeled molecules have not decayed.)
23. **C.** The vertical component is 20 sin 30° = 10 m/s. At the top, v_y = 0, so h = v_y²/(2g) = 100/20 = 5 m. [4A · Skill 2] (A multiplies the vertical speed by the time to the top without subtracting the gravity term; B uses the full speed in v²/2g, ignoring the angle; D uses v²/g instead of v²/2g.)
24. **D.** Between two like charges the fields oppose. Set kq₁/d² = kq₂/(0.30 − d)²: 1/d² = 4/(0.30 − d)², so (0.30 − d) = 2d and d = 0.10 m, closer to the smaller charge. [4C · Skill 2] (A assumes the field vanishes at the midpoint; B swaps the two distances (0.20 m from the weaker charge instead of 0.10 m); C sets the distance ratio equal to the charge ratio of 1 : 4 without the square root.)
25. **A.** A closed pipe has a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end, so only odd harmonics resonate. The fundamental is v/4L = 340/3.4 = 100 Hz, and the next is 3 × 100 = 300 Hz. [4A · Skill 2] (B gives the second harmonic of an open pipe, which a closed pipe lacks; C gives the fundamental, the lowest frequency; D gives the fourth harmonic, which a closed pipe lacks.)
26. **A.** Sulfur in SO₂ has three electron domains (two bonding and one lone pair), which require sp² hybridization and a trigonal planar electron geometry. [4A · Skill 1] (B oxygen has four electron domains and is sp³-hybridized, though it is also bent; C linear with two domains and sp hybridization; D trigonal planar and sp², but has no lone pair on boron.)
27. **B.** If heat is a reactant, raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium toward the products (Le Chatelier), producing more brown NO₂. [4B · Skill 3] (A cooling would favor the exothermic direction, which would make the color lighter if the forward reaction were endothermic; C a catalyst changes the rate but not the equilibrium composition; D argon at constant volume does not change the concentrations or the equilibrium.)
