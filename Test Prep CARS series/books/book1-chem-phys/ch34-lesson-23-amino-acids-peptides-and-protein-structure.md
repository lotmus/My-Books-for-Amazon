# Lesson 23: Amino Acids, Peptides and Protein Structure

**What you will be able to do**

- Draw the general structure of an amino acid and describe its ionization states.
- Classify the 20 amino acids by side-chain properties and recognize their abbreviations and special features.
- Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) and the net charge at a given pH.
- Describe the peptide bond and the four levels of protein structure.
- Explain the forces that stabilize protein folding and what happens when a protein is denatured.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Proteins do most of the work in a cell: they catalyze reactions, carry molecules, build structures, signal and defend. Everything about a protein, from its shape to its function, follows from its amino acid sequence and the chemistry of its side chains. This lesson (category 5D) joins acid-base chemistry ([Lesson 16](chapter:25)), stereochemistry ([Lesson 18](chapter:28)), intermolecular forces ([Lesson 11](chapter:19)) and amides ([Lesson 20](chapter:30)) into a single topic. Questions ask you to predict charges at a given pH, to connect a side chain to a folding force, and to read a purification or denaturation passage. The facts are modest, and the chemistry you already know does the explaining.

Praxis Tip: For each amino acid, learn the *category* of its side chain (nonpolar, polar uncharged, acidic, basic) rather than the exact structure. The category predicts where it sits in a folded protein, and whether it is charged at pH 7.

**Concept Review**

**Amino acid structure.** An α-amino acid has a central **α-carbon** bonded to four groups: an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen, and a variable **side chain (R group)**. Except for glycine (R = H), the α-carbon is a stereocenter. Proteins contain only **L-amino acids** (which are S in the R/S system for all except cysteine, where the sulfur raises the priority of the side chain and makes it R).

**Ionization and zwitterions.** The carboxyl group is acidic (pKa about 2) and the amino group's conjugate acid is weakly acidic (pKa about 9 to 10). At physiological pH (about 7.4), the carboxyl group is deprotonated (–COO⁻) and the amino group is protonated (–NH₃⁺): the amino acid exists as a **zwitterion**, a dipolar ion with no net charge (for an amino acid with a neutral side chain). At low pH, both groups are protonated and the net charge is +1; at high pH, both are deprotonated and the net charge is −1.

**The 20 amino acids by side chain.**

| Class | Amino acids | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nonpolar, aliphatic | Glycine (Gly, G), Alanine (Ala, A), Valine (Val, V), Leucine (Leu, L), Isoleucine (Ile, I), Methionine (Met, M), Proline (Pro, P) | Hydrophobic; Gly is achiral and smallest; Pro's side chain loops back to the amino group, creating a rigid kink |
| Aromatic | Phenylalanine (Phe, F), Tyrosine (Tyr, Y), Tryptophan (Trp, W) | Absorb UV light near 280 nm (Tyr, Trp); mostly hydrophobic |
| Polar, uncharged | Serine (Ser, S), Threonine (Thr, T), Cysteine (Cys, C), Asparagine (Asn, N), Glutamine (Gln, Q) | Hydrophilic; Ser, Thr and Tyr have –OH groups (sites for phosphorylation); Cys has a thiol (–SH) |
| Acidic (negative at pH 7) | Aspartate (Asp, D), Glutamate (Glu, E) | Side-chain carboxylates |
| Basic (positive at pH 7) | Lysine (Lys, K), Arginine (Arg, R), Histidine (His, H) | His has a side-chain pKa near 6, so it can be either charged or neutral near physiological pH |

**Isoelectric point (pI).** The **isoelectric point** is the pH at which the amino acid (or protein) has no net charge. For an amino acid with a nonionizable side chain, it is the average of the two pKa values on either side of the neutral form:

    pI = (pKa₁ + pKa₂) / 2

For an amino acid with an acidic side chain, average the two acidic pKa values; for a basic side chain, average the two basic pKa values. Net charge rules: if pH < pI, the molecule is positively charged; if pH > pI, it is negatively charged. This decides how it behaves in electrophoresis and ion-exchange chromatography ([Lesson 22](chapter:32)).

**Peptides and the peptide bond.** Amino acids join by condensation: the carboxyl group of one reacts with the amino group of the next, releasing water, to form an **amide** called the **peptide bond**. A chain of amino acids is a **peptide** (a **polypeptide** if long); each unit is a **residue**. A peptide has an **N-terminus** (free amino group) and a **C-terminus** (free carboxyl group), and is written from N to C. A tripeptide has three residues and two peptide bonds.

The peptide bond is **planar** and rigid: the nitrogen lone pair delocalizes into the carbonyl (resonance, [Lesson 20](chapter:30)), giving the C–N bond partial double-bond character, so rotation around it is restricted and the bond is usually *trans*. The peptide bond is stable and is hydrolyzed only slowly in water, but readily by **proteases** or by strong acid and heat.

**Disulfide bonds.** Two cysteine side chains can be oxidized to form a covalent **disulfide bond** (–S–S–), linking two parts of a protein. The oxidized dimer is **cystine**. Disulfide bonds stabilize proteins that are secreted from cells or live in oxidizing environments. Reducing agents (such as β-mercaptoethanol) break them.

**Levels of protein structure.**

1. **Primary structure:** the amino acid sequence, held by peptide bonds.
2. **Secondary structure:** regular local folding stabilized by **hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms** (the C=O of one residue and the N–H of another). The two common forms are the **α-helix** (a right-handed coil, 3.6 residues per turn, with each C=O hydrogen-bonded to the N–H four residues later) and the **β-pleated sheet** (strands side by side, hydrogen-bonded between strands; parallel or antiparallel). Proline breaks helices.
3. **Tertiary structure:** the overall three-dimensional shape of one polypeptide, stabilized by interactions between **side chains**: hydrophobic interactions (nonpolar side chains clustered in the core), hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions (salt bridges between acidic and basic side chains), and disulfide bonds.
4. **Quaternary structure:** the arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits (hemoglobin has four).

**Folding and stability.** The major driving force for folding is the **hydrophobic effect**: nonpolar side chains cluster inside the protein, away from water, which increases the entropy of the surrounding water molecules (they no longer need to form an ordered cage around exposed nonpolar groups). Polar and charged side chains tend to be on the surface where they interact with water. The folded state is only marginally more stable than the unfolded one, so modest changes can unfold it.

**Denaturation.** **Denaturation** is the loss of the folded structure (secondary, tertiary and quaternary) without breaking the peptide bonds, so the primary structure is unchanged. Denaturing agents: heat, extreme pH, high concentrations of urea or guanidinium, detergents (SDS), organic solvents, and reducing agents (which break disulfides). Some denatured proteins can **refold** when the agent is removed, showing that the sequence contains the information needed to fold. Others aggregate irreversibly (as when an egg is cooked).

**Nonenzymatic protein functions.** Not all proteins are enzymes. Others **bind** and transport molecules (hemoglobin carries oxygen), provide **structure** (collagen, keratin), act in the **immune system** (antibodies recognize antigens), or act as **motors** (myosin, kinesin, dynein) that convert chemical energy into movement. Cooperative binding, as in hemoglobin, means that binding at one site changes the affinity of the others.

**Synthesis of amino acids.** In the laboratory, the **Strecker synthesis** (an aldehyde, ammonia and cyanide give an α-aminonitrile, then acid hydrolysis gives the amino acid) and the **Gabriel synthesis** (a phthalimide-based route) make α-amino acids. Both generally give **racemic** products unless a chiral reagent is used, because the key intermediate is planar.

**Worked Example 1: Isoelectric points (skill: which pKa values)**

Find the pI of (a) glycine (pKa₁ = 2.34, pKa₂ = 9.60), (b) aspartic acid (pKa values 1.99, 3.90 and 9.90), (c) lysine (2.18, 8.95 and 10.53).

(a) Glycine: pI = (2.34 + 9.60)/2 = 5.97.

(b) Aspartate has an acidic side chain; the neutral species lies between the first and second ionizations (after the α-carboxyl proton is lost, before the side-chain carboxyl proton is lost). The pI uses the two acidic values: (1.99 + 3.90)/2 = 2.95.

(c) Lysine has a basic side chain; the neutral species lies between the second and third ionization steps. The pI uses the two basic values: (8.95 + 10.53)/2 = 9.74.

*Reading it.* At pH 7, aspartate (pI 2.95) is negatively charged and lysine (pI 9.74) is positively charged, so in an electric field they move to opposite electrodes. Glycine (pI 5.97) carries a slight negative charge at pH 7.4.

**Worked Example 2: Peptide bookkeeping (skill: counting bonds and water)**

(a) How many peptide bonds are in the tripeptide Gly-Ala-Ser, and which end is the N-terminus? (b) How many water molecules are released in forming it? (c) How many different tripeptides can be made from the 20 amino acids if each can be used any number of times?

(a) Two peptide bonds. The N-terminus is the free amino end of Gly. (b) Two waters (one for each peptide bond). (c) 20 × 20 × 20 = 8,000.

**Worked Example 3: Folding and a side-chain change (skill: predicting the effect)**

In a folded enzyme, a leucine in the hydrophobic core is mutated to aspartate. A separate mutation changes a surface lysine to arginine. Predict the effects.

Leucine → aspartate puts a charged, hydrophilic side chain into the nonpolar core, which is energetically unfavorable. It would likely destabilize the fold, or the protein may misfold. Lysine → arginine replaces one positively charged surface residue with another; the surface charge is preserved, so the effect is likely small.

*Skill used:* classifying the substitution by side-chain type and location.

**Worked Example 4: Denaturation (skill: what is lost and what is kept)**

A protein is treated with urea and β-mercaptoethanol, then analyzed. Which levels of structure are lost, and does the molecular mass change?

Urea disrupts hydrogen bonds and the hydrophobic interactions, and β-mercaptoethanol reduces disulfide bonds. Secondary, tertiary and quaternary structures are lost. The peptide bonds are unchanged, so the primary structure and the mass of each polypeptide chain stay the same. (If the protein had multiple subunits linked by disulfides, the subunits separate.)

**Worked Example 5: The α-helix (skill: hydrogen-bond pattern)**

In an α-helix, which atoms form the hydrogen bonds, and which residues are paired?

The hydrogen bonds are between backbone atoms: the carbonyl oxygen of residue *i* and the amide hydrogen of residue *i* + 4. The side chains point outward from the helix and take no part in these hydrogen bonds. Proline, which has no amide hydrogen and a rigid ring, cannot fit into the interior of a helix.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Remember that secondary structure uses the backbone and tertiary structure uses the side chains. If a question mentions hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H groups, it is secondary structure.

Praxis Tip: For net charge at a given pH, compare the pH with the relevant pKa values. If pH < pKa, the group is mostly protonated; if pH > pKa, mostly deprotonated.

Praxis Tip: To find a pI quickly, sketch the ionization sequence from the most positive form to the most negative, locate the neutral form, and average the two pKa values on either side of it.

Praxis Tip: A mutation that replaces a residue with one of the same class (nonpolar for nonpolar) is generally mild; one that changes class, especially inside the core or at a binding site, is more likely to disrupt structure or function.

Praxis Tip: The sequence determines the structure. The statement "the information for the folded shape is in the primary structure" is a classic.

**Common Mistakes**

- Using the wrong pair of pKa values in a pI calculation for an acidic or basic amino acid.
- Placing hydrogen bonds between side chains in the α-helix.
- Treating denaturation as breaking peptide bonds.
- Calling the zwitterion "uncharged" instead of "no net charge, but with separated charges".
- Saying all amino acids in proteins are D.
- Forgetting that histidine's side chain pKa is near physiological pH.

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** At pH 7.4 the carboxyl group (pKa about 2) is deprotonated and the amino group (pKa about 9–10) is protonated, giving a dipolar ion with no net charge. (B ignores that both groups are ionized at this pH; C describes the form at low pH; D describes the form at high pH.)
2. **B.** pI = (2.34 + 9.60) / 2 = 5.97, about 6.0. (A is the pKa of the carboxyl group; C is the pKa of the amino group; D assumes the isoelectric point is neutral pH.)
3. **C.** Resonance delocalizes the nitrogen lone pair into the carbonyl, giving the C–N bond partial double-bond character and holding the six atoms around it in a plane. (A ignores the resonance delocalization; B peptide bonds are amides, not esters; D peptide bonds are stable in water.)
4. **D.** In an α-helix, each backbone carbonyl hydrogen-bonds to the amide N–H four residues further along the chain. (A side chains are not the main participants in helix hydrogen bonds; B describes a feature of β-sheets; C describes disulfide bonds, which are covalent.)
5. **C.** Lysine's side-chain amino group is protonated (positive) at pH 7. (A has an acidic side chain; B has a nonpolar methyl side chain; D has a polar, uncharged side chain.)
6. **D.** Two cysteine thiol (–SH) groups are oxidized to form a covalent S–S bond. (A contain –OH groups, not thiols; B contain thioethers, which cannot form disulfides; C contain carboxylates.)
7. **A.** The imine intermediate is planar, so cyanide adds to either face with equal probability, giving equal amounts of both enantiomers. (B needs a chiral influence; C needs a chiral influence; D the product has a stereocenter.)
