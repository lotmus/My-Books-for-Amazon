**Passage 1 (questions 1-4)**

A clinic uses a pulsed ultrasound probe to examine soft tissue. The probe emits a pulse, which travels into the tissue, reflects from a boundary, and returns to the same probe. The speed of sound in the tissue is 1500 m/s. As a pulse travels, its intensity falls with distance. The loss is expressed in decibels (dB), where a loss of 10 dB means the intensity falls to one tenth of its starting value. A technician measures the attenuation coefficient of the tissue at three probe frequencies.

| Frequency (MHz) | Attenuation (dB/cm) |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.5 |
| 2.0 | 1.0 |
| 4.0 | 2.0 |

In a second mode, the probe uses the Doppler shift of the echo from moving red blood cells to estimate blood speed. For blood moving directly toward or away from the probe at speed v, the echo frequency differs from the emitted frequency f by Δf = 2 f v / c, where c is the speed of sound in tissue.

1. What is the wavelength of the ultrasound in the tissue at a frequency of 2.0 MHz?
   A. 0.38 mm
   B. 1.3 mm
   C. 3.0 mm
   D. 0.75 mm

2. At 2.0 MHz, a pulse reflects from a boundary 5.0 cm deep and returns to the probe. Ignoring losses at the reflection itself, the returning echo intensity is what fraction of the emitted intensity?
   A. 1/3
   B. 1/100
   C. 1/10
   D. 9/10

3. A 3.0 MHz probe records an echo frequency shift of 2000 Hz from blood moving directly toward it. The blood speed is closest to
   A. 0.50 m/s
   B. 0.25 m/s
   C. 1.0 m/s
   D. 5.0 m/s

4. A technician wants to test whether tissue attenuation depends on the probe frequency. Which design is best?
   A. Measure the echo loss through one fixed thickness of a tissue phantom at each of several frequencies
   B. Measure the echo loss at one frequency through several different thicknesses of the same phantom
   C. Measure the echo loss at each frequency using a different tissue sample for each frequency
   D. Measure the Doppler shift from flowing blood at each of several frequencies

**Answers and explanations**

1. **D.** For any wave, λ = c / f = 1500 m/s ÷ 2.0 × 10⁶ Hz = 7.5 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.75 mm. [4D · Skill 1] (A divides the correct wavelength by two; B is the reciprocal of the correct value, 1/0.75; C is four times too large, as if the frequency were 0.5 MHz.)
2. **C.** The table gives 1.0 dB/cm at 2.0 MHz. The pulse travels 5.0 cm in and 5.0 cm out, so the loss is 10 cm × 1.0 dB/cm = 10 dB, which is a factor of 10 in intensity. [4D · Skill 4] (A counts only the one-way path of 5 dB; B counts the round trip twice, a loss of 20 dB; D reads a loss of 10 dB as a loss of 10 percent.)
3. **A.** Solve for v: v = Δf c / (2 f) = 2000 × 1500 / (2 × 3.0 × 10⁶) = 3.0 × 10⁶ / 6.0 × 10⁶ = 0.50 m/s. [4D · Skill 2] (B divides by 2 twice when solving for v; C omits the factor of 2 in the Doppler formula; D slips a power of ten when converting MHz to Hz.)
4. **A.** Changing only the frequency, with tissue type, path length and emitted intensity held fixed, isolates the effect of frequency on the loss. [4D · Skill 3] (B varies thickness, not frequency; C changes the tissue along with the frequency; D tests the shift, not attenuation.)
5. **B.** With s mol/L dissolved, [M²⁺] = s and [OH⁻] = 2s, so Ksp = s (2s)² = 4s³. Then s³ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹², and s = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ M. [5A · Skill 2] (A treats Ksp as s², the form for a 1:1 salt; C takes the cube root of Ksp without dividing by 4; D reports Ksp itself as the solubility.)
6. **C.** The solution already supplies [M²⁺] = 0.010 M (the extra from dissolving is negligible). Then [OH⁻]² = Ksp / 0.010 = 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁰, so [OH⁻] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ M. Each M(OH)₂ that dissolves releases two OH⁻, so s = [OH⁻] / 2 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M. [5A · Skill 2] (A sets s equal to [OH⁻] rather than half of it; B ignores the common ion; D reports [OH⁻]² instead of the solubility.)
7. **C.** H⁺ reacts with OH⁻ to form water, lowering [OH⁻]. The ion product falls below Ksp, so the equilibrium shifts toward products until it is restored. Ksp itself does not change, but the solubility does. [5A · Skill 1] (A H⁺ does not react with M²⁺; B the acid removes OH⁻, which drives dissolution, and any ionic-strength effect is small and would raise solubility, not lower it; D Ksp is constant, but solubility is not.)
8. **A.** Ksp = 4 s³ = 4 × (5.0 × 10⁻⁵)³ = 4 × 1.25 × 10⁻¹³ = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³. Ksp falls as the temperature rises, so dissolution is exothermic. [5A · Skill 4] (B cubes s but forgets the factor of 4; C uses 4s² in place of 4s³; D reports s itself as Ksp.)
9. **D.** A solution with the same nitrate but no M²⁺ isolates the variable. If the solubility there stays near 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ M, nitrate is not responsible and M²⁺ is. [5A · Skill 3] (A does not separate the two ions; B repeats the baseline only; C says nothing about the common ion.)
10. **C.** Doubling either concentration doubles the rate, so rate = k [RBr][CN⁻]. From Experiment 1, k = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ (0.10 × 0.10) = 4.0 × 10⁻³ M⁻¹ s⁻¹. [5D · Skill 4] (A divides the rate by only one concentration; B reports the rate itself; D multiplies the rate by both concentrations.)
11. **B.** A backside attack inverts the spatial arrangement. Because the new group has the same priority rank as the old, the label changes from R to S. [5D · Skill 2] (A a one-step backside attack inverts; C no planar intermediate forms in a one-step process; D the product has one stereocenter and no mirror plane.)
12. **D.** A tertiary substrate ionizes slowly to a planar carbocation, which solvent attacks from either face, giving nearly equal amounts of R and S products. The rate depends only on the substrate, consistent with the observation. [5D · Skill 1] (A this describes inversion, which would give an active product; B retention does not give a mirror image; C low concentration does not remove stereochemistry.)
13. **A.** If the rate is unchanged when only the nucleophile concentration changes, the rate law is first order in substrate alone (SN1). If the rate rises in proportion, the nucleophile takes part in the slow step (SN2). [5D · Skill 3] (B does not report on the mechanism; C changes two variables at once; D does not report on the rate law.)
14. **D.** Glutamate has an acidic side chain. Its neutral form lies between the two acidic pKa values, so pI = (2.2 + 4.3) / 2 = 3.25, about 3.3. [5D · Skill 2] (A averages all three pKa values; B averages the two α-group pKa values, as for a neutral amino acid; C averages lysine's two basic pKa values, (9.0 + 10.5)/2, which is the pI of lysine, not glutamate.)
15. **B.** A protein is positively charged when the pH is below its pI. At pH 7.4, only B (pI 8.9) is positive and moves toward the cathode. A and C are negative and move toward the anode. [5D · Skill 4] (A A is negatively charged at pH 7.4, which is above its pI; C C is negatively charged at pH 7.4, which is above its pI; D both are negatively charged above their pI.)
16. **A.** In SDS-PAGE the charge-to-mass ratio is nearly the same for all proteins, so the gel acts as a sieve and the smallest protein (A, 25 kDa) travels farthest. [5D · Skill 4] (B SDS migration does not depend on pI; C larger proteins move more slowly through the gel; D charge scales with length, but mass still sorts them.)
17. **D.** A reducing agent breaks disulfide bonds. If C runs as one band without it and as two smaller bands with it, the subunits were joined by a disulfide. [5D · Skill 3] (A changes net charge but not the covalent structure; B compares different proteins, not the bond; C heating in SDS unfolds the protein but does not break disulfide bonds, so the band would not change.)
18. **C.** Energy conservation on the smooth arc: m g R = ½ m v², so v = √(2 g R) = √(2 × 10 × 0.80) = √16 = 4.0 m/s. [4A · Skill 2] (A omits the factor of 2 in v = √(2gR); B puts the mass inside the square root; D reports v² as v.)
19. **B.** At the bottom, the net force points up (toward the center) and equals m v² / R: N − m g = m v² / R. So N = 20 + 2.0 × 16 / 0.80 = 20 + 40 = 60 N. [4A · Skill 2] (A counts only the weight; C counts only the centripetal term; D adds the weight to twice the centripetal term.)
20. **B.** The starting kinetic energy is ½ (2.0)(4.0)² = 16 J. The slope of the line is −16 J / 2.0 m = −8.0 N, so the friction force is 8.0 N. Dividing by the normal force, m g = 20 N, gives μ = 0.40. [4A · Skill 4] (A takes the friction force as 4 N, half the correct value; C divides the friction force by g but not by the block's mass; D inverts the ratio of normal force to friction.)
21. **A.** Changing only the mass, with release height and surface held fixed, isolates the effect of mass. Both the kinetic energy and the friction force are proportional to m, so the stopping distance is predicted to be independent of mass. [4A · Skill 3] (B varies the speed, not the mass; C changes three variables at once; D does not involve the stopping distance.)
22. **D.** The thermal energy equals the work done against friction: f d = 8.0 N × 1.0 m = 8.0 J. (Half of the 16 J is dissipated in half the distance.) [4A · Skill 2] (A gives the total energy dissipated over the full 2.0 m; B uses a friction force of 4.0 N; C uses the block's weight as the friction force.)
23. **C.** The buoyant force is 2.0 N, so the displaced volume is 2.0 / (1000 × 10) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³. The mass is 8.0 / 10 = 0.80 kg, so ρ = 0.80 / 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.0 × 10³ kg/m³. [4B · Skill 2] (A uses the apparent weight to find the mass; B takes the ratio of the two weights times the density of water; D halves the displaced volume.)
24. **D.** F = q v B sin θ = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(4.0 × 10⁶)(0.50)(0.50) = 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ N. [4C · Skill 2] (A omits the sine of the angle; B uses the cosine of the angle in place of the sine; C applies the factor of 0.50 from the sine twice.)
25. **B.** Snell's law: 1.0 × sin 45° = √2 × sin θ. Then sin θ = 0.707 / 1.414 = 0.50, so θ = 30°. The ray bends toward the normal in the denser medium. [4D · Skill 2] (A assumes that no bending occurs; C reports the angle from the surface (90° − 30°) instead of from the normal; D multiplies the angle by the refractive index.)
26. **A.** Neutral Mn is [Ar] 4s² 3d⁵. The cation loses its 4s electrons first, giving [Ar] 3d⁵. By Hund's rule, the five 3d electrons occupy five orbitals singly, so there are 5 unpaired electrons. [4E · Skill 2] (B removes electrons from 3d before 4s; C counts all the valence electrons of the neutral atom; D assumes all the 3d electrons are paired.)
27. **A.** 3.4 g is 0.10 mol H₂O₂, which gives 0.050 mol O₂. Then V = nRT / P = 0.050 × 0.082 × 400 / 1.0 = 1.64 L. [5E · Skill 2] (B uses the molar volume at 273 K; C omits the 2:1 mole ratio; D applies the 2:1 mole ratio twice.)
28. **C.** Reverse the second reaction (CO₂ → CO + ½ O₂, ΔH = +283 kJ) and add it to the first: ΔH = −394 + 283 = −111 kJ/mol. [5E · Skill 2] (A reverses the sign after combining correctly; B adds the two reactions without reversing the second; D reports one of the given values.)
29. **B.** Reactivity follows the leaving group's ability and the carbonyl's electrophilicity: chloride is the weakest base and the best leaving group. Resonance donation from nitrogen makes the amide the least reactive. [5D · Skill 1] (A reverses the order; C ranks the acid chloride below an ester; D ranks the amide above an ester.)
30. **B.** The cis double bond bends the chain, so neighboring chains cannot pack closely and have fewer London dispersion contacts. The membrane has a lower melting point and is more fluid. [5D · Skill 1] (A a cis bond kinks the chain; C the shape differs even though the length is the same; D a pi bond is weaker than a sigma bond, and in any case bond strength does not govern packing.)
