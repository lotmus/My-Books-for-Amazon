# Lesson 19: Alcohols, Aldehydes and Ketones

**What you will be able to do**

- Classify alcohols and predict their physical properties, acidity and oxidation products.
- Distinguish SN1 from SN2 substitution and predict rate laws and stereochemistry.
- Predict the products of nucleophilic addition to aldehydes and ketones (hydride reduction, hemiacetals and acetals, imines, cyanohydrins).
- Explain keto-enol tautomerism, enolates and the aldol reaction.
- Use protecting groups logically.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Category 5D asks about the structure, function and reactivity of biologically relevant molecules, and the carbonyl group (C=O) is the single most important functional group in biochemistry. Sugars are aldehydes and ketones, their rings are hemiacetals, fats and proteins are built from carbonyl compounds, and almost every metabolic step involves a nucleophile attacking a carbonyl carbon. Learn the logic of the carbonyl and you will understand a large share of organic chemistry and biochemistry with one set of ideas.

The exam rarely asks you to recall obscure reagents. It asks you to apply a *mechanistic idea*: this carbon is electron-poor, that atom is a nucleophile, so what happens? Think of reactions as electrons moving from sources to sinks.

Praxis Tip: For every organic reaction, ask two questions: Where is the nucleophile (electron-rich, wants to donate)? Where is the electrophile (electron-poor, wants to accept)? Draw the curved arrow from the first to the second, and the product follows.

**Concept Review**

**Alcohols: structure and properties.** An alcohol has an –OH group on a saturated carbon. It is **primary (1°)** if that carbon is bonded to one other carbon, **secondary (2°)** if two, **tertiary (3°)** if three.

- **Hydrogen bonding** gives alcohols much higher boiling points than alkanes or ethers of similar mass ([Lesson 11](chapter:19)). Small alcohols are miscible with water; solubility falls as the carbon chain lengthens, because the nonpolar part dominates.
- **Acidity:** alcohols are very weak acids (pKa about 16 to 18, a bit weaker than water). Electron-withdrawing groups nearby increase acidity. **Phenols** (OH on an aromatic ring) are much more acidic (pKa about 10) because the negative charge of the conjugate base is delocalized into the ring.
- A rough acidity ladder: carboxylic acids (pKa about 5) > phenols (about 10) > water (15.7) > alcohols (16 to 18) > hydrogens next to a carbonyl (about 20).

**Oxidation of alcohols.**

| Alcohol | Mild oxidant (e.g., PCC) | Strong oxidant (e.g., CrO₃/H⁺, K₂Cr₂O₇, KMnO₄) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Aldehyde | Carboxylic acid |
| Secondary | Ketone | Ketone |
| Tertiary | No reaction | No reaction |

Oxidation of an alcohol removes the hydrogen on the carbon bearing the OH, so tertiary alcohols (which have none) cannot be oxidized without breaking carbon-carbon bonds.

**Substitution at alcohols and alkyl halides: SN1 and SN2.** A **nucleophile** (Nu) replaces a **leaving group** (LG) at a saturated carbon. Good leaving groups are weak bases (halides, water, tosylate); –OH is a poor leaving group (OH⁻ is a strong base), so an alcohol must be activated first.

| Feature | SN2 | SN1 |
|---|---|---|
| Steps | One (concerted), backside attack | Two: leaving group leaves (rate-determining), then Nu attacks the carbocation |
| Rate law | rate = k [RX][Nu] | rate = k [RX] |
| Substrate preference | Methyl > 1° > 2° (sterics) | 3° > 2° (carbocation stability) |
| Stereochemistry | **Inversion** of configuration | **Racemization** (planar carbocation, attack from either face) |
| Nucleophile | Strong nucleophile preferred | Nucleophile strength does not affect the rate |
| Solvent | Polar aprotic (acetone, DMSO) | Polar protic (water, alcohols) |

**Making alcohols leave.** Protonating the alcohol with a strong acid turns –OH into –OH₂⁺, and water leaves. Alternatively, convert the alcohol to a **mesylate** or **tosylate** (reaction with MsCl or TsCl, usually with pyridine). The sulfonate is an excellent leaving group, and the alcohol's carbon is not touched in forming it, so the configuration is *retained* in that step.

Praxis Tip: Remember "SN2 inverts, SN1 racemizes." A reaction with a tosylate on a chiral center followed by SN2 attack gives inversion overall, because forming the tosylate retains and the SN2 step inverts.

**Protecting groups.** A **protecting group** temporarily masks a reactive group so that a reagent attacks somewhere else. A ketone can be protected as an acetal (stable to base and to hydride reagents); an alcohol can be protected as a silyl ether. After the desired reaction, the protecting group is removed.

**The carbonyl group.** In C=O, oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the carbon carries a partial positive charge: it is an **electrophile**. Nucleophiles attack the carbon, and the C=O π bond breaks, putting the negative charge on oxygen. Aldehydes (RCHO) are more reactive than ketones (RCOR') for two reasons: less steric hindrance, and less electron donation from alkyl groups to the carbon.

**Physical properties.** Aldehydes and ketones are polar but cannot donate hydrogen bonds, so their boiling points lie between those of alkanes and alcohols of similar mass. They can accept hydrogen bonds, so small ones (formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, acetone) mix with water.

**Nucleophilic addition reactions.**

- **Hydride reagents** (NaBH₄, LiAlH₄) deliver H⁻ and, after an acid work-up, reduce aldehydes to **primary alcohols** and ketones to **secondary alcohols**. NaBH₄ is milder; LiAlH₄ is stronger and also reduces esters and carboxylic acids.
- **Cyanohydrin formation:** CN⁻ adds to give a hydroxynitrile.
- **Hemiacetals and acetals:** an alcohol adds to the carbonyl to give a **hemiacetal** (hemiketal for a ketone). With a second molecule of alcohol and acid catalysis, the hemiacetal is converted to an **acetal** (ketal), losing water. Acetals are stable to base but hydrolyze back to the carbonyl in aqueous acid. **Sugars cyclize** by an internal hemiacetal reaction ([Lesson 25](chapter:36)).
- **Imines and enamines:** a primary amine adds and loses water to give an **imine** (C=N); a secondary amine gives an **enamine** (C=C–N).
- **Oxidation:** aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids (by mild oxidants such as Tollens' reagent, and by stronger ones); ketones resist oxidation.

**α-Hydrogens, enols and enolates.** A hydrogen on the carbon adjacent to a carbonyl (the **α-carbon**) is unusually acidic (pKa about 20) because removing it gives an **enolate** whose negative charge is delocalized onto the oxygen. In **keto-enol tautomerism**, the carbonyl form (keto) and the C=C–OH form (enol) interconvert rapidly; the keto form usually predominates. Because the enol or enolate is planar at the α-carbon, a chiral α-carbon can **racemize** through it.

**The aldol reaction.** An enolate is a nucleophile. It can attack the carbonyl of another molecule, forming a new C–C bond and a **β-hydroxy aldehyde or ketone** (an **aldol**). On heating, the aldol can lose water to give an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl (an **aldol condensation**). The reverse is the **retro-aldol** reaction, important in glycolysis.

For an unsymmetrical ketone, two enolates are possible: the **kinetic enolate** (formed fast with a bulky strong base such as LDA at low temperature, from the less hindered α-carbon) and the **thermodynamic enolate** (formed under equilibrating conditions, usually the more substituted, more stable one).

**Worked Example 1: Oxidations (skill: classify, then oxidize)**

What products form when each is treated with (i) PCC and (ii) hot acidic K₂Cr₂O₇? (a) Ethanol, (b) 2-propanol, (c) 2-methyl-2-propanol.

(a) Ethanol is primary: (i) acetaldehyde; (ii) acetic acid. (b) 2-Propanol is secondary: (i) and (ii) both acetone. (c) 2-Methyl-2-propanol is tertiary: no reaction in either case.

**Worked Example 2: SN2 versus SN1 (skill: pick the mechanism)**

(a) CH₃CH₂Br + CN⁻ in acetone. (b) (CH₃)₃CBr in aqueous ethanol. (c) (R)-2-bromobutane + OH⁻ in a polar aprotic solvent. (d) What happens to the rate in (a) and (b) if the nucleophile concentration is doubled?

(a) Primary halide, strong nucleophile, polar aprotic solvent: SN2. Product: CH₃CH₂CN. (b) Tertiary halide, weak nucleophile, polar protic solvent: SN1, through the tert-butyl carbocation. (c) A secondary halide with a strong nucleophile in a polar aprotic solvent favors SN2, with **inversion**: (S)-2-butanol. (d) SN2 rate = k[RX][Nu] doubles; SN1 rate = k[RX] is unchanged.

**Worked Example 3: Nucleophilic addition (skill: reagent to product)**

Predict the product: (a) acetone + NaBH₄, then H₃O⁺; (b) acetaldehyde + excess methanol with an acid catalyst; (c) cyclohexanone + CH₃NH₂.

(a) Hydride adds to the carbonyl carbon, and protonation gives 2-propanol. (b) The hemiacetal CH₃CH(OH)OCH₃ forms first; with acid and more methanol, it converts to the acetal CH₃CH(OCH₃)₂ plus water. (c) The product is the imine (the N-methyl imine of cyclohexanone, CH₃–N=C₆H₁₀), with loss of water.

**Worked Example 4: The aldol reaction (skill: enolate as nucleophile)**

Show the product of base-catalyzed aldol addition of acetaldehyde with itself, and what happens on heating.

Base removes an α-hydrogen from one CH₃CHO to give the enolate ⁻CH₂CHO. The enolate attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second CH₃CHO. After protonation: CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO (3-hydroxybutanal), the aldol. On heating, loss of water gives CH₃CH=CHCHO (2-butenal, crotonaldehyde), an α,β-unsaturated aldehyde.

**Worked Example 5: Stereochemistry through a leaving group (skill: net result of two steps)**

(S)-2-butanol is converted to its tosylate and then treated with a nucleophile Nu⁻ that attacks by SN2. What is the configuration of the product relative to the starting alcohol?

Forming the tosylate does not break any bond at the stereocenter, so it keeps the configuration. The SN2 step then inverts it. Net result: inversion. (The sense of R/S depends on the priorities of the new group, so state the geometric result: the nucleophile ends up on the face opposite to the one the oxygen occupied.)

**Worked Example 6: Protecting groups (skill: choosing a sequence)**

You want to reduce an ester group to an alcohol in a molecule that also has a ketone, without touching the ketone. LiAlH₄ would reduce both. How can you do it?

Protect the ketone as an acetal (ethylene glycol, acid catalyst); acetals are inert to LiAlH₄. Reduce the ester with LiAlH₄ and work up. Remove the acetal with aqueous acid to restore the ketone.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: For acidity questions, remember the question to ask is "how stable is the conjugate base?" Charge delocalization (resonance) is the biggest stabilizer, then electronegativity, then electron-withdrawing groups.

Praxis Tip: For hydride reductions, the reagent supplies H⁻ and the work-up supplies H⁺. Write both steps; without the work-up, the product is the alkoxide.

Praxis Tip: Acetal formation is reversible and driven by removing water; hydrolysis is driven by adding water and acid. Think of it as a dial controlled by water.

Praxis Tip: When a molecule has two carbonyls, the aldehyde is the more reactive electrophile. Many selectivity questions are about this one difference.

Praxis Tip: Draw the mechanism with curved arrows, even a quick sketch. The arrows show electron flow, and the errors, such as an arrow starting from the wrong atom, become obvious.

**Common Mistakes**

- Oxidizing a tertiary alcohol.
- Saying SN2 gives racemization or SN1 gives inversion.
- Choosing SN2 for a tertiary substrate.
- Leaving out the acid work-up in a hydride reduction.
- Confusing hemiacetal with acetal.
- Forgetting that an α-hydrogen is needed for the enolate.

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **A.** 2-Propanol is a secondary alcohol, which is oxidized to a ketone (acetone). (B would result from oxidizing a primary alcohol; C would result from fully oxidizing a primary alcohol; D applies to tertiary alcohols.)
2. **C.** 2-Methyl-2-propanol is tertiary: the carbon bearing the OH has no hydrogen to lose, so it resists oxidation. (A is primary and oxidizes to butanal or butanoic acid; B is secondary and oxidizes to a ketone; D is primary and oxidizes to acetaldehyde or acetic acid.)
3. **D.** SN2 proceeds by backside attack, inverting the configuration at the stereocenter, so R becomes S (here OH takes the same priority rank that Br had, so the descriptor changes along with the geometry). (A would require retention of configuration; B describes an SN1 outcome; C is an elimination product, not the substitution product.)
4. **B.** Sodium borohydride delivers hydride to the carbonyl carbon, and the work-up protonates the alkoxide to give the alcohol. (A is an oxidizing agent; C is a mild oxidizing agent; D oxidizes aldehydes to carboxylic acids.)
5. **C.** Alcohol addition first gives a hemiketal; with acid and a second alcohol, water is lost and the ketal forms. (A stops at the first stage; B would require oxidation; D requires an amine, not an alcohol.)
6. **D.** An enolate adds to a carbonyl carbon and, after protonation, the product has a hydroxyl group two carbons from the carbonyl: a β-hydroxy carbonyl compound. (A has the wrong oxidation state and position; B is an oxidation product; C involves a halogen that is not part of the reaction.)
7. **A.** Removing an α-hydrogen gives an enolate whose negative charge is delocalized onto the oxygen, so these hydrogens (pKa about 20) are the most acidic. (B are not activated by the carbonyl; C ignores the stabilization of the enolate; D ignores that α-hydrogens form enolates.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, look at each wrong choice and name the slip (mechanism mixed up, substrate class, oxidant strength). Log it in your error log ([Strategy: The Question Method](chapter:4)).
