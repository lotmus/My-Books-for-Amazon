# Strategy: Test-Day Plan, Pacing and Stress

**What you will be able to do**

- Turn the exam's published format into a pacing plan with checkpoints you can check at a glance.
- Build a checklist for the week, the night, the morning and the room, and confirm each rule against the exam owner's current instructions.
- Recover from a bad passage, or a bad section, without letting it spill into the next one.
- Decide, on the fly, which questions to answer now, which to flag, and which to guess.

**Why it matters for the exam**

The Chemical and Physical Foundations section is the first one you sit. It also has the section's heaviest quantitative load, and you meet it with no calculator. A strong command of the lessons can still be wasted by a slow first passage, a late arrival, or a wobbling stomach. This chapter is the delivery plan. It is a companion to the general plan in Core Test Skills, "Your Test-Day Plan", and it adds what is specific to this section.

Praxis Tip: Read this chapter twice: once now, and once during the week before the exam. The second reading is the one that counts.

**Pacing: the arithmetic**

With 59 questions in 95 minutes, the average is 95 / 59 = 1.61 minutes per question, or about 1 minute 37 seconds. That average hides a spread. A quick conceptual question can take 30 seconds. A multi-step calculation on an unfamiliar passage can take 2.5 minutes. You bank time on the quick ones and spend it on the slow ones.

Reading time matters too. A passage of about 200 words, with a table, takes 2 to 3 minutes to read and understand if you read actively. Count that as part of the passage's budget, not extra. A passage with four questions has a budget of roughly 4 × 1.61 = 6.4 minutes in total, including reading.

Worked Example: Setting your own checkpoints.

Suppose you will practice with the two-part full-length exams in this book. Each part has 30 questions, and the book gives 48 minutes per part (a hair faster than the real pace, 1.6 minutes per question). Your checkpoints for one part: Question 8 by 13 minutes, Question 15 by 24 minutes, Question 23 by 37 minutes, Question 30 by 48 minutes. Check: 8 × 1.6 = 12.8, 15 × 1.6 = 24, 23 × 1.6 = 36.8, 30 × 1.6 = 48.

*Skill used:* converting an average rate into checkpoints.

Praxis Tip: Do not check the clock after every question. Check at your checkpoints. Constant clock-watching costs you a few seconds each time and a lot of calm.

**The triage rule**

Not every question deserves your full effort. In the first read of a question, decide:

1. **Answer now.** You see the path, and it will take under about two minutes.
2. **Flag and move.** You see a path but it is long, or you can narrow to two choices and cannot decide. Choose your best answer, flag it, and go on. A recorded answer beats a blank one.
3. **Guess and move.** You cannot see a path. Eliminate what you can, pick one, flag it if you wish, and go on. There is no penalty for wrong answers on this exam [VERIFY], so never leave a question blank.

Come back to flagged questions if time remains. Change an answer only when you find a specific reason, such as a misread number or a missed step, not because of a feeling.

Praxis Tip: The question you cannot do costs the same as the one you can, on the score. Spending four minutes on it is a bad trade when three easier questions are waiting.

**Passage strategy in this section**

For a passage-based set, use this order:

1. Read the questions' stems first only if you are short of time; otherwise read the passage first, and write the givens on your scratch paper.
2. Do the questions in the order that is easiest for you, not the order printed. On a computer-based exam you can usually move freely within the section.
3. Use estimation. The test is built so that arithmetic is easy when you round sensibly ([Strategy: The Quantitative Toolkit](chapter:5)).
4. Return to the passage for data rather than recalling it from memory.

Praxis Tip: Write units beside every number on scratch paper. Unit slips are among the most common arithmetic errors in this section.

**Physical logistics, in order**

*One week before*
- Confirm registration, ID, location and time against the official page.
- Take your last full-length simulation earlier than this week. Use this week for light review of your error log and formula card, and for rest.

*Two days before*
- Pack the bag: ID, confirmation, allowed items, a layer of clothing, snacks and water for breaks as permitted. Lay out clothes.
- Stop new material.

*The night before*
- Eat a normal dinner. Stop studying early. Set two alarms.

*Morning*
- Eat the breakfast you have practiced. Leave with a buffer of at least 30 minutes.

*In the room*
- Settle in. Write your checkpoint times on the scratch material you are given, if the rules allow.

*Breaks*
- Use the toilet, drink, eat, move. Do not replay the last section. It is finished.

If you need testing accommodations, apply early. The paperwork can take weeks, and no study technique helps if you have missed the deadline.

Praxis Tip: Pack the bag the night before and put it by the door. Searching for your ID at 7:15 a.m. is exam stress you can simply decline.

**Stress, without drama**

A little arousal sharpens attention; a lot narrows it. Three habits keep you on the useful side:

- **Name it.** "My heart is racing; that is the normal reaction." This sounds trivial and measurably helps for many people.
- **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Four counts in, six counts out, three times. It takes about thirty seconds and slows the heart rate.
- **Anchor to the next action.** Not "I'm failing this passage" but "Read the first question and write down the givens."

If a passage looks like a foreign language, remember that the exam deliberately includes unfamiliar topics. You are expected to use the passage, plus basic science. Read it as a puzzle with all the pieces on the table.

Praxis Tip: Practice stress on purpose: take some timed sets while slightly uncomfortable (a little hungry, a coffee less than usual, a noisy room). It builds range. Do not do this the week of the exam.

**Handling a bad section**

A section will occasionally go badly. It happens even to well-prepared test-takers. The aim is to prevent one bad section from becoming two.

1. Between sections, take one slow breath and say, "That section is done."
2. Do not estimate your score from how it felt. The exam deliberately includes hard questions, so a hard section is normal, not diagnostic.
3. Return to the process: question method, pacing, next question.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Hard questions are a sign of a well-made exam. If every question felt easy, the exam would not be measuring much.

Praxis Tip: If you are running behind at a checkpoint, do not sprint. Pick up two or three easy questions at the next set and let the hard ones wait.

Praxis Tip: On the last two minutes, fill every remaining blank. A guess has a one-in-four chance; a blank has none.

Praxis Tip: Use the periodic table on screen rather than your memory for atomic masses; use your memory (and the Fact Sheet beforehand) for constants and formulas, because only the periodic table is supplied.

**Common mistakes**

- Reading the rules the night before.
- Checking the clock every question.
- Staying with a question for five minutes to "prove" it.
- Changing answers on a feeling rather than a reason.
- Discussing questions at the break.
- Trying a new food or drink on exam morning.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. At 1.6 minutes per question, how many minutes does a 30-question set take?
2. A passage has four questions. What is its approximate time budget, including reading?
3. When should you flag a question rather than answer it now?
4. Why should you never leave a question blank?
5. Name two things to do between sections.
6. Why practice with slightly uncomfortable conditions, and when should you stop doing so?
7. What should you write on your scratch material in the first minute, if the rules allow?

**Stop here.** Answer each question in your notebook before reading the answers.

**Answers and reasons**

1. 48 minutes (30 × 1.6). The average, not a guarantee for any single question.
2. About 6.4 minutes (4 × 1.6), spent on reading and answering together.
3. When you can see a path but it is long, or when you are stuck between two choices. Record your best answer first, then flag.
4. A blank earns nothing, and a guess earns something with probability at least one in four. [VERIFY: no penalty for wrong answers.]
5. Breathe out slowly, say "that section is done," drink water, and avoid replaying questions.
6. To build tolerance to discomfort. Stop in the final week, when you want comfort and routine.
7. Your checkpoint times, and perhaps a short reminder of your triage rule.

**Teach-Back and Spaced Review**

In your notebook, explain to an imaginary friend how to turn 59 questions and 95 minutes into four checkpoints. Then, after two days, one week and three weeks, redo the checkpoint arithmetic from memory for 30 questions in 48 minutes.
