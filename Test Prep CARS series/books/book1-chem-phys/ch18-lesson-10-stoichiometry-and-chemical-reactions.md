# Lesson 10: Stoichiometry and Chemical Reactions

**What you will be able to do**

- Convert among mass, moles and number of particles using molar mass and Avogadro's number.
- Find empirical and molecular formulas from percent composition.
- Balance chemical equations, including redox equations, and assign oxidation numbers.
- Identify limiting reactants and calculate theoretical and percent yield.
- Recognize the main reaction types and the common oxidizing and reducing agents.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Stoichiometry is the bookkeeping of chemistry, and it lives in category 4E alongside atomic structure. It shows up wherever amounts matter: drug doses, concentrations in solution, gas volumes, yields in a synthesis, the stoichiometry of a metabolic reaction. The exam rarely asks for a long calculation, but it frequently asks for the first step of one (what is limiting?) or for a ratio (how many moles of product per mole of reactant?). A student who can set up the mole bridge correctly can answer these in seconds.

Praxis Tip: Almost every stoichiometry problem is a bridge: grams to moles, moles of A to moles of B using the coefficients, moles of B back to whatever the question asks for. Draw the bridge before you calculate.

**Concept Review**

**The mole.** A **mole** is 6.022 × 10²³ particles (**Avogadro's number**, N_A). The **molar mass** of a substance is the mass of one mole in grams, numerically equal to its formula (molecular) weight in atomic mass units. Water: 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.0 g/mol. So 18.0 g of water is 1 mol, which contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules.

    moles = mass / molar mass,    number of particles = moles × N_A

The metric prefixes matter: milli (10⁻³), micro (10⁻⁶), nano (10⁻⁹), kilo (10³).

**Formulas.** The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound. The **molecular formula** is the actual number of each atom in a molecule, a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Glucose has the empirical formula CH₂O and the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆.

**Percent composition.** The mass percent of an element in a compound is (mass of the element in one mole of the compound ÷ molar mass of the compound) × 100. To find an empirical formula from percent composition:

1. Assume 100 g of the compound, so the percentages become grams.
2. Convert each mass to moles.
3. Divide by the smallest number of moles to get a ratio.
4. If a ratio is not near a whole number (for example 1.5), multiply all by a small integer to get whole numbers.

**Balanced equations.** A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom (and the same net charge) on both sides. The coefficients give the mole ratios of the reactants and products. Balancing by inspection: start with the element that appears in the fewest places, leave elements that appear alone (such as O₂ or H₂) until last, and adjust the coefficients (never the subscripts).

Praxis Tip: Balance combustion equations in the order C, H, O. Carbon and hydrogen each appear in one reactant and one product, and oxygen appears in several places, so you tidy it up last.

**Limiting reactant and yield.** In a reaction, the **limiting reactant** is the one that is used up first and so limits how much product can form. To find it, divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient: the smallest quotient identifies the limiting reactant. The **theoretical yield** is the amount of product predicted from the limiting reactant. The **percent yield** compares what you actually obtain with the theoretical amount:

    percent yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100

**Reaction types.**

- **Combination (synthesis):** A + B → AB.
- **Decomposition:** AB → A + B.
- **Single replacement:** A + BC → AC + B.
- **Double replacement (metathesis):** AB + CD → AD + CB (includes precipitation and neutralization).
- **Combustion:** a substance reacts with O₂, producing CO₂ and H₂O for hydrocarbons.
- **Acid-base (neutralization)** and **oxidation-reduction (redox)** reactions, which can overlap with the types above.

**Oxidation numbers.** Oxidation numbers track electron bookkeeping. Rules, in order of priority:

1. An uncombined element has an oxidation number of 0.
2. A monatomic ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge.
3. In compounds, group 1 metals are +1, group 2 metals are +2, and fluorine is −1.
4. Oxygen is usually −2 (exceptions: peroxides, −1), hydrogen is usually +1 (exceptions: metal hydrides, −1).
5. The oxidation numbers in a neutral compound sum to zero; in an ion they sum to the charge.

**Redox.** **Oxidation** is loss of electrons (oxidation number increases). **Reduction** is gain of electrons (oxidation number decreases). The substance oxidized is the **reducing agent**; the substance reduced is the **oxidizing agent**. Common oxidizing agents: O₂, halogens, permanganate (MnO₄⁻), dichromate (Cr₂O₇²⁻). Common reducing agents: metals, hydrogen, hydride reagents (such as NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄). A **disproportionation** reaction is one in which the same element is both oxidized and reduced.

Praxis Tip: Remember "LEO the lion says GER": Loss of Electrons is Oxidation; Gain of Electrons is Reduction. Silly, reliable, and still faster than rederiving it under time pressure.

**Balancing redox equations.** The half-reaction method: split the reaction into oxidation and reduction half-reactions, balance atoms other than O and H, balance O with H₂O, balance H with H⁺ (in acid solution), balance charge with electrons, scale the half-reactions so that the electrons cancel, and add.

**Worked Example 1: The mole bridge (skill: mass to molecules)**

How many molecules are in 36 g of water? Molar mass 18 g/mol.

Moles: 36 / 18 = 2.0 mol. Molecules: 2.0 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.2 × 10²⁴.

**Worked Example 2: Empirical to molecular formula (skill: the 100 g trick)**

A compound is 40.0 percent C, 6.7 percent H and 53.3 percent O by mass, and its molar mass is about 180 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

In 100 g: C = 40.0 g, H = 6.7 g, O = 53.3 g. Moles: C = 40.0 / 12.0 = 3.33; H = 6.7 / 1.008 = 6.65; O = 53.3 / 16.0 = 3.33. Divide by 3.33: C = 1.00, H = 2.00, O = 1.00. Empirical formula: CH₂O.

Empirical formula mass = 12.0 + 2.0 + 16.0 = 30.0 g/mol. Ratio: 180 / 30 = 6. Molecular formula: C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose, though other sugars share it).

**Worked Example 3: Mass relations in a reaction (skill: balancing and conservation)**

Propane burns: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Balance it, and find the masses of O₂ consumed and of CO₂ and H₂O produced when 44 g of propane burns.

Balance C: 3 CO₂. Balance H: 4 H₂O. Count O: 3 × 2 + 4 = 10 oxygen atoms, so 5 O₂. 

C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O.

44 g propane is 1.0 mol (molar mass 44). O₂ consumed: 5 mol × 32 = 160 g. CO₂ produced: 3 mol × 44 = 132 g. H₂O produced: 4 mol × 18 = 72 g.

*Check.* Mass is conserved: reactants 44 + 160 = 204 g; products 132 + 72 = 204 g ✓.

**Worked Example 4: Limiting reactant and percent yield (skill: dividing by coefficients)**

Ammonia is made by N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃. A mixture contains 28 g of N₂ and 9.0 g of H₂. What is the limiting reactant? What is the theoretical yield of NH₃? If 25.5 g of NH₃ forms, what is the percent yield? How much of the excess reactant remains?

Moles: N₂ = 28 / 28 = 1.0 mol. H₂ = 9.0 / 2.0 = 4.5 mol.

Divide by coefficients: N₂: 1.0 / 1 = 1.0; H₂: 4.5 / 3 = 1.5. The smaller value is N₂, so **N₂ is limiting**.

Theoretical NH₃: 1.0 mol N₂ × 2 = 2.0 mol = 2.0 × 17 = 34 g.

Percent yield = 25.5 / 34 × 100 = 75 percent.

Excess H₂: used 3.0 mol, so 4.5 − 3.0 = 1.5 mol remain = 3.0 g.

*Skill used:* the divide-by-coefficient test, which is faster than computing every product amount.

Praxis Tip: The smaller quotient (moles divided by coefficient) always marks the limiting reactant. Memorize the test, not the story.

**Worked Example 5: Oxidation numbers and redox (skill: electron bookkeeping)**

(a) Find the oxidation number of Mn in KMnO₄ and Cr in Cr₂O₇²⁻. (b) In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, identify what is oxidized, reduced, and the reducing agent. (c) Balance MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺ in acid.

(a) KMnO₄: +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0, so x = +7. Cr₂O₇²⁻: 2x + 7(−2) = −2, so x = +6.

(b) Zn goes from 0 to +2: oxidized, and it is the reducing agent. Cu²⁺ goes from +2 to 0: reduced, and it is the oxidizing agent.

(c) Reduction half: MnO₄⁻ + 8 H⁺ + 5 e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4 H₂O. Oxidation half: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Multiply the oxidation half by 5 and add:

MnO₄⁻ + 5 Fe²⁺ + 8 H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5 Fe³⁺ + 4 H₂O.

*Check.* Charge: left −1 + 10 + 8 = +17; right +2 + 15 = +17 ✓. Atoms: Mn 1/1, Fe 5/5, O 4/4, H 8/8 ✓.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Use round atomic masses (H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16) for estimates, and the periodic table provided on screen for anything less familiar.

Praxis Tip: When an equation looks balanced, count the atoms on each side one element at a time. Count charge too, if ions are involved.

Praxis Tip: Percent yield cannot exceed 100 percent in a clean experiment. If you compute one over 100, you probably inverted the ratio.

Praxis Tip: For limiting-reagent multiple choice, you may not need to calculate the products. Find the limiting reactant, scale from it, and see which choice matches.

Praxis Tip: In a redox reaction, electrons gained must equal electrons lost. If you can name the oxidation number change per atom, you can balance the equation by finding the lowest common multiple.

**Common Mistakes**

- Changing subscripts instead of coefficients when balancing.
- Comparing grams rather than moles when finding the limiting reactant.
- Forgetting to divide by the coefficient in the limiting-reactant test.
- Assigning oxygen −2 in a peroxide.
- Confusing the reducing agent (the species oxidized) with the species reduced.
- Forgetting to balance charge in redox half-reactions.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. What is the mass of 0.50 mol of CO₂? (Molar mass of CO₂ = 44 g/mol.)
   A. 88 g
   B. 44 g
   C. 22 g
   D. 0.011 g

2. A hydrocarbon contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass. Its empirical formula is closest to
   A. CH
   B. CH₂
   C. CH₃
   D. C₂H

3. Aluminum reacts with chlorine: 2 Al + 3 Cl₂ → 2 AlCl₃. If 3.0 mol of Al reacts with 3.0 mol of Cl₂, the maximum amount of AlCl₃ that can form is
   A. 3.0 mol
   B. 1.5 mol
   C. 4.5 mol
   D. 2.0 mol

4. What is the oxidation number of chromium in K₂Cr₂O₇?
   A. +6
   B. +7
   C. +3
   D. +12

5. In the reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, the reducing agent is
   A. Cu²⁺
   B. Zn²⁺
   C. Cu
   D. Zn

6. A reaction has a theoretical yield of 20.0 g. The experimenter actually obtains 15.0 g. The percent yield is
   A. 75%
   B. 133%
   C. 25%
   D. 5.0%

7. When the equation C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O is balanced with the smallest whole-number coefficients, the coefficient of O₂ is
   A. 3.5
   B. 5
   C. 7
   D. 9

---

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** Mass = moles × molar mass = 0.50 × 44 = 22 g. (A multiplies the molar mass by 2 instead of by 0.50; B uses the molar mass without scaling by the moles; D divides 0.50 by 44 instead of multiplying.)
2. **B.** In 100 g: C = 85.7 / 12.0 = 7.14 mol; H = 14.3 / 1.008 = 14.2 mol. The ratio H : C is 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH₂. (A uses equal numbers of atoms instead of moles; C overestimates the hydrogen ratio; D reverses the ratio.)
3. **D.** Divide by coefficients: Al: 3.0 / 2 = 1.5; Cl₂: 3.0 / 3 = 1.0. Cl₂ is limiting, and 3.0 mol Cl₂ makes (2/3) × 3.0 = 2.0 mol AlCl₃. (A assumes Al is limiting and all of it reacts; B halves the amount of Al for no reason; C uses the moles of Cl₂ required by 3.0 mol Al.)
4. **A.** 2(+1) + 2x + 7(−2) = 0, so 2x = 12 and x = +6. (B treats Cr as if it were Mn in permanganate; C uses the common Cr³⁺ ion instead of working it out; D does not divide the total between the two chromium atoms.)
5. **D.** Zn is oxidized (0 to +2), so it is the species that reduces the other, making it the reducing agent. (A is reduced, so it is the oxidizing agent; B is a product, not a reactant; C is a product, not a reactant.)
6. **A.** Percent yield = actual / theoretical × 100 = 15.0 / 20.0 × 100 = 75%. (B inverts the ratio; C reports the fraction lost, not the yield; D subtracts the masses and treats the difference as a percent.)
7. **C.** 2 C₂H₆ + 7 O₂ → 4 CO₂ + 6 H₂O. Oxygen atoms: 14 on the left; 8 + 6 = 14 on the right. (A leaves a fractional coefficient; B miscounts the oxygen atoms; D overcounts the oxygen atoms.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, look at each wrong choice and name the slip (grams instead of moles, coefficient ignored, wrong oxidation state, wrong agent). Log it in your error log ([Strategy: The Question Method](chapter:4)).
