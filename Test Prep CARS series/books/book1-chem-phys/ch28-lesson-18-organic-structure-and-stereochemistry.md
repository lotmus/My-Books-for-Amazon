# Lesson 18: Organic Structure and Stereochemistry

**What you will be able to do**

- Recognize the common functional groups and read basic organic nomenclature.
- Classify isomers as constitutional, enantiomers, diastereomers or conformational isomers.
- Identify stereocenters, assign R/S and E/Z configurations, and recognize meso compounds.
- Use optical activity and specific rotation, and distinguish racemic mixtures from pure enantiomers.
- Describe the conformations of ethane, butane and cyclohexane, and identify aromatic compounds.

**Why it matters for the exam**

Organic chemistry is about 15 percent of the section, and its first job is to teach you to see molecules in three dimensions. Biological molecules are overwhelmingly single stereoisomers: the amino acids in your proteins are almost all L, the sugars in your metabolism are almost all D, and a drug's two enantiomers can behave very differently in the body. Stereochemistry is therefore one of the most testable skills in category 5B. It is also pleasantly mechanical: a few definitions and one bookkeeping rule go a long way.

Praxis Tip: When a question shows a molecule, do four things in order: find the functional groups, find any stereocenters, find any double bonds that can show E/Z, and then look at the question. The structure usually answers half of it before you read the stem.

**Concept Review**

**Functional groups.** Organic reactivity is organized by functional groups, specific arrangements of atoms. The ones to recognize on sight:

| Group | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Alkane | C–C single bonds | ethane |
| Alkene | C=C | ethene |
| Alkyne | C≡C | ethyne |
| Alcohol | –OH on carbon | ethanol |
| Ether | C–O–C | diethyl ether |
| Aldehyde | –CHO (C=O at chain end) | acetaldehyde |
| Ketone | C=O between two carbons | acetone |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | acetic acid |
| Ester | –COOR | ethyl acetate |
| Amide | –CONR₂ | acetamide |
| Amine | –NR₂ | methylamine |
| Aromatic ring | benzene ring | benzene, phenol |

**Nomenclature in brief.** The IUPAC name is built from the longest carbon chain containing the principal functional group (prefix for length: meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-), a suffix for the group (-ane, -ene, -yne, -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid), and numbers for the positions of substituents, chosen to give the lowest numbers to the principal group. Substituent names go in alphabetical order. Common names appear in biology (acetone, formaldehyde, glycerol), so recognize the main ones.

**Isomers.** Isomers have the same molecular formula but different structures.

- **Constitutional (structural) isomers:** different connectivity of atoms (butane and isobutane; ethanol and dimethyl ether).
- **Stereoisomers:** same connectivity, different spatial arrangement. These include:
 - **Enantiomers:** non-superimposable mirror images.
 - **Diastereomers:** stereoisomers that are not mirror images, including *cis-trans* (geometric) isomers.
- **Conformational isomers (conformers):** different shapes of the same molecule interconverted by rotation around single bonds.

**Chirality.** An object is **chiral** if it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image (your hands are the standard example). A carbon atom bonded to four *different* groups is a **stereocenter** (chiral center). A molecule with one stereocenter is chiral and exists as a pair of enantiomers. A molecule with *n* stereocenters has up to 2ⁿ stereoisomers. A **meso compound** has stereocenters but also an internal plane of symmetry, so it is achiral (and the count falls below 2ⁿ: tartaric acid has two stereocenters but only three stereoisomers, a pair of enantiomers and one meso form).

**Properties of stereoisomers.** Enantiomers have identical physical properties (melting point, boiling point, solubility in achiral solvents) except for one thing: they rotate plane-polarized light in **opposite directions** by equal amounts. They also react differently with other chiral molecules, which is why enzymes and receptors can tell them apart. Diastereomers have *different* physical properties, which allows them to be separated by ordinary methods ([Lesson 22](chapter:32)).

**R/S configuration.** The Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules assign a label to each stereocenter.

1. Rank the four substituents by priority: the atom directly attached with the higher atomic number has higher priority (I > Br > Cl > S > F > O > N > C > H). If tied, move to the next atoms outward until a difference appears. A double or triple bond counts as duplicated atoms.
2. Orient the molecule so that the lowest-priority group (priority 4, usually H) points *away* from you.
3. Trace the path from priority 1 to 2 to 3. Clockwise is **R** (rectus); counterclockwise is **S** (sinister).

If the lowest-priority group points toward you, determine the direction and then reverse it. The two enantiomers of a compound have opposite descriptors at every stereocenter.

**E/Z configuration.** For a double bond, rank the two groups on each carbon by priority. If the two higher-priority groups are on the **same** side, the alkene is **Z** (zusammen); if on **opposite** sides, it is **E** (entgegen). For simple disubstituted alkenes, Z corresponds to cis and E to trans.

**Optical activity.** A chiral compound that rotates plane-polarized light is **optically active**. Clockwise rotation is **dextrorotatory** (+), counterclockwise is **levorotatory** (−). The sign of rotation cannot be predicted from R/S; it must be measured. The **specific rotation** is

    [α] = α_observed / (l × c)

where l is the path length in decimeters and c is the concentration in g/mL. A **racemic mixture** is a 50:50 mixture of enantiomers: the rotations cancel, so it is optically inactive. For a mixture, the **enantiomeric excess** (ee) is the excess of one enantiomer as a fraction of the total, and the observed rotation is the ee times the specific rotation of the pure enantiomer.

**Relative and absolute configuration.** The **D/L** system (used for sugars and amino acids) relates a compound's configuration to that of glyceraldehyde. Most natural amino acids are L and most natural sugars are D. D/L is a *relative* label and has no fixed connection to the sign of rotation or to R/S.

**Conformations.** Single bonds rotate, producing different arrangements called conformations.

- **Ethane and butane** (Newman projections): **staggered** conformations are lower in energy than **eclipsed** ones because eclipsing creates torsional strain. In butane, the staggered arrangement with the two methyl groups at 180° (**anti**) is the most stable; the staggered arrangement with 60° between them (**gauche**) is somewhat higher in energy; fully eclipsed (methyls aligned) is the highest. (Roughly, gauche sits about 4 kJ/mol above anti, and the fully eclipsed form about 19 kJ/mol above anti. You need the ordering, not the numbers.)
- **Cyclohexane** is not flat. It prefers the **chair** conformation, with substituents in **axial** positions (up or down, parallel to the ring axis) or **equatorial** positions (pointing outward). A ring flip interconverts the two chairs. **Bulky substituents prefer the equatorial position**, which avoids steric crowding with the axial hydrogens on the same side. In 1,4-disubstituted cyclohexanes, *trans* places both groups either both equatorial or both axial, while *cis* places one axial and one equatorial.

**Aromaticity.** A ring is **aromatic** if it is planar, cyclic, fully conjugated, and has 4n + 2 π electrons (Hückel's rule: 2, 6, 10, ...). Aromatic compounds are unusually stable, and they undergo substitution rather than addition. Benzene has 6 π electrons. Cyclobutadiene (4 π electrons) is **antiaromatic** and unstable. Several heterocycles are aromatic and important in biology: **pyridine**, **pyrrole**, **imidazole** (in the side chain of histidine), **indole** (in tryptophan), and the **purine** and **pyrimidine** rings of DNA and RNA bases.

**Worked Example 1: Chirality and counting (skill: find the stereocenters)**

(a) Which of these is chiral: 2-propanol, 2-butanol, 3-pentanol? (b) How many stereoisomers does a molecule with three stereocenters and no symmetry have? (c) Tartaric acid has two stereocenters. How many stereoisomers does it have?

(a) 2-Butanol (CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₂CH₃): the carbon bearing OH has four different groups (H, OH, CH₃, C₂H₅), so it is a stereocenter. 2-Propanol has two identical CH₃ groups, and 3-pentanol has two identical ethyl groups, so neither has a stereocenter.

(b) 2³ = 8 stereoisomers.

(c) The maximum would be 2² = 4, but the molecule has a meso form (an internal mirror plane), so there are 3 stereoisomers: the (R,R) and (S,S) enantiomers and the achiral (R,S) meso compound.

**Worked Example 2: Assigning R/S (skill: priorities and orientation)**

For CHFClBr, assign the configuration when the arrangement is: with the H pointing away from you, Br → Cl → F runs clockwise.

Priorities: Br (35) > Cl (17) > F (9) > H. The lowest priority group (H) points away, as required. The path 1 → 2 → 3 (Br → Cl → F) is clockwise, so the configuration is **R**. Its mirror image would be S.

*A common trap.* If the H pointed toward you and you saw clockwise, the true configuration would be S.

**Worked Example 3: Optical rotation (skill: specific rotation and ee)**

(a) A solution of 0.20 g/mL of a pure compound in a 1.0 dm tube rotates plane-polarized light by +2.4°. What is its specific rotation? (b) A different sample is 75 percent of this compound and 25 percent of its enantiomer. What is the enantiomeric excess, and what would the observed rotation be under the same conditions?

(a) [α] = 2.4 / (1.0 × 0.20) = +12°.

(b) ee = 75 − 25 = 50 percent. The observed rotation is 50 percent of the full +2.4°, which is +1.2°. A 50:50 mixture (racemic) would show 0°.

**Worked Example 4: Cyclohexane (skill: axial versus equatorial)**

Which is more stable: cis-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane or trans-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane?

In the trans isomer, both methyl groups can be equatorial at the same time (diequatorial chair). In the cis isomer, one methyl group must be axial and the other equatorial in either chair. An axial methyl suffers steric strain from the axial hydrogens on the same side of the ring, so trans is more stable.

**Worked Example 5: Aromatic or not? (skill: Hückel's rule)**

Classify benzene, cyclobutadiene, cyclohexene and pyrrole.

Benzene: planar, cyclic, conjugated, 6 π electrons (4n + 2 with n = 1): aromatic. Cyclobutadiene: planar, cyclic, conjugated, 4 π electrons (4n with n = 1): antiaromatic. Cyclohexene: has only one double bond; not fully conjugated: not aromatic. Pyrrole: five-membered ring, planar, with two C=C double bonds (4 π electrons) plus a lone pair on nitrogen that sits in a p orbital and joins the π system (6 π electrons total): aromatic.

*Check.* In pyridine, by contrast, the nitrogen's lone pair is in the plane of the ring and is not part of the π system; the ring is aromatic through its six π electrons from three C=C-type bonds.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: To find stereocenters quickly, look for carbons with four different attached groups. A carbon with two identical groups (two H, two CH₃) cannot be a stereocenter. Ring carbons need a check that the two paths around the ring differ.

Praxis Tip: Do not try to rotate molecules in your head. Swap two groups on paper: swapping any two substituents on a stereocenter changes R to S. Two swaps return you to the start.

Praxis Tip: When an answer choice mentions both enantiomers having "different melting points," that is wrong. Enantiomers differ only in how they interact with polarized light and with other chiral objects.

Praxis Tip: Priority is decided by the atoms attached directly to the stereocenter first. If two groups both begin with carbon, go one atom out and compare the sets of attached atoms.

Praxis Tip: A meso compound has stereocenters and a plane of symmetry. If a molecule with two stereocenters looks symmetric when you fold it down the middle, check for meso.

**Common Mistakes**

- Calling a carbon a stereocenter when two of its groups are the same.
- Forgetting to reverse R/S when the lowest-priority group points toward you.
- Assuming that (+) means R and (−) means S.
- Saying a racemic mixture has a rotation of zero because each molecule is achiral (each molecule is chiral; the mixture's rotation cancels).
- Calling enantiomers "different compounds with different properties" when their physical properties are the same.
- Placing the largest substituent in the axial position of a chair.

**Checkpoint Questions**

1. Which of the following compounds is chiral?
   A. 2-Propanol
   B. Ethanol
   C. 2-Butanol
   D. 3-Pentanol

2. Which statement about a pair of enantiomers is correct?
   A. They have different boiling points and rotate light in the same direction
   B. They have the same boiling point but rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions
   C. They have the same boiling point and rotate light in the same direction
   D. They have different boiling points but rotate light in opposite directions

3. A molecule has three stereocenters and no internal symmetry. The maximum number of stereoisomers is
   A. 8
   B. 3
   C. 6
   D. 9

4. A racemic mixture of a chiral compound
   A. rotates light clockwise, because it contains the (+) enantiomer
   B. is a meso compound
   C. contains only achiral molecules
   D. is optically inactive, because the rotations of the two enantiomers cancel

5. In the chair conformation of methylcyclohexane, the most stable arrangement places the methyl group
   A. in an axial position, to maximize its distance from the ring carbons
   B. in an equatorial position, to avoid steric crowding with axial hydrogens
   C. in a position that is eclipsed with the ring
   D. in the same position in either chair, because they are identical

6. Which of the following is aromatic?
   A. Benzene
   B. Cyclobutadiene
   C. Cyclohexene
   D. Cyclooctatetraene (nonplanar)

7. A solution containing 0.30 g/mL of a compound in a 1.0 dm tube rotates plane-polarized light by +3.0°. The specific rotation of the compound is
   A. +0.90°
   B. +3.0°
   C. +100°
   D. +10°

**Stop here.** Answer every question on scratch paper before you read the reasons.

**Answers and reasons**

1. **C.** The carbon bearing the OH in 2-butanol is bonded to H, OH, CH₃ and CH₂CH₃: four different groups, so it is a stereocenter. (A has two identical methyl groups on the carbon bearing the OH; B has no carbon with four different groups; D has two identical ethyl groups on the carbon bearing the OH.)
2. **B.** Enantiomers have identical physical properties in an achiral environment but rotate plane-polarized light by equal amounts in opposite directions. (A is wrong on both counts: enantiomers share boiling points and rotate light oppositely; C is wrong because enantiomers rotate light in opposite directions; D is wrong because enantiomers share their physical properties.)
3. **A.** The maximum number of stereoisomers is 2ⁿ = 2³ = 8. (B counts the stereocenters instead of the combinations; C multiplies 2 × 3; D squares the number of stereocenters.)
4. **D.** A 50:50 mixture of enantiomers shows no net rotation, but each molecule is still chiral. (A ignores the (−) enantiomer; B confuses a mixture of two compounds with a single achiral compound; C each molecule in the mixture is chiral.)
5. **B.** An axial substituent suffers steric interactions with the other axial hydrogens on the same side; the equatorial position avoids them. (A reverses the preference; C describes a higher-energy conformation; D the two chairs are not equivalent for a substituted ring.)
6. **A.** Benzene is planar, cyclic and fully conjugated, with 6 π electrons (4n + 2 with n = 1). (B has 4 π electrons and is antiaromatic; C has a single double bond, so it is not fully conjugated; D is not planar, so it is not aromatic.)
7. **D.** [α] = α_obs / (l × c) = 3.0 / (1.0 × 0.30) = +10°. (A multiplies the concentration by the rotation; B uses the observed rotation as the specific rotation; C misplaces a decimal point, as if the concentration were 0.030 g/mL.)

Praxis Tip: After the checkpoint questions, look at each wrong choice and name the slip (stereocenter missed, R/S reversed, racemic misunderstood, axial placement). Log it in your error log ([Strategy: The Question Method](chapter:4)).
