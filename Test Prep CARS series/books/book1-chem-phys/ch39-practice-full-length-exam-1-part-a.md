# Practice: Full-Length Exam 1, Chemical and Physical Section, Part A

**Timing and conditions**

This is the first half of a full-length practice exam of original questions in the style of the Chemical and Physical Foundations section: 30 questions, 48 minutes, which is about 1.6 minutes per question. Work in one sitting, with a timer, scratch paper and no notes. Do not use a calculator. You may consult a periodic table (the one in the Appendix "Formula, Constant and Fact Sheet") but nothing else from it; on test day the real exam provides a periodic table on screen. Use g = 10 m/s² unless a passage says otherwise. The set has five passages (22 questions) followed by eight independent questions (23 to 30).

An ebook cannot be written in, so record your answer letter for every question on scratch paper or in your notebook, together with a confidence rating: high, medium or low. Check your answers only after Question 30.

Praxis Tip: Start the timer before you read Passage 1, not after you have settled in. Settling in is how six minutes go missing, and nobody has ever found them again.

Praxis Tip: In each passage, spend your first half minute on the givens and the table, and your pencil on the question stems. Reread the passage only for the number you need, not for the experience.

Praxis Tip: When two answer choices differ only by a factor of 2, 4 or 16, estimate the exponent before you do any arithmetic. Most traps in this section are sized by the wrong power.

**Passage 1 (Questions 1-4)**

To study viscous losses over a long uniform length, she then pumps a model fluid at the same volume flow rate through glass tubes, each 5.0 cm long, and measures the pressure drop across each tube.

| Tube radius (mm) | Pressure drop (Pa) |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | 2560 |
| 2.0 | 160 |
| 4.0 | 10 |

   A. higher by about 300 Pa

**Answers and explanations**

1. **D.** The volume flow rate is constant (continuity), so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. The area is proportional to r², so halving the radius quarters the area and multiplies the speed by 4: 0.20 × 4 = 0.80 m/s. [4B · Skill 2] (A scales the speed with the inverse of the radius instead of the area; B scales the speed with the inverse of the fourth power of the radius; C assumes the speed falls where the vessel narrows.)
2. **B.** For a horizontal tube, P₁ − P₂ = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) = 500 × (0.64 − 0.04) = 300 Pa. The faster flow in the narrowing has the lower pressure. [4B · Skill 2] (A reverses the sign: faster flow means lower pressure; C omits the factor of one half in the kinetic energy term; D uses only the kinetic energy of the narrowed stretch and ignores the upstream speed.)
3. **B.** Each doubling of radius lowers the drop by a factor of 16 (2560, 160, 10), so the drop varies as 1/r⁴, as Poiseuille's law predicts at fixed flow rate. Doubling 4.0 mm to 8.0 mm gives 10 / 16 ≈ 0.6 Pa. [4B · Skill 4] (A assumes the drop varies inversely with the radius; C assumes the drop varies inversely with the radius squared; D applies the inverse eighth power of the radius.)
4. **A.** Poiseuille's law gives ΔP = 8ηLQ / (πr⁴). To isolate the radius dependence, flow rate Q, length L and viscosity η must stay fixed, while r is varied and ΔP is measured. [4B · Skill 3] (B holding the speed fixed while the radius changes would change the flow rate; C the pressure drop is the measured outcome and cannot be held fixed; D the radius is the independent variable being changed.)
5. **C.** At 12.5 mL, exactly half the HA has been converted to A⁻, so [A⁻] = [HA] and pH = pKa + log 1 = pKa = 4.75. [5A · Skill 4] (A is the initial pH of the acid solution, not the pKa; B is the pH after one-fifth of the acid has been neutralized; D is the pH at the equivalence point.)
6. **D.** At equivalence all HA has become A⁻, the conjugate base of a weak acid. A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HA + OH⁻ makes the solution basic (here pH ≈ 8.7). [5A · Skill 1] (A the NaOH has been added in exactly stoichiometric amount, with none left over; B HA is a weak acid, and none is left at this point; C Na⁺ is the cation of a strong base and does not hydrolyze.)
7. **A.** An indicator should change color at the pH of the equivalence point. For a weak acid and strong base, that is above 7, so indicator Z (8.2 to 10.0) brackets 8.7. [5A · Skill 3] (B the starting pH is irrelevant to the equivalence-point color change; C the equivalence pH of this weak acid titration is well above 7; D an indicator that changes color outside the steep region gives a visibly wrong end point.)
8. **D.** By the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). Dilution changes both concentrations equally and leaves the ratio at 1, so the pH of a buffer stays near 4.75. [5A · Skill 2] (A assumes dilution raises the pH by one unit, as for a strong acid; B assumes dilution lowers the pH by one unit; C assumes that a lot of water brings the pH to neutral.)
9. **B.** The excess is 5.0 mL × 0.100 M = 0.50 mmol OH⁻ in 55.0 mL, about 0.009 M. pOH ≈ 2.0, so pH ≈ 12.0. [5A · Skill 2] (A takes the concentration of the NaOH solution (0.10 M) without dilution; C reports the pOH of the mixture as if it were the pH; D repeats the equivalence-point pH.)
10. **A.** A steady capacitor carries no current, so the equivalent resistance of R₂ and R₃ is (6 × 12) / 18 = 4.0 Ω. In series with R₁ the total is 8.0 Ω, so I = 12 / 8.0 = 1.5 A. [4C · Skill 2] (B divides the voltage by R₁ alone, ignoring the parallel resistors; C is the current through R₂ alone; D adds all three resistances as if they were in series.)
11. **C.** The capacitor is in parallel with R₂ and R₃, so its voltage is V = IR = 1.5 A × 4.0 Ω = 6.0 V. Q = CV = 50 μF × 6.0 V = 300 μC. [4C · Skill 2] (A uses the full 12 V battery voltage across the capacitor; B uses 9 V, the product of the battery current and R₂; D uses half of the correct potential difference.)
12. **A.** With the battery branch open, the capacitor discharges through R₂ and R₃ in parallel (4.0 Ω). The time constant is τ = RC = 4.0 Ω × 50 μF = 200 μs, the time to fall to 1/e (about 37%). [4C · Skill 2] (B includes R₁, which is no longer in the discharge loop; C uses only R₃, ignoring that R₂ also drains the capacitor; D uses only R₂, ignoring that R₃ also drains the capacitor.)
13. **B.** Ammeters have very low resistance and must be in series with the element; voltmeters have very high resistance and must be placed across the element in parallel. [4C · Skill 3] (A an ammeter across a resistor shorts it, and a series voltmeter blocks the current; C a voltmeter in series passes almost no current; D an ammeter in parallel shorts R₃ and cannot measure its current.)
14. **C.** The two lines have the same y-intercept (0.02 min/μmol, so the same Vmax), but a steeper slope with I (0.12 versus 0.04). That pattern means competitive inhibition: the apparent Km rises while Vmax is unchanged. [5E · Skill 4] (A this type lowers Vmax, which would raise the y-intercept; B this type gives parallel lines, which these are not; D an activator would lower 1/v rather than raise it.)
15. **A.** Without inhibitor, 1/v = 0.04 (1/[S]) + 0.02. The y-intercept is 1/Vmax = 0.02, so Vmax = 50 μmol/min. The slope is Km/Vmax = 0.04, so Km = 0.04 × 50 = 2.0 mM. [5E · Skill 4] (B reads the x-intercept's magnitude (−0.5) as Km, rather than taking its reciprocal; C is the apparent Km in the presence of inhibitor; D confuses Vmax (in μmol/min) with Km.)
16. **C.** A competitive inhibitor competes with substrate for the active site. At very high [S], substrate outcompetes the inhibitor and v approaches the same Vmax. [5E · Skill 1] (A this would hold for a noncompetitive inhibitor, not a competitive one; B no enzyme can exceed the Vmax set by its concentration; D adding substrate does not shut the enzyme off.)
17. **D.** To isolate the effect of the solvent, change only that variable: run the no-inhibitor assays with an equal volume of DMSO alone. If rates match the original no-inhibitor data, DMSO is not responsible. [5E · Skill 3] (A this does not separate the effect of the inhibitor from that of the solvent; B this changes the enzyme concentration, not the solvent; C this measures non-enzymatic background and does not test for a solvent effect.)
18. **A.** q = mcΔT = 100 g × 4.2 J/g·°C × (30.0 − 20.0) °C = 4200 J = 4.2 kJ. [5E · Skill 2] (B uses the final temperature (30 °C) as the temperature change; C omits the temperature change in the calculation; D has a decimal error of a factor of ten.)
19. **D.** The metal's temperature change is 100 − 30 = 70 °C. c = q / (mΔT) = 4200 / (150 × 70) = 0.40 J/g·°C. [5E · Skill 2] (A uses the initial temperature difference of 80 °C for the metal; B uses the mass of the water in place of the mass of the metal; C uses 100 °C, the metal's starting temperature, as its temperature change.)
20. **D.** The metal released heat that went to both the water and the cup. Using only the water's gain underestimates the heat released by the metal, so the computed c is too low. [5E · Skill 3] (A the heat absorbed by the cup is missing from the water's gain, so the metal's energy loss is underestimated, not overestimated; B the metal's heat goes to everything it contacts, so the missing heat matters; C the cup energy is not returned to the water in the calculation.)
21. **B.** At constant volume and moles, P / T is constant: P₂ = 1.0 atm × (360 / 300) = 1.2 atm. Temperature must be in kelvins. [4B · Skill 2] (A inverts the temperature ratio; C converts to Celsius (27 °C to 87 °C) before taking the ratio; D argues that no change occurs because the volume is fixed.)
22. **C.** The readings fall steadily by 0.2 °C per minute. Extending that straight line back one minute, to the time of mixing, gives 30.0 + 0.2 = 30.2 °C, the temperature that would have been seen without heat loss. [5E · Skill 4] (A the last reading includes the most heat loss; B the peak reading has already lost some heat; D averaging includes the heat lost over the whole interval.)
23. **C.** With constant deceleration, the average speed is (20 + 0) / 2 = 10 m/s, so the distance is 10 × 5.0 = 50 m. [4A · Skill 2] (A multiplies the initial speed by the time, ignoring the deceleration; B halves the correct distance; D is the magnitude of the acceleration, not a distance.)
24. **A.** For rotational equilibrium the torques about the pivot must be equal: 30 × 2.0 = 20 × d, so d = 3.0 m. The lighter child must sit farther out. [4A · Skill 2] (B balances the distances and ignores the masses; C inverts the mass ratio; D squares the mass ratio.)
25. **B.** Ionization energy generally rises across a period as the effective nuclear charge rises, with a dip from Mg to Al (the outermost electron is in a higher-energy 3p subshell). Approximate first IEs (kJ/mol): Al 578, Mg 738, Si 786, S 1000. [4E · Skill 1] (A assumes a steady increase across the period, ignoring that the 3p electron of Al is easier to remove than the 3s electron of Mg; C reverses the trend across the period; D swaps S and Si, though sulfur's ionization energy is clearly the highest of the four.)
26. **B.** The mixture contains 1.0 mol N₂ and 1.5 mol H₂. The N₂ could make 2.0 mol NH₃, but the H₂ can make only 1.5 × (2/3) = 1.0 mol. H₂ is limiting, so the yield is 1.0 mol × 17 g/mol = 17 g. [4E · Skill 2] (A assumes N₂ is limiting; C uses a 1:1 mole ratio between H₂ and NH₃; D uses half of the correct amount.)
27. **A.** The molecule has two stereocenters, but the substituents on each are the same, so the (2R,3S) form is a meso compound. The stereoisomers are (2R,3R), (2S,3S) (a pair of enantiomers) and meso, giving three. [5B · Skill 1] (B applies 2ⁿ for two stereocenters and ignores the meso form; C counts only the enantiomeric pair; D treats all forms as identical.)
28. **B.** At pH 12 every pKa except the carboxyl's is below the pH, so both amino groups have lost their protons and are neutral. Only the carboxylate (−1) carries charge. [5D · Skill 2] (A is the charge of a zwitterion, which would exist near pH 9.8; C is the charge at about pH 7; D counts a second negative charge that has no acidic group to supply it.)
29. **D.** Ethanol has an O–H group that can donate a hydrogen bond to the oxygen of another molecule. Dimethyl ether has no O–H and cannot hydrogen bond to itself, so its intermolecular forces are weaker and its boiling point is much lower. [5B · Skill 1] (A the two molecules are isomers with identical molar mass; B dimethyl ether is polar (bent C–O–C), and all molecules have dispersion forces; C ethanol is covalent throughout.)
30. **C.** By the work-energy theorem, ½mv² = μmg d, so d = v² / (2μg) = 36 / (2 × 0.30 × 10) = 6.0 m. The mass cancels. [4A · Skill 2] (A omits the one half in the kinetic energy; B ignores the friction coefficient; D applies the factor of two twice.)
