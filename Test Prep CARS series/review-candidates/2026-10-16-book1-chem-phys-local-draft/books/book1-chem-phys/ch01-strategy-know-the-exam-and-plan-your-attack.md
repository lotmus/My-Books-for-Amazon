# Strategy: Know the Exam and Plan Your Attack

**What you will be able to do**

- State the format, timing and scoring of the Chemical and Physical Foundations section from the exam owner's own published facts.
- Read the section's content outline as a map, and see where each lesson in this book sits on it.
- Turn the section's time limit into a pacing plan.
- Know what math the section expects, and what it does not.

**Why it matters**

Every year, someone prepares for this section by memorizing a friend's account of what was on their test. The friend's account is faithful, vivid, and wrong about at least one thing that matters. The exam owner publishes the facts for free, and this chapter fills in the fact sheet from [Core Test Skills, Chapter 2] using that source. It is the one hour in your preparation that is certain to be correctly spent.

Praxis Tip: Do this chapter with the official pages open in another tab. Where this book and the official pages disagree, the official pages win. They are more current than any book, including this one.

**A note on using this ebook.** You cannot write in or mark an ebook, so keep a notebook (paper or digital) beside you. Use it for answer sheets, confidence notes, your error log, your flashcard ideas and your study plan. Every practice set tells you to write answers on scratch paper and to check the explanations only afterward; the answers and explanations start on a separate page so that you do not see them by accident. The tables in this book appear as pictures; copy the ones you want into your notes.

This book is independent. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by the owner of the exam, and it does not contain any actual exam questions.

**The facts of the section**

The following facts were checked against the exam owner's published pages when this chapter was written. Formats change, so re-check them before you rely on them (the links to look for are "What's on the MCAT Exam?" and the Chemical and Physical Foundations section guide on the exam owner's student site).

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| Section | Chemical and Physical Foundations of Biological Systems |
| Position | First of four sections, followed by an optional 10-minute break |
| Questions | 59 |
| Time | 95 minutes |
| Structure | 10 passage-based sets of 4 to 6 questions each, plus 15 independent (stand-alone) questions |
| Section score scale | 118 to 132; the four sections add to a total of 472 to 528 |
| Wrong answers | No extra penalty; a wrong answer scores the same as a blank |
| Unscored items | Some questions are field-test items that do not count toward your score |
| Provided resource | A periodic table is available on screen during the section |
| Calculus | Not required |
| Calculator | No calculator is provided. The test center gives you a noteboard booklet and a marker for calculations. |

Two consequences follow at once. First, since a wrong answer costs nothing extra, you never leave a question blank (see [Core Test Skills, Chapter 12]). Second, a score of 125 on this section is a statement about your performance on this section, not a prediction of anything else; the total is the sum of four.

Praxis Tip: Write "never leave blank" at the top of your scratch page when you begin each section. It is a rule too obvious to forget, and people forget it.

**Counting the questions**

Ten passage sets of 4 to 6 questions plus 15 independent questions makes 59. If the 15 independent questions are fixed, the passage sets must contain 59 − 15 = 44 questions, an average of 4.4 per set. That tells you something useful: the typical passage set is small, and its passage is probably the longer part of the work.

**How the content is organized**

The section is built on two "foundational concepts," each with five content categories.

*Foundational Concept 4 (about 40 percent of the questions).* Living organisms transport materials, sense their environment, process signals and respond, using processes that physics describes. The five categories:

- 4A: translational motion, forces, work, energy and equilibrium in living systems
- 4B: fluids for circulation of blood, gas movement and gas exchange
- 4C: electrochemistry, electrical circuits and their elements
- 4D: how light and sound interact with matter
- 4E: atoms, nuclear decay, electronic structure and atomic chemical behavior

*Foundational Concept 5 (about 60 percent).* The principles governing chemical interactions and reactions underlie the molecular behavior of living systems. The five categories:

- 5A: the unique nature of water and its solutions
- 5B: nature of molecules and intermolecular interactions
- 5C: separation and purification methods
- 5D: structure, function and reactivity of biologically relevant molecules
- 5E: principles of chemical thermodynamics and kinetics

The exam owner also gives an approximate split by discipline: general chemistry about 30 percent, first-semester biochemistry about 25 percent, introductory physics about 25 percent, organic chemistry about 15 percent, and introductory biology about 5 percent. Percentages are approximate, rounded to the nearest 5, and vary from test to test.

**Worked Example 1: From percentages to question counts**

Convert the discipline split into rough question counts for a 59-question section.

| Discipline | Share | Questions (59 × share) | Rounded |
|---|---|---|---|
| General chemistry | 30% | 17.7 | about 18 |
| Biochemistry | 25% | 14.75 | about 15 |
| Physics | 25% | 14.75 | about 15 |
| Organic chemistry | 15% | 8.85 | about 9 |
| Biology | 5% | 2.95 | about 3 |

The rounded numbers add to 60 rather than 59, which is fine: the shares are approximate, and the point is the proportions. Physics and biochemistry are each roughly a quarter. General chemistry is the single largest block. Organic chemistry, which students tend to dread, is under one-sixth.

*Reading the table.* If your hours are proportional to the shares, general chemistry earns about twice the time of organic chemistry. That does not mean you split time exactly by share; a weak area deserves extra. But it stops you from spending a third of your calendar on the smallest block because it is the most irritating.

*Skill used:* converting a published percentage into a study-time and attention budget.

Praxis Tip: Copy the table above onto a card and keep it where you study. When you catch yourself spending an evening on a topic that is 3 percent of the section, a table on the wall is a polite way to stop.

**The four reasoning skills**

Every question is written at the intersection of a content category and a skill. The skills, with approximate shares of the section:

| Skill | Approximate share | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Knowledge of scientific concepts and principles | 35% | Know the concept and how it relates to its neighbors |
| 2. Scientific reasoning and problem-solving | 45% | Apply principles, theories and models; evaluate explanations and predictions |
| 3. Reasoning about the design and execution of research | 10% | Understand components of research; ethical issues |
| 4. Data-based and statistical reasoning | 10% | Interpret patterns in tables, figures and graphs; draw conclusions |

For a 59-question section: 35 percent is about 21 questions, 45 percent about 27, and 10 percent about 6 each (total about 60). Skills 2, 3 and 4 together are about 65 percent, so roughly two out of every three questions ask you to *apply* or interpret something, not just recall it. This is why flashcards alone will take you only partway, and why the practice sets in this book come with long explanations.

Praxis Tip: When you review a missed question, tag it with its skill as well as its topic. If all your misses are skill 2, you need more reasoning practice; if they are skill 1, you need more content review.

**The math the section expects**

The exam owner lists the general mathematics that the science sections draw on. In summary:

- Reading and interpreting linear, semilog and log-log scales; calculating slopes from graphs, figures and tables.
- Significant digits, and reasonable numerical estimates in calculations.
- Metric units (conversions within the metric system and between metric and English units, with conversion factors given when needed) and dimensional analysis.
- Arithmetic with probability, proportion, ratio, percentage and square-root estimation.
- Algebra II-level understanding of exponentials and logarithms (natural and base 10), scientific notation and simultaneous equations.
- Basic trigonometry: sine, cosine and tangent and their inverses; sine and cosine of 0°, 90° and 180°; side relationships in right triangles with 30°, 45° and 60° angles.
- Vector addition and subtraction, and the right-hand rule. (Dot and cross products are not required.)
- Calculus is not required.

The math is modest in its level and relentless in its frequency. [Strategy: The Quantitative Toolkit](chapter:5) turns this list into habits.

Praxis Tip: The math list tells you what you may safely skip as well as what you must know. Do not spend a week on integrals. Do spend a week on logs and powers of ten.

**Worked Example 2: A pacing plan**

Use the official numbers: 59 questions, 95 minutes. Reserve 5 minutes at the end for flagged questions.

- Working time: 95 − 5 = 90 minutes.
- Time per question: 90 ÷ 59 ≈ 1.525 minutes, about 1 minute 31 seconds.
- Checkpoints (elapsed minutes at the end of question n): n = 15 → 22.9; n = 30 → 45.8; n = 45 → 68.6; n = 59 → 90.

([Strategy: Test-Day Plan, Pacing and Stress](chapter:38) uses the same plan: 90 working minutes and a 5-minute reserve. Without a reserve the checkpoints would be 24, 48, 72 and 95.)

(Check: 1.5254 × 15 = 22.88; × 30 = 45.76; × 45 = 68.64; × 59 = 90.)

A passage set has a *reading cost* before the first question, so a set with five questions and a dense passage will use more than 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 minutes. Independent questions often take less. The checkpoints are an average, not a quota. If you are 3 minutes behind at question 30, you are not in trouble: you have the 5-minute buffer and the rule to flag and move on ([Core Test Skills, Chapter 11]).

*Skill used:* converting the published time limit into a few clock times you can check mid-section.

Praxis Tip: Write the four checkpoint times on your scratch page at the start of the section: 23, 46, 69, 90. It takes fifteen seconds, and it replaces constant clock-watching with four glances.

**More Praxis Tips**

Praxis Tip: Read the official content outline for each lesson as you reach it. It is a topic checklist, written by the people who write the exam, and it is free.

Praxis Tip: Familiarize yourself with the exam's on-screen tools (highlighting, strikethrough, flagging) by using the exam owner's free tutorial and practice tools, and practice with them before test day. Fumbling with the interface costs time you cannot spare.

Praxis Tip: The section comes first. You walk in rested and nervous, which is the best and worst combination for the longest passage you will see all day. Practice starting a timed section cold, without a warm-up.

Praxis Tip: Do not use this section's score as a prediction of your other three. Chemistry and physics proficiency and reading proficiency are separate skills.

**Common mistakes**

- Studying from a friend's account of the test instead of the official outline.
- Spending time in proportion to interest instead of to the section's weights.
- Treating physics as optional because it feels less relevant. A quarter of the section is physics.
- Memorizing facts and ignoring that about two out of three questions ask you to apply or interpret them.
- Leaving questions blank.

**Recap**

Fifty-nine questions in 95 minutes, 10 passage sets plus 15 independent questions, scored 118 to 132, no penalty for wrong answers, a periodic table provided. Two foundational concepts, ten categories, four skills, and an approximate split among general chemistry, biochemistry, physics, organic chemistry and biology. Plan your time in proportion, and check the facts at the source.
