# Appendix: Formula, Constant and Fact Sheet

**What this appendix is for**

This is the one-stop reference for the whole book. Every formula, constant and convention here is stated the way the lessons state it, and each group of formulas points back to the lesson that teaches it. Use it to check your memory, to rebuild your own one-page summary, and to settle arguments with yourself at 11 p.m.

**How to use this sheet**

This sheet supports review. It is not a crutch for the exam. The real exam supplies an on-screen periodic table, and, as far as this book's reading of the exam owner's public pages goes, it does not supply a formula sheet. Plan as if every formula must come out of your own head, and treat the exam owner's page as the final word on what is provided.

Here is a routine that works.

- **Cover and recall.** Pick one section, cover it, and write the formulas from memory on scratch paper. Then uncover and mark every error. The errors are your study list.
- **Say what each symbol is and what its unit is.** A formula you cannot read aloud is a formula you will misuse.
- **Know the scaling, not just the formula.** Most exam questions ask what happens to one quantity when another changes. The lessons keep returning to ratios for this reason.
- **Follow the lessons on conventions.** Where a textbook elsewhere may differ (for example, the sign convention for work in the first law), the lessons rule, and this sheet follows them.
- **Do not memorize the tables cold.** Memorize the trends and the reasons. The tables are there to confirm.

Praxis Tip: Reading this sheet is not studying, in the same way that owning running shoes is not exercise. Cover it, write from memory, check. The uncomfortable part is the part that works.

Praxis Tip: Everything here has a home lesson. If a formula looks unfamiliar, go to the lesson, not to the internet. The lesson was written to match this book's conventions; the internet was written to match everyone's.

**Conventions used throughout**

- Temperature in gas laws, thermodynamics and kinetics is in kelvin: K = °C + 273 (273.15 if you are being formal).
- Use g = 9.8 m/s², or 10 m/s² for estimates. Many worked examples in the lessons use 10 to keep arithmetic friendly.
- Logarithms are base 10 unless written ln. Useful values: log 2 ≈ 0.30, log 3 ≈ 0.48, log 5 ≈ 0.70, ln 2 ≈ 0.693.
- Positive work is done by a force along the motion. The first law is written ΔU = Q − W, with W the work done by the system. Chemistry texts often write ΔU = q + w with w the work done on the system. The two agree physically; check which is meant.

**Kinematics ([Lesson 1](chapter:7))**

For constant acceleration (each equation omits one of the five quantities Δx, v₀, v, a, t):

    v = v₀ + a t          (no Δx)
    Δx = v₀ t + ½ a t²    (no v)
    v² = v₀² + 2 a Δx     (no t)
    Δx = ½ (v₀ + v) t     (no a)

Definitions: average velocity v_avg = Δx / Δt, and acceleration a = Δv / Δt. On a position-time graph the slope is velocity. On a velocity-time graph the slope is acceleration and the area is displacement. Free fall near Earth has a = g downward, including at the top of a toss, where the velocity is momentarily zero but the acceleration is not.

Projectile launched at speed v and angle θ above level ground:

    v_x = v cos θ,  v_y = v sin θ
    time of flight = 2 v_y / g
    maximum height = v_y² / (2g)
    range = v² sin(2θ) / g

The range formula holds when launch and landing heights are equal; it is maximum at 45°, and complementary angles (30° and 60°) give the same range. Horizontal and vertical motions share only time. For relative velocity, add velocities as vectors; perpendicular components combine with the Pythagorean theorem.

Praxis Tip: List the five kinematic quantities on scratch paper and cross off the one you neither know nor want. That crossed-off letter picks the equation. It is the cheapest five seconds on the whole section.

**Forces, equilibrium and torque ([Lesson 2](chapter:8))**

    F_net = m a
    W = m g
    f_s ≤ μ_s N
    f_k = μ_k N

Newton's second law is applied along each axis separately. Weight is W = m g. The normal force N comes from the perpendicular-axis equation, not from habit. Static friction is only "up to" μ_s N.

Inclines (angle θ above horizontal):

    N = m g cos θ
    a = g sin θ                (frictionless)
    a = g (sin θ − μ_k cos θ)  (kinetic friction)
    tan θ_crit = μ_s           (onset of slipping)

Circular motion and gravitation:

    a_c = v² / r = ω² r
    F_c = m v² / r
    v = ω r
    F = G m₁ m₂ / r²

The centripetal force is the name for the real forces that point toward the center, not an extra force. Gravity is an inverse-square law: double the distance and the force falls to one quarter. Torque, equilibrium and the center of mass:

    τ = r F sin θ
    ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0   (static equilibrium)
    x_cm = (m₁x₁ + m₂x₂ + ...) / (m₁ + m₂ + ...)

The lever arm is r sin θ, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force. Choose the pivot at an unknown force to remove it from the torque equation. Two masses over an ideal pulley accelerate at a = (m₂ − m₁) g / (m₁ + m₂), and the tension lies between the two weights. At terminal velocity, drag equals the driving force.

Praxis Tip: When the speed doubles in a circle, the force quadruples. When the distance doubles in an inverse-square law, the force falls to a quarter. Both involve a square, and neither is "doubles." Say it with confidence and move on.

**Work, energy and power ([Lesson 3](chapter:9))**

    W = F d cos θ
    KE = ½ m v²
    W_net = ΔKE
    PE = m g h             (gravity, near Earth)
    PE = ½ k x²            (spring)
    KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f
    KE_i + PE_i + W_nc = KE_f + PE_f
    P = W / t = F v
    MA = F_out / F_in = d_in / d_out

Work is a scalar. W_nc is negative for friction, and the lost mechanical energy appears as heat. An ideal machine conserves work, trading force for distance. Optional extra, whose place in the exam outline is flagged in Lesson 3: momentum p = m v and impulse F Δt = Δp. Muscles are commonly described as about 20 to 25 percent efficient.

**Fluids and circulation ([Lesson 4](chapter:11))**

    ρ = m / V
    P = F / A
    P = P₀ + ρ g h             (absolute pressure)
    F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂          (Pascal's principle)
    F_b = ρ_fluid V_displaced g
    A₁ v₁ = A₂ v₂              (continuity; Q = A v)
    P + ½ ρ v² + ρ g h = constant   (Bernoulli)
    Q = π ΔP r⁴ / (8 η L)      (Poiseuille)

Specific gravity is the density divided by the density of water. The term ρ g h is the gauge pressure at depth h. A floating object has F_b equal to its weight, and the submerged fraction is ρ_object / ρ_fluid. The apparent weight of a submerged object is its true weight minus F_b. Halving the radius quarters the area (continuity: speed ×4) but cuts Poiseuille flow by a factor of 16. Every 10 m of water adds about 1 atm.

**Electrostatics, circuits and magnetism ([Lesson 5](chapter:12))**

    F = k q₁ q₂ / r²
    E = F / q,   E = k Q / r²,   E = V / d
    W = q ΔV,   V = k Q / r,   U = k q₁ q₂ / r
    I = ΔQ / Δt
    V = I R
    R = ρ L / A
    P = I V = I² R = V² / R

Resistors and capacitors have opposite combination rules:

    resistors in series:      R = R₁ + R₂ + ...
    resistors in parallel:    1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...
    capacitors in parallel:   C = C₁ + C₂ + ...
    capacitors in series:     1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + ...

Two resistors in parallel: R = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂), always smaller than the smaller one. For a capacitor:

    C = Q / V
    U = ½ C V² = ½ Q V = Q² / (2C)

Magnetism:

    F = q v B sin θ
    r = m v / (q B)
    F = I L B sin θ

A magnetic force does no work and never changes speed. Kirchhoff's rules: current in equals current out at a junction, and voltage changes sum to zero around a loop. An ammeter goes in series (low resistance); a voltmeter goes in parallel (high resistance).

Praxis Tip: Resistors add in series. Capacitors add in parallel. The other rule in each case is the reciprocal one. If you mix them up, you are in good company and also wrong.

**Periodic motion, waves and sound ([Lesson 6](chapter:13))**

    f = 1 / T
    ω = 2π f = 2π / T
    F = −k x                (simple harmonic motion)
    T = 2π √(m / k)         (mass on a spring)
    T = 2π √(L / g)         (simple pendulum, small angles)
    v_max = A ω
    v = f λ

The period does not depend on amplitude (SHM) or on the mass of a pendulum. The frequency is set by the source, the speed by the medium, and the wavelength is what is left.

    I = P / A
    β = 10 log₁₀ (I / I₀),  I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m²
    f_o = f_s (v ± v_o) / (v ∓ v_s)

Decibel rules: +10 dB multiplies intensity by 10; +3 dB roughly doubles it; doubling the distance from a point source is about −6 dB. Doppler signs: upper signs when source and observer approach (pitch rises). Resonance:

    string fixed at both ends:  f_n = n v / (2L),  n = 1, 2, 3, ...
    pipe open at both ends:     f_n = n v / (2L),  n = 1, 2, 3, ...
    pipe closed at one end:     f_n = n v / (4L),  n = 1, 3, 5, ...

Audible sound runs from roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; ultrasound is above that. Higher-frequency ultrasound is attenuated more, penetrating less but imaging more finely.

**Light, electromagnetic radiation and optics ([Lesson 7](chapter:14))**

    c = f λ
    E = h f = h c / λ
    E (eV) ≈ 1,240 / λ (nm)
    n = c / v,   λ = λ₀ / n
    n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂           (Snell's law)
    sin θ_c = n₂ / n₁   (n₁ > n₂)   (critical angle)

Spectrum from low to high frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray. Visible light spans roughly 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). At a boundary the frequency is unchanged; speed and wavelength shrink by the factor n. Light entering a higher-n medium bends toward the normal.

Mirrors and thin lenses:

    1/p + 1/q = 1/f
    m = −q / p
    f = R / 2            (spherical mirror)
    P (diopters) = 1 / f (f in meters)

Sign conventions: p is positive for a real object; q is positive for a real image and negative for a virtual one; f is positive for a concave mirror or converging lens and negative for a convex mirror or diverging lens; negative m means inverted. Nearsightedness is corrected with a diverging lens (negative power), farsightedness with a converging lens (positive power). Wave optics:

    d sin θ = m λ        (double slit bright fringes, gratings)
    Δy = λ L / d         (fringe spacing)
    a sin θ = m λ        (single-slit dark fringes, m = 1, 2, ...)
    I = I₀ cos² θ        (Malus's law)

Unpolarized light loses half its intensity at the first ideal polarizer.

Praxis Tip: For any "which stays constant when light or sound enters a new medium" question, the answer is frequency. This is true often enough that you should be suspicious only of answer choices that disagree.

**The nucleus and radioactive decay ([Lesson 8](chapter:15))**

Conserve both the top number (A) and the bottom number (Z) in every nuclear equation.

    alpha:       Z − 2,  A − 4
    beta-minus:  Z + 1,  A unchanged
    beta-plus:   Z − 1,  A unchanged
    electron capture:  Z − 1,  A unchanged
    gamma:       no change in Z or A

    N = N₀ (1/2)^(t / T½)
    λ = ln 2 / T½ ≈ 0.693 / T½
    activity = λ N      (1 Bq = 1 decay per second)
    E = Δm c²           (1 u = 931.5 MeV)

After n half-lives the fraction remaining is (1/2)ⁿ. Activity decays with the same half-life as the number of nuclei. Binding energy per nucleon is largest near iron; fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light ones both release energy. Penetration: alpha is stopped by paper, beta by thin metal, gamma needs dense shielding such as lead. In a mass spectrometer, r = m v / (q B), and at equal accelerating voltage r is proportional to √m. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years, and technetium-99m about 6 hours.

**Electronic structure and periodic trends ([Lesson 9](chapter:17))**

    E_n = −13.6 eV / n²
    ΔE = E_f − E_i = 13.6 eV × (1/n_i² − 1/n_f²)
    KE_max = h f − φ,   threshold f₀ = φ / h

Falling to a lower n emits a photon; rising to a higher n requires absorbing one. Intensity changes the number of ejected electrons, not their energy. Quantum numbers:

    n = 1, 2, 3, ...        l = 0 to n − 1
    mₗ = −l to +l           mₛ = +½ or −½

Subshells hold s: 2, p: 6, d: 10, f: 14 electrons; a shell holds 2n². Filling order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s. Cations lose outermost-shell electrons first (4s before 3d). Cr is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ and Cu is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰. Paramagnetic means one or more unpaired electrons; diamagnetic means all paired.

| Property | Across a period | Down a group |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
| Ionization energy | Increases | Decreases |
| Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases |
| Metallic character | Decreases | Increases |

Exceptions to remember: first ionization energy is lower for B than Be and lower for O than N. A cation is smaller than its atom; an anion is larger. Among isoelectronic species, more protons means smaller.

**Stoichiometry and reactions ([Lesson 10](chapter:18))**

    moles = mass / molar mass
    number of particles = moles × N_A
    percent yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Limiting reactant: divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient; the smallest quotient limits. Empirical formula from percent composition: assume 100 g, convert to moles, divide by the smallest, and scale to whole numbers. Oxidation numbers: elements are 0; monatomic ions equal their charge; group 1 is +1, group 2 is +2, fluorine is −1; oxygen is usually −2 (peroxides −1); hydrogen is usually +1 (metal hydrides −1); the sum equals the overall charge. Oxidation is loss of electrons (oxidation number rises) and the species oxidized is the reducing agent. Redox half-reaction method in acid: balance atoms other than O and H, balance O with H₂O, balance H with H⁺, balance charge with electrons, then scale so the electrons cancel.

**Bonding and shape ([Lesson 11](chapter:19))**

    formal charge = valence e⁻ − nonbonding e⁻ − ½ (bonding e⁻)

Intermolecular forces in rising strength for molecules of similar size: dispersion, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding, ion-dipole. Stronger forces mean higher boiling and melting points, higher viscosity and lower vapor pressure. A π bond exists only in addition to a σ bond: double is 1σ + 1π, triple is 1σ + 2π. More bonds between two atoms means a shorter and stronger bond. The shapes are in the reference tables below.

**The gas phase ([Lesson 12](chapter:20))**

    P V = n R T
    P₁V₁ / T₁ = P₂V₂ / T₂       (fixed n)
    P_total = P₁ + P₂ + ...,   P_i = X_i P_total
    KE_avg = (3/2) k_B T
    v_rms = √(3 R T / M)
    rate₁ / rate₂ = √(M₂ / M₁)  (Graham's law)
    (P + a n²/V²)(V − n b) = n R T   (van der Waals)

Special cases: Boyle (P V constant, at fixed T and n), Charles (V / T constant, at fixed P and n), Gay-Lussac (P / T constant, at fixed V and n), Avogadro (V / n constant, at fixed P and T). All ideal gases have the same average kinetic energy at the same temperature; lighter molecules are faster. Real gases deviate most at high pressure and low temperature. Respiration: air is about 21 percent O₂, so P_O₂ ≈ 0.21 × 760 ≈ 160 mmHg at sea level, and the alveolar value is around 100 mmHg.

**Solutions, solubility and water ([Lesson 13](chapter:21))**

    molarity M = moles solute / liters solution
    molality m = moles solute / kg solvent
    mass percent = (mass solute / mass solution) × 100
    M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
    Ksp = [Aʸ⁺]ˣ [Bˣ⁻]ʸ      for AₓBᵧ(s)

For a 1:1 salt, Ksp = s² and s = √Ksp; for MX₂, Ksp = 4s³. Compare Q with Ksp: Q < Ksp unsaturated; Q = Ksp saturated; Q > Ksp precipitate forms. Remember to account for the dilution on mixing. A common ion lowers solubility; complex-ion formation raises it; salts of basic anions dissolve better at lower pH.

Colligative properties (count particles):

    P_solution = X_solvent P°_solvent
    ΔT_b = i K_b m
    ΔT_f = i K_f m
    Π = i M R T

The van't Hoff factor i is ideally 1 for glucose, 2 for NaCl and 3 for CaCl₂. Osmosis moves water toward the higher solute concentration. Hypotonic: cell swells. Isotonic: no net movement. Hypertonic: cell shrinks. Physiological saline is about 0.9 percent NaCl, roughly 0.15 M. Gas solubility in liquids falls as temperature rises (Henry's law: solubility is proportional to partial pressure).

**Thermochemistry and thermodynamics ([Lesson 14](chapter:23))**

    ΔU = Q − W
    q = m c ΔT
    q = m L                      (phase change; temperature constant)
    ΔH°_rxn = Σ ΔH_f°(products) − Σ ΔH_f°(reactants)
    ΔH ≈ Σ (bonds broken) − Σ (bonds formed)
    ΔG = ΔH − T ΔS
    ΔG° = −R T ln K_eq
    ΔG = ΔG° + R T ln Q
    ΔG° = −n F E°_cell
    W = P ΔV                     (constant pressure)
    ΔL = α L₀ ΔT

H, S, G and U are state functions; q and w are not. Break is positive, make is negative. Spontaneity at constant T and P:

| ΔH | ΔS | Spontaneous? |
|---|---|---|
| − | + | Always |
| + | − | Never |
| − | − | At low temperature |
| + | + | At high temperature |

The crossover temperature is T = ΔH / ΔS. A negative ΔG° means K > 1. Every −5.7 kJ/mol of ΔG° at 298 K is a factor of 10 in K. On a PV diagram the work is the area under the curve; isochoric means no work, adiabatic means Q = 0. The hydrolysis of ATP has ΔG°′ of about −30 kJ/mol (Lesson 24 uses −30.5 in a worked example). Spontaneous says nothing about speed.

Praxis Tip: Thermodynamics tells you whether a reaction can go, kinetics how fast, and equilibrium where it ends up. A wrong answer is usually a correct answer to a neighboring question.

**Kinetics and equilibrium ([Lesson 15](chapter:24))**

    rate = k [A]^m [B]^n         (orders found by experiment)
    k = A e^(−E_a / R T)         (Arrhenius)
    ln (k₂ / k₁) = (E_a / R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
    ΔH = E_a(forward) − E_a(reverse)

Integrated forms and straight-line plots:

    zero order:    [A] vs t is a line
    first order:   ln [A] vs t, slope −k;  t½ = 0.693 / k
    second order:  1/[A] vs t, slope k

Method of initial rates: (factor change in rate) = (factor change in concentration)^order. The slowest elementary step sets the rate law, and an elementary step's orders equal its molecularity. A catalyst lowers E_a for both directions and changes neither K, ΔG nor ΔH. Equilibrium:

    K_c = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b)

Pure solids and liquids are omitted. Q < K goes forward; Q > K goes in reverse. Only temperature changes K.

| Stress | Shift |
|---|---|
| Add reactant or remove product | Toward products |
| Add product or remove reactant | Toward reactants |
| Increase pressure | Side with fewer moles of gas |
| Increase temperature | Endothermic direction |
| Add a catalyst or inert gas (constant V) | No shift |

**Acids, bases, buffers and titration ([Lesson 16](chapter:25))**

    Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (25 °C)
    pH = −log [H⁺],  pOH = −log [OH⁻]
    pH + pOH = 14
    Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA],  pKa = −log Ka
    Ka × Kb = Kw,  pKa + pKb = 14
    [H⁺] ≈ √(Ka C)          (valid if x < 5% of C)
    pH = pKa + log ([A⁻] / [HA])   (Henderson-Hasselbalch)

A smaller pKa means a stronger acid. At [A⁻] = [HA], pH = pKa. A buffer works within about ±1 pH unit of its pKa; its capacity grows with total concentration. After adding strong acid or base, do the stoichiometry in moles first, then use the new ratio. A concentration written a × 10⁻ⁿ has pH = n − log a.

| Titration | pH at equivalence | Half-equivalence |
|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | 7 | Not applicable |
| Weak acid + strong base | Above 7 | pH = pKa |
| Weak base + strong acid | Below 7 | pOH = pKb |

Salts: strong acid plus strong base is neutral; weak acid plus strong base is basic; strong acid plus weak base is acidic. Blood buffer: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻, effective pKa about 6.1, so at pH 7.4 the ratio [HCO₃⁻] / [CO₂ + H₂CO₃] = 10^1.3 ≈ 20. Acidosis is blood pH below about 7.35; alkalosis is above about 7.45.

**Electrochemistry ([Lesson 17](chapter:26))**

    E°_cell = E°_cathode − E°_anode
    ΔG° = −n F E°_cell
    E°_cell = (0.0592 / n) log K     (25 °C)
    E = E° − (0.0592 / n) log Q      (25 °C)
    charge Q = I t
    moles of electrons = I t / F
    E_ion ≈ (61.5 mV / z) log ([ion]_out / [ion]_in)   (37 °C)

Oxidation is at the anode and reduction at the cathode, in every type of cell. In a galvanic cell E°_cell is positive, electrons flow anode to cathode through the wire, and the anode is negative. In an electrolytic cell the reaction is non-spontaneous, the anode is positive and the cathode is negative. Do not multiply E° by coefficients. For a concentration cell, E° = 0, and reduction occurs in the more concentrated half-cell. A dead battery is at equilibrium: E = 0 and Q = K.

Praxis Tip: An Ox, Red Cat. Anode is oxidation, reduction is cathode. The signs on the electrodes are the part that changes between cell types; the processes never do.

**Spectroscopy and structure determination ([Lesson 21](chapter:31))**

    DoU = (2x + 2 + z − y) / 2      for CₓHᵧNzOw (halogens counted as H)
    A = ε b c                       (Beer-Lambert)
    n + 1 rule: n equivalent neighbor H gives n + 1 lines

Which region does what: radio waves for NMR, infrared for bond vibrations, visible and ultraviolet for electronic transitions. Longer conjugation means longer λ_max. The color seen is the complement of the color absorbed (blue light absorbed near 450 nm looks yellow-orange). In mass spectrometry the M⁺ peak gives the molecular mass; one chlorine gives M and M + 2 in a 3 : 1 ratio, one bromine in a 1 : 1 ratio. Higher wavenumber means higher energy and shorter wavelength.

**Stereochemistry, separations and organic quick facts (Lessons [18](chapter:28) to [22](chapter:32))**

    specific rotation [α] = α_obs / (l × c)    (l in dm, c in g/mL)
    stereoisomers ≤ 2ⁿ    (n stereocenters)
    aromatic if planar, cyclic, conjugated, 4n + 2 π electrons
    partition coefficient K = [solute]_org / [solute]_aq
    fraction left in water after one extraction = V_aq / (V_aq + K V_org)
    Rf = distance spot traveled / distance solvent front traveled

A racemic mixture shows no rotation; observed rotation equals the enantiomeric excess times the pure specific rotation. Swapping any two groups on a stereocenter flips R and S. Bulky groups prefer the equatorial position of a chair. SN2 inverts and has rate = k[RX][Nu]; SN1 racemizes and has rate = k[RX]. Oxidation: primary alcohol gives aldehyde (mild) or acid (strong); secondary gives ketone; tertiary does not react. Carboxylic acid derivative reactivity: acid chloride > anhydride > ester ≈ acid > amide. LiAlH₄ reduces acids and esters to primary alcohols and amides to amines; NaBH₄ reduces aldehydes and ketones and not, under ordinary conditions, esters, acids or amides. Saponification gives a carboxylate and an alcohol, irreversibly. Many small extractions beat one large one. On silica, polar compounds have low Rf; in size-exclusion chromatography large molecules elute first; in SDS-PAGE small proteins travel farthest.

**Amino acids, enzymes and biomolecules (Lessons [23](chapter:34) to [25](chapter:36))**

Amino acids and proteins:

    pI = (pKa₁ + pKa₂) / 2
    (average the two pKa values on either side of the neutral form)

For an acidic side chain average the two acidic pKa values; for a basic side chain, the two basic values. If pH < pI the molecule is positive; if pH > pI it is negative. A tripeptide has two peptide bonds and two waters released. The α-helix has 3.6 residues per turn, with each backbone C=O hydrogen-bonded to the N–H four residues later. Secondary structure uses backbone hydrogen bonds; tertiary uses side chains (hydrophobic effect, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, disulfides). Denaturation breaks folding but not peptide bonds.

Enzyme kinetics ([Lesson 24](chapter:35)):

    v = Vmax [S] / (Km + [S])
    Vmax = k_cat [E]_total
    1/v = (Km / Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax
    catalytic efficiency = k_cat / Km

Km is the substrate concentration at which v = ½ Vmax. On a Lineweaver-Burk plot, the y-intercept is 1/Vmax, the x-intercept is −1/Km and the slope is Km/Vmax. When [S] ≪ Km the rate is first order in S; when [S] ≫ Km it approaches Vmax (zero order).

| Inhibitor | Binds | Vmax | Km |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive | Active site | Unchanged | Increases |
| Noncompetitive | Another site (E and ES) | Decreases | Unchanged |
| Uncompetitive | ES complex only | Decreases | Decreases |
| Mixed | Another site | Decreases | Increases or decreases |

On a Lineweaver-Burk plot, competitive lines meet on the y-axis, noncompetitive lines meet on the x-axis, and uncompetitive lines are parallel. Only competitive inhibition is overcome by more substrate. A sigmoidal (S-shaped) rate curve signals cooperativity or allosteric behavior.

Carbohydrates, lipids and nucleic acids ([Lesson 25](chapter:36)):

- Epimers differ at exactly one stereocenter (glucose and galactose differ at C4; glucose and mannose at C2). Anomers differ at the anomeric carbon, the former carbonyl carbon.
- Maltose is glucose α(1→4) glucose, lactose is galactose β(1→4) glucose, and sucrose is a non-reducing disaccharide because both anomeric carbons are tied up in the bond.
- Starch and glycogen are α-linked storage polymers; cellulose is β(1→4) and indigestible by humans.
- More cis double bonds in a fatty acid means a lower melting point.
- DNA bases: A, G, C, T. RNA bases: A, G, C, U. A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds), G pairs with C (three). Chargaff: A = T and G = C. Strands are antiparallel and written 5′ to 3′. Higher G–C content means a higher melting temperature.

Praxis Tip: When you need the complement of 5′-ATGC-3′, write 3′-TACG-5′, then flip it to read 5′-GCAT-3′. The flip is where the points go.
