*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Where the Money Actually Comes From

*What a platform might pay you later, and where the same offer link can be tapped before any of those programs open.*

The offer in the first chapter can pay you before any platform does. A subscriber count is not that payment. A monetized channel is not that payment either. This chapter is the extra: YouTube’s two gates, what a second channel does not inherit, what other sites pay for the same file, and where a stranger can tap your link.

Every number below is a snapshot. These terms move faster than tool prices and faster than a content-safety policy. Two of the programs here changed entirely in the months before this chapter was written. Confirm each figure on that platform’s own help page before you plan around it.

## I. YouTube’s own bar, and how to clear it

YouTube pays through two gates, and neither one is the first money. The offer in chapter 1 can pay on a sale before either gate opens. YouTube’s own ads on your videos, before you are accepted, are not your revenue. [YouTube’s eligibility page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851) says the platform may run those ads and keep the share until you qualify for the higher gate.

In countries where YouTube has opened the expanded program, the first gate is fan funding and Shopping: channel memberships, Super Chat, Super Stickers, Super Thanks, and product tags. [YouTube’s monetization menu](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/94522) sets that gate at 500 subscribers, three public uploads in the last 90 days, and either 3,000 qualified watch hours on long-form videos in the last 365 days or 3 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days. It does not include a share of watch-page ads.

The second gate is that ad share, plus Shorts Feed ads and YouTube Premium revenue. It needs 1,000 subscribers and either 4,000 qualified long-form watch hours in the last 12 months or 10 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days. Watch hours from Shorts in the Shorts feed do not count toward the 3,000-hour bar or the 4,000-hour bar. Private, unlisted, and deleted videos do not count. An ad campaign’s views do not count.

Pick one route and keep the counters separate. Long-form means public videos whose hours accumulate. Shorts means public Shorts in the Shorts feed, counted as views. A channel that posts both still has to choose which number it is trying to fill. Shorts reach does not fill the hour bucket. Use a Short to send a viewer to the long video, which is where an offer link can be tapped.

Both gates also require the channel monetization policies, no active Community Guidelines strikes, and 2-Step Verification. They require advanced features, an AdSense for YouTube account, and a country where the program is offered. Meeting the number does not accept you. You apply under Earn in YouTube Studio and accept the terms. YouTube then reviews the whole channel. It says that review typically takes about a month. If the count drops while you wait, the review still proceeds. If the review says no, you can appeal within 21 days. You can apply again after 30 days, or after 90 days if this was not the first rejection. Starting 1 February 2027, creators who want to keep full monetization must accept updated terms in Studio by 31 January 2027. That date is on YouTube’s eligibility page. Confirm the live checklist in Earn before you plan a month around any figure here. The lower gate did not exist in this form a few years ago.

None of those thresholds move on their own. The tactics below are for those counters, and only after the offer link from chapter 1 actually works. If you have an hour, spend it on the page and the offer video. A subscribe button on a channel with nothing to buy collects an audience for a sale you have not made. None of the tactics cost money. None of them is a formula YouTube publishes.

- State a reason to subscribe, tied to the next video. A bare “subscribe” is the weaker test. Creators who watch their own numbers often prefer the reason. YouTube does not publish a comparison. Test it. Do not treat it as a law.
- Set a custom subscribe-confirmation link: `youtube.com/@YourHandle?sub_confirmation=1`. Build it from Customization, then Basic info, in Studio. Put it in the channel description, every video description, and a pinned comment. Clicking it opens a subscribe prompt. Creators who track their own numbers often prefer that to telling a viewer to hunt for the button. YouTube does not publish a comparison. Treat the preference as a test, not as a measured law.
- Turn on the end screen’s subscribe element once you are eligible, and pair it with a related-video link rather than leaving it bare. A viewer who clicks through to a second video has a second chance to subscribe. Eligibility for that element is YouTube’s, and the chapter’s end-screen rules still apply.
- Group videos into playlists. Ten minutes watched counts as ten minutes toward the hour bar whether it happens in one video or across several. A playlist only helps if the viewer keeps watching. YouTube does not publish that they will.
- Ask once per video, not several times. One ask tied to a benefit is the version worth testing. A handful of generic asks is the version that costs attention. Again, this is a test, not a published weight.
- Open on the subject. Cut the introduction that has not yet given the viewer a reason to stay, and point at a related video when this one ends. That is a way watch time and a second view can happen. It is not a weight YouTube publishes.

None of this is published mechanics — YouTube does not publish a conversion-rate formula for any of it — but it is close to universal agreement among creators who track their own numbers, and every item on the list costs nothing to test.

## II. A second channel starts back at zero

Running a second channel is allowed: to protect a name, to redirect traffic, or to branch later into Shorts or another language. YouTube’s own rules permit multiple channels under one account. A channel can have no videos at all. You may link to the main channel in the About section, the banner, the description, the featured-channels list, and a pinned comment on a placeholder video. What crosses the line is spam, a name that impersonates someone else, or a trick meant to inflate the main channel’s numbers. A simple redirect is none of those.

A second channel does not inherit the main channel’s monetized status. It clears the thresholds in §I on its own: its own subscribers, its own watch hours, and its own review. A placeholder with one video and a pinned link is not a monetized channel by association. If it later becomes a real show, it starts the clock at zero.

## III. The rest of the landscape, and how fast it moves

YouTube is not the only platform that will pay for a video, and treating it as the only one leaves money on the table that a second upload of the same file could pick up elsewhere. Every program below shares the same basic shape — a follower or subscriber floor, a recent-activity or view floor, sometimes a minimum content length, almost always a geographic restriction — but the specific numbers differ, and they move.

| Platform | Program | Threshold (at time of writing) | Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | Partner Program | 1,000 subscribers + 4,000 watch hours/12 months, or 10M Shorts views/90 days | Ad revenue share |
| [TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/article/eligibility) | Creator Rewards | 10,000 followers + 100,000 views/30 days; 18+; videos 60s or longer; limited countries | Pay per qualified view |
| [Facebook](https://creators.facebook.com/tools/ad-breaks/v2/) | Content Monetization | 10,000 followers + 600,000 minutes watched/60 days + 5 videos on the Page | Ad revenue share |
| [Snapchat](https://help.snapchat.com/hc/en-us/articles/7012310905876) | Spotlight monetization | 50,000 followers + 25 posts/month + 100 hours of Spotlight view time/28 days | Ad revenue share |
| [X](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/original-content-rewards) | Original Content Rewards | Active Premium subscription + 500 verified followers + 500,000 impressions/90 days | Pay per qualified impression |
| [Rumble](https://rumble.support/help/rumble-creator-program) | Partner Program | No follower minimum; 30-day-old account in good standing, one original upload | Ad revenue share |
| [Dailymotion](https://faq.dailymotion.com/hc/en-us/articles/11526400930194-Become-a-Creator) | Partner Program | Sources disagree — confirm directly with Dailymotion | Ad revenue share |
| Instagram | Gifts / Subscriptions | No fixed public threshold; direct-payout bonus programs are mostly invite-only | Viewer tipping / subscriptions |
| Vimeo | — | On Demand is being discontinued in 2026, not replaced with an equivalent | Was pay-per-view; replaced by a paid-subscription tool, not a payout program |
| [Tumblr](https://help.tumblr.com/knowledge-base/paid-feature-eligibility/) | — | No dedicated video ad-monetization program | Traffic and referral platform, not currently a payout source |

Two platforms in this table changed their entire monetization program within the same year this chapter was written. Facebook’s old In-Stream Ads program ended outright — for on-demand video in 2025, for Live video in mid-2026 — replaced by the unified Content Monetization Program above. X retired its original revenue-sharing program in September 2026, the same month this chapter was drafted, in favor of the Original Content Rewards Program shown here, which pays for qualified impressions on original content rather than for engagement on replies. Neither change was unusual; it is the normal pace at which a platform’s monetization terms move, and it is the reason every number in this table needs reconfirming before you rely on it.

Dailymotion’s own published requirements were inconsistent across sources at the time of writing — some describing a threshold that mirrors YouTube’s almost exactly, others describing a much lower bar. That inconsistency is itself useful information: check the platform’s own creator help center directly, here and for every platform in this table, rather than a secondhand guide.

Instagram and Tumblr are worth naming because creators ask about them, not because either is currently a strong ad-revenue-share platform for video the way YouTube or TikTok are. Instagram’s direct-payout bonus programs are largely invite-only; its dependable monetization tools are viewer-funded — Gifts and Subscriptions — which pay only if an audience is already willing to pay directly. Tumblr has no comparable video ad-monetization program at all; treat it as a place to post and link back to a monetized upload elsewhere, not as a payout source in its own right.

## IV. Affiliate links: revenue that survives a platform’s program changing

Every program in §III can change or disappear. Two already did while this chapter was being written. An affiliate link pays on a sale. It does not need the site’s ad program to exist. Set it up before you clear any threshold.

Disclose next to the link: “affiliate link” or “#ad.” The FTC pages in the sources apply on every site. Put that one link in the first two or three lines, the part a viewer sees before “more.” On a long video whose comments are clickable, a pinned comment can repeat it. Three to five links is a ceiling this book treats as creator consensus. Past that, the list is a menu. The first chapter already asked you to pick one product.

YouTube can later tag a product inside the video: Shopping, a card, or an end screen, and only after you are eligible. That is a convenience once the description link already works. This book has no cited test that an in-video tag outsells the description link. Use the tag when you are allowed to. Do not wait for it.

A pasted address is not a link until YouTube will make it clickable. [YouTube’s sharing-links page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13748639) says a long-form description and a long-form comment become clickable only after the channel has advanced features. Studio shows that under Settings, Channel, Feature eligibility. Phone verification is the earlier step. Advanced features also ask for channel history, or for an identity check where YouTube offers one, and only the channel owner can complete that check. Until the feature is on, the viewer can read the address and cannot tap it. The same page says an address in a Short’s description or a Short’s comments is not clickable, including a comment you pin. A Short can send someone to a long video. It cannot be the page where they buy.

An [info card](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6140493) or an [end screen](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789) that opens a site outside YouTube requires the [YouTube Partner Program](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851). An end screen can sit only in the last 5–20 seconds, and only on a video at least 25 seconds long. Those overlays are a later convenience. The first sale uses a long-form description, a pinned comment on that long video, and the links on the channel profile — YouTube’s sharing-links page allows up to 14 of those — once advanced features are on.

## V. The same offer on any of these sites

The table in §III is how a site might pay you for attention. This section is how a stranger pays you for the offer, on YouTube and on the other sites in that table.

Post the same teaching video where you have the right to post it. Use the same page and the same price. Do not invent a separate product for TikTok, another for Facebook, and another for YouTube. One offer. More than one door.

On each site, find the one field that turns an address into a tap. Read that site’s own help before you trust a screenshot from last year. Check in this order:

- The profile or bio.
- The long description or caption, if that site makes it clickable.
- A pinned comment, if the site has one and the comment can be tapped.
- A sticker, a card, or a product tag, only where the site says you are allowed to use it.

A few of those fields are named on the site’s own help.

- Instagram’s help lists “Add a website to your Instagram profile” from its [editing-your-profile](https://help.instagram.com/936495066470190/) page. Separately, [Instagram’s link-sticker page](https://help.instagram.com/192168966243613) says a sticker on an organic Story sends the tap to a website.
- TikTok’s help has a page for [adding a website to your profile](https://support.tiktok.com/en/getting-started/setting-up-your-profile/adding-a-website-to-your-profile). Whether your account shows the control is answered there. This book does not add a follower number the page did not show in plain text.
- Every other row in the table already links to that platform’s creator help. Use the profile, About, or description field that page names.

Then test the tap on your phone, the same way you test the payment page. If the caption is not clickable, the profile is the shelf. YouTube is the strict case already in §IV. A Short’s description and comments are not clickable. A long-form description waits until advanced features are on. The other sites are different. Confirm each one on your phone.

Besides the offer, a site can pay you in two extra ways. Neither one replaces the price.

- Its ad or rewards program, after you clear the row in the table. Write that money on the platform line of the money sheet, separate from offer money.
- Tips or gifts, where the site offers them and you are allowed to turn them on. A tip is a thank-you. It is not a price. Say the offer once, and point at the link. Do not replace the price with “send a gift.”

A live video can carry the same offer. Show the work, say the price once at the end, and point at the same link. Live does not need a new product.

A shirt is usually a weak first offer. You can ship it, and the margin still has to cover your time. Most first channels are selling a method. A shirt is a souvenir of a method nobody has bought yet. Sell the file, the call, or the ticket first. Add the shirt later if people ask for it.

> **Key takeaway:** The offer can pay before either YouTube gate. Shorts views do not fill the hour bars. A pasted address is not a link until the site makes it clickable. The same offer can use every site that will send a stranger to your page.

## VI. Claims that do not survive a platform’s own eligibility page

- That hitting a platform’s follower or subscriber number is the same as being approved for monetization. Most programs add a second gate — watch time, a view count in a trailing window, sometimes manual review — that a follower count alone does not satisfy. On YouTube the number only starts the review. It does not start the payment.
- That Shorts views fill the 4,000-hour bar. YouTube’s eligibility page says watch hours from the Shorts feed do not count toward it. Shorts have their own view bar. Long videos have the hour bar. Pick one.
- That a platform’s monetization program, once joined, is permanent. Facebook’s entire In-Stream Ads program and X’s original revenue-sharing program both ended within the year this chapter was written, each replaced by a different program with different rules.
- That a second channel inherits the first one’s monetized status. It does not; every channel clears its own thresholds from zero, with its own subscriber count and its own watch hours.
- That more affiliate links are a better recommendation. Past three to five, the list is a menu. Chapter 1 already asked for one product.
- That an in-video product tag outsells the description link. This book has no cited test of that. The tag is a later convenience, after you are eligible. The first sale is the description link on a long video, once YouTube has made it clickable.
- That pasting an affiliate address into a Short, or into a new channel’s description, gives the viewer a way to buy. YouTube’s sharing-links page says a Short’s description and comments are not clickable, and a long-form description is clickable only after advanced features are on.
- That Instagram and TikTok pay the way YouTube does. Both lean more heavily on viewer-funded tipping and invite-only bonus programs than on YouTube’s comparatively open, formula-based ad-revenue share.
- That each site deserves its own product. The same file, call, or ticket can be the offer on every site that will send a person to the page. A second product per logo is how one channel becomes several hobbies.
- That tips and gifts are a price. They are extra. The price stays on the page.
- That merch is the easy first dollar. A shirt is a later souvenir. The first dollar is the file, the call, or the ticket you can deliver this month.

A last limit. Every number in this chapter is a snapshot, and platform monetization terms are the fastest-moving fact category in this book. Confirm each threshold on the platform’s own creator or help pages before planning a channel, a second channel, or a cross-posting schedule around any of them.
