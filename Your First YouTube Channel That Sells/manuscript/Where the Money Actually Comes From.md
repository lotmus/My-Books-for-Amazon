*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Where the Money Actually Comes From

*The specific numbers a platform requires before it pays you anything, how fast those numbers change, and the tactics — on YouTube and beyond — that actually move them.*

A subscriber count and a monetized channel are not the same thing, and treating them as the same thing is the single most common reason a channel that looks successful earns nothing. Every platform that pays for video sets a specific bar first — a follower floor, a watch-time or view floor, sometimes a content-length minimum, almost always a country restriction — and none of it happens by accident. This chapter covers YouTube's own bar and how creators actually clear it, what a second channel does and does not inherit, the wider landscape of platforms willing to pay for the same video, and the one revenue stream that does not depend on any platform's program surviving intact.

Every specific number below is a snapshot. Platform monetization terms move faster than almost anything else in this book — faster than tool pricing, faster than a content-safety policy — and two of the programs described here changed entirely within the months before this chapter was written. Confirm each one on the platform's own creator or help pages before you plan around it.

## I. YouTube's own bar, and how to clear it

YouTube pays through two separate gates, not one. The first unlocks fan-funding features — Super Thanks, channel memberships, YouTube Shopping — at 500 subscribers, three public uploads in the trailing 90 days, and either 3,000 watch hours in the trailing year or 3 million Shorts views in the trailing 90 days. The second, full ad monetization through the YouTube Partner Program, needs 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours in the trailing 12 months, or 10 million Shorts views in the trailing 90 days. Both require an account at least 30 days old, a linked AdSense account, and residence in one of the roughly 120 countries the program currently covers. Confirm all of this directly in YouTube Studio's own monetization checklist — the fan-funding tier did not exist in this form a few years ago, which is itself a reminder that the rest of this list can change the same way.

None of those thresholds move on their own. A handful of tactics do more to move them than most channels realize, and none of them cost anything:

- State the reason to subscribe, not just the request. "Subscribe" converts worse than "subscribe if you want part two" or "subscribe so this shows up when I post the next one" — a viewer who arrived through search in particular needs a reason tied to future value, not a bare command.
- Set a custom subscribe-confirmation link — `youtube.com/@YourHandle?sub_confirmation=1`, built from Customization → Basic info in Studio — and put it in the channel description, every video description, and a pinned comment. Clicking it prompts an instant subscribe pop-up, which measurably outperforms asking a viewer to find the button themselves.
- Turn on the end screen's subscribe element once you are eligible, and pair it with a related-video link rather than leaving it bare. A viewer who clicks through to a second video is a viewer with a second chance to subscribe.
- Group videos into playlists. A playlist keeps a viewer who found one video through search inside the channel for the next one, which is where watch-hour totals actually accumulate — a single ten-minute video and ten minutes spread across a playlist move the number by the same amount, but the playlist is far more likely to actually happen.
- Ask once per video, not several times. A single, benefit-tied ask converts better than a handful of generic ones, which read as pushy and cost retention.

None of this is published mechanics — YouTube does not publish a conversion-rate formula for any of it — but it is close to universal agreement among creators who track their own numbers, and every item on the list costs nothing to test.

## II. A second channel starts back at zero

Running a second, minimal channel — to protect a name, redirect traffic, or eventually branch into Shorts-only content or a different language — is allowed. YouTube's own rules permit multiple channels under one account, a channel with no videos at all, and links to the main channel in the About section, the banner, the description, the featured-channels list, and a pinned comment on a placeholder video. What crosses the line is spam (mass-posting links), a name that impersonates someone else, or anything designed to artificially inflate the main channel's numbers — a simple redirect channel is none of those things.

What a second channel does not get is the main channel's monetized status. Every channel clears the thresholds in §I on its own: its own subscribers, its own watch hours, its own 30-day account age. A placeholder channel with one video and a pinned link to the real channel is not, and will never become, a monetized channel by association. If it later turns into something real — a Shorts-only spinoff, a second-language version, a genuinely separate show — it starts the clock at zero, exactly like any new channel would.

## III. The rest of the landscape, and how fast it moves

YouTube is not the only platform that will pay for a video, and treating it as the only one leaves money on the table that a second upload of the same file could pick up elsewhere. Every program below shares the same basic shape — a follower or subscriber floor, a recent-activity or view floor, sometimes a minimum content length, almost always a geographic restriction — but the specific numbers differ, and they move.

*Where YouTube's bar sits relative to the rest of the field it competes with for upload time — the one dimension of each program that's directly comparable across all of them. Figures as of the time of writing; see the table below for the fuller picture, since a follower count alone doesn't determine eligibility on most of these platforms.*

| Platform | Program | Threshold (at time of writing) | Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | Partner Program | 1,000 subscribers + 4,000 watch hours/12 months, or 10M Shorts views/90 days | Ad revenue share |
| TikTok | Creator Rewards | 10,000 followers + 100,000 views/30 days; 18+; videos 60s or longer; limited countries | Pay per qualified view |
| Facebook | Content Monetization | 10,000 followers + 600,000 minutes watched/60 days + 5 videos on the Page | Ad revenue share |
| Snapchat | Spotlight monetization | 50,000 followers + 25 posts/month + 100 hours of Spotlight view time/28 days | Ad revenue share |
| X | Original Content Rewards | Active Premium subscription + 500 verified followers + 500,000 impressions/90 days | Pay per qualified impression |
| Rumble | Partner Program | No follower minimum; 30-day-old account in good standing, one original upload | Ad revenue share |
| Dailymotion | Partner Program | Sources disagree — confirm directly with Dailymotion | Ad revenue share |
| Instagram | Gifts / Subscriptions | No fixed public threshold; direct-payout bonus programs are mostly invite-only | Viewer tipping / subscriptions |
| Vimeo | — | On Demand is being discontinued in 2026, not replaced with an equivalent | Was pay-per-view; replaced by a paid-subscription tool, not a payout program |
| Tumblr | — | No dedicated video ad-monetization program | Traffic and referral platform, not currently a payout source |

Two platforms in this table changed their entire monetization program within the same year this chapter was written. Facebook's old In-Stream Ads program ended outright — for on-demand video in 2025, for Live video in mid-2026 — replaced by the unified Content Monetization Program above. X retired its original revenue-sharing program in September 2026, the same month this chapter was drafted, in favor of the Original Content Rewards Program shown here, which pays for qualified impressions on original content rather than for engagement on replies. Neither change was unusual; it is the normal pace at which a platform's monetization terms move, and it is the reason every number in this table needs reconfirming before you rely on it.

Dailymotion's own published requirements were inconsistent across sources at the time of writing — some describing a threshold that mirrors YouTube's almost exactly, others describing a much lower bar. That inconsistency is itself useful information: check the platform's own creator help center directly, here and for every platform in this table, rather than a secondhand guide.

Instagram and Tumblr are worth naming because creators ask about them, not because either is currently a strong ad-revenue-share platform for video the way YouTube or TikTok are. Instagram's direct-payout bonus programs are largely invite-only; its dependable monetization tools are viewer-funded — Gifts and Subscriptions — which pay only if an audience is already willing to pay directly. Tumblr has no comparable video ad-monetization program at all; treat it as a place to post and link back to a monetized upload elsewhere, not as a payout source in its own right.

## IV. Affiliate links: revenue that survives a platform's program changing

Every program in §III can change or disappear — two already did while this chapter was being written. An affiliate link does not depend on any of them; it pays on a sale, regardless of whether the platform hosting the video has an ad-share program at all, which makes it worth setting up before a channel clears any monetization threshold, not after.

The mechanics are the same across any platform that allows a clickable link in a description. Disclose clearly and prominently — "affiliate link" or "#ad" near the link itself, not buried in a wall of text — since FTC disclosure requirements apply regardless of which platform hosts the video. Put the best-converting link first, within the first two or three lines of the description, since that is the part visible without a viewer expanding it. A pinned comment repeating the top link gets read even by viewers who never expand the description at all. Three to five links is a practical ceiling before a list starts reading as spam rather than a recommendation — put the ones actually worth recommending first, and drop the rest.

Where a platform supports it — YouTube's Shopping tab for eligible channels, cards, and end screens among them — a product tagged directly inside the video converts better than a link a viewer has to leave the video to find, because it does not ask them to stop watching in order to act on it. Where a platform does not support that, the description and the pinned comment do nearly all of the work anyway.

## V. Claims that do not survive a platform's own eligibility page

- That hitting a platform's follower or subscriber number is the same as being approved for monetization. Most programs add a second gate — watch time, a view count in a trailing window, account age, sometimes manual review — that a follower count alone does not satisfy.
- That a platform's monetization program, once joined, is permanent. Facebook's entire In-Stream Ads program and X's original revenue-sharing program both ended within the year this chapter was written, each replaced by a different program with different rules.
- That a second channel inherits the first one's monetized status. It does not; every channel clears its own thresholds from zero, with its own subscriber count and its own watch hours.
- That more affiliate links convert better than fewer. Past three to five in one description, a list reads as spam rather than a recommendation, and conversion tends to drop rather than rise.
- That Instagram and TikTok pay the way YouTube does. Both lean more heavily on viewer-funded tipping and invite-only bonus programs than on YouTube's comparatively open, formula-based ad-revenue share.

A last limit. Every number in this chapter is a snapshot, and platform monetization terms are the fastest-moving fact category in this book. Confirm each threshold on the platform's own creator or help pages before planning a channel, a second channel, or a cross-posting schedule around any of them.
