*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# The Rules That Can Close the Shop

*A warning, a strike, a claim, and a missing disclosure are four different problems with four different clocks. Know which one you are holding before you react to it.*

A channel that sells has more to lose from a rule than a hobby channel does. A hobby channel that loses a video loses a video. A channel that sells can lose the offer video in the week it was finally reaching the page, or lose the ability to upload at all in the week the ticket date was announced. The rules are not mysterious. YouTube publishes them, with the penalties and the clocks. Most first-time trouble comes from confusing one kind of problem with another and answering it the wrong way.

This chapter is the short map. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for the policy page each section names. Those pages change. Open them before you rely on a detail.

## I. Four problems that look alike in your inbox

| Problem | What causes it | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Community Guidelines warning | A first violation of a Community Guidelines policy | No penalty at first. An optional policy training lets it expire. |
| Community Guidelines strike | A further violation after a warning | A pause on posting. Three in 90 days can end the channel. |
| Copyright strike | A valid legal removal request from a rights holder | The video comes down. Three in 90 days can end the channel. |
| Content ID claim | An automatic match against a rights holder’s reference file | Affects that video’s money or visibility. Not a strike. |

A disclosure is a fifth thing, and it is the one you control completely. Sections V and VI cover the settings.

## II. Community Guidelines: the warning, then the clock

YouTube’s [strike basics page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2802032) says the Community Guidelines apply to all content, including unlisted and private videos, comments, links, posts, and thumbnails. Notice two words in that list. **Links** means the offer page you point at is inside the rules. **Thumbnails** means the picture is held to them too, not just the video.

The sequence, as YouTube describes it:

- **The first violation is typically a warning.** You can take an optional policy training. If you finish it, the warning expires 90 days after you finished the training, not 90 days after it was issued. Violate the same policy inside that window and the warning may not expire, and the channel may get a strike. Violate a different policy after the training and you get another warning. YouTube says not every warning is eligible for training, and that repeat offenders may lose the option.
- **A first strike** stops you posting for one week. YouTube’s [community guidelines tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12950271) spell out what that covers: videos, live streams, stories, custom thumbnails, and posts. The strike expires 90 days after it was issued.
- **A second strike within 90 days** stops posting for two more weeks.
- **A third strike within the same 90 days** terminates the channel.
- **A single case of severe abuse** can terminate a channel without the warning or the strikes.

If you believe the decision is wrong, appeal it from the notice. If you believe it is right, the useful response is to read the specific policy the email names, fix the pattern, and wait out the clock without testing the edge again.

Three policies matter more than the rest to a channel built around a price.

- **Spam.** YouTube’s [spam policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973) lists, among other things, maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, and descriptions that promise what the video does not deliver; rewards offered in exchange for likes, views, or subscribers; repetitive comments posted to drive traffic; and “get rich quick” schemes. It says plainly that asking viewers to like, comment, or subscribe is fine. It also names automated or AI mass-production: high volumes of near-identical videos with minimal changes. The production chapter’s generated footage and synthetic voices are allowed tools. Forty copies of the same generated video with the keyword swapped are not a channel.
- **Fake engagement.** Paying a service for views, subscribers, or likes falls under YouTube’s [fake engagement policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767). *When Nothing Sells* already calls those services a faster loss. They are also a policy risk.
- **External links.** YouTube’s [external links policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9054257) bars links that send people to content that would break the guidelines, such as malware, phishing, or sites selling regulated goods. It covers typed links, addresses shown in the picture, spelled-out addresses, and addresses said aloud. It also says affiliate content does not violate YouTube’s Terms of Use, and that YouTube may remove a link it cannot verify as safe. Your offer page is a link. Keep it on a normal, secure checkout, and if the page makes a claim you could not make in the video, fix the page.

## III. Copyright strikes: a legal request, not an algorithm

A copyright strike comes from a person or company sending YouTube a formal removal request. YouTube’s [copyright strike page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2814000) says these requests are reviewed, and that if one appears valid, YouTube has to remove the content to comply with copyright law. The [strikes FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9235777) says there is no warning step for this one. The first valid request is a strike.

Some removal requests are scheduled. YouTube’s [removal request page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13823830) says a scheduled request gives the uploader seven days to act, for example by removing the video or seeking a retraction, before the removal and the strike take effect. If you receive one, that week is the time to decide, not to argue in the comments.

Three ways a copyright strike is resolved, per YouTube:

1. **Complete Copyright School and wait.** The strike expires 90 days after it was applied, once Copyright School is done and the channel has fewer than three strikes. The removed video is not reinstated.
2. **Ask for a retraction.** Contact the person who filed the request and ask them to withdraw it. This is the route when the request was a mistake that a polite email can fix, such as a licensed clip you can show the license for.
3. **File a counter notification.** A legal statement that the removal was a mistake, or that the use qualifies as an exception such as fair use. YouTube says it cannot decide ownership or mediate between you and the rights holder. A counter notification is a legal step. Treat it as one.

Three copyright strikes within 90 days make the channel subject to termination. For a channel that sells, the defense is upstream. The production chapter’s rule is to read the license on every clip and track before you publish. Keep the proof that you did: a screenshot of the license page with the date, saved next to the project file. When a request arrives two years later, that screenshot is the retraction email.

## IV. Content ID claims: the video, not the channel

A Content ID claim is generated automatically when an upload matches a reference file a rights holder has given YouTube. YouTube’s [claims page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6013276) says the rights holder chooses what happens: monetize, track, or block. Monetize is the most common choice for music. A claimed video longer than three minutes that is tracked or monetized stays up. A Short between one and three minutes long with an active claim is blocked, whatever the policy. YouTube says claims affect videos and usually do not affect the channel, and that a claim is not a strike.

The trap is in the dispute. The same page says that if you dispute a claim without a valid reason, the rights holder can send a removal request, and if that request is valid, the channel gets a copyright strike. A claim you could have lived with becomes a strike you cannot ignore.

What a claim means for a channel that sells:

- **A monetize claim on the offer video can put ads on it for someone else.** That is a stranger’s first look at your price with an advertisement from a business that is not yours. If the claimed part is a music bed, replacing the track is usually faster than disputing it, if your editor or Studio’s own tools let you remove or swap the claimed audio. Check what Studio offers on that video’s claim before you re-upload anything.
- **A block can break the path in some countries or everywhere.** If the path video sends people to a blocked method video, the path has a hole in it. Fix the video, or point the path at another one.
- **Dispute only when you have the reason in writing.** A license you can show, music you composed, a public-domain recording you can document. A dispute is a statement, not a lottery ticket.

## V. Paid promotion: the box and the sentence

If a company paid you, gave you the product, or otherwise compensated you for a video, YouTube’s [paid promotion page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235) says you have to tell YouTube by ticking the box that reads, “My video contains paid promotion like a product placement, sponsorship, or endorsement.” On a computer it is in Studio, in the video’s details, under more options. On the mobile apps it sits under paid promotion and brands. Once it is ticked, YouTube shows viewers a disclosure message at the start of the video. YouTube’s [branded content policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/17596007) add that if its systems detect undisclosed branded content, they may apply the label themselves and notify you.

The box is not the whole disclosure. YouTube’s own page says you may have further obligations under the laws where you are, and its branded content policies say you are ultimately responsible for making the disclosure clear and prominent. The offer chapter’s sponsor sentence — “[Sponsor] paid for this video. The link is theirs.” — is the part a viewer hears. The money chapter’s FTC links are the US guidance on what clear means. Do both: the box for YouTube, the sentence for the person watching.

An affiliate link alone is a commission on a sale, not necessarily a paid placement. Whether your particular arrangement also needs the box is a question for the paid promotion page and the affiliate program’s own terms. The disclosure next to the link, which this book already requires, is not optional either way.

## VI. AI use: the setting for realistic generated material

The production chapter shows how to make a video without a camera: generated footage, a generated presenter, a synthetic voice. YouTube asks you to say so when the result could be mistaken for real.

YouTube’s [AI disclosure page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491) requires disclosure when generative AI is used to meaningfully alter or generate content that appears realistic: making a real person appear to say or do something they did not, altering footage of a real event or place, or generating a realistic scene that did not happen. The setting is under “AI use” in the upload flow in Studio, on a computer or a phone. Answer Yes when the video meets that bar.

What does not need it, per YouTube’s [announcement of the requirement](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/): clearly unrealistic or animated content, color and lighting adjustments, special effects, and AI used for productivity, such as writing a script, generating ideas, or making automatic captions. A script drafted with an assistant and read by you in your own voice is the last case.

Two changes are worth knowing. YouTube’s [2026 update on AI labels](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/) moved the label for photorealistic AI content to a more prominent place: below the player on long videos, as an overlay on Shorts. The same post says that starting in May 2026, YouTube began automatically labeling videos its systems detect as having significant photorealistic AI use, where the creator had not answered either way. It also says the label by itself does not change how a video is recommended or whether it can earn money.

For a channel that sells, the reasoning is short. The offer video is where a stranger decides whether to trust you with money. A generated presenter that looks like a real person, undisclosed, and then labeled by YouTube under the player, is a bad way to learn that trust was the product. When the video meets the bar, answer Yes. The production chapter’s line on voice consent still applies on top of this: a disclosure does not make cloning someone else’s voice acceptable.

## VII. Made for kids: a legal statement, not a reach setting

Every channel and every video has an audience setting: made for kids, or not. YouTube’s [audience setting page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9527654) says the setting exists to comply with the US Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, COPPA, and other laws. When a video is set as made for kids, YouTube turns off a long list of features on it. The list includes comments, cards and end screens, the notification bell, personalized advertising, merchandise and ticketing, the miniplayer, and saving to a playlist or Watch Later. A channel set as made for kids also loses posts and channel memberships, among others.

For most channels in this book the answer is plainly no. The buyer is an adult with a card. The setting describes who the video is directed to, not who it helps in the end. A channel teaching parents how to choose a reading tutor is directed to parents. A cartoon of the alphabet song is directed to children, whatever the parent who pressed play intended.

If your videos really are directed to children, the setting is not a marketing choice. Mark them correctly, and accept that the features the rest of this book relies on — the comment where people ask the price, the end screen that names the next video — will not be there. A channel that needs to sell to adults should make videos for adults.

## VIII. Keep a small record

Nothing in this chapter requires a lawyer to follow. It does require a memory you will not have in two years. Keep three short lists next to the money sheet:

- **License log.** For each video: every clip, image, and track you did not make, where it came from, the license line, and the date you saved a screenshot of it.
- **Disclosure log.** For each video: paid promotion yes or no, AI use yes or no, audience setting, and the affiliate disclosure line if there is one.
- **Notice log.** Any warning, strike, or claim: the date, the video, the policy or the claimant, what you did, and the date the clock runs out.

The notice log is how you know, before you upload a borderline video, whether the channel has room for a mistake this month. With an active strike, the answer is no.

> **Key takeaway:** A warning has a training. A strike has a 90-day clock. A copyright strike is a legal request. A claim is about one video. Tick the paid promotion and AI use settings when they apply, set the audience honestly, and keep the license screenshots.

## IX. Before every upload

- [ ] Every clip, image, and track has a license line and a dated screenshot in the license log.
- [ ] The title and the thumbnail promise only what the video contains.
- [ ] The offer page linked in the description makes no claim you could not make in the video.
- [ ] If a company paid you or gave you the product, the paid promotion box is ticked and you said so out loud.
- [ ] If any realistic scene, person, or voice was generated or meaningfully altered, AI use is set to Yes.
- [ ] The audience setting says who the video is directed to.
- [ ] The notice log has no active strike that this video’s subject could add to.

## X. Claims that do not survive a policy page

- That a first mistake ends a channel. YouTube says a first Community Guidelines violation is typically a warning, with a training that lets it expire. Severe abuse is the exception.
- That a Content ID claim is a strike. YouTube says it is not. Disputing one without a valid reason is how it can become one.
- That private and unlisted videos are outside the rules. The strike basics page says the guidelines apply to them, and to links and thumbnails as well.
- That an affiliate link breaks YouTube’s rules. The external links policy says affiliate content does not violate the Terms of Use. The disclosure is still yours to make.
- That crediting the artist makes a track legal to use. Credit is not a license. The production chapter’s license line is what decides it.
- That ticking the paid promotion box is the whole disclosure. YouTube’s own page says local law may require more. Say it in the video and next to the link.
- That the AI label hurts reach. YouTube says the label by itself does not change recommendations or earning eligibility. Whether a stranger trusts an undisclosed fake is a separate question, and it is the one that sells.
- That made for kids is a way to reach more families. It is a legal designation that removes comments, end screens, and more. Set it for who the video is directed to.

A last limit. This chapter will not keep a determined rights holder from filing a request, or tell you whether a particular use is fair use. It will keep you from turning a claim into a strike, a warning into a pause, or a missing checkbox into a label you did not choose.
