*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Read the Count

*Studio shows how strangers arrived and when they left. Your page shows whether they paid. Read both, in that order, once a week.*

YouTube Studio is generous with numbers. Open Analytics on a quiet Tuesday and you can find views, unique viewers, impressions, a click-through rate, average view duration, average percentage viewed, watch time, subscribers gained, new viewers, casual viewers, regular viewers, and a dozen traffic sources. Every one of them is real. Most of them are not the question this book asks.

The question is whether a stranger can find the price and pay it. Studio can answer the first half of that. It cannot answer the second, because the payment happens on a page YouTube does not own. This chapter is how to read the half Studio shows, collect the half it does not, and put the two side by side on the week sheet without fooling yourself.

## I. Three counts, three places

A sale has three moments, and each one is counted somewhere different.

| Moment | Where it is counted | The number to write down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A stranger watched | YouTube Studio, the video’s Analytics | Views and average view duration on the offer video |
| They went to the page | Your page or link tool, not Studio | Visits from YouTube, by video if you tagged the link |
| They paid or booked | Your payment or booking tool | Payments, including zero |

Studio’s traffic reports describe how viewers reached your video. They are not built to report how many viewers left your video for your page. That count lives on the page side. If your page tool shows visits, and where they came from, that is where the second line of the week sheet comes from. If it shows nothing, the fallback is the one the calendar already uses: ask the people you sent in week two what they tapped.

Write all three, every week, for the offer video first. A channel with ten thousand views and no page visits has a different problem from a channel with two hundred views and four page visits. *When Nothing Sells* calls those different breaks. These three lines are how you know which one you have.

## II. Label the door, keep one destination

The rule in this book is one URL. The description, the pinned comment, and one profile slot repeat it. A second product is a menu.

A tag does not break that rule. A tag is text after a question mark at the end of the same address, which many page and analytics tools read as a label for where the visit came from. The page is the same. The price is the same. Only the label differs. The widely used convention is a set of parameters whose names begin with `utm_`:

`https://yourpage.example/checklist?utm_source=youtube&utm_medium=description&utm_campaign=offer-video`

The pinned comment on the same video can carry `utm_medium=pinned`. The method video can carry `utm_campaign=method-video`. After a week, the page tool can tell you that four visits came from the offer video’s description and none from the pinned comment, which is a fact you can act on.

Three checks before you rely on it:

- **Open the tagged link on your phone,** from the description, the way the phone test in the offer chapter already asks. Confirm it lands on the same page with the price visible. Some tools drop the tag, and a few misbehave with it. If yours does, use the plain link and the fallback.
- **Confirm your tool shows the tags.** Look for a report of visit sources or campaigns. If there is none, the tag is harmless and useless. Do not add a second tool just to read it during the first six weeks.
- **Keep the labels boring and consistent.** Lowercase, one word per slot, written in the money sheet. “offer-video” this week and “OfferVid” next week are two rows that should have been one.

A link shortener that counts clicks does the same job, with one caution. A stranger can see where a plain address goes before they tap it. A short code hides that. For a channel asking a stranger to pay, a readable address is part of the trust.

## III. Impressions and the click rate, read with YouTube’s own caveats

An impression, in Studio, is not every time your video exists somewhere. YouTube’s [impressions page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314486) counts one when a thumbnail is shown on YouTube for more than one second with at least half of it visible. It does not count impressions on external sites and embeds, the mobile website, the YouTube Kids and YouTube Music apps, or inside cards and end screens, or in emails and notifications. The impressions click-through rate is how often a counted impression turned into a view.

YouTube’s [impressions and click-through rate FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154) gives the most useful single number Studio has to offer about this: half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions click-through rate somewhere between 2% and 10%. That is a wide band on purpose. The same page says the rate depends on the content, the audience, and where the thumbnail was shown; that a video shown widely on the home page will naturally have a lower rate; and that a video seen mostly from your own channel page may have a higher one. It also says new channels and videos with fewer than 100 views can see an even wider range.

So read it this way:

- **Wait for enough impressions.** YouTube’s FAQ says to avoid checking the rate right after uploading. On a new channel, a percentage built on forty impressions is a coin landing a few times.
- **Compare a video to your own videos,** over weeks, not to a number you read on someone else’s channel.
- **Read it next to view duration.** The same FAQ calls out the clickbait pattern: a high click rate with a low average view duration and fewer impressions than you expected. A thumbnail that wins the click and loses the viewer is not a win.
- **Remember what it leaves out.** Views from your email, your page, or a forum post are not impressions. A video can have more views than impressions if most of its traffic comes from outside YouTube. YouTube’s FAQ says so.

For a selling channel, the click rate matters on the four long videos a stranger has to reach. It matters less than any line in §I. A 9% rate on a video nobody follows to the page is a well-dressed dead end.

## IV. How they found you, and what it tells you about the buyer

Open a video’s Analytics and choose Reach. The report YouTube’s [reach page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314355) calls “How viewers find this video” splits views by traffic source. The sources are YouTube’s definitions, and some of them are not what the name suggests.

| Traffic source | YouTube’s own definition, in short | What it can tell a channel that sells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| YouTube search | Views from search results. The search terms card lists the words people typed. | Whether the buyer’s phrase from the offer chapter is the phrase they actually use. |
| Suggested videos | Views from suggestions beside or after other videos, and from links in video descriptions. | Whether your eight are leading into each other, and which other channels’ videos you appear next to. |
| Browse features | Views from the home page, subscriptions, Watch Later, and other browsing features. | Whether YouTube is offering the video to people who did not search for it. |
| Channel pages | Views from your channel page or other channels’ pages. | Visitors already looking around your channel, often after another video. |
| External | Views from websites and apps that embed or link to the video. | Whether your email, page, or a forum post is sending people back to watch. |
| Direct or unknown | Direct entry, bookmarks, signed-out viewers, unidentified apps. | Not much. It is the bucket for what YouTube cannot attribute. |

Two of those deserve a weekly look.

**The search terms card is the buyer talking.** It lists what people typed before they found the video. If your title says “quote checklist” and the search terms say “how much should a bathroom repair cost,” the buyer has told you their phrase. That is the problem video’s next title, and maybe the first line of the offer page. If the search terms are about something you do not sell, *When Nothing Sells* §III is the break: the wrong people are watching.

**Suggested videos includes your own description links.** YouTube files views that came from links in video descriptions under Suggested videos. When the path video from the videos chapter links the other seven, those taps show up here, alongside any suggestions YouTube made. The “Content suggesting this video” card names the specific videos. If your method video sends people to your proof video, it is on that card. That is the path working.

Everything else on the Reach tab is context. It is worth a glance once a month. It is not worth a decision once a week.

## V. Where they left, measured against where you asked

The click chapter covered the retention report: where to find it, the thirty-second intro line, and what “above typical intros” means. This section adds one reading that matters only to a channel that sells.

Open the offer video’s retention graph and find the moment you say the price. Note the percentage still watching at that second. Then note it on the method video at the moment you say the twenty-second offer near the end.

Those two numbers are the share of viewers who heard the price. A graph that drops off a cliff a minute before the price means most people never heard what you sell. That is not an offer problem. Move the ask earlier in the offer video, or make the offer video shorter, and say the price within its first minute. The videos chapter’s four-minute offer video exists for this reason.

Studio’s [content page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281) also lists a report called “Key moments for audience retention,” which shows how different moments held attention and compares against your ten latest videos of similar length. Use it to find the sentence where people left. Watch that sentence as a stranger. Usually it is a repeat, a long setup, or a step you explained before you showed it.

## VI. The definitions move. Note the date.

A count is only comparable to itself if it is counted the same way. YouTube changes how it counts.

YouTube’s content page says that beginning August 24, 2026, views are counted the moment a video starts to play, across Shorts, long videos, and live streams. It separates that from “engaged views,” which count viewers who stayed past the initial seconds, and it says Partner Program earnings and eligibility still rest on engaged and qualified counts, not on the new view count. The money chapter already warns that platform programs change underneath you. This is the same lesson for the analytics: a view in September 2026 is not the same measurement as a view in July 2026.

The practical rule: when YouTube announces a change to a metric you track, write the date in the decision log. Compare a video to itself on either side of that date only with that note beside it. And keep the three lines in §I as your main record, because two of them are counted by your own tools, which do not change their definitions because a video site did.

> Worked example. These are invented numbers to show the arithmetic, not results anyone measured. In week five, the bathroom quote checklist’s offer video has 1,000 impressions and a click-through rate of 4%, so about 40 views came from those impressions. Another 25 views came from the email and the forum post, which Studio files under External and which were never impressions. That is 65 views. The page tool shows 6 visits tagged with the offer video’s description and 1 tagged with the pinned comment. The payment tool shows 1 payment at $30 after fees. Read it in order: 7 of 65 viewers reached the page, about 1 in 9. One of 7 visitors paid. The retention graph shows 45% still watching at the moment the price is said. The earliest break is not the click and not the page. More than half the viewers never heard the price. The one change for next week is to say the price in the first minute. Same title, same thumbnail, same page.

## VII. Fifteen minutes, once a week

Pick a day. Open the week sheet from *The Channel Workbook*. Do this, in this order, and nothing else:

1. Payments this week, from the payment tool, including zero.
2. Page visits from YouTube, by tag if you have tags.
3. Views and average view duration on the offer video.
4. The percentage still watching when the price is said, on the offer video.
5. The top three search terms on the offer video and the problem video, copied word for word.
6. The one change you will make, the date you will look again, and what you will not change.

Then close Studio. Reading the same graph every day is not research. A video’s numbers on day two are a forecast of almost nothing, and checking hourly is how a sensible plan turns into three changes before the first one had time to show.

> **Key takeaway:** Count the watch in Studio, the visit on your page, and the payment in your payment tool. Read them in that order. Change one thing. Note the date when YouTube changes a definition.

## VIII. Claims that do not survive the week sheet

- That a higher click-through rate is always better. YouTube’s own FAQ names high clicks with low view duration as the clickbait pattern, and says videos shown widely naturally have lower rates.
- That there is a good click-through rate to aim for. YouTube says half of channels and videos fall between 2% and 10%, depending on content, audience, and placement. That is a band, not a target.
- That Studio tells you how many people tapped your link. Studio describes how people reached the video. Your page tool counts who left it for the page.
- That a tagged link is a second link. Same page, same price, same offer. The tag labels where the tap came from.
- That views are a stable unit. YouTube changed when a view is counted on August 24, 2026. Note dates like that one.
- That more data is better on day one. YouTube’s FAQ itself says to avoid reading the click rate right after upload. Read on the day you set.
- That subscribers gained is the scoreboard. It is a separate test, covered in the money chapter. The payment line is the scoreboard this book keeps.

A last limit. This chapter will not tell you what a good number is for your channel. Nobody honest can. It will tell you which number answers which question, and refuse the habit of answering a payment question with a view count.
