## Prologue

On 21 July 2024, a rover on Mars drilled a finger-sized core out of a reddish rock in a dry riverbed, slid it into a titanium tube, and sealed the tube. The rock had odd spots on it, and the spots are the best hint anyone has yet found that something once lived on that planet. Nobody knows when the tube will come back to Earth.

On Earth, in the same years, more than thirty million people spat into plastic tubes and mailed them to laboratories. Each tube held a few hundred thousand cells, and each cell held about six billion letters of a text that had been copied, with small mistakes, for something like four billion years.

Two tubes. One holds a question we cannot yet answer: has life happened anywhere else? The other holds the answer to a question that, for most of history, nobody could even ask properly: what is life, and how does it keep itself going? This book is about both, and about the thread that connects them.

### The Thread

Here is the thread, in one sentence. Life is chemistry that copies itself, imperfectly, and lets the imperfections be judged.

Each part of that sentence is a part of the book. The chemistry comes first: why carbon and water, where the energy comes from, and how, about four billion years ago, chemistry that merely happened might have become chemistry that copies. Then the judging, which is Darwin’s machine: copies with mistakes, competing for limited resources, so that the mistakes that work are kept and the rest are lost. Run for billions of years, that machine built cells with engines, bodies, brains, and finally a species that writes books about it, and wonders whether its own minds could be copied into something else.

Then the book opens the copier. In 1953 the molecule that does the copying turned out to be a twisted ladder whose shape explained how it copies itself. Within seventy years, we learned to read its letters, then to cut them, write them, clone them and change them in living patients. The copy is never exact, and now we are learning to correct it.

Last, the book leaves Earth. If life is chemistry that copies itself, nothing in the chemistry says it had to happen only once. The final Part asks what life needs, where in the solar system and the galaxy those needs are met, how we are searching, and what a second, independent beginning would tell us about the first.

### What This Book Covers

Part I, Chemistry That Learned to Copy, asks what life is and how it could have started, and follows the first cells through three billion years in which nothing bigger than a microbe existed.

Parts II to IV are evolution. Part II explains Darwin’s machine and how selection, sex and cooperation work. Part III follows the great transitions, from the cell that swallowed an engine to the Cambrian explosion and the mass extinctions that cleared the board. Part IV is our own branch, still being edited.

Part V, Minds, Loops and Machines, asks what a self is, whether one could be built, and what evolution has to say about artificial intelligence.

Parts VI to X are the molecule and what we have done with it. Part VI is DNA itself: its shape, its letters, its copying machine and its mistakes. Part VII is inheritance, from a friar’s peas to a room full of flies, and the dark turn of eugenics. Part VIII is the reading and writing: sequencing, PCR, CRISPR, cloning and the first cures. Part IX follows the editing to our food and to the whole human genome. Part X brings it home: what a test of your own text can and cannot tell you, who else is reading it, and whether we should edit our children.

Part XI, Life Elsewhere, takes the thread off the planet: to Mars, to the buried oceans of the outer moons, to the air of planets around other stars, to the radio silence, and to the question of whether there is anyone else at all.

The Epilogue gathers the thread together. After it come a short list of core ideas, a glossary, further reading, a bibliography of the papers named in the text, and an index.

### How to Read This Book

You do not need any biology, chemistry or mathematics. Every technical word is explained the first time it appears, and again in the glossary. The chapters are meant to be read in order, but each one stands on its own, and cross-references point back to where an idea was first explained.

Why is a book about life in a series called Physics, Actually? Because the question was put most sharply by a physicist, Erwin Schrödinger, in 1943, and because the answers keep turning out to be physics: energy flowing downhill, molecules held together by quantum rules, light carrying the fingerprints of the gases it passed through. The earlier volumes explain that physics in depth. This one shows what it builds.

Where the status of a claim matters, I say which of three things it is. Settled: measured, repeated, and used every day. Working: the best current account, with real gaps. Speculative: allowed by what we know, not shown. Most of this book is settled. The last chapters of Parts V, X and XI are not, and say so. Where I give my own opinion, I mark it as mine.

Three people appear from Part VI onward: Ruth, born in 1948, her daughter Anna, born in 1978, and Anna’s son Theo, born in 2011. They are invented. Their freckles, their tolerance for milk and their arguments at the kitchen table are there so that the mechanisms have somewhere to happen.

Gene names are in italics (*BRCA1*); the proteins they encode are not (the BRCA1 protein). Prices are in United States dollars and carry a year. The science reflects published research as of October 2026, and where a story was still moving at that date, as several in Part XI are, the text says so.

None of the people who did this work were trying to write one story. Schrödinger did not know about the double helix, Darwin did not know about genes, and the engineers who built the Mars rovers were not thinking about spit kits. But it is one story. It begins about four billion years ago, with molecules that started making copies of themselves.
