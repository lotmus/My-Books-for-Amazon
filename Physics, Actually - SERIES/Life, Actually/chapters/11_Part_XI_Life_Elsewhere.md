# PART XI — Life Elsewhere

If life is chemistry that copies itself, nothing in the chemistry says it had to happen only once. This Part leaves Earth to ask where else the copying could have started, how we would know, and what finding it would teach us about ourselves.

## 77. What Life Needs — Water, Energy and the Short List

### The Meat That Would Not Stay Sterile

In 1956, at the agricultural experiment station in Corvallis, Oregon, a microbiologist named Arthur Anderson was testing a new way to preserve food. The idea was simple. Seal the meat in a can, hit it with a dose of gamma rays large enough to shred the DNA of anything living inside, and the meat should keep forever. The cans were irradiated. Some of them spoiled anyway.

Anderson cultured what was growing in them and found a pink bacterium that shrugged off radiation thousands of times stronger than the dose that would kill a person. It was later named *Deinococcus radiodurans*, roughly “terrible berry that withstands radiation.” Its trick is not armor. The radiation breaks its chromosome into hundreds of pieces, just as it would break ours. The bacterium carries several copies of its genome and a repair system good enough to stitch the fragments back into working order within hours.

That is the right place to begin a Part about life elsewhere, because it resets the question. When people ask whether a planet could support life, they usually picture a person standing on it, and then ask whether the person could breathe. That is the wrong test. Most of the history of life on Earth, as Part I and Part III described, was microbes, and microbes have turned out to be much harder to kill than we assumed. The question is not “Could we live there?” It is “What does the copying chemistry of the first ten chapters actually require?”

The answer is a short list. This chapter goes through it.

### The Definition, One More Time

Chapter 1 settled on a working definition: life is chemistry that copies itself, imperfectly, and lets the imperfections be judged. NASA’s astrobiologists use a version of the same idea, worked out by a panel in the 1990s and usually quoted as “a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.”

That definition is useful here because it tells you what to look for and what not to. It does not say “made of cells,” or “based on DNA,” or “breathes oxygen.” Those are facts about the one example we have. It says that somewhere there must be chemistry that stores information, copies it with small errors, and runs for long enough that the errors can be sorted by what survives. Everything else on the short list follows from what that chemistry needs to keep going.

There are four items. A liquid to do the chemistry in. A supply of energy. The right elements. And time, with enough stability that the copying is not wiped out before it gets anywhere.

### A Liquid to Work In

Chemistry that copies needs molecules that can meet, react, separate and meet again. In a solid, molecules are locked in place. In a gas, they are too far apart and anything large falls apart or drifts away. A liquid is the middle state: dense enough that molecules collide all the time, loose enough that they keep moving.

On Earth the liquid is water, and Chapter 2 explained why it is unusually good at the job. It dissolves salts and many small organic molecules. It stays liquid over a wide range of temperatures. It is polar, which is what lets oily molecules gather into membranes, the trick that makes a cell possible. And ice floats, so a freezing lake or ocean is insulated by its own lid instead of freezing solid from the bottom up. That last fact will matter enormously in Chapter 80.

Could another liquid do it? This is a working question, not a settled one. Liquid ammonia dissolves many of the same things water does, and stays liquid at colder temperatures. Liquid methane and ethane exist on the surface of Saturn’s moon Titan, at about minus 179 degrees Celsius. They are poor solvents for the kind of charged and polar molecules our chemistry uses, but that does not prove a different chemistry could not use them. Nobody knows. What can be said is that water is common in the universe, because it is made of the first and third most abundant chemically active elements, hydrogen and oxygen, and that every living thing we have ever found depends on it. So the search starts with water, and keeps the other liquids on a second list.

### Energy, Which Means Electrons Moving

Chapter 2 also made the point that biological energy is, at bottom, electrons moving downhill: from a molecule that holds them loosely to one that holds them tightly, with the cell skimming off some of the energy on the way. Chapter 4 traced the machinery, the proton battery every cell on Earth runs on.

That gives a much broader picture of “energy” than sunlight. Plants and many microbes use light. But a large share of Earth’s microbes have never seen the Sun. At hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor, and in fractured rock kilometers underground, microbes live on chemical energy alone: hydrogen made when water reacts with iron-rich rock, combined with carbon dioxide; or iron, sulfur and methane compounds that hold electrons at different strengths and can be made to trade them.

Chemists call this a redox gradient, short for reduction and oxidation, the two halves of an electron changing hands. A place where two kinds of chemistry meet without being at balance, such as hot reduced fluid from rock pouring into cold oxidized seawater, is a place where there is energy to harvest. Chapter 3 described why some origin-of-life researchers think life began at exactly that kind of boundary.

For the search, this is the most important single idea in the Part. It means a world does not need sunlight on its surface to have energy for life. It needs a liquid in contact with chemistry that is out of balance. That is why the most promising places in the solar system, outside Earth, include oceans buried under kilometers of ice.

### The Elements: CHNOPS and a Few Friends

Biologists have an ugly acronym for the elements most of life’s mass is made of: CHNOPS, for carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur. Chapter 2 explained why carbon is the backbone: it makes four strong bonds, it bonds to itself in long chains and rings, and those bonds are strong enough to last but weak enough to be rearranged by enzymes at ordinary temperatures. Chapters 35 and 36 showed the same six elements at work in DNA: the sugar-phosphate backbone, the nitrogen-rich bases, the hydrogen bonds between them.

The cosmic supply is not the problem. Hydrogen, oxygen, carbon and nitrogen are among the most common elements in the universe. Comets and some meteorites carry complex organic molecules made without any life at all. Chapter 84 will look at the most recent and cleanest evidence, a sample returned from the asteroid Bennu in 2023.

The scarce ones may be phosphorus and, for some chemistries, a handful of metals. Phosphorus sits in the backbone of DNA and RNA and in ATP, the molecule cells use as small change for energy. It is much rarer than carbon in the universe and, on Earth, tends to be locked into minerals that dissolve poorly. Several origin-of-life chemists think the availability of phosphorus may be one of the real bottlenecks for life, here or anywhere. That is a working idea, and it is one reason the detection of phosphates in the ocean spray of a small moon of Saturn, in 2023, made such news. Chapter 80 tells that story.

What about silicon, the element science fiction offers as the alternative to carbon? It sits under carbon in the periodic table and also makes four bonds. But silicon-silicon bonds are weaker than carbon-carbon bonds, silicon’s bond with oxygen is so strong that it tends to end up locked in rock, and its oxide is a solid, sand, rather than a gas like carbon dioxide that can be breathed in and out. Silicon life is not impossible in any proven sense. It is just much less likely than carbon life, and nobody has a convincing design for its chemistry. Like the other liquids, it stays on the second list.

### Time, and a Place That Holds Still

The fourth item is the least discussed and maybe the most important. Chapter 4 described the gap between the first cells and the first complex ones: something like two billion years in which life was entirely microbial. Chapter 16 described the mass extinctions that repeatedly cleared the board. Life on Earth needed hundreds of millions of years to get started and billions to get interesting, and it got them because the planet stayed habitable the whole time.

That requires a stable star, an orbit that does not swing the planet between frying and freezing, and some way of keeping the climate in range. On Earth that last job is done partly by a slow geological thermostat. Carbon dioxide is pulled out of the air when rain weathers rock, and returned by volcanoes. When the planet warms, weathering speeds up and draws down more carbon dioxide, which cools it again. When it cools, weathering slows and volcanic carbon dioxide builds up. The loop runs on timescales of hundreds of thousands of years, and it needs plate tectonics, liquid water and land.

Whether a planet has to have all that machinery to keep life going for billions of years is a working question. It is part of a larger argument, taken up in Chapter 83, about whether Earth is ordinary or rare.

### The Edges of the Envelope

Here is what Earth’s own microbes say about how wide the list really is. These are measured limits, not guesses.

Heat. The record for growth is held by an archaeon called *Methanopyrus kandleri*, strain 116, which in 2008 was shown to multiply at 122 degrees Celsius under high pressure, in water that would boil at the surface. Earlier, in 2003, another archaeon nicknamed Strain 121 had been shown to survive a sterilizing autoclave.

Cold. Microbes keep metabolizing in brine channels inside sea ice and in permafrost at well below zero degrees Celsius, as long as salt keeps a little liquid water around them.

Acid and alkali. Microbes thrive in mine drainage with a pH near zero and in soda lakes with a pH above eleven.

Depth. Living cells have been recovered from rock several kilometers below the surface, and from sediments far beneath the sea floor, where they divide perhaps once in centuries. Some estimates put the total mass of this deep biosphere at a sizable fraction of all the microbial life on Earth.

Dryness and radiation. *Deinococcus*, from the meat can, also survives severe drying, which may be the real reason it can repair so much damage: drying breaks DNA too. The tardigrade, a half-millimeter animal with eight stubby legs, can dry itself into a dormant state called a tun and survive vacuum, extreme cold and heavy radiation. In 2007, on a European satellite mission called FOTON-M3, dried tardigrades were exposed directly to space for ten days, and some revived and reproduced afterward.

These organisms are called extremophiles, lovers of the extreme, although that is our label, not theirs. To the archaeon at a vent, 100 degrees is normal and the sea floor at 4 degrees is the extreme. The lesson is that life, once it exists, adapts to an extraordinarily wide range of conditions, provided liquid water is present at least some of the time. Dormancy is not growth. A tardigrade in space is surviving, not living. But it does show that the boundaries are wider than anyone in 1950 would have drawn them.

### Habitable Is Not Inhabited

There is a trap in all of this, and it is worth naming before the next chapters walk into it.

Showing that a place has liquid water, energy, the right elements and time shows that it is habitable: that life as we know it could live there. It does not show that life began there, or that it ever arrived. Habitable is a statement about conditions. Inhabited is a statement about history.

On Earth, the two are nearly the same, because life has had four billion years to reach every corner. Almost every habitable niche on this planet has been colonized, from the boiling vents to the clouds, and that makes it easy to forget that the colonization took time and had to start somewhere. A sterile habitable world is perfectly possible. Chapter 3 described how hard the origin is to explain even on a planet where we know it happened. Whether a habitable place usually becomes inhabited is the deepest unknown in this Part, and the only way to answer it is to find a second example, or a convincing lack of one.

So when the following chapters say that Mars once had lakes, or that a moon of Saturn has a salty ocean with energy and phosphorus, read it as “the first three boxes are ticked.” The fourth box, whether anything is actually there, is the one the missions are trying to open.

### The Punchline

The list of what life needs is short: a liquid, almost certainly water; a source of energy, which can be chemical as well as light; carbon and a handful of other common elements, with phosphorus possibly the scarce one; and a long, stable stretch of time.

Earth’s extremophiles show that, once life exists, it can work at the edges of all four. Nothing on the list is rare in the universe. Water, carbon and chemical energy are found almost everywhere we look. What remains unknown is the one step that is not on the list: how often the ingredients actually start copying themselves.

The next chapter takes the first item, liquid water, and asks where in a planetary system it can exist. The answer is a zone around each star, and it is less tidy than the textbooks make it sound.

## 78. The Goldilocks Problem — Habitable Zones and the Planets in Them

### Twins That Went Wrong

Venus and Earth are nearly the same size. Venus has about 95 percent of Earth’s diameter and 82 percent of its mass. Both formed from the same disk of gas and dust, at about the same time, from much the same rocks. If you had looked at the inner solar system from far away four billion years ago, you might have called them twins.

Today the surface of Venus is about 465 degrees Celsius, hot enough to melt lead, under an atmosphere of carbon dioxide about 90 times as heavy as ours, with clouds of sulfuric acid. The Soviet Venera landers that reached the surface between 1970 and 1982 lasted between about 20 minutes and two hours before the heat and pressure killed them. Earth, meanwhile, has oceans and forests and you.

The difference is not mostly size or composition. It is mostly distance. Venus orbits at about 72 percent of Earth’s distance from the Sun and receives nearly twice as much sunlight. Somewhere in its history, any water it had evaporated, the water vapor trapped heat, which evaporated more water, and the planet ran away into the furnace it is now. Water vapor high in the atmosphere was broken apart by ultraviolet light, and the hydrogen escaped into space. The water is gone for good.

Mars sits on the other side, at about 1.52 times Earth’s distance. Chapter 79 will show that it once had rivers and lakes. Now it is a cold desert with an atmosphere less than one percent as dense as ours.

Three rocky planets, three outcomes, and the main variable is how much starlight each one gets. That is the idea behind the habitable zone.

### The Zone, Defined

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star at which a rocky planet with the right kind of atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface. Astronomers also call it the Goldilocks zone, after the porridge that was neither too hot nor too cold.

The inner edge is set by the runaway greenhouse that happened to Venus. Move a watery planet closer to its star and at some point the oceans start to evaporate faster than the planet can shed the heat. The outer edge is set by the opposite problem. Farther out, a planet needs more and more carbon dioxide in its air to stay warm, and at some distance carbon dioxide itself starts to freeze out as clouds and frost, and its greenhouse warming stops being able to keep up.

The classic calculation was made by the climate scientist James Kasting and colleagues in 1993 and updated by Ravi Kopparapu and colleagues in 2013. For a star like the Sun, the conservative version of the zone runs from roughly 0.95 to 1.7 times Earth’s distance. More optimistic versions, which allow for the possibility that early Venus and early Mars were both habitable for a while, stretch it from about 0.75 to 1.8. Earth is inside every version. Venus, at 0.72, lies just inside the inner edge of even the most generous one, and Mars is near the outer edge.

The physics behind the zone is nothing more than energy balance, which is the same physics a radiator or a greenhouse runs on. A planet warms until it radiates as much energy as it absorbs. The amount it absorbs falls with the square of the distance from the star: twice as far, a quarter of the light. That is why the zone scales with the star’s brightness. A star a hundred times brighter than the Sun has its zone about ten times farther out, because the square root of 100 is 10. A star a hundred times dimmer has its zone about ten times closer in.

### What the Zone Leaves Out

The habitable zone is a useful first filter. It is not a list of planets with life, and even as a list of planets with liquid water it is rough. At least four things can push a planet out of or into habitability without changing its distance.

Atmosphere. Without an atmosphere, liquid water cannot exist on a surface at all; it boils off or freezes. The Moon sits in the middle of the Sun’s habitable zone and is dry and airless. Too much atmosphere can be just as bad. A thick envelope of hydrogen can keep a planet warm far beyond the outer edge, which is one reason the edges are fuzzy.

Mass. A small planet loses its internal heat quickly, its volcanism dies, and its atmosphere leaks away to space. Mars is about a tenth of Earth’s mass and seems to have lost its global magnetic field about four billion years ago. NASA’s MAVEN orbiter, in Mars orbit since 2014, has measured the planet’s air being stripped by the solar wind today and concluded that much of the ancient atmosphere was lost that way. Too large, and a planet tends to hold on to a deep envelope of hydrogen and helium and become a small Neptune instead of a big Earth.

Clouds and chemistry. Clouds can reflect light and cool a planet or trap heat and warm it, and the climate models disagree about how they behave near the edges of the zone.

Time. Stars brighten as they age. The Sun is about 30 percent brighter now than it was when Earth formed, which raises a puzzle of its own: early Earth should have been frozen, and it was not. Geologists call this the faint young Sun problem, and the leading explanation is a stronger greenhouse effect from more carbon dioxide and methane in the early air. Looking forward, the brightening continues at roughly ten percent per billion years. In something like a billion years, the inner edge of the Sun’s habitable zone will move outward past Earth and the oceans will start to go the way of Venus’s. Earth’s habitable lifetime is long, but it is not forever.

And then there is the most important exception of all, which is that the habitable zone is a zone for surface water. It says nothing about water under ice, heated from inside by a planet’s own warmth or by tides. Chapter 80 is about worlds far outside the Sun’s habitable zone that have more liquid water than Earth does.

### Small Red Stars

Most stars are not like the Sun. About three in every four stars in the Milky Way are red dwarfs, also called M dwarfs: stars between about a tenth and a half of the Sun’s mass, and between a thousandth and a few percent of its brightness. They are dim, cool and very long-lived. A red dwarf can shine steadily for hundreds of billions or trillions of years, far longer than the universe has existed.

Their habitable zones are tiny and close in. For a small red dwarf, the zone may sit at a few percent of Earth’s distance from the Sun, and a planet there completes an orbit in days. That has two consequences that matter for life.

First, such a planet is probably tidally locked. Just as the Moon always shows Earth the same face, a close-in planet tends to end up with one side always facing its star, in permanent day, and the other in permanent night. Early models suggested the night side would freeze the atmosphere out. Later models showed that an atmosphere of reasonable thickness can carry heat around the planet and keep it from collapsing. A tidally locked world might have a habitable ring around the boundary between day and night, or a single warm ocean under the star. That is speculative but physically allowed.

Second, red dwarfs are violent when young. They flare often and strongly, and for their first billion years or so they put out much more ultraviolet and X-ray light, relative to their total output, than the Sun does. A planet in the habitable zone of a red dwarf takes that blast from close range. It may lose its atmosphere and much of its water before the star settles down. Whether the planets of red dwarfs can keep any air at all is one of the most pressing questions in the field right now, and as Chapter 81 shows, the James Webb Space Telescope has started answering it.

### The Planets We Have Found

Until 1995, the number of planets known around other Sun-like stars was zero. The first was found in 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz at the Geneva Observatory: a planet about half the mass of Jupiter orbiting the star 51 Pegasi every 4.2 days, far too close and hot for life. It earned them a share of the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2019.

By September 2026 NASA’s Exoplanet Archive listed more than 6,300 confirmed planets around other stars. Most were found by two methods.

The transit method watches a star’s brightness. If a planet’s orbit happens to be lined up edge-on from our point of view, the planet passes in front of the star once per orbit and dims it slightly. Earth passing in front of the Sun would dim it by less than one part in ten thousand. NASA’s Kepler space telescope, which stared at one patch of sky from 2009 to 2013 and then worked a series of shorter campaigns until 2018, found most of the planets known today this way. Its successor, TESS, launched in 2018, surveys the whole sky for planets around nearby, bright stars.

The radial velocity method watches a star wobble. A planet and its star both orbit their shared center of mass, so the star moves slightly toward and away from us, and its light is shifted very slightly blue and red by the Doppler effect. Earth makes the Sun wobble at about nine centimeters per second, slower than a walking tortoise. The best spectrographs today can reach below a meter per second.

From Kepler’s statistics, astronomers have tried to estimate how common rocky planets in the habitable zones of Sun-like stars are. A widely cited 2021 analysis by Steve Bryson and colleagues put the figure, for the conservative zone, somewhere between about a third and three fifths of a planet per star, with uncertainties wider than the estimates themselves. The exact number is uncertain. The order of magnitude is not. In a galaxy of a few hundred billion stars, temperate rocky planets number in the billions.

Some of them are close. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun at about 4.2 light-years, has a planet, Proxima b, at least as massive as Earth, inside its habitable zone. It orbits a flaring red dwarf every 11.2 days and is not known to transit, so its atmosphere, if any, cannot be studied the easy way. About 40 light-years away, the red dwarf TRAPPIST-1 has seven Earth-sized planets, all of which do transit, three or four of them in or near the habitable zone. They have become the most studied small planets outside the solar system, and Chapter 81 will say what the Webb telescope has found there.

### Settled, Working, Speculative

It helps to sort this chapter by confidence.

Settled. Liquid water on a surface requires a certain range of energy from a star, and that range defines a zone that scales with the star’s brightness. Rocky planets are common, and many orbit in the habitable zones of their stars.

Working. The exact edges of the zone, the role of clouds, how much atmosphere small planets keep, and whether red dwarf planets can hold air at all. The value of the fraction of stars with temperate rocky planets, to better than a factor of two or three.

Speculative. That planets in the zone usually have oceans, or plate tectonics, or a carbon thermostat like Earth’s. These are reasonable hopes, not measurements, and we have exactly one example of a planet that has all of them.

### The Punchline

The habitable zone is the band around a star where a planet could keep liquid water on its surface. Earth is in the middle of the Sun’s zone, and Venus and Mars, at its edges, show what happens when the balance tips one way or the other.

The zone is a filter, not a verdict. It ignores atmosphere, mass, clouds, the age of the star, and water under ice. Within those limits it has done its job: it has told astronomers that temperate, rocky planets are not rare. There are billions in our galaxy alone, and a few of the nearest are now being looked at closely.

But the best place to test whether habitable means inhabited is not light-years away. It is the planet next door, which had water, lost it, and has been visited by more spacecraft than any other world. The next chapter goes to Mars.

## 79. Mars — The Planet That Dried Out

### Leopard Spots

On 21 July 2024, NASA’s Perseverance rover drilled into a reddish rock in an ancient river channel on Mars. The rock was about a meter long, shaped roughly like an arrowhead, and the rover team had nicknamed it Cheyava Falls, after a waterfall in the Grand Canyon. It was full of white veins of calcium sulfate, the mineral left behind when water once seeped through cracks. What had made the team stop, though, were the spots.

Scattered across the rock were pale patches a millimeter or so across, each ringed by a dark rim. The team called them leopard spots. On Earth, spots like that form in sediments when chemical reactions shift iron and other elements around, and very often the reactions are run by microbes, which gain energy by moving electrons between iron, sulfur and organic matter. Perseverance’s instruments found that the rims were rich in two minerals: vivianite, an iron phosphate, and greigite, an iron sulfide. Its spectrometer had also picked up signs of organic carbon in the rock.

The core sample was named Sapphire Canyon. It went into a sealed titanium tube inside the rover. In September 2025, after more than a year of analysis, the team published its paper in Nature. The careful phrase the team used was “potential biosignature”: a feature that could have been made by life, for which the non-biological explanations are possible but look less likely. NASA’s leadership called it the clearest sign of life yet found on Mars.

It may be. This chapter explains why the scientists themselves are being so careful, why the answer is sitting in a tube on another planet, and why, as of 2026, nobody knows when the tube will come home.

### A Wet Young Mars

The first close images of Mars, from Mariner 4 in 1965, showed a cratered surface that looked like the Moon, and the disappointment was immediate. Six years later Mariner 9 went into orbit and mapped the whole planet, and the picture changed. There were enormous canyons, giant volcanoes and, most important, channels that looked as though they had been cut by flowing water.

Since then, the evidence for an ancient wet Mars has become overwhelming. The two rovers Spirit and Opportunity, which landed in January 2004, found minerals that form only in water, including hematite spheres that the team called blueberries, and sulfates that suggested the water had sometimes been acidic. Orbiters mapped clays, which form when water alters rock over long periods. River deltas, ancient lake shorelines and gravel conglomerates, rounded pebbles that can only be made by flowing streams, all point the same way.

The working picture is this. More than about 3.5 billion years ago, Mars had a thicker atmosphere and, at least at times, lakes, rivers and perhaps a northern sea. That is roughly the same period in which Chapters 3 and 4 placed the origin of life on Earth. Then Mars lost most of its air. Its global magnetic field shut down, the solar wind stripped the upper atmosphere, and the small planet, with less internal heat than Earth, lost the volcanic activity that might have replaced it. The water froze into the ground and the polar caps, or escaped to space.

If life began on Mars in that early window, it might have died out, or it might have retreated underground where liquid water could persist. Either way, the place to look for its traces is in rocks from the wet period.

### Viking: The Experiment That Said Yes

The first serious attempt to look was the most ambitious. Viking 1 landed on Mars on 20 July 1976, seven years to the day after Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. Viking 2 followed on 3 September. Each carried a biology package with three experiments designed to detect living microbes in the soil.

The most famous was the Labeled Release experiment, designed by the engineer Gilbert Levin. It dripped a nutrient broth tagged with radioactive carbon-14 onto a soil sample and watched for radioactive gas. If microbes ate the nutrients, they should breathe out radioactive carbon dioxide. They seemed to. Gas came off at once. When the experiment was repeated with soil that had been heated to sterilize it, the gas did not come off. By the criteria written before launch, that was a positive result.

But another instrument, a gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer, heated the soil and looked for organic molecules, the carbon compounds any life would be made of. It found essentially none, to a sensitivity of parts per billion. Life without organic molecules made no sense. Most of the Viking team concluded that the soil contained some reactive chemical that mimicked metabolism. Levin argued for the rest of his life that Viking had found life.

The likely culprit turned up 32 years later. In 2008 NASA’s Phoenix lander, near the north pole, found perchlorate in the soil, a salt of chlorine and oxygen that is a strong oxidizer when heated. Perchlorate and the chemicals radiation makes from it can break down organic molecules, which may also explain why Viking’s oven found nothing: heating the soil with perchlorate in it would have burned up any organics before they could be measured. Most researchers now think the Labeled Release result was chemistry, not biology, and that Viking’s organics test was blinded by the same salt.

Viking taught a lesson that has shaped every mission since. You cannot design a single experiment that says “life” with certainty, because a planet’s own chemistry can fake most of what a microbe does. You need to understand the environment first.

### ALH84001: The Rock on the White House Lawn

On 27 December 1984, a team collecting meteorites from the ice of the Allan Hills in Antarctica picked up a potato-sized greenish rock. It was cataloged as ALH84001 and, about a decade later, identified as a piece of Mars: its trapped gases matched the Martian atmosphere measured by Viking. It had crystallized about four billion years ago, was knocked off Mars by an impact about 17 million years ago, and fell on Antarctica about 13,000 years ago.

On 7 August 1996, a team led by David McKay at NASA’s Johnson Space Center announced in Science that the rock contained possible evidence of ancient Martian life. There were tiny globules of carbonate minerals, organic molecules called polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, chains of magnetite crystals of the sort some Earth bacteria make, and, in electron microscope images, worm-shaped objects a few tens of nanometers long. President Bill Clinton spoke about the rock on the South Lawn of the White House.

Each piece of evidence was challenged, and over the next decades each found a plausible non-biological explanation. The worm shapes are far smaller than any known cell, too small to hold the machinery Chapter 38 described. The magnetite and the carbonate can form without life. In 2022, a study led by Andrew Steele showed that the rock’s organic molecules most likely formed when hot water reacted with minerals, a purely geological process. Today very few researchers think ALH84001 contains fossils.

But it changed the field. It helped create NASA’s astrobiology program, and it showed that pieces of Mars do reach Earth. About 400 Martian meteorites are now known. That fact will matter when Chapter 84 asks whether life could have traveled between the planets.

### Curiosity: The Lake That Could Have Been Lived In

In August 2012 NASA landed Curiosity, a rover the size of a small car, in Gale Crater, a 154-kilometer basin with a mountain of layered sediment in the middle. Its job was not to look for life but to find out whether Mars had ever been able to support it.

Within months it had the answer. In mudstone at a site called Yellowknife Bay, it drilled out powder that showed clays, low salinity, a neutral pH, and a mix of chemicals in different states of oxidation, the redox gradient that Chapter 77 described as an energy source. The team announced in 2013 that Gale Crater had once held a lake that microbes like some on Earth could have lived in. Habitable, in the strict sense: the first three boxes ticked.

Curiosity has since found more. In 2018 it reported organic molecules, including sulfur-bearing ring compounds, preserved in mudstone about three billion years old. In 2025 its team reported the largest organic molecules yet found on Mars: decane, undecane and dodecane, chains of 10 to 12 carbon atoms, in a rock sample drilled years earlier and stored on board. On Earth, chains like that are often fragments of fatty acids, which are made by life, although they can also be made without it. And Curiosity has measured methane in the air of Gale Crater, with a low background that seems to rise and fall with the seasons and occasional spikes. The European Space Agency’s Trace Gas Orbiter, which can measure methane from above with great sensitivity, has seen almost none high in the atmosphere. Where the methane comes from, and where it goes, is one of the open puzzles of Mars.

None of this is evidence of life. All of it is consistent with a planet that once had the conditions for it, and that preserves organic molecules in its rocks better than anyone expected.

### Perseverance and the Tubes

Perseverance landed on 18 February 2021 in Jezero Crater, a basin 45 kilometers wide that once held a lake fed by a river. The river’s delta, a fan of layered sediment, is still visible from orbit. River deltas on Earth are among the best places to preserve traces of microbial life, because they bury fine sediment quickly.

The rover was designed for a two-part plan. It would study the rocks itself, and it would collect the most promising samples into sealed tubes for a later mission to bring back to Earth, where laboratories with equipment far too large to send to Mars could examine them. By 2026 it had filled 33 tubes, including 27 rock cores, along with samples of soil and atmosphere and empty “witness” tubes that record any contamination the rover carried from Earth. Ten tubes were set down on the crater floor in a backup depot in late 2022 and early 2023, in case the rover failed.

Sapphire Canyon, the core from Cheyava Falls, is one of the tubes still on board. Here is why its scientists say “potential.” The minerals in the leopard spots, vivianite and greigite, form on Earth mainly when microbes reduce iron and sulfate while consuming organic matter. They can also form without life, but usually at higher temperatures, and the rocks of the Bright Angel formation, where Cheyava Falls sits, show no sign of having been heated. So the biological explanation fits better. But “fits better” is not “proven,” and a mudstone on Mars cannot be put under the microscopes, isotope instruments and repeated independent tests that a claim of extraterrestrial life demands. That needs the rock on Earth.

### The Return That Did Not Happen

The plan to bring the tubes home was called Mars Sample Return, a joint program of NASA and the European Space Agency. It required a lander, a small rocket to launch the samples into Mars orbit, and an orbiter to catch them and fly them back. It would have been the first launch from the surface of another planet.

It was also expensive. In 2023 an independent review estimated its cost at 8 to 11 billion dollars and said the original schedule had no realistic chance of being met. NASA spent 2024 and 2025 looking for cheaper designs and narrowed the options to two, each estimated at roughly 6 to 7 billion dollars. In January 2026, Congress passed NASA’s budget for fiscal year 2026 without funding the program. The money was redirected to a broader line for future Mars missions. Mars Sample Return, as designed, is effectively canceled.

That leaves the tubes waiting. A different American return mission may yet be designed. China, meanwhile, plans a mission of its own, Tianwen-3, to launch around 2028 and bring Martian samples to Earth around 2031. It would land at a site of China’s choosing, not at Jezero. And the European rover Rosalind Franklin, delayed for years and now planned for launch in 2028, carries a drill designed to reach two meters below the surface, deep enough to get beneath the layer where radiation and perchlorate destroy organic molecules.

### What Would Count

Suppose a sample does come back. What would convince scientists that something in it was alive once?

No single test. The standard that has emerged since ALH84001 is a combination: shapes that look like cells or microbial mats, at sizes that make sense; chemistry that matches what cells do, such as minerals made by microbial redox reactions; organic molecules with patterns that chemistry alone tends not to produce, such as chains of a preferred length or molecules of one handedness, a point Chapter 84 returns to; and isotope ratios showing that light carbon atoms were preferred over heavy ones, a fingerprint of enzymes. Each line can be faked by geology. All of them together, in the same place, would be hard to fake.

NASA has even written down a seven-step scale for life detection, from “a signal is detected” to “independent follow-up confirms the result is biological.” Cheyava Falls, on that scale, is in the early steps: a signal detected, contamination largely ruled out, non-biological explanations considered but not excluded.

### The Punchline

Mars was wet when Earth’s life was young, and then it dried out and lost its air. Viking’s experiment seemed to say yes and was most likely fooled by perchlorate. ALH84001’s fossils most likely turned out to be minerals. Curiosity showed that Gale Crater once held a habitable lake and that Martian rocks keep organic molecules for billions of years.

Perseverance has found, in a rock called Cheyava Falls, the best candidate yet: chemistry that on Earth is usually made by microbes, in rocks that were never heated. It is a potential biosignature, not a discovery. The core that could settle it is sealed in a tube on Mars, and the mission to bring it home has lost its funding.

If Mars disappoints, there is another kind of place to look, one that the habitable zone of Chapter 78 leaves out entirely. Some of the largest bodies of liquid water in the solar system are hundreds of millions of kilometers past Mars, under ice. They are the subject of the next chapter.

## 80. Oceans Under Ice — Europa, Enceladus and Titan

### Seventeen Kilometers a Second

On 9 October 2008, NASA’s Cassini spacecraft, which had been orbiting Saturn for four years, dived toward a small white moon called Enceladus and flew straight through a fountain of ice coming out of its south pole. It passed about 25 kilometers above the surface at 17.7 kilometers per second, about twenty times faster than a rifle bullet. In the few seconds it spent inside the spray, an instrument called the Cosmic Dust Analyzer caught individual ice grains. Each one hit a metal plate so hard that it vaporized into a puff of charged fragments, and the instrument weighed the fragments.

Enceladus is only about 500 kilometers across. It is about as wide as Great Britain. At Saturn’s distance, sunlight is about a hundredth of what Earth gets, and the moon’s surface is about minus 200 degrees Celsius. By the habitable zone of Chapter 78, it should be dead and frozen solid. Yet it was spraying water into space.

Seventeen years later, in October 2025, a team led by Nozair Khawaja published its analysis of those grains. They were freshly ejected, only minutes old when they hit Cassini, and they carried organic molecules that had not been seen before in Enceladus’s spray: esters, ethers, and chains and rings of carbon, with hints of nitrogen and oxygen built in. These are the kinds of compounds that form in warm water reacting with rock. Two years earlier another team had found phosphates in the moon’s ice. Put together with what Cassini had already learned, they described a small world with a salty ocean, a source of chemical energy, organic chemistry and phosphorus. Three boxes ticked, and the third one ticked more firmly than anywhere else outside Earth.

This chapter is about how worlds far outside any habitable zone can have liquid water, and the three moons that are now the main targets of the search.

### Heat From Squeezing

The habitable zone assumes that a planet’s warmth comes from its star. For moons of giant planets there is a second source: tides.

A moon in a slightly elongated orbit is pulled harder by its planet when it is close and less hard when it is far. The changing pull stretches and relaxes the moon’s interior every orbit, like a rubber ball squeezed in a hand. The flexing turns orbital energy into heat. If neighboring moons keep tugging the orbit so that it never becomes perfectly circular, the heating never stops.

The idea earned one of the best-timed predictions in planetary science. In March 1979, three researchers, Stanton Peale, Patrick Cassen and Ray Reynolds, published a paper arguing that tidal heating should melt the interior of Jupiter’s moon Io and might make it volcanically active. The paper appeared three days before Voyager 1 flew past Io. Voyager’s images showed eight erupting volcanoes. Io is now known to be the most volcanic body in the solar system.

Farther out from Jupiter, the same heating is gentler. It is not enough to melt rock, but it can be enough to keep water liquid beneath a shell of ice. Ice is a good insulator, and it floats, as Chapter 77 pointed out, so a frozen surface protects the water below it. Add the heat of radioactive elements decaying in the rocky core, and several moons of Jupiter and Saturn appear to hold oceans that have been liquid for billions of years.

![Figure 49. A schematic cross-section of an icy ocean moon such as Europa or Enceladus: a shell of ice over a global salty ocean, in contact with a rocky interior where hot water may react with rock at the seafloor. Thicknesses are illustrative, not to scale.](figures/figs/fig49.png)

### Europa: More Water Than Earth

Europa is slightly smaller than our Moon. Its surface is bright, young ice crossed by long reddish cracks, with very few craters, which means something keeps resurfacing it. The idea that an ocean lies beneath was proposed in the late 1970s and strengthened by Voyager images, but the decisive evidence came from NASA’s Galileo spacecraft, which orbited Jupiter from 1995 to 2003.

Galileo carried a magnetometer. As Europa moved through Jupiter’s powerful, tilted magnetic field, the magnetometer detected a second, induced field coming from Europa itself, which flipped as Jupiter’s field changed direction. That is what happens when a changing magnetic field passes through an electrical conductor. Ice does not conduct well. Salty water does. In 2000 the Galileo team, led by Margaret Kivelson, reported that the best explanation was a global layer of salt water under the ice.

The current picture is an ice shell perhaps 15 to 25 kilometers thick, though estimates range from a few kilometers to more, over an ocean perhaps 60 to 150 kilometers deep. That ocean would hold about twice as much water as all of Earth’s oceans combined. Its floor is rock, which is important. Water in contact with rock can react with it, as it does at Earth’s hydrothermal vents, releasing hydrogen and other chemicals that hold energy.

The surface adds another energy source. Jupiter’s radiation belts bombard Europa’s ice and split water molecules, producing oxygen and hydrogen peroxide, which are oxidants. If the ice shell slowly overturns, it could carry these oxidants down to the ocean, where they could meet the reduced chemicals coming up from the rock. That is the redox gradient of Chapter 77, built across a whole world. In 2023 the James Webb Space Telescope found carbon dioxide concentrated in a region of disrupted terrain called Tarr Regio, in a form that suggests the carbon came up from inside. Sodium chloride, ordinary table salt, had already been identified on the surface.

When Galileo’s fuel ran low in 2003, NASA deliberately crashed it into Jupiter. The reason was planetary protection: the spacecraft might have carried Earth microbes, and an accidental crash on Europa could contaminate the ocean everyone wanted to explore.

### Enceladus: The Ocean That Sprays Itself Into Space

Enceladus was a surprise. Nobody expected a moon that small to be active. In 2005, Cassini’s magnetometer noticed that Saturn’s magnetic field was being disturbed near the moon’s south pole, and later that year cameras showed jets of ice spraying from four long, warm fractures the team named the tiger stripes. More than a hundred individual jets have since been mapped. Some of the spray falls back as snow. The rest feeds a faint ring around Saturn, the E ring.

Over the following decade Cassini built up the case, one measurement at a time.

The spray is salty water, not just ice, and its grains include sodium salts that mean the water has been in contact with rock.

In 2015, tiny grains of silica, a few nanometers across, were traced to the moon. On Earth, grains like that form when water hotter than about 90 degrees Celsius dissolves rock and then cools. The likely explanation is hydrothermal activity on Enceladus’s seafloor.

Also in 2015, careful measurement of how the moon wobbles as it orbits showed that its ice shell is not attached to its core. The ocean is global.

In 2017, Cassini reported molecular hydrogen in the plume. On Earth, the reaction between hydrogen and carbon dioxide to make methane is one of the oldest ways microbes make a living. The hydrogen shows that a chemical energy source of that kind exists in Enceladus’s ocean today.

In 2023, a team led by Frank Postberg found sodium phosphates in ice grains of the E ring, at concentrations at least a hundred times higher than in Earth’s oceans. Phosphorus, the element Chapter 77 marked as the possible bottleneck, is there.

And the 2025 work on fresh grains added more complex organic molecules, the kind that could be steps on the road to biochemistry, or simply the products of hot water on rock.

Cassini itself was destroyed in September 2017, steered into Saturn for the same planetary-protection reason as Galileo. Its instruments were never designed to detect life. A future mission, which the European Space Agency has chosen as the theme of one of its large missions in the 2040s, would fly through the plume, or land near the tiger stripes, with instruments that can tell biology from chemistry. The appeal is obvious. On Europa you have to get through the ice. Enceladus sends its ocean up to you.

### Titan: The Strangest Familiar World

Titan, Saturn’s largest moon, is bigger than the planet Mercury and is the only moon in the solar system with a thick atmosphere. Its air is mostly nitrogen, like ours, and about one and a half times as dense at the surface. But at minus 179 degrees, water is rock-hard ice. The liquid on Titan is methane and ethane, which rain from the clouds, carve river channels and fill lakes and seas near the poles. The largest, Kraken Mare, is bigger than the Caspian Sea.

On 14 January 2005, the European Huygens probe parachuted through Titan’s haze and landed on a damp plain strewn with rounded pebbles of water ice. It sent back data for about 72 minutes from the surface before Cassini, its relay, sank below the horizon. It remains the most distant landing ever made.

Titan’s atmosphere is a vast organic chemistry factory. Sunlight and particles from Saturn’s magnetic field break up methane and nitrogen high in the air, and the fragments recombine into larger molecules that drift down as an orange haze and coat the surface with sand-like organic grains. The moon may also have a water ocean deep beneath its ice crust: Cassini measured the way Titan flexes under Saturn’s tides, and the flexing is too large for a solid interior.

That gives Titan two very different chances. One is life as we know it, in the buried water ocean, which is probably hard to reach. The other is life as we do not know it, in the methane lakes on the surface, using some chemistry that works in a nonpolar solvent at extreme cold. In 2015, chemists proposed a kind of membrane, nicknamed an azotosome, made from small nitrogen-bearing molecules, that might work in liquid methane. In 2017 the ALMA radio telescope detected one candidate building block, acrylonitrile, in Titan’s atmosphere. Later calculations, in 2020, suggested that such membranes would not actually assemble under Titan’s conditions. The idea of methane life is speculative, and it remains unsupported by any evidence. But Titan is the one place where it could be tested.

NASA’s Dragonfly mission is designed to do some of that testing. Dragonfly is a rotorcraft, a nuclear-powered drone about the size of a car with eight rotors, which can fly comfortably in Titan’s dense air and low gravity. It is scheduled to launch in July 2028 and arrive in 2034, landing near a crater called Selk, where an impact may once have melted water ice and mixed it with surface organics. It will fly from site to site, sampling surface material and analyzing its chemistry. It is not a life detector. It is a prebiotic chemistry laboratory, sent to the best natural experiment in the solar system on what happens when organic molecules and water meet without life getting there first.

### Who Is Going

As of 2026, three missions are on their way to the outer solar system, or soon will be.

Europa Clipper launched on 14 October 2024. After a flyby of Mars in 2025 and one of Earth in December 2026, it is scheduled to enter orbit around Jupiter in April 2030 and then make 49 close flybys of Europa, some as low as 25 kilometers. It carries ice-penetrating radar to measure the shell, a magnetometer to measure the ocean, and instruments to analyze dust and gas it passes through. Its question is whether Europa is habitable, not whether it is inhabited.

The European Space Agency’s JUICE mission launched in April 2023 and will arrive at Jupiter in 2031. It will fly past Europa twice and then go into orbit around Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system, which is also thought to have a buried ocean, probably sandwiched between layers of ice.

Dragonfly is being built for its 2028 launch to Titan.

None of them can detect life directly. All of them will narrow down where a later mission should look, and what instruments it should carry.

### The Punchline

The habitable zone was drawn for water on a surface. Some of the largest bodies of liquid water in the solar system are under ice, kept warm by tides and radioactive decay, around planets far outside the Sun’s zone.

Europa probably has twice as much water as Earth’s oceans, in contact with rock, with oxidants arriving from above. Enceladus sprays its ocean into space, and Cassini found in the spray salt, hydrogen, silica from hot water, phosphates and complex organic molecules. Titan has organic chemistry on a planetary scale, methane lakes that might host a chemistry unlike ours, and possibly a water ocean beneath.

Our own solar system may therefore hold not one second chance for life but several. To look farther, around other stars, we cannot send spacecraft. We can only read starlight. The next chapter explains how a planet’s air can be read from light-years away, and what happened the first time someone claimed to have read a sign of life in it.

## 81. Reading Alien Air — Starlight, Biosignatures and the K2-18 b Affair

### Carl Sagan Looks for Life on Earth

On 8 December 1990, NASA’s Galileo spacecraft, on its way to Jupiter, swung past Earth to borrow some of its orbital speed. The astronomer Carl Sagan saw an opportunity in the maneuver. Here was a robotic probe carrying the kind of instruments that might one day visit an unknown world. Why not point them at Earth and ask, without cheating, whether they could tell that it was inhabited?

In 1993 Sagan and his colleagues published the answer in Nature, under the title “A search for life on Earth from the Galileo spacecraft.” Galileo saw an atmosphere rich in oxygen. It saw methane, at a concentration enormously higher than it should be in air full of oxygen, since the two react and destroy each other; something had to be replenishing the methane fast. It saw a sharp rise in reflected light just beyond the red end of the visible spectrum over land, which is the signature of chlorophyll-bearing plants. And it picked up narrowband radio transmissions, modulated in ways no natural source produces. The team’s conclusion: life was likely, and some of it was probably technological.

The exercise did two things. It showed that a planet can announce its biology in its light. And it showed that the strongest evidence was not any single gas but a combination that could not last without something keeping it going. This chapter is about how astronomers are trying to repeat Sagan’s test on planets around other stars, where the probe is replaced by a telescope trillions of kilometers away.

### Starlight Through a Planet’s Air

The trick is the transit, described in Chapter 78. When a planet passes in front of its star, most of the starlight that would have reached us is blocked by the solid planet. But a thin ring of light passes through the planet’s atmosphere on the way. Molecules in that air absorb light at particular wavelengths, fingerprints set by the quantum energy levels of molecules, the same physics the earlier volumes of this series use to read the light of stars. At those wavelengths the planet looks very slightly larger, because its atmosphere is opaque there.

So astronomers measure the depth of the transit, the dip in starlight, at many wavelengths at once. A plot of transit depth against wavelength is called a transmission spectrum. Bumps in it are molecules.

![Figure 50. The principle of transmission spectroscopy. During a transit, a thin ring of starlight filters through the planet’s atmosphere. Wavelengths that the atmosphere’s molecules absorb are dimmed slightly more, so the planet appears larger at those wavelengths, producing bumps in the measured spectrum.](figures/figs/fig50.png)

The signals are tiny. For an Earth-sized planet in front of a Sun-like star, the extra dimming from the atmosphere would be less than a part per million. The method works best for planets with puffy atmospheres around small stars, because the planet blocks a larger fraction of the star. That is one reason so much attention goes to red dwarfs.

The first atmosphere of a planet outside the solar system was detected this way in 2001, when the Hubble Space Telescope saw sodium in the air of a hot Jupiter called HD 209458 b. The real change came with the James Webb Space Telescope, launched on 25 December 2021, with a 6.5-meter gold-coated mirror and instruments built for infrared light, where many important molecules have their strongest fingerprints. In 2022, in its first months of science, Webb found carbon dioxide in the atmosphere of a hot giant planet called WASP-39 b, with a clarity no previous telescope had come close to.

### What Counts as a Biosignature

A biosignature is anything that is best explained by life. For a planet’s air, that means gases, or combinations of gases, that living things make and geology does not, or not in the same amounts.

Oxygen is the obvious candidate. Earth’s air is about 21 percent oxygen only because photosynthesis keeps it topped up, as Chapter 4 explained in describing the first pollution disaster. Without life, oxygen reacts with rock and volcanic gases and disappears in a few million years. Ozone, which forms from oxygen in sunlight, is easier to detect at some wavelengths and serves as a proxy.

But oxygen alone can be faked. On a planet that loses its water, ultraviolet light from the star splits water molecules, the hydrogen escapes, and the oxygen is left behind. This false positive is expected to be most likely around the violent young red dwarfs of Chapter 78, which is exactly where the planets easiest to observe are.

The strongest biosignature is a combination out of balance. The chemist James Lovelock argued this in 1965, while working with NASA on how to detect life on Mars. A lifeless planet’s atmosphere drifts toward chemical equilibrium, a state in which nothing reacts any more. Life holds an atmosphere away from equilibrium. Oxygen and methane together, which Galileo saw on Earth, are the textbook example: they destroy each other, so if both are present in quantity, something must be producing both all the time.

Other candidates include nitrous oxide, made by soil microbes, and a family of sulfur compounds made on Earth almost only by marine life. The most famous of these is dimethyl sulfide, or DMS, the molecule that gives the seaside its smell. It is produced mostly by plankton. Remember that molecule.

### What Webb Has Found So Far

Webb’s most important result on small planets so far is a negative one, which is useful precisely because it narrows the search.

TRAPPIST-1, the red dwarf with seven Earth-sized planets, was an early target. In 2023, measurements of the heat glowing from the innermost planet, TRAPPIST-1 b, showed a dayside at about 230 degrees Celsius, consistent with bare rock and no thick atmosphere to carry heat around. The second planet, TRAPPIST-1 c, which some had hoped might be a Venus-like world with a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere, turned out not to have one either.

The planet in the middle of the habitable zone, TRAPPIST-1 e, is harder. Its first four transits, analyzed in 2025, ruled out a thick atmosphere dominated by hydrogen and made thick carbon dioxide atmospheres like those of Venus or ancient Mars unlikely. They could not distinguish bare rock from a nitrogen-rich atmosphere with traces of other gases, like Earth’s. A longer program of transits is running, and additional observations in 2026 have not changed the picture. The biggest obstacle is the star itself. Red dwarfs are covered in dark spots and bright patches, and those can imprint false features on a transmission spectrum. Separating star from planet is now one of the main jobs of the field.

Other results have been more encouraging. In 2024, Webb detected what appears to be a genuine secondary atmosphere, probably of carbon dioxide or carbon monoxide, around 55 Cancri e, a scorching rocky planet covered in molten lava. It is no home for life, but it showed that a rocky planet can hold on to an atmosphere close to its star. And measurements of LHS 1140 b, a temperate planet about 49 light-years away, have offered tentative hints of a nitrogen-rich atmosphere that need confirmation.

### The K2-18b Affair

Then there is the story that made headlines around the world.

K2-18 b orbits a red dwarf about 124 light-years away in the constellation Leo. It was found in 2015 by Kepler’s second mission. It is about 8.6 times the mass of Earth and about 2.6 times its diameter, too big to be a rocky planet like ours and too small to be a gas giant: a type astronomers call a sub-Neptune, which has no counterpart in our solar system. It orbits in its star’s habitable zone.

In 2021, Nikku Madhusudhan and colleagues at the University of Cambridge proposed that some sub-Neptunes could be what they named hycean worlds: planets with a global ocean of water under a hydrogen-rich atmosphere. In September 2023, Webb observations analyzed by Madhusudhan’s team detected methane and carbon dioxide in K2-18 b’s atmosphere, with no ammonia, a combination they said fitted a hycean world. The same paper reported a weak hint of DMS.

In April 2025 the team published new observations, with a different Webb instrument at longer wavelengths, and reported a signal they attributed to DMS or its close relative dimethyl disulfide, at a statistical significance of about three sigma. They described it as the strongest evidence yet for possible biological activity beyond the solar system, while saying that more data were needed. The story went around the world.

A word about sigma. It measures how likely a signal is to be a fluke of random noise, assuming the analysis is otherwise correct. Three sigma means roughly a three-in-a-thousand chance of a fluke. That sounds convincing, but particle physicists, who have been burned many times, require five sigma, less than one chance in a million, before calling something a discovery. And sigma says nothing about systematic errors: a wrong model of the star, the instrument or the atmosphere can produce a three-sigma signal that is entirely false.

Within months, independent teams reanalyzed the same data. They used different methods to process the raw images, different models of the atmosphere, and different lists of molecules that might be present. One study, published in 2025 in the Astronomical Journal, found that most of its analyses did not favor DMS at all, and its title said the claim did not meet the standards of evidence. A joint analysis of all the available Webb data, across the whole wavelength range, found no significant evidence for DMS or its relative, and estimated that about 25 more transits with the longer-wavelength instrument would be needed to settle the question. Other teams pointed out that molecules such as ethane, which can be made without life, have similar fingerprints in the same part of the spectrum. A 2026 reanalysis found that the DMS signal largely disappeared once the data were processed in a different way.

Meanwhile, two other facts weakened the biological reading. In 2024, DMS was found in the gas around comet 67P, measured years earlier by the European Rosetta mission, and it has also been found in interstellar space. So DMS can be made without life. And several modeling groups showed that K2-18 b’s observed gases can be explained without any ocean, either by a deep hydrogen-rich atmosphere with no surface at all, or by a planet with a magma ocean under its air.

As of 2026, the consensus is that K2-18 b has methane and carbon dioxide in its atmosphere, that it is not known whether it has an ocean, and that DMS has not been detected. Madhusudhan’s team continues to collect data. This is how science is supposed to work. A bold claim was made in public with its uncertainties stated, other researchers tested it with the same data, and the claim did not hold up at the level needed. Nobody behaved badly. But it is a preview of how the first real biosignature claim will go, and a reason to expect that it will take years, not a press conference, to settle.

### Bigger Mirrors

The next generation of instruments is being built to do what Webb can only start.

The European Southern Observatory’s Extremely Large Telescope, under construction on a mountaintop in Chile’s Atacama Desert, has a main mirror 39 meters across made of 798 hexagonal segments. Its first light is now planned for March 2029, with science beginning in late 2030. It will be able to study the reflected light of some nearby rocky planets directly, including perhaps Proxima b.

The European Space Agency’s Ariel mission, due to launch in 2029, will survey the atmospheres of about a thousand known planets, most of them large and hot, to learn how planetary atmospheres work in general.

NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, launched on 30 August 2026, is mainly a cosmology mission, but it carries a coronagraph, an instrument that blocks a star’s light to reveal faint planets beside it, as a technology test.

The big prize is a telescope designed from the start to find life. NASA calls it the Habitable Worlds Observatory: a space telescope with a mirror perhaps six to eight meters across and a coronagraph good enough to suppress a Sun-like star’s light by a factor of ten billion, so that an Earth-like planet next to it can be seen directly. Its goal is to find and image about 25 potentially Earth-like planets around nearby Sun-like stars and take spectra of their reflected light, looking for oxygen, water, methane and the Lovelock imbalance. In 2026 it is still a concept in technology development, aimed at the 2040s.

### The Punchline

A planet’s atmosphere can be read from light-years away. During a transit, starlight filtered through the planet’s air carries the fingerprints of its molecules. Webb has made this routine for large planets and has begun to do it for small ones.

The best biosignature is a combination of gases that should destroy each other but persists, as oxygen and methane do on Earth, which Carl Sagan’s Galileo test detected in 1990. Single gases, including oxygen, can be faked by geology and stellar light.

So far, Webb has found that the inner TRAPPIST-1 planets are probably bare rock and that TRAPPIST-1 e has no thick hydrogen or carbon dioxide atmosphere. The claimed detection of DMS on K2-18 b has not survived independent analysis. No biosignature has been confirmed anywhere. The telescopes able to find one around a Sun-like star are being built.

Gases are one way a planet can announce life. There is another, which only works if the life has learned to build transmitters. The next chapter is about listening.

## 82. Listening for Neighbors — SETI and the Search for Technology

### Wow!

A few days after 15 August 1977, an astronomer named Jerry Ehman sat at his kitchen table in Ohio with a stack of computer printouts. He was a volunteer for the Big Ear, a radio telescope run by Ohio State University in a field near Delaware, Ohio. Big Ear did not move. It scanned the sky as Earth turned, and a computer printed the strength of the signal in each of 50 radio channels as a column of numbers and letters, with the letters standing for higher values.

Most of the printout was ones and spaces: noise. In one column, Ehman found a short string, 6EQUJ5, rising and then falling. It meant a signal had swept through the telescope’s beam exactly as a point in the sky should, over 72 seconds, in a single narrow channel, at a strength about thirty times the background. He circled it in red pen and wrote in the margin: “Wow!”

The channel was close to 1420 megahertz, the frequency at which cold hydrogen atoms emit radio waves, which is exactly where some astronomers had argued, eighteen years earlier, that a civilization trying to be heard would broadcast. Big Ear and many other telescopes went back to the spot, in the constellation Sagittarius, dozens of times over the following decades. The signal never repeated.

In 2025, a team that re-examined the Wow! signal using archived data from the Arecibo Observatory proposed that it was most likely natural: a cold cloud of hydrogen, briefly brightened by a burst from some distant object behind it. The case is not closed. But that has been the pattern of every promising signal in sixty years of listening. This chapter explains what the search is, what it has covered, and why silence so far tells us much less than it seems to.

### A Frequency Everyone Knows

The modern search began with a two-page paper. In September 1959, two physicists at Cornell University, Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison, published “Searching for Interstellar Communications” in Nature. Their argument was short. Radio waves cross interstellar space easily. The galaxy is quiet at radio wavelengths in a band between the noise of electrons in our own galaxy and the noise of Earth’s atmosphere. And any civilization that knows physics knows the 1420-megahertz line of hydrogen, the most common element. If they wanted to be found, that was where they would call. “The probability of success is difficult to estimate,” Cocconi and Morrison wrote, “but if we never search, the chance of success is zero.”

The next spring, a young radio astronomer named Frank Drake, working independently, pointed the 85-foot telescope of the National Radio Astronomy Observatory at Green Bank, West Virginia, at two nearby Sun-like stars, Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani. He called it Project Ozma, after the princess of the land of Oz. He listened for about 150 hours between April and July 1960 and heard nothing but one false alarm from a secret military aircraft. In November 1961, Drake hosted a small meeting at Green Bank to discuss the subject. To organize the agenda, he wrote an equation on the blackboard. Chapter 83 will look at it.

The field took a name: SETI, the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. The band between the hydrogen line at 1420 megahertz and the hydroxyl lines near 1662 megahertz was nicknamed the water hole, since hydrogen and hydroxyl together make water. It seemed a natural meeting place for water-based life.

### Sixty Years of Listening

The search has been run on a shoestring for most of its history. NASA began a formal search, the High Resolution Microwave Survey, on 12 October 1992, the five-hundredth anniversary of Columbus’s landing in the Americas. Congress canceled it within a year, after a senator dismissed it as “the Great Martian Chase.” Since then most SETI has been privately funded.

The SETI Institute, founded in California in 1984, ran Project Phoenix, which from 1995 to 2004 examined about 800 nearby stars with some of the world’s largest radio telescopes, and found nothing. With money from the Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen, it built the Allen Telescope Array in northern California, which began observing in 2007. From 1999 to 2020 a project called SETI@home let millions of volunteers lend their home computers to sift data from Arecibo, then the largest single-dish radio telescope in the world. In 1974 Arecibo had also sent the most famous deliberate message from Earth, a picture of 1,679 bits aimed at a star cluster 25,000 light-years away. The telescope collapsed in December 2020.

The largest search in history began on 20 July 2015, when the investor Yuri Milner and the physicist Stephen Hawking announced Breakthrough Listen, a 100-million-dollar, ten-year program. It bought time on the 100-meter Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia and the 64-meter Parkes telescope in Australia, later added the MeerKAT array in South Africa, and runs an optical search for laser pulses as well. Its computers can examine billions of narrow radio channels at once.

In 2020 Breakthrough Listen reported on its survey of 1,327 nearby stars: no signals. In 2019, while observing Proxima Centauri, the nearest star, it recorded a narrow signal at 982 megahertz that appeared only when the telescope was pointed at the star. It was named BLC1, for Breakthrough Listen Candidate 1. A careful study published in 2021 traced it to interference from electronic equipment on Earth. In 2025 the project listened to the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS as it passed through the solar system, after some speculation that it might be artificial, and heard nothing.

### How Big Is the Haystack?

Sixty years of silence sounds like strong evidence that nobody is there. It is not, and the reason is worth understanding.

A search has to choose where to point, at what frequencies to listen, how narrow a signal to look for, how faint a signal it can detect, and for how long. Each choice cuts down the volume of possibilities it covers. In 2018, the astronomer Jason Wright and two students calculated how much of this many-dimensional haystack all the radio searches to date had examined. Their answer was a small fraction: they compared it to looking for fish by examining a hot tub’s worth of water out of all of Earth’s oceans. Later searches have added more, but the comparison still holds in spirit.

The detection limits matter too. Earth’s own unintentional radio leakage, from television and radar, would be very hard to detect at the distance of even the nearest stars with present instruments. Most searches could detect only a strong beacon deliberately aimed at us, or an extremely powerful transmitter. A civilization exactly like ours, a few hundred light-years away, would probably go unheard.

So the honest summary is: we have ruled out very loud, persistent beacons pointed at us from a few thousand of the nearest stars, at the frequencies we chose. That is not nothing. But it is a small corner of the possibilities.

### Little Green Men and Natural Lighthouses

Every candidate signal so far has turned out to be one of two things: interference from our own technology, or a natural object we had not understood.

The classic natural example is from 1967. At Cambridge, a graduate student named Jocelyn Bell noticed a tiny bit of “scruff” on the paper chart recording of a new radio telescope. It turned out to be a train of pulses arriving every 1.34 seconds, so regular that she and her supervisor half-seriously labeled the source LGM-1, for Little Green Men. Within months she had found more of them in other parts of the sky, which made aliens much less likely. They were pulsars: spinning neutron stars, the collapsed cores of exploded stars, sweeping a beam of radio waves past Earth like a lighthouse. The discovery won a Nobel Prize in 1974, although not for Bell.

The lesson has been learned many times since. The universe is full of natural processes that produce surprising signals, and most of them have been found by people who were looking for something else. A SETI claim has to rule out every natural explanation and every possible human source. That is very hard to do.

### Beyond Radio: Technosignatures

In recent years the field has broadened its name and its methods. Instead of “intelligence,” which is hard to define and impossible to observe directly, astronomers now speak of technosignatures: any observable sign of technology.

Lasers are one. A pulsed laser, focused by a telescope, could outshine its own star for a billionth of a second. Optical SETI searches for such flashes.

Waste heat is another. In 1960 the physicist Freeman Dyson pointed out in Science that a civilization using a large fraction of its star’s energy would have to radiate the waste as infrared heat. A star wrapped in energy-collecting structures, later called a Dyson sphere, would look dimmer in visible light and brighter in infrared than it should. Searches of infrared sky surveys have looked for such stars. In 2024, a project called Hephaistos reported seven red dwarfs with puzzling infrared excesses. Within months, other astronomers showed that at least some of them were likely contaminated by distant, dusty galaxies in the same direction.

Strange dimming is a third. In 2015, the astronomer Tabetha Boyajian and a group of citizen scientists reported that a star called KIC 8462852, now known as Tabby’s star, sometimes dimmed by as much as 22 percent, irregularly, in a way no planet could cause. Briefly, the idea of alien megastructures made headlines. Follow-up observations showed that the dimming was deeper in blue light than in red, which is what dust does and what a solid structure would not do.

Atmospheres can carry technosignatures too. The same telescopes that look for biosignatures could, in principle, detect industrial gases such as chlorofluorocarbons, which nature does not make, in the air of a nearby planet. Earth’s own atmosphere has been carrying such a signal since the 1930s.

And there are objects. In 2017, the first known interstellar object, ʻOumuamua, passed through the solar system, oddly elongated and accelerating slightly as if pushed. One astronomer suggested it might be an artificial light sail. Most researchers concluded that the acceleration was caused by gas escaping from an icy body, even though no gas was seen directly. The third known interstellar visitor, 3I/ATLAS, discovered in July 2025, is clearly a comet, with a tail. It was still listened to.

### What Would Happen

Suppose a signal does survive all the checks. What then?

Researchers in the field have drawn up a set of principles, first agreed in 1989 and revised since. A detection should be confirmed independently by other observatories before it is announced. It should be announced publicly, to everyone, not kept secret. And no reply should be sent until there has been international consultation. These are agreements among scientists, not laws.

The likeliest form of a detection is also the least dramatic. It would almost certainly be one-way. Even a signal from a nearby star would take decades to answer, and from most of the galaxy, thousands of years. We would learn that someone existed, or had existed, since the signal would be old. We would probably not learn what they were saying.

### The Punchline

SETI has been listening since 1960, mostly at radio frequencies near the 1420-megahertz line of hydrogen, and more recently for laser flashes, waste heat and other technosignatures. Every candidate, from the Wow! signal of 1977 to BLC1 in 2019, has either failed to repeat or been traced to a natural source or to our own technology.

The silence is real, but the search has covered only a small part of the possibilities, and most searches could detect only a deliberate, powerful beacon aimed our way. A civilization like ours at a few hundred light-years could go unnoticed.

Still, there is a deeper version of the silence that does not depend on radio telescopes at all. If the galaxy is old and life is common, someone should have spread across it long ago, and should be here already. In 1950, over lunch, a physicist asked where they are. The next chapter takes his question seriously.

## 83. Where Is Everybody — The Fermi Paradox and the Great Filter

### Lunch at Los Alamos

In the summer of 1950, four physicists walked to lunch at the Fuller Lodge at Los Alamos, New Mexico. They were Enrico Fermi, who had built the first nuclear reactor; Edward Teller, who was then working on the hydrogen bomb; Herbert York, later the first director of the Lawrence Livermore laboratory; and Emil Konopinski. On the walk they talked about a recent rash of flying saucer reports in the newspapers and a cartoon in The New Yorker that blamed aliens for the disappearance of New York City’s trash cans. They joked about it, and moved on to other subjects.

Then, in the middle of lunch, Fermi asked suddenly: “But where is everybody?”

The others laughed, because they understood at once what he meant. York later recalled that Fermi followed up with a quick series of rough calculations about how likely Earth-like planets were, how likely life was, and how long it would take a civilization to spread. The conclusion was that, if any of those numbers were reasonable, we should have been visited long ago, and many times.

Fermi never wrote it down. The account was pieced together decades later from the memories of the three other men, collected by the Los Alamos scientist Eric Jones in 1985. But the question has outlived nearly everything else said at that lunch. This chapter takes it seriously, because it is the place where everything in the book so far, from the origin of life to the future of technology, meets the evidence of an empty sky.

### Why the Question Bites

Fermi’s argument does not depend on radio telescopes. It depends on time.

The Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years across and something like 13 billion years old. Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, and Chapter 78 showed that temperate rocky planets are common. Many of them must be billions of years older than ours.

Now suppose that even one civilization, anywhere in the galaxy, ever developed the ability to travel between stars, even slowly. Suppose its ships traveled at one percent of the speed of light, much slower than many studies think possible, and that each colony waited a few thousand years before sending out ships of its own. The wave of colonization would cross the galaxy in something like ten million to a hundred million years. That sounds long. It is less than one percent of the galaxy’s age.

In 1975, the astronomer Michael Hart made the argument formally in a paper titled “An explanation for the absence of extraterrestrials on Earth.” He called the absence Fact A: there are no intelligent beings from outer space on Earth now. Hart argued that every explanation for Fact A that depended on aliens choosing not to come had to apply to every alien civilization, forever, without exception, which seemed implausible. His conclusion was that we are probably the first, or the only, technological civilization in our galaxy. In 1980 the physicist Frank Tipler extended the argument to self-copying robotic probes, which would not even need living travelers. That is a striking application of this book’s theme: once something can copy itself, it spreads.

The argument is not airtight. But it changes the question. The puzzle is not why we have not heard anyone. It is why, if technological life is common, the galaxy is not already full.

### The Drake Equation

Frank Drake’s equation, mentioned in Chapter 82, was written in 1961 to organize a meeting, not to calculate an answer. But it is the clearest way to see where the uncertainty lies. It estimates the number of civilizations in our galaxy whose signals we could detect now, N, as a product of seven factors:

N = R* × fp × ne × fl × fi × fc × L

R* is the rate at which stars form in the galaxy, about one to a few per year. fp is the fraction of stars with planets. ne is the number of planets per planetary system that could support life. fl is the fraction of those on which life actually begins. fi is the fraction of those on which intelligence evolves. fc is the fraction of those that develop detectable technology. And L is the length of time such a civilization remains detectable.

In 1961 every factor was a guess. Since then, astronomy has measured the first three. Star formation is known to within a factor of two or so. The fraction of stars with planets turns out to be close to one: almost every star has planets. The number of temperate rocky planets per star, from Kepler, is something like a few tenths, as Chapter 78 described.

The last four factors are biology and history, and they are as unknown now as they were in 1961. We have one example of a planet where life began, one example of life producing intelligence, one example of intelligence producing radio, and no idea how long our own civilization will last. Each of those factors could be close to one or smaller than one in a billion. With a range like that, the equation can produce any answer from millions of civilizations to far less than one per galaxy.

In 2018, Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord at Oxford did something simple that had rarely been done. Instead of plugging in single best guesses, they used the full range of scientific uncertainty for each factor and multiplied the ranges. The result was a distribution, not a number, and a large part of it lay below one. Their conclusion was that, given what we actually know, there is a substantial probability that we are alone in the galaxy, and perhaps in the observable universe. The paradox, they argued, partly dissolves. It only seems strange that the sky is empty if you assumed, without evidence, that the unknown factors were large.

### The Great Filter

In 1996, the economist Robin Hanson gave the problem its most useful shape. Consider all the steps between dead rock and a civilization spreading across the galaxy. A planet must form in the right place. Life must start. It must evolve the kind of cells that can build complex bodies. Those bodies must produce intelligence. Intelligence must produce technology. And technology must survive itself long enough to spread.

Since the galaxy seems empty, Hanson argued, at least one of those steps must be extremely improbable. He called it the Great Filter. The urgent question is where the filter lies: behind us, or ahead of us.

This book has spent eighty chapters on candidates for a filter behind us.

The origin of life itself. Chapter 3 showed how hard the step from chemistry to copying is to explain. It might be the improbable step. Against that, life appeared on Earth quickly. Chapter 4 placed the last universal common ancestor of all living things at about 4.2 billion years ago, according to a 2024 analysis, only a few hundred million years after Earth became habitable. That looks as though life starts easily. But a 2012 analysis by David Spiegel and Edwin Turner pointed out a catch: we could only exist on a planet where life started early enough for intelligence to evolve before the Sun becomes too bright. So an early start on Earth is weaker evidence than it seems.

The complex cell. Chapter 12 described the merger in which one cell swallowed another and kept it as an engine, the origin of the eukaryotes. As far as anyone can tell, it happened once in four billion years. Every plant, animal and fungus descends from that single event. Bacteria, for all their chemical brilliance, never built a brain.

Intelligence. Chapter 17 asked whether evolution, run again, would repeat itself. Some features evolve over and over, like eyes and flight. Technological intelligence has evolved exactly once, in one lineage of one ape, after more than half a billion years of animal evolution. That might mean it is rare.

A filter ahead of us is less comfortable. It would mean that civilizations like ours commonly reach our stage and then destroy themselves, or stall, before spreading. Part V of this book discussed the technologies, from artificial intelligence to self-copying machines, that thoughtful people worry about. Part X discussed the power to rewrite our own genetic text. It is not hard to imagine ways for a species with nuclear weapons, engineered pathogens and minds it does not understand to end itself.

That is why, in 2008, the Oxford philosopher Nick Bostrom wrote an essay explaining why he hoped the search for life on Mars would find nothing. If life turned out to have started twice in one solar system, the origin of life would not be the filter, and the improbable step would more likely lie later, perhaps ahead of us. A barren Mars would be good news.

### Other Answers

The literature on the Fermi paradox now runs to hundreds of proposed solutions. They fall into a few families.

They are here, or have been, and we have not noticed. Probes might be small, quiet or dormant. The solar system has been searched only lightly for artifacts. There is no evidence for this, and the large majority of UFO reports that have been investigated have ordinary explanations.

They exist but stay home, or stay quiet. In 1973 the radio astronomer John Ball proposed the zoo hypothesis: that advanced civilizations deliberately leave developing ones alone, as we might leave a nature reserve undisturbed. The science fiction writer Liu Cixin gave a darker version in his 2008 novel The Dark Forest, in which every civilization hides because revealing itself invites destruction. The problem with all such answers is Hart’s: they have to apply to every civilization, without exception, for billions of years.

They exist but do not spread. Perhaps interstellar travel is too expensive, or perhaps civilizations turn inward, into simulated worlds, long before they would think of colonizing. Again, this has to hold for all of them.

We are early. This is the most interesting of the recent answers, because it rests on measurement. Stars like the Sun will keep forming for billions of years, and red dwarfs will shine for trillions. A 2015 study by Peter Behroozi and Molly Peeples estimated that Earth formed before about 92 percent of all the Earth-like planets the universe will ever have. Hanson and colleagues developed this in 2021 into a model they called grabby aliens, in which expanding civilizations eventually fill the universe, and the reason we see none is that we are near the beginning of the story and they have not reached us yet. Whether that is right is speculative. But it reminds us that the universe is young compared with how long it will be habitable.

And there is the simplest answer of all: that the steps from chemistry to civilization are, together, so improbable that there is no one else in the galaxy to see.

### Settled, Working, Speculative

Settled: there is no confirmed evidence of extraterrestrial technology, on Earth, in the solar system or in any signal. The galaxy is old enough that a civilization able to travel between stars, even slowly, could have spread across it many times.

Working: the values of the astronomical factors in the Drake equation. They are now measured roughly, and they say that habitable planets are common.

Speculative: everything else. Every solution to the Fermi paradox, including the claim that we are alone, rests on factors that have exactly one example, ours.

### The Punchline

Fermi’s question, asked over lunch in 1950, is not about radio telescopes. It is about time. A galaxy this old, with this many temperate planets, should have been filled long ago by anyone who could copy themselves across interstellar space. It does not seem to have been.

Either something on the road from chemistry to civilization is extremely improbable, a Great Filter, or something about civilizations keeps them all at home, or we are among the first. When the real uncertainties are taken into account, the possibility that we are alone in the galaxy is not strange; it is one of the likely outcomes.

All of this turns on one factor more than any other: how often life begins. One example cannot tell us. A second example, a life that began independently of ours, would settle it at once. The last chapter of this Part asks how we would recognize a second genesis if we found one, and why it might already be hiding in a sample tube.

## 84. A Second Genesis — Panspermia, Mirror Life and What One More Example Would Prove

### Pasteur’s Tweezers

In 1848, a 25-year-old chemist in Paris named Louis Pasteur was studying the crystals of a salt of tartaric acid, a compound that forms in wine barrels. Two forms of the acid were known. One rotated polarized light to the right. The other, chemically identical as far as anyone could tell, did not rotate it at all. Pasteur looked at the crystals of the second form under a magnifying glass and noticed that they came in two shapes, each the mirror image of the other, like a left and a right hand. Patiently, with tweezers, he sorted them into two piles. Dissolved separately, one pile rotated light to the right and the other rotated it to the left. Mixed together, they canceled.

He had discovered that molecules can have handedness. A molecule built around a carbon atom with four different groups attached can exist in two forms that are mirror images and cannot be superimposed, as your left hand cannot be fitted into a right-hand glove. Chemists call this chirality, from the Greek for hand.

Here is the strange part, which Pasteur himself spent years thinking about. Chemistry without life makes both hands in equal amounts. Life does not. Every protein in every living thing on Earth is built from left-handed amino acids. The sugars in DNA and RNA are all right-handed. Part VI showed the consequence: the double helix twists one way because its sugars are one-handed, and the enzymes that copy it fit only that twist.

Handedness is one of the clearest ways to tell life’s chemistry from ordinary chemistry. It may also be one of the best ways to tell whether life somewhere else began on its own, or is a distant cousin of ours. That is the question of this last chapter of the Part.

### One Tree, One Code

All the life ever studied on Earth is one family. Chapter 4 told the story of the last universal common ancestor, dated by a 2024 study to about 4.2 billion years ago. Chapter 38 described the genetic code, the table that translates three-letter words of DNA into amino acids. With small variations, every living thing uses the same table, the same four letters, the same twenty amino acids, the same handedness, the same energy currency.

That is the strongest single piece of evidence for common descent, and it means that, in a strict sense, biology is a science with one example. Every generalization about life, from what it needs to how it evolves, has been made by studying one family tree. The question of Chapter 83, how often life begins, cannot be answered from one tree, because we would have to exist on a planet where it began at least once, whether that was likely or not.

A second example would change that at once. If life began twice in one solar system, independently, then it is not a fluke. Given the number of planets described in Chapter 78, it would almost certainly be common across the universe.

But the second example would have to be independent. And there is a complication.

### Rocks Between Planets

The idea that life traveled to Earth from somewhere else is old. The Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius proposed in the early 1900s that bacterial spores could drift between stars pushed by the pressure of starlight. He called it panspermia, seeds everywhere. In 1973 Francis Crick, co-discoverer of the double helix in Part VI, and the chemist Leslie Orgel proposed, half seriously, directed panspermia: that Earth might have been seeded deliberately by another civilization. Neither idea has any evidence behind it, and both have the drawback that they move the problem of the origin of life somewhere else without solving it.

A more modest version, lithopanspermia, is taken much more seriously. Large impacts throw rock off planets, and some of that rock lands on other planets. Chapter 79 described ALH84001, one of about 400 known meteorites from Mars. Mars rocks have been landing on Earth throughout its history. A microbe deep inside such a rock could, in principle, survive the shock of ejection, the years or millions of years in space, and the heat of entry, since the inside of a large meteorite stays cool.

The survival experiments support the idea partly. In 1984 NASA’s Long Duration Exposure Facility carried bacterial spores into orbit. When it was retrieved nearly six years later, a small fraction of the protected spores were still alive. In Japan’s Tanpopo experiment, dried pellets of *Deinococcus radiodurans*, the bacterium from the meat can of Chapter 77, were left outside the International Space Station, and some cells in the thicker pellets survived for three years. Millions of years remain much harder, because cosmic radiation slowly destroys DNA. But the trip from Mars can be much shorter, and in the early solar system impacts were far more frequent.

So if life is found on Mars, the first question will be whether it is related to us. A Mars microbe that used DNA, the same code and left-handed amino acids would most likely be a cousin, sharing an ancestor with Earth life through a meteorite, in one direction or the other. It would tell us that life can travel. It would not tell us that life is common. A Mars microbe with a different code, different building blocks or the opposite handedness would be a second genesis.

### Ingredients From the Sky

Whatever the answer, the raw materials clearly arrive from space.

On 28 September 1969, a meteorite broke up over the town of Murchison in Australia, and more than 100 kilograms of fragments were collected. It turned out to be packed with organic molecules, including dozens of amino acids, some of which life uses and many it does not. The worry with any meteorite is contamination: it has landed on a planet covered in life.

That is why sample return missions matter. In December 2020 the Japanese Hayabusa2 spacecraft brought back material from the asteroid Ryugu, and in 2023 researchers reported finding uracil, one of the four letters of RNA, in it.

On 24 September 2023, NASA’s OSIRIS-REx capsule landed in the Utah desert with about 120 grams of rock and dust from the asteroid Bennu, collected and sealed in space. In January 2025, two papers described what it contained. There were 14 of the 20 amino acids that life uses to build proteins. There were all five nucleobases used in DNA and RNA: adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil. There was a great deal of ammonia, and minerals showing that salty water had once evaporated inside the asteroid’s parent body, leaving behind a sequence of salts, including phosphates, of a kind found in dried-up lake beds on Earth. The authors were careful to say that Bennu contains no life and never did. It contains the ingredients, made without life, in an environment that had liquid water.

The amino acids in Bennu had one more lesson. Unlike in some earlier meteorites, they came in nearly equal amounts of left and right hands. So whatever made life on Earth choose one hand, it was probably not handed to us ready-made from space. It happened later, during the origin itself or soon after.

### A Shadow Biosphere

There is another place a second genesis could be hiding: here.

In 2005, the physicist Paul Davies and the astrobiologist Charles Lineweaver suggested that Earth might host a shadow biosphere, microbes descended from a separate origin, using different chemistry, which we have missed because every test we use for life is designed for our kind. Most of Earth’s microbes cannot be grown in a laboratory, and the methods for finding them rely on DNA sequences. A microbe without DNA would be invisible to those methods.

The idea had its one famous test in December 2010, when a NASA-funded team published a paper in Science claiming that a bacterium from Mono Lake in California, called GFAJ-1, could use arsenic in place of phosphorus in its DNA. It would not have been a second genesis, but it would have been a different chemistry. Other laboratories repeated the experiments within two years and found that the bacterium was simply very good at scavenging traces of phosphorus while tolerating arsenic. On 24 July 2025, Science retracted the paper, stating that its data did not support its conclusion; the authors disagreed. No shadow biosphere has been found. But nobody has searched very thoroughly either.

### Venus, Again

The most unexpected candidate in recent years was the planet Chapter 78 called the twin that went wrong. The surface of Venus is hopeless for life. But about 50 to 60 kilometers up, in its cloud layer, temperatures and pressures are close to Earth’s at sea level, although the droplets are concentrated sulfuric acid.

In September 2020, a team led by Jane Greaves reported detecting phosphine, a molecule of one phosphorus atom and three hydrogens, in the clouds of Venus, at a concentration of about 20 parts per billion. On Earth, phosphine is made in significant amounts only by microbes living without oxygen, and by industry. The claim was challenged at once. Reanalysis of the data reduced the signal, other observations at different wavelengths set strict upper limits, and some argued that the signal was sulfur dioxide or an artifact of processing. The original team has continued observing and reports recovering a signal in later campaigns, with a further campaign approved for 2026.

As of 2026 the phosphine on Venus is neither confirmed nor ruled out. Even if confirmed, it would be an unexplained chemical anomaly, not a detection of life. The episode, like K2-18 b in Chapter 81, is a lesson in how hard a single-molecule biosignature is to make stick.

### Mirror Life

The last idea in this Part is not about finding a second genesis but about making one, and it brings the Part back to Part X.

Since chirality is a choice, a mirror-image cell is chemically possible: a bacterium built entirely from right-handed amino acids and left-handed sugars. Laboratories have already made mirror-image versions of some proteins and of short strands of DNA and RNA, and research toward mirror-image ribosomes, the protein-building machines of Chapter 38, has begun. A complete mirror bacterium is probably still a decade or more away.

In December 2024, a group of 38 scientists, including some who had worked toward that goal, published a warning in Science. A mirror bacterium, they argued, might not be recognized by the immune systems of humans, animals or plants, since those systems detect invaders by the shapes of their molecules. It might also escape the viruses and predators that keep ordinary bacteria in check. If it escaped, it could spread unchecked. They recommended that mirror bacteria not be created at all unless compelling evidence showed they would be safe, and they called for a global conversation about where to draw the line. The discussions continued through 2025 and 2026, and several research funders have said they will not support work aimed at building a mirror cell.

It is a striking moment in the long story this book has told. Chemistry that copies itself ran for four billion years with one handedness. Now one of its products has learned enough to make the other, and has had to decide whether it should.

### The Punchline

All known life is one family, with one code and one handedness. That makes biology a science with a single example, and it is why the question of how often life begins cannot be answered from Earth alone.

A second genesis would answer it. Life on Mars or in an icy moon with different chemistry, or a shadow biosphere on Earth, would show that the origin of life is not a fluke. Life on Mars with our chemistry would more likely be a cousin, carried by meteorite, which would show that life can travel but not that it is common.

Meanwhile, the sky delivers ingredients: Bennu brought amino acids, all five nucleobases, ammonia, phosphates and evidence of water, in a mix with no handedness. Venus’s phosphine is unresolved. And on Earth, the ability to build a mirror-image cell has arrived before the wisdom about whether to use it.

No second example has yet been found. The tubes on Mars, the plumes of Enceladus and the light of nearby planets are the places it may be waiting. The book ends where it began, with a copy and a question, in the Epilogue.
