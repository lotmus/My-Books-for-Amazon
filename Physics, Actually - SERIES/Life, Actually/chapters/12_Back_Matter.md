## Epilogue: Two Tubes

### The Kitchen Table, Once More

It is an evening in the autumn of 2026, and the three of them are at Anna’s kitchen table again. They are still invented. Theo is fifteen and has just been told, at school, that there might once have been life on Mars. He wants to know why nobody is going to get the rock.

Anna has the news open on her laptop. It is not quite that nobody is going to get the rock, she says. It is that nobody has paid for the trip yet. The rock is in a tube, and the tube is in a rover, and the rover is still driving.

Ruth, who was born in the years when a leading scientist could still call DNA a stupid molecule, points out that there is another tube in this house, or was. Anna mailed it in the spring. It came back as a web page: ancestry to one decimal place, a variant that makes one disease a little more likely, a variant that lets her drink milk, a second cousin in Ohio.

Two tubes. The one on Mars might hold the first evidence that life has begun more than once. The one that went through the mail held the latest pages of a text that began only once, as far as anyone knows, about four billion years ago, and has been copied without a break ever since. Theo is the most recent copy at the table.

This book has been about the distance between those two tubes. It is a good place to stop and look back along the thread.

### The Thread, Pulled Tight

Chapter 1 gave the working definition: life is chemistry that copies itself, imperfectly, and lets the imperfections be judged. Eighty-four chapters later, it still holds, and it is worth seeing how much it carries.

Chemistry. Part I showed that the atoms of life are among the most common in the universe, that its energy is electrons moving downhill, and that its first cells may have been built at the boundary between hot rock and cold water. Part XI found the same ingredients nearly everywhere we have looked: amino acids and all five nucleobases in the dust of an asteroid, phosphates and organic molecules in the spray of a small moon of Saturn, water under the ice of Europa. The chemistry is not rare.

That copies itself. Schrödinger guessed in 1943 that heredity must be written in a stable molecule whose pattern does not repeat, an aperiodic crystal. Ten years later the molecule turned out to be a ladder whose two halves each carry the information to rebuild the other. Part VI followed the copying machine down to its proofreaders, and Part VIII showed us doing the same trick in a test tube.

Imperfectly. The copying enzymes and their repair crews get about one letter in a billion wrong. Every child carries sixty or seventy new mistakes. Most do nothing, some do harm, and a very few help. Without them there would be no variation, no evolution, no diversity of life, and no one to read this book. The copy is never exact, and that is not a flaw in the system. It is the system.

And lets the imperfections be judged. Darwin found the judge in 1859 without knowing what was being judged. Mendel found the units without knowing what they were made of. The Modern Synthesis joined the two, and the double helix explained both. Parts II to IV showed what the judging built: engines inside cells, bodies, brains, our own branch, still being edited by milk, altitude and disease.

### The Turn

Somewhere in the last seventy years, the thread did something it had never done before. A product of the copying learned to read the text, and then to write it.

Part VIII told how that happened, from a virus read letter by letter in 1977 to bacterial scissors turned into an editing tool in 2012. Part X brought it to the kitchen table: a full genome for a few hundred dollars, a child in Philadelphia treated with an edit designed for him alone, and an argument, not settled, about whether we should ever edit the text we pass to our children. Part XI added one more case, a warning signed by scientists in 2024 that a mirror-image cell, chemistry copied in reverse, should probably never be built.

Those are new questions. For four billion years, nothing alive could choose what to copy. Now one species can, a little, and it is learning, slowly, how little it knows about what it wants. Part V asked whether the copying could jump from chemistry to machines, and whether a mind is the kind of pattern that could survive the jump. Nobody knows. But the question only makes sense because of everything before it: a mind is what the copying chemistry built when it began to model itself.

### The Question That Is Left

Which brings the thread back to the tube on Mars, and to Fermi’s lunch.

Every generalization in this book has been drawn from one family tree. One code, one handedness, one ancestor, one origin. Chapter 83 showed why that matters. If life starts easily, the galaxy should hold many origins, and the silence of the sky needs a special explanation. If it starts rarely, the silence is what we should expect, and the copy on this planet may be the only one in a very large space.

We do not know which. What we do know is how to find out. A rock from Jezero Crater with traces of microbes that used a different code. A plume from Enceladus with molecules of only one handedness and the wrong one. A spectrum from a planet around a nearby star with oxygen and methane together, out of balance. Any one of them would turn biology from a science of one example into a science of two, and the second example would change the first. We would know, at last, which features of life are necessary and which are accidents of our history.

None of that is close. The missions are slow, the telescopes are years away, and the tube on Mars may wait a long time. But the instruments exist, the targets have been chosen, and for the first time in history the question has an experiment attached.

### Theo’s Turn

At the table, Theo has moved on. He wants to know whether he could send his own tube in, and whether it would show anything Anna’s did not.

It would, Anna says. Not much. Sixty or seventy letters that are new in him, that neither she nor his father carries. Mistakes, in the copying, made somewhere in the cells that became him. Most of them do nothing. One or two might matter someday, to him or to someone downstream of him.

He is not impressed. He wants to know what they are for.

They are not for anything, Ruth says, and the whole book has been her answer. They are just what happens when chemistry copies itself and does not get it quite right. Everything on this planet that has ever lived is what those mistakes added up to: the bacterium that survives a can of irradiated meat, the friar’s peas, the flies in the bottle, the woman at the mailbox, the boy at the table, and whatever, if anything, once left leopard spots in a rock on Mars.

In the hour that the three of them sit there, in the lining of each of their guts, cells divide, and each division copies about six billion letters, and checks them, and checks them again, and gets a few wrong. It has been doing that for about four billion years. It is doing it now, in you.

The copy is never exact. That is how we got here, and it is the only way anything ever will.

## Appendix: Core Ideas Worth Keeping

This book covered a lot of ground, from the first copying molecules to the air of planets around other stars. If you remember nothing else, remember these.

### Life is chemistry that copies itself, imperfectly

Every definition of life fails at the edges, but this one works well enough: a chemical system that stores information, copies it with small errors, and lets the errors be sorted by what survives. It describes a process, not a substance. See Chapter 1.

### Order is paid for with disorder

A living thing keeps itself organized by exporting disorder to its surroundings, as heat and waste. Life does not break the second law of thermodynamics. It runs on it, like a waterwheel runs on falling water. See Chapters 1 and 2.

### Biological energy is electrons moving downhill

Food, sunlight and the chemistry of hot rock all come down to electrons moving from where they are held loosely to where they are held tightly. Every cell on Earth stores that energy in the same way, as a battery of protons across a membrane. See Chapters 2 and 4.

### Selection has no foresight

Natural selection is a filter, not a designer. Variants that happen to work in the current situation become more common, and that is all. It builds things that look designed because it has had billions of years and keeps whatever works. See Chapters 7 and 9.

### Most of the history of life was microbes

For about three billion years, life was single cells. Complex cells came from one merger, in which a cell kept another as an engine. Bodies, brains and everything you can see are the last fifth of the story. See Chapters 4, 12 and 13.

### Evolution is still running, including in us

Lactase persistence, high-altitude adaptation and resistance to disease all spread through human populations within the last few tens of thousands of years. Selection did not stop when civilization started. See Chapters 19 and 20.

### Heredity is written in a four-letter text

DNA is a two-stranded molecule whose letters pair A with T and G with C. Each strand carries the information needed to rebuild the other, which is how the shape explains the copying. See Chapters 31 to 37.

### The copy is never exact

Copying enzymes and repair systems leave about one wrong letter in a billion. Every child carries sixty or seventy new mutations. Those errors are the raw material of all evolution and the cause of much disease. See Chapters 37 and 40.

### Genes are not destiny, and not irrelevant

Some traits depend on a single gene, many on thousands of small effects, and nearly all on environment and chance as well. Heritability describes populations, not individuals. A polygenic score shifts an average; it does not place one person. See Chapters 43, 44 and 71.

### Reading the text became cheap; writing it is beginning

A human genome cost about three billion dollars to read the first time and costs a few hundred dollars now. Editing tools from bacteria now cure some diseases in individual patients. Whether we should edit the text passed to children is not a scientific question alone. See Chapters 54 to 56 and 73 to 75.

### Habitable is not inhabited

Liquid water, an energy source, the right elements and time make a place habitable. They do not show that life ever began there. Water-bearing worlds turn out to be common, including oceans under the ice of moons far from the Sun. See Chapters 77, 78 and 80.

### No sign of life beyond Earth has been confirmed

Mars has a promising potential biosignature, sealed in a sample tube. Claims of biosignatures on Venus and on the exoplanet K2-18 b have not survived independent checks. Sixty years of listening have found no technology. The absence of evidence is still mostly an absence of looking. See Chapters 79, 81 and 82.

### One example is not enough

All known life shares one ancestor, one code and one handedness. Until a second, independent origin is found or convincingly ruled out, nobody can say whether life is common or rare in the universe. See Chapters 83 and 84.

## Glossary

Terms are explained where they first appear. This list is a reminder, with the chapter where each term is developed.

**AAV** — adeno-associated virus, a small virus used as an envelope to carry a gene into a patient’s cells. See Chapter 73.

**Abiogenesis** — the origin of living things from non-living chemistry. See Chapter 3.

**Adaptation** — a trait that spread because it improved survival or reproduction in a particular environment. See Chapter 7.

**Allele** — one of the alternative versions of a gene at a given position. See Chapter 43.

**Amino acid** — a small building-block molecule; twenty kinds are strung together to make proteins.

**Archaea** — one of the three domains of life: single cells without a nucleus, chemically distinct from bacteria, including many extremophiles. See Chapter 4.

**ATP** — adenosine triphosphate, the molecule cells use as small change for energy. See Chapters 2 and 36.

**Base** — one of the four chemical units, adenine, thymine, guanine, or cytosine, that form the “letters” of DNA. See Chapter 35.

**Base editing** — a form of gene editing that changes one letter without cutting both strands of the DNA. See Chapter 56.

**Base pair** — two bases joined across the double helix, A with T or G with C. See Chapter 33.

**Biosignature** — any feature, such as a gas, a mineral or a chemical pattern, best explained by life. A potential biosignature is one for which non-biological explanations have not been ruled out. See Chapters 79 and 81.

**Cambrian explosion** — the burst of animal body plans in the fossil record beginning about 539 million years ago. See Chapter 14.

**Cas9** — the protein in the CRISPR system that cuts DNA where a guide RNA tells it to. See Chapter 55.

**Centromere** — the pinched region of a chromosome where the two copies are held together before a cell divides.

**Chirality** — handedness: the property of a molecule that exists in two mirror-image forms that cannot be superimposed. Life on Earth uses left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars. See Chapter 84.

**Chromatin** — DNA wound around histones, compact spool-like proteins that help pack it tightly. See Chapter 35.

**Chromosome** — a single long DNA molecule, together with its packaging proteins; humans have forty-six. See Chapter 35.

**Clone** — an organism grown from the nucleus of one donor cell, so that its nuclear DNA matches that donor. Identical twins are natural clones. See Chapter 60.

**Codon** — a group of three DNA or RNA letters that specifies one amino acid or a stop signal. See Chapter 38.

**Coevolution** — the evolution of two or more species in response to each other, as in predators and prey or hosts and parasites. See Chapter 18.

**Convergent evolution** — the independent evolution of similar features in unrelated lineages, such as eyes or wings. See Chapter 17.

**CRISPR** — a bacterial immune system, repurposed as a programmable gene-editing tool guided by RNA. See Chapter 55.

**Diploid** — having two parental sets of chromosomes, the usual state of a body cell. In a person that is about 6.2 billion letters. See Chapter 41.

**Dominant** — a version of a gene that shows its effect when only one copy is present. It does not mean common, and it does not mean healthy. See Chapter 43.

**Drake equation** — Frank Drake’s 1961 product of factors estimating the number of detectable civilizations in the galaxy. See Chapter 83.

**Endosymbiosis** — one cell living inside another; the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts. See Chapter 12.

**Enhancer** — a stretch of DNA that boosts a gene’s activity, sometimes from far away along the chromosome. See Chapter 39.

**Entropy** — a measure of disorder, or of the number of ways a system’s parts can be arranged. Living things keep their own entropy low by exporting it. See Chapter 1.

**Enzyme** — a protein (occasionally an RNA) that speeds up a chemical reaction.

**Epigenetics** — chemical marks on DNA or its packaging proteins that change gene activity without changing the underlying letters. See Chapter 44.

**Eugenics** — the program, named by Francis Galton in 1883, of trying to improve a population by encouraging some people to have children and discouraging or preventing others. See Chapter 49.

**Eukaryote** — a cell with a nucleus and internal compartments, or an organism made of such cells. Plants, animals and fungi are eukaryotes. See Chapter 12.

**Event** — in plant biotechnology, one particular insertion or edit of a trait in one line; regulators approve events, not abstract genes. See Chapter 63.

**Evo-devo** — evolutionary developmental biology, the study of how changes in the genes that build bodies produce new forms. See Chapter 15.

**Exon** — a coding segment of a gene, the part kept once the non-coding pieces are cut out. See Chapter 39.

**Exoplanet** — a planet orbiting a star other than the Sun. More than 6,000 have been confirmed. See Chapter 78.

**Extremophile** — an organism that thrives in conditions extreme by human standards, such as boiling water, acid or deep rock. See Chapter 77.

**Fermi paradox** — the contrast between the apparent ease with which a technological civilization could spread across the galaxy and the absence of any evidence that one has. See Chapter 83.

**Fitness** — in evolution, reproductive success: how many copies of an organism’s genes reach the next generation. See Chapter 7.

**Franklin, Rosalind** — the scientist at King’s College London (1920–1958) who used X-rays to work out the shape of DNA. Photo 51 and her measurements underpinned the 1953 model. Her paper with Raymond Gosling appeared in the same issue of *Nature* as Watson and Crick’s. See Chapter 32.

**Gene** — a stretch of DNA that specifies a protein or a functional RNA molecule.

**Gene-edited** — of a crop, a plant whose own DNA has been cut and repaired, usually with CRISPR or TALENs, without leaving a gene from another species. Contrast transgenic. See Chapter 63.

**Genetic drift** — random change in how common a gene variant is, caused by chance in which individuals happen to reproduce. See Chapter 7.

**Genome** — the complete set of DNA in an organism.

**Genome-wide association study (GWAS)** — a search that compares hundreds of thousands of common variants across many people with and without a trait, looking for positions that differ more often than chance. See Chapter 68.

**Genotyping** — reading a chosen set of positions where people already differ, rather than the whole genome. A spit-kit chip does this. See Chapter 69.

**Germline** — the cells that give rise to eggs or sperm; changes here pass to offspring. See Chapter 74.

**Great Filter** — Robin Hanson’s name for an improbable step, somewhere between dead chemistry and a spreading civilization, that would explain why the galaxy appears empty. See Chapter 83.

**Guide RNA** — a short RNA strand that leads Cas9, or a base editor, to one address in the DNA. See Chapter 55.

**Habitable zone** — the range of distances from a star at which a planet could keep liquid water on its surface. See Chapter 78.

**Haplogroup** — a branch on the maternal or paternal tree, defined by a shared set of mitochondrial or Y-chromosome variants, used to group people by deep ancestry along one line. See Chapter 42.

**Haploid** — having one parental set of chromosomes, as in an egg or a sperm, or in the reference genome of about 3.1 billion letters.

**Heritability** — the fraction of variation in a trait, within a specific population, attributable to genetic differences. See Chapter 43.

**Heterozygous** — carrying two different alleles of a gene, one from each parent.

**Histone** — a spool-like protein that DNA winds around for compact packaging.

**Homozygous** — carrying two identical alleles of a gene.

**Horizontal gene transfer** — the passing of genes between organisms other than from parent to offspring, common in bacteria. See Chapter 4.

**Hycean world** — a proposed type of planet with a global water ocean under a hydrogen-rich atmosphere. None has been confirmed. See Chapter 81.

**Hydrothermal vent** — an opening in the seafloor where water heated by contact with hot rock flows out, rich in dissolved chemicals that hold energy. See Chapters 3 and 80.

**Intron** — a non-coding segment of a gene, cut out before the gene’s message is used. See Chapter 39.

**Kin selection** — evolution of behavior that helps relatives, who share copies of the same genes. See Chapter 9.

**LUCA** — the last universal common ancestor of all living things, estimated to have lived about 4.2 billion years ago. See Chapter 4.

**Manhattan plot** — a graph of a genome-wide association study, chromosomes along the floor, each variant a point whose height is how unlikely the association would be by chance; a real signal stands up as a tower. See Chapter 68.

**Mass extinction** — the loss of a large fraction of Earth’s species in a geologically short time; there have been five major ones. See Chapter 16.

**Meiosis** — the cell division that produces eggs or sperm, each with half the usual number of chromosomes. See Chapter 41.

**Meme** — Richard Dawkins’s term for a unit of culture that is copied from mind to mind. See Chapter 9.

**Messenger RNA (mRNA)** — the RNA copy of a gene that carries instructions to the ribosome, the cell’s protein-making machine. See Chapter 38.

**Mirror life** — hypothetical organisms built from molecules of the opposite handedness to all known life. See Chapter 84.

**Mitochondrial DNA** — the small circular genome inside the mitochondria, 16,569 letters in people, inherited from the mother. See Chapter 42.

**Mitosis** — ordinary cell division, producing two genetically identical daughter cells.

**Modern Synthesis** — the joining of Darwin’s natural selection with Mendel’s genetics in the 1930s and 1940s. See Chapter 8.

**Mutation** — a permanent change to the sequence of DNA letters. See Chapter 40.

**Nanopore sequencing** — reading a DNA strand by threading it through a protein pore and watching each letter disturb an electrical current. See Chapter 54.

**Natural selection** — the process by which heritable variants that improve survival or reproduction become more common over generations. See Chapter 7.

**Nucleotide** — the basic unit of DNA or RNA: a sugar, a phosphate, and a base.

**Nucleus** — the compartment of a cell that holds its DNA.

**Pangenome** — a reference genome built from many individuals rather than one, to capture human variation more fully. See Chapter 67.

**Panspermia** — the idea that life, or its seeds, travels between planets or stars. Lithopanspermia is transfer inside rocks thrown off by impacts. See Chapter 84.

**Penetrance** — the probability that a person carrying a particular genetic variant actually develops the associated trait or disease. See Chapter 70.

**Perchlorate** — a chlorine-oxygen salt found in Martian soil that destroys organic molecules when heated. See Chapter 79.

**Phenotype** — an observable trait, as distinct from the genetic makeup that produces it.

**Photo 51** — the May 1952 X-ray fiber photograph of wet (B-form) DNA, taken by Raymond Gosling under Rosalind Franklin at King’s College London. The white X on the film is the signature of a helix. See Chapter 32.

**Plasmid** — a small ring of DNA, used by bacteria and by genetic engineers to carry a gene from one cell to another. See Chapter 50.

**Point mutation** — a change to a single DNA letter. See Chapter 40.

**Polygenic** — influenced by many genes acting together, each with a small effect.

**Polygenic score** — a single number that adds up many small genetic effects on a trait. It shifts an average. It does not place one person. See Chapter 71.

**Polymerase** — an enzyme that builds a new strand of DNA or RNA using an existing strand as a template.

**Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** — a way to copy a chosen stretch of DNA over and over in a tube, with no cell required. See Chapter 52.

**Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT)** — the reading of an embryo’s DNA during IVF so that one embryo can be chosen for transfer. See Chapter 74.

**Prime editing** — a form of gene editing that nicks one strand and writes a short new sequence from a template carried on the guide RNA. See Chapter 56.

**Promoter** — the DNA address where the copying of a gene into RNA begins.

**Protocell** — a simple membrane-bound bubble of chemistry, a possible step between chemistry and the first cells. See Chapter 3.

**Quantum tunneling** — the ability of a particle, such as a proton or electron, to cross an energy barrier it classically could not. See Chapters 5 and 40.

**Recessive** — a version of a gene whose effect is hidden when a dominant version is also present. Two copies are needed for the trait to show. See Chapter 43.

**Recombination** — the exchange of DNA segments between paired chromosomes during meiosis. See Chapter 41.

**Red dwarf** — a small, cool, dim star, also called an M dwarf; about three in four stars in the Milky Way. See Chapter 78.

**Red Queen** — the idea that species must keep evolving just to keep up with their parasites, predators and competitors. See Chapters 10 and 18.

**Redox** — reduction and oxidation: the transfer of electrons between molecules, the basis of all biological energy. See Chapters 2 and 77.

**Restriction enzyme** — a bacterial enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific sequence. See Chapter 50.

**Ribosome** — the cellular machine, built of RNA and protein, that translates mRNA into protein. See Chapter 38.

**RNA world** — the hypothesis that early life used RNA both to store information and to catalyze reactions, before DNA and proteins. See Chapter 3.

**Semiconservative replication** — the copying scheme in which each new DNA molecule contains one old strand and one newly made strand. See Chapter 33.

**SETI** — the search for extraterrestrial intelligence, mostly by listening for radio signals. See Chapter 82.

**Sexual selection** — selection driven by competition for mates or by mate choice. See Chapter 10.

**Shadow biosphere** — hypothetical microbes on Earth descended from a separate origin of life. None has been found. See Chapter 84.

**Sigma** — in statistics, a measure of how unlikely a signal is to be a fluke of random noise; physicists require five sigma to claim a discovery. See Chapter 81.

**Somatic cell** — any cell of the body other than the germline; changes here do not pass to offspring.

**Splicing** — the removal of introns from a freshly built RNA transcript. See Chapter 39.

**Stacked trait** — two or more engineered traits, such as herbicide tolerance and insect resistance, combined in one seed. See Chapter 65.

**Strange loop** — Douglas Hofstadter’s term for a system that, by moving through levels, comes back to represent itself. See Chapter 22.

**Tautomer** — an alternative chemical form of a molecule, differing in the position of a hydrogen atom. See Chapter 40.

**Technosignature** — any observable sign of technology, such as a radio signal, laser flashes, waste heat or industrial gases. See Chapter 82.

**Telomere** — a repetitive, protective stretch of DNA at the end of a chromosome. See Chapter 37.

**Tidal heating** — heat generated inside a moon or planet by the changing pull of a nearby body. See Chapter 80.

**Tidal locking** — the state in which a body always turns the same face toward the body it orbits. See Chapter 78.

**Transcription** — the copying of a DNA gene into RNA. See Chapter 38.

**Transcription factor** — a protein that binds DNA to help control transcription.

**Transfer RNA (tRNA)** — an adaptor molecule that matches a codon to its amino acid during translation. See Chapter 38.

**Transgenic** — carrying DNA from another species, as Roundup Ready soy carries a bacterial gene. Contrast gene-edited. See Chapter 63.

**Transit method** — finding planets by the small dip in a star’s brightness when a planet passes in front of it. See Chapter 78.

**Translation** — the process by which a ribosome builds a protein from an mRNA sequence. See Chapter 38.

**Transmission spectrum** — the measurement of how a planet’s apparent size during transit varies with wavelength, revealing the molecules in its atmosphere. See Chapter 81.

**Transposon** — a DNA sequence capable of copying and inserting itself elsewhere in the genome. See Chapter 39.
