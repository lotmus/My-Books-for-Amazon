## Reading Alien Air — Starlight, Biosignatures and the K2-18 b Affair

### Carl Sagan Looks for Life on Earth

On 8 December 1990, NASA's Galileo spacecraft, on its way to Jupiter, swung past Earth to borrow some of its orbital speed. The astronomer Carl Sagan saw an opportunity in the maneuver. Here was a robotic probe carrying the kind of instruments that might one day visit an unknown world. Why not point them at Earth and ask, without cheating, whether they could tell that it was inhabited?

In 1993 Sagan and his colleagues published the answer in Nature, under the title "A search for life on Earth from the Galileo spacecraft." Galileo saw an atmosphere rich in oxygen. It saw methane, at a concentration enormously higher than it should be in air full of oxygen, since the two react and destroy each other; something had to be replenishing the methane fast. It saw a sharp rise in reflected light just beyond the red end of the visible spectrum over land, which is the signature of chlorophyll-bearing plants. And it picked up narrowband radio transmissions, modulated in ways no natural source produces. The team's conclusion: life was likely, and some of it was probably technological.

The exercise did two things. It showed that a planet can announce its biology in its light. And it showed that the strongest evidence was not any single gas but a combination that could not last without something keeping it going. This chapter is about how astronomers are trying to repeat Sagan's test on planets around other stars, where the probe is replaced by a telescope trillions of kilometers away.

### Starlight Through a Planet's Air

The trick is the transit, described in Chapter 78. When a planet passes in front of its star, most of the starlight that would have reached us is blocked by the solid planet. But a thin ring of light passes through the planet's atmosphere on the way. Molecules in that air absorb light at particular wavelengths, fingerprints set by the quantum energy levels of molecules, the same physics the earlier volumes of this series use to read the light of stars. At those wavelengths the planet looks very slightly larger, because its atmosphere is opaque there.

So astronomers measure the depth of the transit, the dip in starlight, at many wavelengths at once. A plot of transit depth against wavelength is called a transmission spectrum. Bumps in it are molecules.

![Figure 50. The principle of transmission spectroscopy. During a transit, a thin ring of starlight filters through the planet's atmosphere. Wavelengths that the atmosphere's molecules absorb are dimmed slightly more, so the planet appears larger at those wavelengths, producing bumps in the measured spectrum.](figures/figs/fig50.png)

The signals are tiny. For an Earth-sized planet in front of a Sun-like star, the extra dimming from the atmosphere would be less than a part per million. The method works best for planets with puffy atmospheres around small stars, because the planet blocks a larger fraction of the star. That is one reason so much attention goes to red dwarfs.

The first atmosphere of a planet outside the solar system was detected this way in 2001, when the Hubble Space Telescope saw sodium in the air of a hot Jupiter called HD 209458 b. The real change came with the James Webb Space Telescope, launched on 25 December 2021, with a 6.5-meter gold-coated mirror and instruments built for infrared light, where many important molecules have their strongest fingerprints. In 2022, in its first months of science, Webb found carbon dioxide in the atmosphere of a hot giant planet called WASP-39 b, with a clarity no previous telescope had come close to.

### What Counts as a Biosignature

A biosignature is anything that is best explained by life. For a planet's air, that means gases, or combinations of gases, that living things make and geology does not, or not in the same amounts.

Oxygen is the obvious candidate. Earth's air is about 21 percent oxygen only because photosynthesis keeps it topped up, as Chapter 4 explained in describing the first pollution disaster. Without life, oxygen reacts with rock and volcanic gases and disappears in a few million years. Ozone, which forms from oxygen in sunlight, is easier to detect at some wavelengths and serves as a proxy.

But oxygen alone can be faked. On a planet that loses its water, ultraviolet light from the star splits water molecules, the hydrogen escapes, and the oxygen is left behind. This false positive is expected to be most likely around the violent young red dwarfs of Chapter 78, which is exactly where the planets easiest to observe are.

The strongest biosignature is a combination out of balance. The chemist James Lovelock argued this in 1965, while working with NASA on how to detect life on Mars. A lifeless planet's atmosphere drifts toward chemical equilibrium, a state in which nothing reacts any more. Life holds an atmosphere away from equilibrium. Oxygen and methane together, which Galileo saw on Earth, are the textbook example: they destroy each other, so if both are present in quantity, something must be producing both all the time.

Other candidates include nitrous oxide, made by soil microbes, and a family of sulfur compounds made on Earth almost only by marine life. The most famous of these is dimethyl sulfide, or DMS, the molecule that gives the seaside its smell. It is produced mostly by plankton. Remember that molecule.

### What Webb Has Found So Far

Webb's most important result on small planets so far is a negative one, which is useful precisely because it narrows the search.

TRAPPIST-1, the red dwarf with seven Earth-sized planets, was an early target. In 2023, measurements of the heat glowing from the innermost planet, TRAPPIST-1 b, showed a dayside at about 230 degrees Celsius, consistent with bare rock and no thick atmosphere to carry heat around. The second planet, TRAPPIST-1 c, which some had hoped might be a Venus-like world with a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere, turned out not to have one either.

The planet in the middle of the habitable zone, TRAPPIST-1 e, is harder. Its first four transits, analyzed in 2025, ruled out a thick atmosphere dominated by hydrogen and made thick carbon dioxide atmospheres like those of Venus or ancient Mars unlikely. They could not distinguish bare rock from a nitrogen-rich atmosphere with traces of other gases, like Earth's. A longer program of transits is running, and additional observations in 2026 have not changed the picture. The biggest obstacle is the star itself. Red dwarfs are covered in dark spots and bright patches, and those can imprint false features on a transmission spectrum. Separating star from planet is now one of the main jobs of the field.

Other results have been more encouraging. In 2024, Webb detected what appears to be a genuine secondary atmosphere, probably of carbon dioxide or carbon monoxide, around 55 Cancri e, a scorching rocky planet covered in molten lava. It is no home for life, but it showed that a rocky planet can hold on to an atmosphere close to its star. And measurements of LHS 1140 b, a temperate planet about 49 light-years away, have offered tentative hints of a nitrogen-rich atmosphere that need confirmation.

### The K2-18b Affair

Then there is the story that made headlines around the world.

K2-18 b orbits a red dwarf about 124 light-years away in the constellation Leo. It was found in 2015 by Kepler's second mission. It is about 8.6 times the mass of Earth and about 2.6 times its diameter, too big to be a rocky planet like ours and too small to be a gas giant: a type astronomers call a sub-Neptune, which has no counterpart in our solar system. It orbits in its star's habitable zone.

In 2021, Nikku Madhusudhan and colleagues at the University of Cambridge proposed that some sub-Neptunes could be what they named hycean worlds: planets with a global ocean of water under a hydrogen-rich atmosphere. In September 2023, Webb observations analyzed by Madhusudhan's team detected methane and carbon dioxide in K2-18 b's atmosphere, with no ammonia, a combination they said fitted a hycean world. The same paper reported a weak hint of DMS.

In April 2025 the team published new observations, with a different Webb instrument at longer wavelengths, and reported a signal they attributed to DMS or its close relative dimethyl disulfide, at a statistical significance of about three sigma. They described it as the strongest evidence yet for possible biological activity beyond the solar system, while saying that more data were needed. The story went around the world.

A word about sigma. It measures how likely a signal is to be a fluke of random noise, assuming the analysis is otherwise correct. Three sigma means roughly a three-in-a-thousand chance of a fluke. That sounds convincing, but particle physicists, who have been burned many times, require five sigma, less than one chance in a million, before calling something a discovery. And sigma says nothing about systematic errors: a wrong model of the star, the instrument or the atmosphere can produce a three-sigma signal that is entirely false.

Within months, independent teams reanalyzed the same data. They used different methods to process the raw images, different models of the atmosphere, and different lists of molecules that might be present. One study, published in 2025 in the Astronomical Journal, found that most of its analyses did not favor DMS at all, and its title said the claim did not meet the standards of evidence. A joint analysis of all the available Webb data, across the whole wavelength range, found no significant evidence for DMS or its relative, and estimated that about 25 more transits with the longer-wavelength instrument would be needed to settle the question. Other teams pointed out that molecules such as ethane, which can be made without life, have similar fingerprints in the same part of the spectrum. A 2026 reanalysis found that the DMS signal largely disappeared once the data were processed in a different way.

Meanwhile, two other facts weakened the biological reading. In 2024, DMS was found in the gas around comet 67P, measured years earlier by the European Rosetta mission, and it has also been found in interstellar space. So DMS can be made without life. And several modeling groups showed that K2-18 b's observed gases can be explained without any ocean, either by a deep hydrogen-rich atmosphere with no surface at all, or by a planet with a magma ocean under its air.

As of 2026, the consensus is that K2-18 b has methane and carbon dioxide in its atmosphere, that it is not known whether it has an ocean, and that DMS has not been detected. Madhusudhan's team continues to collect data. This is how science is supposed to work. A bold claim was made in public with its uncertainties stated, other researchers tested it with the same data, and the claim did not hold up at the level needed. Nobody behaved badly. But it is a preview of how the first real biosignature claim will go, and a reason to expect that it will take years, not a press conference, to settle.

### Bigger Mirrors

The next generation of instruments is being built to do what Webb can only start.

The European Southern Observatory's Extremely Large Telescope, under construction on a mountaintop in Chile's Atacama Desert, has a main mirror 39 meters across made of 798 hexagonal segments. Its first light is now planned for March 2029, with science beginning in late 2030. It will be able to study the reflected light of some nearby rocky planets directly, including perhaps Proxima b.

The European Space Agency's Ariel mission, due to launch in 2029, will survey the atmospheres of about a thousand known planets, most of them large and hot, to learn how planetary atmospheres work in general.

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, launched on 30 August 2026, is mainly a cosmology mission, but it carries a coronagraph, an instrument that blocks a star's light to reveal faint planets beside it, as a technology test.

The big prize is a telescope designed from the start to find life. NASA calls it the Habitable Worlds Observatory: a space telescope with a mirror perhaps six to eight meters across and a coronagraph good enough to suppress a Sun-like star's light by a factor of ten billion, so that an Earth-like planet next to it can be seen directly. Its goal is to find and image about 25 potentially Earth-like planets around nearby Sun-like stars and take spectra of their reflected light, looking for oxygen, water, methane and the Lovelock imbalance. In 2026 it is still a concept in technology development, aimed at the 2040s.

### The Punchline

A planet's atmosphere can be read from light-years away. During a transit, starlight filtered through the planet's air carries the fingerprints of its molecules. Webb has made this routine for large planets and has begun to do it for small ones.

The best biosignature is a combination of gases that should destroy each other but persists, as oxygen and methane do on Earth, which Carl Sagan's Galileo test detected in 1990. Single gases, including oxygen, can be faked by geology and stellar light.

So far, Webb has found that the inner TRAPPIST-1 planets are probably bare rock and that TRAPPIST-1 e has no thick hydrogen or carbon dioxide atmosphere. The claimed detection of DMS on K2-18 b has not survived independent analysis. No biosignature has been confirmed anywhere. The telescopes able to find one around a Sun-like star are being built.

Gases are one way a planet can announce life. There is another, which only works if the life has learned to build transmitters. The next chapter is about listening.
