## Mars — The Planet That Dried Out

### Leopard Spots

On 21 July 2024, NASA's Perseverance rover drilled into a reddish rock in an ancient river channel on Mars. The rock was about a meter long, shaped roughly like an arrowhead, and the rover team had nicknamed it Cheyava Falls, after a waterfall in the Grand Canyon. It was full of white veins of calcium sulfate, the mineral left behind when water once seeped through cracks. What had made the team stop, though, were the spots.

Scattered across the rock were pale patches a millimeter or so across, each ringed by a dark rim. The team called them leopard spots. On Earth, spots like that form in sediments when chemical reactions shift iron and other elements around, and very often the reactions are run by microbes, which gain energy by moving electrons between iron, sulfur and organic matter. Perseverance's instruments found that the rims were rich in two minerals: vivianite, an iron phosphate, and greigite, an iron sulfide. Its spectrometer had also picked up signs of organic carbon in the rock.

The core sample was named Sapphire Canyon. It went into a sealed titanium tube inside the rover. In September 2025, after more than a year of analysis, the team published its paper in Nature. The careful phrase the team used was "potential biosignature": a feature that could have been made by life, for which the non-biological explanations are possible but look less likely. NASA's leadership called it the clearest sign of life yet found on Mars.

It may be. This chapter explains why the scientists themselves are being so careful, why the answer is sitting in a tube on another planet, and why, as of 2026, nobody knows when the tube will come home.

### A Wet Young Mars

The first close images of Mars, from Mariner 4 in 1965, showed a cratered surface that looked like the Moon, and the disappointment was immediate. Six years later Mariner 9 went into orbit and mapped the whole planet, and the picture changed. There were enormous canyons, giant volcanoes and, most important, channels that looked as though they had been cut by flowing water.

Since then, the evidence for an ancient wet Mars has become overwhelming. The two rovers Spirit and Opportunity, which landed in January 2004, found minerals that form only in water, including hematite spheres that the team called blueberries, and sulfates that suggested the water had sometimes been acidic. Orbiters mapped clays, which form when water alters rock over long periods. River deltas, ancient lake shorelines and gravel conglomerates, rounded pebbles that can only be made by flowing streams, all point the same way.

The working picture is this. More than about 3.5 billion years ago, Mars had a thicker atmosphere and, at least at times, lakes, rivers and perhaps a northern sea. That is roughly the same period in which Chapters 3 and 4 placed the origin of life on Earth. Then Mars lost most of its air. Its global magnetic field shut down, the solar wind stripped the upper atmosphere, and the small planet, with less internal heat than Earth, lost the volcanic activity that might have replaced it. The water froze into the ground and the polar caps, or escaped to space.

If life began on Mars in that early window, it might have died out, or it might have retreated underground where liquid water could persist. Either way, the place to look for its traces is in rocks from the wet period.

### Viking: The Experiment That Said Yes

The first serious attempt to look was the most ambitious. Viking 1 landed on Mars on 20 July 1976, seven years to the day after Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. Viking 2 followed on 3 September. Each carried a biology package with three experiments designed to detect living microbes in the soil.

The most famous was the Labeled Release experiment, designed by the engineer Gilbert Levin. It dripped a nutrient broth tagged with radioactive carbon-14 onto a soil sample and watched for radioactive gas. If microbes ate the nutrients, they should breathe out radioactive carbon dioxide. They seemed to. Gas came off at once. When the experiment was repeated with soil that had been heated to sterilize it, the gas did not come off. By the criteria written before launch, that was a positive result.

But another instrument, a gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer, heated the soil and looked for organic molecules, the carbon compounds any life would be made of. It found essentially none, to a sensitivity of parts per billion. Life without organic molecules made no sense. Most of the Viking team concluded that the soil contained some reactive chemical that mimicked metabolism. Levin argued for the rest of his life that Viking had found life.

The likely culprit turned up 32 years later. In 2008 NASA's Phoenix lander, near the north pole, found perchlorate in the soil, a salt of chlorine and oxygen that is a strong oxidizer when heated. Perchlorate and the chemicals radiation makes from it can break down organic molecules, which may also explain why Viking's oven found nothing: heating the soil with perchlorate in it would have burned up any organics before they could be measured. Most researchers now think the Labeled Release result was chemistry, not biology, and that Viking's organics test was blinded by the same salt.

Viking taught a lesson that has shaped every mission since. You cannot design a single experiment that says "life" with certainty, because a planet's own chemistry can fake most of what a microbe does. You need to understand the environment first.

### ALH84001: The Rock on the White House Lawn

On 27 December 1984, a team collecting meteorites from the ice of the Allan Hills in Antarctica picked up a potato-sized greenish rock. It was catalogued as ALH84001 and, about a decade later, identified as a piece of Mars: its trapped gases matched the Martian atmosphere measured by Viking. It had crystallized about four billion years ago, was knocked off Mars by an impact about 17 million years ago, and fell on Antarctica about 13,000 years ago.

On 7 August 1996, a team led by David McKay at NASA's Johnson Space Center announced in Science that the rock contained possible evidence of ancient Martian life. There were tiny globules of carbonate minerals, organic molecules called polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, chains of magnetite crystals of the sort some Earth bacteria make, and, in electron microscope images, worm-shaped objects a few tens of nanometers long. President Bill Clinton spoke about the rock on the South Lawn of the White House.

Each piece of evidence was challenged, and over the next decades each found a plausible non-biological explanation. The worm shapes are far smaller than any known cell, too small to hold the machinery Chapter 38 described. The magnetite and the carbonate can form without life. In 2022, a study led by Andrew Steele showed that the rock's organic molecules most likely formed when hot water reacted with minerals, a purely geological process. Today very few researchers think ALH84001 contains fossils.

But it changed the field. It helped create NASA's astrobiology program, and it showed that pieces of Mars do reach Earth. About 400 Martian meteorites are now known. That fact will matter when Chapter 84 asks whether life could have traveled between the planets.

### Curiosity: The Lake That Could Have Been Lived In

In August 2012 NASA landed Curiosity, a rover the size of a small car, in Gale Crater, a 154-kilometer basin with a mountain of layered sediment in the middle. Its job was not to look for life but to find out whether Mars had ever been able to support it.

Within months it had the answer. In mudstone at a site called Yellowknife Bay, it drilled out powder that showed clays, low salinity, a neutral pH, and a mix of chemicals in different states of oxidation, the redox gradient that Chapter 77 described as an energy source. The team announced in 2013 that Gale Crater had once held a lake that microbes like some on Earth could have lived in. Habitable, in the strict sense: the first three boxes ticked.

Curiosity has since found more. In 2018 it reported organic molecules, including sulfur-bearing ring compounds, preserved in mudstone about three billion years old. In 2025 its team reported the largest organic molecules yet found on Mars: decane, undecane and dodecane, chains of 10 to 12 carbon atoms, in a rock sample drilled years earlier and stored on board. On Earth, chains like that are often fragments of fatty acids, which are made by life, although they can also be made without it. And Curiosity has measured methane in the air of Gale Crater, with a low background that seems to rise and fall with the seasons and occasional spikes. The European Space Agency's Trace Gas Orbiter, which can measure methane from above with great sensitivity, has seen almost none high in the atmosphere. Where the methane comes from, and where it goes, is one of the open puzzles of Mars.

None of this is evidence of life. All of it is consistent with a planet that once had the conditions for it, and that preserves organic molecules in its rocks better than anyone expected.

### Perseverance and the Tubes

Perseverance landed on 18 February 2021 in Jezero Crater, a basin 45 kilometers wide that once held a lake fed by a river. The river's delta, a fan of layered sediment, is still visible from orbit. River deltas on Earth are among the best places to preserve traces of microbial life, because they bury fine sediment quickly.

The rover was designed for a two-part plan. It would study the rocks itself, and it would collect the most promising samples into sealed tubes for a later mission to bring back to Earth, where laboratories with equipment far too large to send to Mars could examine them. By 2026 it had filled 33 tubes, including 27 rock cores, along with samples of soil and atmosphere and empty "witness" tubes that record any contamination the rover carried from Earth. Ten tubes were set down on the crater floor in a backup depot in late 2022 and early 2023, in case the rover failed.

Sapphire Canyon, the core from Cheyava Falls, is one of the tubes still on board. Here is why its scientists say "potential." The minerals in the leopard spots, vivianite and greigite, form on Earth mainly when microbes reduce iron and sulfate while consuming organic matter. They can also form without life, but usually at higher temperatures, and the rocks of the Bright Angel formation, where Cheyava Falls sits, show no sign of having been heated. So the biological explanation fits better. But "fits better" is not "proven," and a mudstone on Mars cannot be put under the microscopes, isotope instruments and repeated independent tests that a claim of extraterrestrial life demands. That needs the rock on Earth.

### The Return That Did Not Happen

The plan to bring the tubes home was called Mars Sample Return, a joint program of NASA and the European Space Agency. It required a lander, a small rocket to launch the samples into Mars orbit, and an orbiter to catch them and fly them back. It would have been the first launch from the surface of another planet.

It was also expensive. In 2023 an independent review estimated its cost at 8 to 11 billion dollars and said the original schedule had no realistic chance of being met. NASA spent 2024 and 2025 looking for cheaper designs and narrowed the options to two, each estimated at roughly 6 to 7 billion dollars. In January 2026, Congress passed NASA's budget for fiscal year 2026 without funding the program. The money was redirected to a broader line for future Mars missions. Mars Sample Return, as designed, is effectively cancelled.

That leaves the tubes waiting. A different American return mission may yet be designed. China, meanwhile, plans a mission of its own, Tianwen-3, to launch around 2028 and bring Martian samples to Earth around 2031. It would land at a site of China's choosing, not at Jezero. And the European rover Rosalind Franklin, delayed for years and now planned for launch in 2028, carries a drill designed to reach two meters below the surface, deep enough to get beneath the layer where radiation and perchlorate destroy organic molecules.

### What Would Count

Suppose a sample does come back. What would convince scientists that something in it was alive once?

No single test. The standard that has emerged since ALH84001 is a combination: shapes that look like cells or microbial mats, at sizes that make sense; chemistry that matches what cells do, such as minerals made by microbial redox reactions; organic molecules with patterns that chemistry alone tends not to produce, such as chains of a preferred length or molecules of one handedness, a point Chapter 84 returns to; and isotope ratios showing that light carbon atoms were preferred over heavy ones, a fingerprint of enzymes. Each line can be faked by geology. All of them together, in the same place, would be hard to fake.

NASA has even written down a seven-step scale for life detection, from "a signal is detected" to "independent follow-up confirms the result is biological." Cheyava Falls, on that scale, is in the early steps: a signal detected, contamination largely ruled out, non-biological explanations considered but not excluded.

### The Punchline

Mars was wet when Earth's life was young, and then it dried out and lost its air. Viking's experiment seemed to say yes and was most likely fooled by perchlorate. ALH84001's fossils most likely turned out to be minerals. Curiosity showed that Gale Crater once held a habitable lake and that Martian rocks keep organic molecules for billions of years.

Perseverance has found, in a rock called Cheyava Falls, the best candidate yet: chemistry that on Earth is usually made by microbes, in rocks that were never heated. It is a potential biosignature, not a discovery. The core that could settle it is sealed in a tube on Mars, and the mission to bring it home has lost its funding.

If Mars disappoints, there is another kind of place to look, one that the habitable zone of Chapter 78 leaves out entirely. Some of the largest bodies of liquid water in the solar system are hundreds of millions of kilometers past Mars, under ice. They are the subject of the next chapter.
