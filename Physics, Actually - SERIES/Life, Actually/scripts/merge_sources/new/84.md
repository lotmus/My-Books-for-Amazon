## A Second Genesis — Panspermia, Mirror Life and What One More Example Would Prove

### Pasteur's Tweezers

In 1848, a 25-year-old chemist in Paris named Louis Pasteur was studying the crystals of a salt of tartaric acid, a compound that forms in wine barrels. Two forms of the acid were known. One rotated polarized light to the right. The other, chemically identical as far as anyone could tell, did not rotate it at all. Pasteur looked at the crystals of the second form under a magnifying glass and noticed that they came in two shapes, each the mirror image of the other, like a left and a right hand. Patiently, with tweezers, he sorted them into two piles. Dissolved separately, one pile rotated light to the right and the other rotated it to the left. Mixed together, they canceled.

He had discovered that molecules can have handedness. A molecule built around a carbon atom with four different groups attached can exist in two forms that are mirror images and cannot be superimposed, as your left hand cannot be fitted into a right-hand glove. Chemists call this chirality, from the Greek for hand.

Here is the strange part, which Pasteur himself spent years thinking about. Chemistry without life makes both hands in equal amounts. Life does not. Every protein in every living thing on Earth is built from left-handed amino acids. The sugars in DNA and RNA are all right-handed. Part VI showed the consequence: the double helix twists one way because its sugars are one-handed, and the enzymes that copy it fit only that twist.

Handedness is one of the clearest ways to tell life's chemistry from ordinary chemistry. It may also be one of the best ways to tell whether life somewhere else began on its own, or is a distant cousin of ours. That is the question of this last chapter of the Part.

### One Tree, One Code

All the life ever studied on Earth is one family. Chapter 4 told the story of the last universal common ancestor, dated by a 2024 study to about 4.2 billion years ago. Chapter 38 described the genetic code, the table that translates three-letter words of DNA into amino acids. With small variations, every living thing uses the same table, the same four letters, the same twenty amino acids, the same handedness, the same energy currency.

That is the strongest single piece of evidence for common descent, and it means that, in a strict sense, biology is a science with one example. Every generalization about life, from what it needs to how it evolves, has been made by studying one family tree. The question of Chapter 83, how often life begins, cannot be answered from one tree, because we would have to exist on a planet where it began at least once, whether that was likely or not.

A second example would change that at once. If life began twice in one solar system, independently, then it is not a fluke. Given the number of planets described in Chapter 78, it would almost certainly be common across the universe.

But the second example would have to be independent. And there is a complication.

### Rocks Between Planets

The idea that life traveled to Earth from somewhere else is old. The Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius proposed in the early 1900s that bacterial spores could drift between stars pushed by the pressure of starlight. He called it panspermia, seeds everywhere. In 1973 Francis Crick, co-discoverer of the double helix in Part VI, and the chemist Leslie Orgel proposed, half seriously, directed panspermia: that Earth might have been seeded deliberately by another civilization. Neither idea has any evidence behind it, and both have the drawback that they move the problem of the origin of life somewhere else without solving it.

A more modest version, lithopanspermia, is taken much more seriously. Large impacts throw rock off planets, and some of that rock lands on other planets. Chapter 79 described ALH84001, one of about 400 known meteorites from Mars. Mars rocks have been landing on Earth throughout its history. A microbe deep inside such a rock could, in principle, survive the shock of ejection, the years or millions of years in space, and the heat of entry, since the inside of a large meteorite stays cool.

The survival experiments support the idea partly. In 1984 NASA's Long Duration Exposure Facility carried bacterial spores into orbit. When it was retrieved nearly six years later, a small fraction of the protected spores were still alive. In Japan's Tanpopo experiment, dried pellets of *Deinococcus radiodurans*, the bacterium from the meat can of Chapter 77, were left outside the International Space Station, and some cells in the thicker pellets survived for three years. Millions of years remain much harder, because cosmic radiation slowly destroys DNA. But the trip from Mars can be much shorter, and in the early solar system impacts were far more frequent.

So if life is found on Mars, the first question will be whether it is related to us. A Mars microbe that used DNA, the same code and left-handed amino acids would most likely be a cousin, sharing an ancestor with Earth life through a meteorite, in one direction or the other. It would tell us that life can travel. It would not tell us that life is common. A Mars microbe with a different code, different building blocks or the opposite handedness would be a second genesis.

### Ingredients From the Sky

Whatever the answer, the raw materials clearly arrive from space.

On 28 September 1969, a meteorite broke up over the town of Murchison in Australia, and more than 100 kilograms of fragments were collected. It turned out to be packed with organic molecules, including dozens of amino acids, some of which life uses and many it does not. The worry with any meteorite is contamination: it has landed on a planet covered in life.

That is why sample return missions matter. In December 2020 the Japanese Hayabusa2 spacecraft brought back material from the asteroid Ryugu, and in 2023 researchers reported finding uracil, one of the four letters of RNA, in it.

On 24 September 2023, NASA's OSIRIS-REx capsule landed in the Utah desert with about 120 grams of rock and dust from the asteroid Bennu, collected and sealed in space. In January 2025, two papers described what it contained. There were 14 of the 20 amino acids that life uses to build proteins. There were all five nucleobases used in DNA and RNA: adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil. There was a great deal of ammonia, and minerals showing that salty water had once evaporated inside the asteroid's parent body, leaving behind a sequence of salts, including phosphates, of a kind found in dried-up lake beds on Earth. The authors were careful to say that Bennu contains no life and never did. It contains the ingredients, made without life, in an environment that had liquid water.

The amino acids in Bennu had one more lesson. Unlike in some earlier meteorites, they came in nearly equal amounts of left and right hands. So whatever made life on Earth choose one hand, it was probably not handed to us ready-made from space. It happened later, during the origin itself or soon after.

### A Shadow Biosphere

There is another place a second genesis could be hiding: here.

In 2005, the physicist Paul Davies and the astrobiologist Charles Lineweaver suggested that Earth might host a shadow biosphere, microbes descended from a separate origin, using different chemistry, which we have missed because every test we use for life is designed for our kind. Most of Earth's microbes cannot be grown in a laboratory, and the methods for finding them rely on DNA sequences. A microbe without DNA would be invisible to those methods.

The idea had its one famous test in December 2010, when a NASA-funded team published a paper in Science claiming that a bacterium from Mono Lake in California, called GFAJ-1, could use arsenic in place of phosphorus in its DNA. It would not have been a second genesis, but it would have been a different chemistry. Other laboratories repeated the experiments within two years and found that the bacterium was simply very good at scavenging traces of phosphorus while tolerating arsenic. On 24 July 2025, Science retracted the paper, stating that its data did not support its conclusion; the authors disagreed. No shadow biosphere has been found. But nobody has searched very thoroughly either.

### Venus, Again

The most unexpected candidate in recent years was the planet Chapter 78 called the twin that went wrong. The surface of Venus is hopeless for life. But about 50 to 60 kilometers up, in its cloud layer, temperatures and pressures are close to Earth's at sea level, although the droplets are concentrated sulfuric acid.

In September 2020, a team led by Jane Greaves reported detecting phosphine, a molecule of one phosphorus atom and three hydrogens, in the clouds of Venus, at a concentration of about 20 parts per billion. On Earth, phosphine is made in significant amounts only by microbes living without oxygen, and by industry. The claim was challenged at once. Reanalysis of the data reduced the signal, other observations at different wavelengths set strict upper limits, and some argued that the signal was sulfur dioxide or an artifact of processing. The original team has continued observing and reports recovering a signal in later campaigns, with a further campaign approved for 2026.

As of 2026 the phosphine on Venus is neither confirmed nor ruled out. Even if confirmed, it would be an unexplained chemical anomaly, not a detection of life. The episode, like K2-18 b in Chapter 81, is a lesson in how hard a single-molecule biosignature is to make stick.

### Mirror Life

The last idea in this Part is not about finding a second genesis but about making one, and it brings the Part back to Part X.

Since chirality is a choice, a mirror-image cell is chemically possible: a bacterium built entirely from right-handed amino acids and left-handed sugars. Laboratories have already made mirror-image versions of some proteins and of short strands of DNA and RNA, and research toward mirror-image ribosomes, the protein-building machines of Chapter 38, has begun. A complete mirror bacterium is probably still a decade or more away.

In December 2024, a group of 38 scientists, including some who had worked toward that goal, published a warning in Science. A mirror bacterium, they argued, might not be recognized by the immune systems of humans, animals or plants, since those systems detect invaders by the shapes of their molecules. It might also escape the viruses and predators that keep ordinary bacteria in check. If it escaped, it could spread unchecked. They recommended that mirror bacteria not be created at all unless compelling evidence showed they would be safe, and they called for a global conversation about where to draw the line. The discussions continued through 2025 and 2026, and several research funders have said they will not support work aimed at building a mirror cell.

It is a striking moment in the long story this book has told. Chemistry that copies itself ran for four billion years with one handedness. Now one of its products has learned enough to make the other, and has had to decide whether it should.

### The Punchline

All known life is one family, with one code and one handedness. That makes biology a science with a single example, and it is why the question of how often life begins cannot be answered from Earth alone.

A second genesis would answer it. Life on Mars or in an icy moon with different chemistry, or a shadow biosphere on Earth, would show that the origin of life is not a fluke. Life on Mars with our chemistry would more likely be a cousin, carried by meteorite, which would show that life can travel but not that it is common.

Meanwhile, the sky delivers ingredients: Bennu brought amino acids, all five nucleobases, ammonia, phosphates and evidence of water, in a mix with no handedness. Venus's phosphine is unresolved. And on Earth, the ability to build a mirror-image cell has arrived before the wisdom about whether to use it.

No second example has yet been found. The tubes on Mars, the plumes of Enceladus and the light of nearby planets are the places it may be waiting. The book ends where it began, with a copy and a question, in the Epilogue.
