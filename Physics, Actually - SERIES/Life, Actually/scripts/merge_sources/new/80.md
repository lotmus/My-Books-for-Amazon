## Oceans Under Ice — Europa, Enceladus and Titan

### Seventeen Kilometers a Second

On 9 October 2008, NASA's Cassini spacecraft, which had been orbiting Saturn for four years, dived toward a small white moon called Enceladus and flew straight through a fountain of ice coming out of its south pole. It passed about 25 kilometers above the surface at 17.7 kilometers per second, about twenty times faster than a rifle bullet. In the few seconds it spent inside the spray, an instrument called the Cosmic Dust Analyzer caught individual ice grains. Each one hit a metal plate so hard that it vaporized into a puff of charged fragments, and the instrument weighed the fragments.

Enceladus is only about 500 kilometers across. It is about as wide as Great Britain. At Saturn's distance, sunlight is about a hundredth of what Earth gets, and the moon's surface is about minus 200 degrees Celsius. By the habitable zone of Chapter 78, it should be dead and frozen solid. Yet it was spraying water into space.

Seventeen years later, in October 2025, a team led by Nozair Khawaja published its analysis of those grains. They were freshly ejected, only minutes old when they hit Cassini, and they carried organic molecules that had not been seen before in Enceladus's spray: esters, ethers, and chains and rings of carbon, with hints of nitrogen and oxygen built in. These are the kinds of compounds that form in warm water reacting with rock. Two years earlier another team had found phosphates in the moon's ice. Put together with what Cassini had already learned, they described a small world with a salty ocean, a source of chemical energy, organic chemistry and phosphorus. Three boxes ticked, and the third one ticked more firmly than anywhere else outside Earth.

This chapter is about how worlds far outside any habitable zone can have liquid water, and the three moons that are now the main targets of the search.

### Heat From Squeezing

The habitable zone assumes that a planet's warmth comes from its star. For moons of giant planets there is a second source: tides.

A moon in a slightly elongated orbit is pulled harder by its planet when it is close and less hard when it is far. The changing pull stretches and relaxes the moon's interior every orbit, like a rubber ball squeezed in a hand. The flexing turns orbital energy into heat. If neighboring moons keep tugging the orbit so that it never becomes perfectly circular, the heating never stops.

The idea earned one of the best-timed predictions in planetary science. In March 1979, three researchers, Stanton Peale, Patrick Cassen and Ray Reynolds, published a paper arguing that tidal heating should melt the interior of Jupiter's moon Io and might make it volcanically active. The paper appeared three days before Voyager 1 flew past Io. Voyager's images showed eight erupting volcanoes. Io is now known to be the most volcanic body in the solar system.

Farther out from Jupiter, the same heating is gentler. It is not enough to melt rock, but it can be enough to keep water liquid beneath a shell of ice. Ice is a good insulator, and it floats, as Chapter 77 pointed out, so a frozen surface protects the water below it. Add the heat of radioactive elements decaying in the rocky core, and several moons of Jupiter and Saturn appear to hold oceans that have been liquid for billions of years.

### Europa: More Water Than Earth

Europa is slightly smaller than our Moon. Its surface is bright, young ice crossed by long reddish cracks, with very few craters, which means something keeps resurfacing it. The idea that an ocean lies beneath was proposed in the late 1970s and strengthened by Voyager images, but the decisive evidence came from NASA's Galileo spacecraft, which orbited Jupiter from 1995 to 2003.

Galileo carried a magnetometer. As Europa moved through Jupiter's powerful, tilted magnetic field, the magnetometer detected a second, induced field coming from Europa itself, which flipped as Jupiter's field changed direction. That is what happens when a changing magnetic field passes through an electrical conductor. Ice does not conduct well. Salty water does. In 2000 the Galileo team, led by Margaret Kivelson, reported that the best explanation was a global layer of salt water under the ice.

The current picture is an ice shell perhaps 15 to 25 kilometers thick, though estimates range from a few kilometers to more, over an ocean perhaps 60 to 150 kilometers deep. That ocean would hold about twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans combined. Its floor is rock, which is important. Water in contact with rock can react with it, as it does at Earth's hydrothermal vents, releasing hydrogen and other chemicals that hold energy.

The surface adds another energy source. Jupiter's radiation belts bombard Europa's ice and split water molecules, producing oxygen and hydrogen peroxide, which are oxidants. If the ice shell slowly overturns, it could carry these oxidants down to the ocean, where they could meet the reduced chemicals coming up from the rock. That is the redox gradient of Chapter 77, built across a whole world. In 2023 the James Webb Space Telescope found carbon dioxide concentrated in a region of disrupted terrain called Tarr Regio, in a form that suggests the carbon came up from inside. Sodium chloride, ordinary table salt, had already been identified on the surface.

When Galileo's fuel ran low in 2003, NASA deliberately crashed it into Jupiter. The reason was planetary protection: the spacecraft might have carried Earth microbes, and an accidental crash on Europa could contaminate the ocean everyone wanted to explore.

### Enceladus: The Ocean That Sprays Itself Into Space

Enceladus was a surprise. Nobody expected a moon that small to be active. In 2005, Cassini's magnetometer noticed that Saturn's magnetic field was being disturbed near the moon's south pole, and later that year cameras showed jets of ice spraying from four long, warm fractures the team named the tiger stripes. More than a hundred individual jets have since been mapped. Some of the spray falls back as snow. The rest feeds a faint ring around Saturn, the E ring.

Over the following decade Cassini built up the case, one measurement at a time.

The spray is salty water, not just ice, and its grains include sodium salts that mean the water has been in contact with rock.

In 2015, tiny grains of silica, a few nanometers across, were traced to the moon. On Earth, grains like that form when water hotter than about 90 degrees Celsius dissolves rock and then cools. The likely explanation is hydrothermal activity on Enceladus's seafloor.

Also in 2015, careful measurement of how the moon wobbles as it orbits showed that its ice shell is not attached to its core. The ocean is global.

In 2017, Cassini reported molecular hydrogen in the plume. On Earth, the reaction between hydrogen and carbon dioxide to make methane is one of the oldest ways microbes make a living. The hydrogen shows that a chemical energy source of that kind exists in Enceladus's ocean today.

In 2023, a team led by Frank Postberg found sodium phosphates in ice grains of the E ring, at concentrations at least a hundred times higher than in Earth's oceans. Phosphorus, the element Chapter 77 marked as the possible bottleneck, is there.

And the 2025 work on fresh grains added more complex organic molecules, the kind that could be steps on the road to biochemistry, or simply the products of hot water on rock.

Cassini itself was destroyed in September 2017, steered into Saturn for the same planetary-protection reason as Galileo. Its instruments were never designed to detect life. A future mission, which the European Space Agency has chosen as the theme of one of its large missions in the 2040s, would fly through the plume, or land near the tiger stripes, with instruments that can tell biology from chemistry. The appeal is obvious. On Europa you have to get through the ice. Enceladus sends its ocean up to you.

### Titan: The Strangest Familiar World

Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is bigger than the planet Mercury and is the only moon in the solar system with a thick atmosphere. Its air is mostly nitrogen, like ours, and about one and a half times as dense at the surface. But at minus 179 degrees, water is rock-hard ice. The liquid on Titan is methane and ethane, which rain from the clouds, carve river channels and fill lakes and seas near the poles. The largest, Kraken Mare, is bigger than the Caspian Sea.

On 14 January 2005, the European Huygens probe parachuted through Titan's haze and landed on a damp plain strewn with rounded pebbles of water ice. It sent back data for about 72 minutes from the surface before Cassini, its relay, sank below the horizon. It remains the most distant landing ever made.

Titan's atmosphere is a vast organic chemistry factory. Sunlight and particles from Saturn's magnetic field break up methane and nitrogen high in the air, and the fragments recombine into larger molecules that drift down as an orange haze and coat the surface with sand-like organic grains. The moon may also have a water ocean deep beneath its ice crust: Cassini measured the way Titan flexes under Saturn's tides, and the flexing is too large for a solid interior.

That gives Titan two very different chances. One is life as we know it, in the buried water ocean, which is probably hard to reach. The other is life as we do not know it, in the methane lakes on the surface, using some chemistry that works in a nonpolar solvent at extreme cold. In 2015, chemists proposed a kind of membrane, nicknamed an azotosome, made from small nitrogen-bearing molecules, that might work in liquid methane. In 2017 the ALMA radio telescope detected one candidate building block, acrylonitrile, in Titan's atmosphere. Later calculations, in 2020, suggested that such membranes would not actually assemble under Titan's conditions. The idea of methane life is speculative, and it remains unsupported by any evidence. But Titan is the one place where it could be tested.

NASA's Dragonfly mission is designed to do some of that testing. Dragonfly is a rotorcraft, a nuclear-powered drone about the size of a car with eight rotors, which can fly comfortably in Titan's dense air and low gravity. It is scheduled to launch in July 2028 and arrive in 2034, landing near a crater called Selk, where an impact may once have melted water ice and mixed it with surface organics. It will fly from site to site, sampling surface material and analyzing its chemistry. It is not a life detector. It is a prebiotic chemistry laboratory, sent to the best natural experiment in the solar system on what happens when organic molecules and water meet without life getting there first.

### Who Is Going

As of 2026, three missions are on their way to the outer solar system, or soon will be.

Europa Clipper launched on 14 October 2024. After a flyby of Mars in 2025 and one of Earth in December 2026, it is scheduled to enter orbit around Jupiter in April 2030 and then make 49 close flybys of Europa, some as low as 25 kilometers. It carries ice-penetrating radar to measure the shell, a magnetometer to measure the ocean, and instruments to analyze dust and gas it passes through. Its question is whether Europa is habitable, not whether it is inhabited.

The European Space Agency's JUICE mission launched in April 2023 and will arrive at Jupiter in 2031. It will fly past Europa twice and then go into orbit around Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system, which is also thought to have a buried ocean, probably sandwiched between layers of ice.

Dragonfly is being built for its 2028 launch to Titan.

None of them can detect life directly. All of them will narrow down where a later mission should look, and what instruments it should carry.

### The Punchline

The habitable zone was drawn for water on a surface. Some of the largest bodies of liquid water in the solar system are under ice, kept warm by tides and radioactive decay, around planets far outside the Sun's zone.

Europa probably has twice as much water as Earth's oceans, in contact with rock, with oxidants arriving from above. Enceladus sprays its ocean into space, and Cassini found in the spray salt, hydrogen, silica from hot water, phosphates and complex organic molecules. Titan has organic chemistry on a planetary scale, methane lakes that might host a chemistry unlike ours, and possibly a water ocean beneath.

Our own solar system may therefore hold not one second chance for life but several. To look farther, around other stars, we cannot send spacecraft. We can only read starlight.
