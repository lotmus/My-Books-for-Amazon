## Listening for Neighbors — SETI and the Search for Technology

### Wow!

A few days after 15 August 1977, an astronomer named Jerry Ehman sat at his kitchen table in Ohio with a stack of computer printouts. He was a volunteer for the Big Ear, a radio telescope run by Ohio State University in a field near Delaware, Ohio. Big Ear did not move. It scanned the sky as Earth turned, and a computer printed the strength of the signal in each of 50 radio channels as a column of numbers and letters, with the letters standing for higher values.

Most of the printout was ones and spaces: noise. In one column, Ehman found a short string, 6EQUJ5, rising and then falling. It meant a signal had swept through the telescope's beam exactly as a point in the sky should, over 72 seconds, in a single narrow channel, at a strength about thirty times the background. He circled it in red pen and wrote in the margin: "Wow!"

The channel was close to 1420 megahertz, the frequency at which cold hydrogen atoms emit radio waves, which is exactly where some astronomers had argued, eighteen years earlier, that a civilization trying to be heard would broadcast. Big Ear and many other telescopes went back to the spot, in the constellation Sagittarius, dozens of times over the following decades. The signal never repeated.

In 2025, a team that re-examined the Wow! signal using archived data from the Arecibo Observatory proposed that it was most likely natural: a cold cloud of hydrogen, briefly brightened by a burst from some distant object behind it. The case is not closed. But that has been the pattern of every promising signal in sixty years of listening. This chapter explains what the search is, what it has covered, and why silence so far tells us much less than it seems to.

### A Frequency Everyone Knows

The modern search began with a two-page paper. In September 1959, two physicists at Cornell University, Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison, published "Searching for Interstellar Communications" in Nature. Their argument was short. Radio waves cross interstellar space easily. The galaxy is quiet at radio wavelengths in a band between the noise of electrons in our own galaxy and the noise of Earth's atmosphere. And any civilization that knows physics knows the 1420-megahertz line of hydrogen, the most common element. If they wanted to be found, that was where they would call. "The probability of success is difficult to estimate," Cocconi and Morrison wrote, "but if we never search, the chance of success is zero."

The next spring, a young radio astronomer named Frank Drake, working independently, pointed the 85-foot telescope of the National Radio Astronomy Observatory at Green Bank, West Virginia, at two nearby Sun-like stars, Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani. He called it Project Ozma, after the princess of the land of Oz. He listened for about 150 hours between April and July 1960 and heard nothing but one false alarm from a secret military aircraft. In November 1961, Drake hosted a small meeting at Green Bank to discuss the subject. To organize the agenda, he wrote an equation on the blackboard. Chapter 83 will look at it.

The field took a name: SETI, the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. The band between the hydrogen line at 1420 megahertz and the hydroxyl lines near 1662 megahertz was nicknamed the water hole, since hydrogen and hydroxyl together make water. It seemed a natural meeting place for water-based life.

### Sixty Years of Listening

The search has been run on a shoestring for most of its history. NASA began a formal search, the High Resolution Microwave Survey, on 12 October 1992, the five-hundredth anniversary of Columbus's landing in the Americas. Congress cancelled it within a year, after a senator dismissed it as "the Great Martian Chase." Since then most SETI has been privately funded.

The SETI Institute, founded in California in 1984, ran Project Phoenix, which from 1995 to 2004 examined about 800 nearby stars with some of the world's largest radio telescopes, and found nothing. With money from the Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen, it built the Allen Telescope Array in northern California, which began observing in 2007. From 1999 to 2020 a project called SETI@home let millions of volunteers lend their home computers to sift data from Arecibo, then the largest single-dish radio telescope in the world. In 1974 Arecibo had also sent the most famous deliberate message from Earth, a picture of 1,679 bits aimed at a star cluster 25,000 light-years away. The telescope collapsed in December 2020.

The largest search in history began on 20 July 2015, when the investor Yuri Milner and the physicist Stephen Hawking announced Breakthrough Listen, a 100-million-dollar, ten-year program. It bought time on the 100-meter Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia and the 64-meter Parkes telescope in Australia, later added the MeerKAT array in South Africa, and runs an optical search for laser pulses as well. Its computers can examine billions of narrow radio channels at once.

In 2020 Breakthrough Listen reported on its survey of 1,327 nearby stars: no signals. In 2019, while observing Proxima Centauri, the nearest star, it recorded a narrow signal at 982 megahertz that appeared only when the telescope was pointed at the star. It was named BLC1, for Breakthrough Listen Candidate 1. A careful study published in 2021 traced it to interference from electronic equipment on Earth. In 2025 the project listened to the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS as it passed through the solar system, after some speculation that it might be artificial, and heard nothing.

### How Big Is the Haystack?

Sixty years of silence sounds like strong evidence that nobody is there. It is not, and the reason is worth understanding.

A search has to choose where to point, at what frequencies to listen, how narrow a signal to look for, how faint a signal it can detect, and for how long. Each choice cuts down the volume of possibilities it covers. In 2018, the astronomer Jason Wright and two students calculated how much of this many-dimensional haystack all the radio searches to date had examined. Their answer was a small fraction: they compared it to looking for fish by examining a hot tub's worth of water out of all of Earth's oceans. Later searches have added more, but the comparison still holds in spirit.

The detection limits matter too. Earth's own unintentional radio leakage, from television and radar, would be very hard to detect at the distance of even the nearest stars with present instruments. Most searches could detect only a strong beacon deliberately aimed at us, or an extremely powerful transmitter. A civilization exactly like ours, a few hundred light-years away, would probably go unheard.

So the honest summary is: we have ruled out very loud, persistent beacons pointed at us from a few thousand of the nearest stars, at the frequencies we chose. That is not nothing. But it is a small corner of the possibilities.

### Little Green Men and Natural Lighthouses

Every candidate signal so far has turned out to be one of two things: interference from our own technology, or a natural object we had not understood.

The classic natural example is from 1967. At Cambridge, a graduate student named Jocelyn Bell noticed a tiny bit of "scruff" on the paper chart recording of a new radio telescope. It turned out to be a train of pulses arriving every 1.34 seconds, so regular that she and her supervisor half-seriously labeled the source LGM-1, for Little Green Men. Within months she had found more of them in other parts of the sky, which made aliens much less likely. They were pulsars: spinning neutron stars, the collapsed cores of exploded stars, sweeping a beam of radio waves past Earth like a lighthouse. The discovery won a Nobel Prize in 1974, although not for Bell.

The lesson has been learned many times since. The universe is full of natural processes that produce surprising signals, and most of them have been found by people who were looking for something else. A SETI claim has to rule out every natural explanation and every possible human source. That is very hard to do.

### Beyond Radio: Technosignatures

In recent years the field has broadened its name and its methods. Instead of "intelligence," which is hard to define and impossible to observe directly, astronomers now speak of technosignatures: any observable sign of technology.

Lasers are one. A pulsed laser, focused by a telescope, could outshine its own star for a billionth of a second. Optical SETI searches for such flashes.

Waste heat is another. In 1960 the physicist Freeman Dyson pointed out in Science that a civilization using a large fraction of its star's energy would have to radiate the waste as infrared heat. A star wrapped in energy-collecting structures, later called a Dyson sphere, would look dimmer in visible light and brighter in infrared than it should. Searches of infrared sky surveys have looked for such stars. In 2024, a project called Hephaistos reported seven red dwarfs with puzzling infrared excesses. Within months, other astronomers showed that at least some of them were likely contaminated by distant, dusty galaxies in the same direction.

Strange dimming is a third. In 2015, the astronomer Tabetha Boyajian and a group of citizen scientists reported that a star called KIC 8462852, now known as Tabby's star, sometimes dimmed by as much as 22 percent, irregularly, in a way no planet could cause. Briefly, the idea of alien megastructures made headlines. Follow-up observations showed that the dimming was deeper in blue light than in red, which is what dust does and what a solid structure would not do.

Atmospheres can carry technosignatures too. The same telescopes that look for biosignatures could, in principle, detect industrial gases such as chlorofluorocarbons, which nature does not make, in the air of a nearby planet. Earth's own atmosphere has been carrying such a signal since the 1930s.

And there are objects. In 2017, the first known interstellar object, ʻOumuamua, passed through the solar system, oddly elongated and accelerating slightly as if pushed. One astronomer suggested it might be an artificial light sail. Most researchers concluded that the acceleration was caused by gas escaping from an icy body, even though no gas was seen directly. The third known interstellar visitor, 3I/ATLAS, discovered in July 2025, is clearly a comet, with a tail. It was still listened to.

### What Would Happen

Suppose a signal does survive all the checks. What then?

Researchers in the field have drawn up a set of principles, first agreed in 1989 and revised since. A detection should be confirmed independently by other observatories before it is announced. It should be announced publicly, to everyone, not kept secret. And no reply should be sent until there has been international consultation. These are agreements among scientists, not laws.

The likeliest form of a detection is also the least dramatic. It would almost certainly be one-way. Even a signal from a nearby star would take decades to answer, and from most of the galaxy, thousands of years. We would learn that someone existed, or had existed, since the signal would be old. We would probably not learn what they were saying.

### The Punchline

SETI has been listening since 1960, mostly at radio frequencies near the 1420-megahertz line of hydrogen, and more recently for laser flashes, waste heat and other technosignatures. Every candidate, from the Wow! signal of 1977 to BLC1 in 2019, has either failed to repeat or been traced to a natural source or to our own technology.

The silence is real, but the search has covered only a small part of the possibilities, and most searches could detect only a deliberate, powerful beacon aimed our way. A civilization like ours at a few hundred light-years could go unnoticed.

Still, there is a deeper version of the silence that does not depend on radio telescopes at all. If the galaxy is old and life is common, someone should have spread across it long ago, and should be here already. In 1950, over lunch, a physicist asked where they are. The next chapter takes his question seriously.
