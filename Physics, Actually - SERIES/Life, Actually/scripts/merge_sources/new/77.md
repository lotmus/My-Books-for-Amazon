## What Life Needs — Water, Energy and the Short List

### The Meat That Would Not Stay Sterile

In 1956, at the agricultural experiment station in Corvallis, Oregon, a microbiologist named Arthur Anderson was testing a new way to preserve food. The idea was simple. Seal the meat in a can, hit it with a dose of gamma rays large enough to shred the DNA of anything living inside, and the meat should keep forever. The cans were irradiated. Some of them spoiled anyway.

Anderson cultured what was growing in them and found a pink bacterium that shrugged off radiation thousands of times stronger than the dose that would kill a person. It was later named *Deinococcus radiodurans*, roughly "terrible berry that withstands radiation." Its trick is not armor. The radiation breaks its chromosome into hundreds of pieces, just as it would break ours. The bacterium carries several copies of its genome and a repair system good enough to stitch the fragments back into working order within hours.

That is the right place to begin a Part about life elsewhere, because it resets the question. When people ask whether a planet could support life, they usually picture a person standing on it, and then ask whether the person could breathe. That is the wrong test. Most of the history of life on Earth, as Part I and Part III described, was microbes, and microbes have turned out to be much harder to kill than we assumed. The question is not "Could we live there?" It is "What does the copying chemistry of the first ten chapters actually require?"

The answer is a short list. This chapter goes through it.

### The Definition, One More Time

Chapter 1 settled on a working definition: life is chemistry that copies itself, imperfectly, and lets the imperfections be judged. NASA's astrobiologists use a version of the same idea, worked out by a panel in the 1990s and usually quoted as "a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution."

That definition is useful here because it tells you what to look for and what not to. It does not say "made of cells," or "based on DNA," or "breathes oxygen." Those are facts about the one example we have. It says that somewhere there must be chemistry that stores information, copies it with small errors, and runs for long enough that the errors can be sorted by what survives. Everything else on the short list follows from what that chemistry needs to keep going.

There are four items. A liquid to do the chemistry in. A supply of energy. The right elements. And time, with enough stability that the copying is not wiped out before it gets anywhere.

### A Liquid to Work In

Chemistry that copies needs molecules that can meet, react, separate and meet again. In a solid, molecules are locked in place. In a gas, they are too far apart and anything large falls apart or drifts away. A liquid is the middle state: dense enough that molecules collide all the time, loose enough that they keep moving.

On Earth the liquid is water, and Chapter 2 explained why it is unusually good at the job. It dissolves salts and many small organic molecules. It stays liquid over a wide range of temperatures. It is polar, which is what lets oily molecules gather into membranes, the trick that makes a cell possible. And ice floats, so a freezing lake or ocean is insulated by its own lid instead of freezing solid from the bottom up. That last fact will matter enormously in Chapter 80.

Could another liquid do it? This is a working question, not a settled one. Liquid ammonia dissolves many of the same things water does, and stays liquid at colder temperatures. Liquid methane and ethane exist on the surface of Saturn's moon Titan, at about minus 179 degrees Celsius. They are poor solvents for the kind of charged and polar molecules our chemistry uses, but that does not prove a different chemistry could not use them. Nobody knows. What can be said is that water is common in the universe, because it is made of the first and third most abundant chemically active elements, hydrogen and oxygen, and that every living thing we have ever found depends on it. So the search starts with water, and keeps the other liquids on a second list.

### Energy, Which Means Electrons Moving

Chapter 2 also made the point that biological energy is, at bottom, electrons moving downhill: from a molecule that holds them loosely to one that holds them tightly, with the cell skimming off some of the energy on the way. Chapter 4 traced the machinery, the proton battery every cell on Earth runs on.

That gives a much broader picture of "energy" than sunlight. Plants and many microbes use light. But a large share of Earth's microbes have never seen the Sun. At hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor, and in fractured rock kilometers underground, microbes live on chemical energy alone: hydrogen made when water reacts with iron-rich rock, combined with carbon dioxide; or iron, sulfur and methane compounds that hold electrons at different strengths and can be made to trade them.

Chemists call this a redox gradient, short for reduction and oxidation, the two halves of an electron changing hands. A place where two kinds of chemistry meet without being at balance, such as hot reduced fluid from rock pouring into cold oxidized seawater, is a place where there is energy to harvest. Chapter 3 described why some origin-of-life researchers think life began at exactly that kind of boundary.

For the search, this is the most important single idea in the Part. It means a world does not need sunlight on its surface to have energy for life. It needs a liquid in contact with chemistry that is out of balance. That is why the most promising places in the solar system, outside Earth, include oceans buried under kilometers of ice.

### The Elements: CHNOPS and a Few Friends

Biologists have an ugly acronym for the elements most of life's mass is made of: CHNOPS, for carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur. Chapter 2 explained why carbon is the backbone: it makes four strong bonds, it bonds to itself in long chains and rings, and those bonds are strong enough to last but weak enough to be rearranged by enzymes at ordinary temperatures. Chapters 35 and 36 showed the same six elements at work in DNA: the sugar-phosphate backbone, the nitrogen-rich bases, the hydrogen bonds between them.

The cosmic supply is not the problem. Hydrogen, oxygen, carbon and nitrogen are among the most common elements in the universe. Comets and some meteorites carry complex organic molecules made without any life at all. Chapter 84 will look at the most recent and cleanest evidence, a sample returned from the asteroid Bennu in 2023.

The scarce ones may be phosphorus and, for some chemistries, a handful of metals. Phosphorus sits in the backbone of DNA and RNA and in ATP, the molecule cells use as small change for energy. It is much rarer than carbon in the universe and, on Earth, tends to be locked into minerals that dissolve poorly. Several origin-of-life chemists think the availability of phosphorus may be one of the real bottlenecks for life, here or anywhere. That is a working idea, and it is one reason the detection of phosphates in the ocean spray of a small moon of Saturn, in 2023, made such news. Chapter 80 tells that story.

What about silicon, the element science fiction offers as the alternative to carbon? It sits under carbon in the periodic table and also makes four bonds. But silicon-silicon bonds are weaker than carbon-carbon bonds, silicon's bond with oxygen is so strong that it tends to end up locked in rock, and its oxide is a solid, sand, rather than a gas like carbon dioxide that can be breathed in and out. Silicon life is not impossible in any proven sense. It is just much less likely than carbon life, and nobody has a convincing design for its chemistry. Like the other liquids, it stays on the second list.

### Time, and a Place That Holds Still

The fourth item is the least discussed and maybe the most important. Chapter 4 described the gap between the first cells and the first complex ones: something like two billion years in which life was entirely microbial. Chapter 16 described the mass extinctions that repeatedly cleared the board. Life on Earth needed hundreds of millions of years to get started and billions to get interesting, and it got them because the planet stayed habitable the whole time.

That requires a stable star, an orbit that does not swing the planet between frying and freezing, and some way of keeping the climate in range. On Earth that last job is done partly by a slow geological thermostat. Carbon dioxide is pulled out of the air when rain weathers rock, and returned by volcanoes. When the planet warms, weathering speeds up and draws down more carbon dioxide, which cools it again. When it cools, weathering slows and volcanic carbon dioxide builds up. The loop runs on timescales of hundreds of thousands of years, and it needs plate tectonics, liquid water and land.

Whether a planet has to have all that machinery to keep life going for billions of years is a working question. It is part of a larger argument, taken up in Chapter 83, about whether Earth is ordinary or rare.

### The Edges of the Envelope

Here is what Earth's own microbes say about how wide the list really is. These are measured limits, not guesses.

Heat. The record for growth is held by an archaeon called *Methanopyrus kandleri*, strain 116, which in 2008 was shown to multiply at 122 degrees Celsius under high pressure, in water that would boil at the surface. Earlier, in 2003, another archaeon nicknamed Strain 121 had been shown to survive a sterilizing autoclave.

Cold. Microbes keep metabolizing in brine channels inside sea ice and in permafrost at well below zero degrees Celsius, as long as salt keeps a little liquid water around them.

Acid and alkali. Microbes thrive in mine drainage with a pH near zero and in soda lakes with a pH above eleven.

Depth. Living cells have been recovered from rock several kilometers below the surface, and from sediments far beneath the sea floor, where they divide perhaps once in centuries. Some estimates put the total mass of this deep biosphere at a sizable fraction of all the microbial life on Earth.

Dryness and radiation. *Deinococcus*, from the meat can, also survives severe drying, which may be the real reason it can repair so much damage: drying breaks DNA too. The tardigrade, a half-millimeter animal with eight stubby legs, can dry itself into a dormant state called a tun and survive vacuum, extreme cold and heavy radiation. In 2007, on a European satellite mission called FOTON-M3, dried tardigrades were exposed directly to space for ten days, and some revived and reproduced afterward.

These organisms are called extremophiles, lovers of the extreme, although that is our label, not theirs. To the archaeon at a vent, 100 degrees is normal and the sea floor at 4 degrees is the extreme. The lesson is that life, once it exists, adapts to an extraordinarily wide range of conditions, provided liquid water is present at least some of the time. Dormancy is not growth. A tardigrade in space is surviving, not living. But it does show that the boundaries are wider than anyone in 1950 would have drawn them.

### Habitable Is Not Inhabited

There is a trap in all of this, and it is worth naming before the next chapters walk into it.

Showing that a place has liquid water, energy, the right elements and time shows that it is habitable: that life as we know it could live there. It does not show that life began there, or that it ever arrived. Habitable is a statement about conditions. Inhabited is a statement about history.

On Earth, the two are nearly the same, because life has had four billion years to reach every corner. Almost every habitable niche on this planet has been colonized, from the boiling vents to the clouds, and that makes it easy to forget that the colonization took time and had to start somewhere. A sterile habitable world is perfectly possible. Chapter 3 described how hard the origin is to explain even on a planet where we know it happened. Whether a habitable place usually becomes inhabited is the deepest unknown in this Part, and the only way to answer it is to find a second example, or a convincing lack of one.

So when the following chapters say that Mars once had lakes, or that a moon of Saturn has a salty ocean with energy and phosphorus, read it as "the first three boxes are ticked." The fourth box, whether anything is actually there, is the one the missions are trying to open.

### The Punchline

The list of what life needs is short: a liquid, almost certainly water; a source of energy, which can be chemical as well as light; carbon and a handful of other common elements, with phosphorus possibly the scarce one; and a long, stable stretch of time.

Earth's extremophiles show that, once life exists, it can work at the edges of all four. Nothing on the list is rare in the universe. Water, carbon and chemical energy are found almost everywhere we look. What remains unknown is the one step that is not on the list: how often the ingredients actually start copying themselves.

The next chapter takes the first item, liquid water, and asks where in a planetary system it can exist. The answer is a zone around each star, and it is less tidy than the textbooks make it sound.
