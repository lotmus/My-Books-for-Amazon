0009	Heading1	A NOTE TO THE READER
0010		This one isn’t a retelling. The other nine books on this shelf each stood behind a real film or television series and told its story again in prose, careful never to reprint a line of dialogue that wasn’t theirs to reprint. This one has no series to stand behind. It only has cousins — nine of them now, which is enough to make a family and, this book would argue, already enough to make a committee.
0011		It borrows its shape from the quiet cousins and a few tricks from the loud ones. From The Invaders and Dark Skies it takes the private fight: a person who saw something and cannot get anyone official to believe them. From OVNI(s) and UFO it takes the government effort: an office too small to matter, reporting to a commander who answers to people with flags on their desks. From They Live it takes the idea, without the eyewear, that the scariest occupation is the one that has already incorporated and is currently doing the quarterly numbers through the very screens we volunteer to watch. And because a household should not raise a child on only its solemn relatives, it borrows a little mischief from the loud side of the family too — a countdown hidden in noise, from the picture about the holiday; a cellar survivalist convinced he alone saw it coming, from the picture about the tripods under the street; a white flag offered in good faith and answered badly, from the picture about the Martians and the hymn; and the old, better lesson that a name spoken correctly can do what a fleet cannot, from the picture about the two enemies and the rock that outlasted their war.
0012		Unlike those four loud cousins, nothing in this book gets announced to the public. The sky here behaves the way it behaves in the quiet half of the family: badly, briefly, and only in front of witnesses somebody then has to manage. If you are looking for burning capitals and hangar speeches, this is the wrong shelf. If you are looking for a wedding video that will not stay deleted and a committee that has never once adjourned, you have found the right one.
0013		It also tries to answer two questions a patient reader eventually asks of any invasion story. How did they get here, given that faster-than-light travel is not, so far as this book is aware, a real thing? And once they were here, was a fight actually the right response? Those answers are the story. This note will not give them away. No character in this book wears special glasses. No fleet announces itself over a capital.
0014		The tone leans a little British, for fun, and a little absurd on purpose, in the general direction of Douglas Adams, who understood before most people that the end of the world is, for somebody, always a filing problem. No character, place, or organization in this book is drawn from his work, or from any of the nine film and television properties named above. Every name here was checked against all of them and belongs only to this book.
0015		In 2023 the United States really did open an office to study objects seen in the sky and gave it a name only a committee could love: the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office. Real allied governments really do run joint intelligence liaisons out of buildings near NATO headquarters that have nothing to do with any of this. This book does not belong to any of them, does not speak for them, and does not know anything they don’t. It invents a smaller office, a more anxious committee, and a satellite company that is, regrettably, more competent than several real ones.
0016		The people are invented. The alpaca, sadly, had to be invented too, because reality has not produced one yet, though reality is trying.
0017		The truth, everyone here will eventually agree, is not the same thing as an explanation. But for once, at least one of them is allowed to end.

0077	Heading2	The Best Man Speech
0078		On weddings: statistically, more unexplained aerial activity is recorded at weddings than at any other civilian gathering. No department has explained this. Most suspect it has something to do with the open bar.
0079		— The New Starter’s Handbook, uncleared draft
0080		Nora Calder had filmed forty-one weddings, and this one was, by every metric she used to judge such things — light, timing, an open bar that closed at a decent hour — shaping up to be a good one, right up until the water tower started doing something water towers do not do. She was thirty-four, dark-haired in the specific unglamorous way of someone who cuts it herself between jobs, and had the low, unhurried voice of a person who has spent a decade talking brides down from panic in loud rooms; put a camera in her hand and she stopped noticing she had a body at all, which was, she’d tell you, the whole appeal of the job.
0081		She had driven in that morning from Indianapolis with the second camera on the passenger seat and a paper cup of gas-station coffee going cold in the cup holder. Lissome announced itself the way small towns did: a speed-limit sign, a grain co-op, a water tower with LISSOME painted in letters that had been fading since before Nora had a license. The rented barn sat at the end of a gravel drive that popped under the tires. Somebody had strung Edison bulbs across the rafters. The air outside was cut-grass and manure and the sweet chemical punch of hydrangeas in buckets of warm water. Inside, the floorboards gave a little under a dance heel. Nora checked her batteries, taped a spare card into the lining of her jacket, and started working the room the way she always did — wide, then faces, then hands.
0082		By six the lot was a tangle of trucks and a single borrowed school bus from the church. Someone had put a hose on the side of the barn and children were already using it as a dare. Nora taped a channel list inside the equipment case, checked the battery again because she always checked the battery again, and walked the perimeter once the way a person walks a set before the actors arrive. Behind the barn a generator coughed and settled. The fields went on until they didn’t. A red-winged blackbird yelled from a ditch as if the whole county had offended it.
0083		She shot the empty tables first. White cloth, mason jars with wildflowers already wilting in the heat that hadn’t left the wood. A cousin tested the microphone and produced a sound like a fork in a socket. Nora winced, laughed, told him to tap it not slap it. Mabs appeared in the loft door for thirty seconds, barefoot on purpose, and blew her a kiss she would later pretend not to have on camera.
0084		The vows themselves were almost ordinary, which Nora would later be grateful for. Dominic said his line without looking at the sky. Mabs cried in the efficient way of a person who had budgeted for it. A flower girl sat down in the aisle and had to be lifted like luggage. Nora moved when she needed to move and was still when she needed to be still. The light had not happened yet. The barn still smelled like a good decision.
0085		Mabs found her by the cake table, already in the dress, already laughing. Two years younger, hair the same near-black piled up with pins that would not last the night, a smear of frosting already on her thumb. Dominic came in behind her in a rented suit that actually fit, kissed Mabs’s temple, and asked Nora whether she wanted water or something with bubbles. He had the easy manners of a man who liked being useful. Nora liked him, which later made the footage worse.
0086		It began as a pressure change more than a sight: the hairs on Nora’s arms lifted, the fiddle went sour on a high note, and the Edison bulbs overhead dimmed a half-step as if the barn had taken a breath. People looked up because people always look up. The light over the tower was not white and was not quite gold. It hung. No rotor chop. No firework whistle. The only sound was the band failing to decide whether to keep playing, and then a phone hitting a salad plate two tables over, a bright ceramic crack that made three people flinch in their chairs.
0087		Nora did not flinch. She braced the second camera against her shoulder, zoomed until the tower’s faded logo filled the frame, and held. Forty seconds is long in a viewfinder. She counted it without meaning to. When the light ceased — not fading, not climbing, simply absent — the dusk rushed back in with the ordinary smell of gravel dust and someone’s cologne, and for a moment nobody in the barn made any noise at all.
0088		Six phones dropped. The best man, Curtis — big through the shoulders, sunburned to exactly the line his shirt collar drew, three drinks into a speech about a fishing trip nobody else remembered attending — stopped mid-sentence and did not start again for eleven seconds, which is a long time to stand at the front of a room holding a microphone and looking at nothing.
0089		Nora lowered the camera a degree and found him in the frame without hunting: Curtis at the mic, mouth open, eyes not tracking the room. A woman in a mauve wrap reached for his elbow and then thought better of it. Dominic, three feet to Curtis’s left, had already dropped his own phone. The screen hit the runner, lit, went dark. Dominic bent, picked it up in one clean motion, and slid it into his jacket as if he were tidying a place setting. He did not glance at the glass. He did not glance at the sky. He smiled at Mabs, who had both hands over her mouth and was starting to laugh because the alternative was not laughing.
0090		The alpaca took the gap between the gift table and the cousin from Fort Wayne as if it had rehearsed the aisle. A wine glass went. White wine ran along a crease in the tablecloth and dripped onto a pair of rented shoes. A child shouted that it was a llama. Steve — they would not have the name yet — stopped at Dominic’s chair, considered the groom with the polite distaste of a creature who had smelled something he did not work for, and then stood at Mabs’s shoulder instead, chewing nothing, smelling of wet hay and the cold coming off the fields. Nora got the whole approach on the B-camera. She got Dominic not looking at the animal. She got the aunt’s laugh starting high and catching on.
0091		Gary Huff, three tables back, did not laugh. He was somewhere past sixty, built like a man who still did his own gutters, with a voice that had spent forty years being ignored at town meetings and had thickened accordingly — a flat, carrying Midwestern baritone built for cutting through a room. He had a bunker under his garage, a shortwave radio he serviced more lovingly than his marriage had survived, and forty years of telling anyone who’d sit still long enough that a Tuesday was coming. He stood up, announced with real satisfaction that he had, in fact, called it, and began distributing laminated cards with instructions for a scenario that did not remotely match the one presently unfolding. Nobody took a card. He kept three for himself, on principle.
0092		He tried the head table first. Mabs took a card because she was that kind of hostess and set it under a salt shaker. Dominic thanked him without reading it. When Huff reached the barn door and pointed toward the tree line, where his own property began and where, he said, the bunker could take fourteen if they didn’t mind close quarters, the band started again on purpose. People sat. Huff stood in the doorway a minute longer with the night air at his back, listening to a sky that had gone back to being a sky, and then he walked home alone across the gravel with his remaining cards tapping his thigh.
0093		After the last toast Nora packed in the order she always packed: cards, batteries, the A-camera last because it had seen the thing and she did not want it jostling in the trunk like groceries. A groomsman asked if she’d gotten the alpaca. She said she had gotten everything. He wanted a selfie with the animal. Steve declined by simply not being where the groomsman was. On the county road a sheriff’s cruiser passed her going the other way, no lights, in no hurry, which was how Lissome preferred its mysteries.
0094		Nora did not file the water tower under funniest thing. She had it on one memory card and the alpaca on another, because she always shot redundant, and when she got home and watched both back at half speed, she noticed the thing nobody at the reception had been sober or curious enough to notice: her new brother-in-law, Dominic — broad-faced, easy-smiling, the kind of ordinary handsome that photographs well at every angle, which had made him a gift to shoot all day — had been one of the six people who dropped their phone, and was also the only one of the six who picked it back up without once checking whether the screen had cracked.
0096		What Nora could not have seen, because she was in the viewfinder, was the half-second before the drop. Something in Dominic — not a voice, not a picture, a pressure like a change in weather behind the eyes — had said now, and his hand had opened. Then the pressure went. He was at his wedding. Mabs was laughing. He picked up the phone because that is what a person does with a phone on the floor, and he did not check the glass because he was suddenly, unreasonably sure it would not be broken, and the sureness embarrassed him. He smiled at Mabs because smiling was the part he knew. For the rest of the night he kept waiting for the weather to come back. It did not. He told himself it had been the open bar he had not used.
0097		She told herself it was nothing, the way you tell yourself a lot of things at one in the morning with a wedding to edit and a groom’s family to please by Thursday. She kept the footage anyway. She had learned, in eleven years of filming other people’s most important day, that the camera generally saw things the guests were too busy having a nice time to notice, and that a wedding video’s whole job, in the end, was to remember what everyone else was too happy to write down.
0098		Before she left Lissome she walked as far as the fence around the water tower. The grass there was wet and ordinary. No scorch. No flattened ring. A beer can and a length of binder twine. She filmed the tank’s logo anyway, close enough to hear her own breath in the microphone, and a dog started barking two streets over as if she had come for it. On the drive back the highway was all tail lights and corn dark, and the B-camera sat on the passenger floor in a way that made her keep checking it was still there.
0100	Heading2	The Inside Desk
0101		On this office: you have not been assigned here as a punishment, whatever you have been told. You have been assigned here because someone, somewhere, believes you will be less trouble in a basement.
0102		— The New Starter’s Handbook, uncleared draft
0103		Two states away and four days later, Dana Osei — late thirties, close-cropped hair going prematurely silver at the temple in a way she’d stopped fighting, a launch engineer’s flat, precise way of finishing sentences as if reading them off a checklist — was still explaining, to anyone who asked and several people who hadn’t, that the confetti had been meant for the launch party, not the payload, and that an intern named Evan had reused a checklist that did not belong to that rocket. Four million people had watched. It trended for six days. On the seventh, a government attorney named Nowak — a soft, apologetic voice on the phone that Dana never once matched to a face — offered her a choice gentle enough that it took her a while to recognize it as an order: resign quietly and accept a “detail” to an inter-agency liaison desk with a name longer than its budget.
0104		The countdown had been clean through T-minus ninety. Dana liked the clean ones. She stood on the painted line and watched a hawk turn over the range as if it had not been briefed. When the fairing opened early the sound reached her as a soft, expensive sneeze. Confetti rose, hung, and came down on helmets and sagebrush and a lieutenant who later asked, sincerely, whether this was the payload. Dana said no. She said it twice. The livestream chat, which she made the mistake of opening in the van, had already named the event Confetti-1 and assigned it a fandom.
0105		In the days that followed she walked the Ibex lot like a person who no longer worked there, which was accurate. The intern, Evan, found her by the vending machines and tried to apologize with a sentence that kept acquiring clauses. She told him to keep the clause about the checklist and lose the rest. He nodded so hard his badge bounced. She never saw him again.
0106		Nowak’s call found her in a rental kitchen in Ogden that smelled of other people’s garlic. She stood at the sink and watched a drip form on the faucet while he said resign and detail and unclassified in the same mild register. She said yes because the alternative was a hearing and a name that would follow her into any room that still built things that flew.
0107		“What does it do?” Dana had asked.
0108		“Officially, it assesses unclassified aerial phenomena of a non-national-security nature.”
0109		“Unofficially?”
0110		“Unofficially it answers the phone so a bigger office two floors up doesn’t have to.”
0111		The National Sky Incident Desk occupied a basement shared with a regional passport office, a low-ceilinged room that smelled permanently of the passport office’s toner and somebody’s microwaved lunch, lit by fluorescent tubes that hummed a half-tone flat. Every visitor arrived already annoyed by a different line. Warren Iyer — sixty-something, silver-bearded, built like a man who had spent thirty years sitting very still in rooms other people were shouting in, with a voice that never rose above a library register even when the news did — had a desk ringed by four filing cabinets like a moat and did not speak to Dana on her first morning beyond handing her a case number, on the theory, which he eventually explained, that a person should have a case before they were allowed to have opinions. Priya Chen — late twenties, a halo of static-charged hair from three monitors’ worth of screen glow, a habit of talking faster the more excited she got until the words nearly outran the thought — ran those three monitors and a color-coded spreadsheet of sighting clusters she promised made sense and did not explain further. Gladys Okoro — somewhere past fifty, a warm, unhurried Lagos-inflected voice that had talked exactly as many people down from a bad diagnosis as up from a panic attack, cardigans regardless of the thermostat — worked the tip line, a cell phone in a drawer wearing a landline sticker for reasons of institutional dignity nobody would defend out loud, and answered every call the way you’d answer a relative who might be about to say something sad.
0112		Dana’s first morning was a security badge that still had someone else’s first name under the laminate, a folding chair that chirped when she sat, and a heating pipe that knocked twice a minute as if keeping time for a different building. The passport line upstairs leaked through the stairwell: a baby crying, a man arguing about a middle name, the wet-rubber smell of raincoats. Warren handed her a manila folder with a case number written twice, once in pencil. Priya did not look away from her screens. On the left monitor a map of the Midwest crawled with yellow dots. On the middle one a waveform shivered and would not settle. Gladys’s drawer-phone rang. Gladys answered it, listened, and wrote Venus in the margin without sighing.
0113		At 09:10 a man from the passport office came downstairs to demand the return of a folding table the Desk had been using as a side desk since before Dana’s badge existed. Warren did not stand. Priya did not mute her waveform. The man took one end of the table. Dana took the other. For ten undignified seconds the National Sky Incident Desk and a regional passport queue were in a tug-of-war over laminate. Warren said, at library volume, that the table was in the inventory as item 4. The man left without the table and with the look of someone who would be back with a form. Gladys put a bowl of mints on the recovered surface as if that closed the matter.
0114		Two floors up, in a room with real windows and an actual working coffee maker, sat the desk Dana’s calls were meant to escalate to when they mattered, run by a woman named Dr. Elena Marsh — tall, precise, a voice trained by a decade of congressional testimony to sound calm about anything — who Dana had once, in an earlier and better-funded life, been engaged to marry. Elena’s welcome memo on Dana’s first day was signed with a title and nothing warmer. Dana noticed, and hated that she noticed.
0115		Elena’s office faced a parking structure and pretended it faced the city. The glass was the kind that made sirens sound like they were happening in another building. A spider plant on the sill had been watered by someone who feared both drought and drowning. The coffee maker hissed on a credenza that still had a wedding-gift sheen; the pot smelled better than anything two floors down and slightly of the lemon tablets she used to descale it. Elena herself was taller than the room wanted her to be — dark blazer, silver at the temples she had not stopped fighting, a fountain pen she clicked when she was about to be kind and then usually wasn’t. When she stood, the chair sighed. When she sat, the whole floor seemed to remember it had a chain of command.
0116		Dana took the stairs two floors up at lunch because the elevator smelled of the passport office and because she wanted to see whether the coffee maker was real. It was. Elena was in a meeting behind glass, standing, one hand on a briefing binder, not looking at the stairwell. Dana did not knock. She poured coffee she did not want into a paper cup, burned her tongue, and went back down.
0117		The week’s calls ran the ordinary course — Venus, a drone, a Chinese lantern, a man who had definitely seen something and had also definitely had a few — until Friday afternoon, when Gladys took a call from a wedding videographer in Indiana who wanted to report, in a voice trying very hard to sound calmer than it was, a light that had landed on nothing, an alpaca that had arrived from nowhere, and a groomsman who had stopped talking for eleven seconds and never mentioned it again.
0118		What the Desk did not say out loud, because the Handbook forbade saying it where the passport line might hear, was that the unclassified pile was how the classified pile stayed clean. Two floors up, Elena’s shop took anything with a flag on it. The basement took the rest and was expected to make it stay the rest. Dana learned the split on Wednesday, when a call about lights over a reservoir came back stamped NOT FOR DESK in a hand she recognized as Elena’s. Warren put the stamp in a drawer already full of them. “We are the drain,” he said. “Drains do not get briefed on the pipes.”
0119		Thursday morning Buchanan came downstairs in person, which the basement treated as a weather event. She stood in the aisle between the cabinets and asked for Priya’s cluster map without saying please. Priya gave it to her. Buchanan looked at the Midwest for a long time and said the curve was not allowed to look like 1947 if they still intended to call this a liaison function. Warren asked what she wanted it called. Buchanan said she would let them know when Brussels had chosen a noun.
0120		Gladys wrote it all down, the way Gladys wrote everything down, in the neat printing she used for calls she intended to remember whether or not the form required it.
0122	Heading2	Content Moderation
0123		On evidence: the public will believe almost anything except the thing that is actually happening, which it will assume is a filter.
0124		— The New Starter’s Handbook, uncleared draft
0125		Nora posted eighty seconds of the water tower footage on Sunday night with a caption that tried to be responsible — if anyone else saw this over Lissome Saturday night, please reach out — and woke up Monday to eleven thousand comments, most of which fell into three camps: people convinced it was obviously AI because the light moved wrong for something real; people convinced it was real and had been sent, personally, to warn them about a specific unrelated apocalypse Nora had not been trying to discuss; and a smaller, more confident third camp who had watched the same eighty seconds and concluded, with total sincerity and several paragraphs of supporting argument, that it was ball lightning, Chinese surveillance, a drone light show gone wrong, swamp gas reflecting off a weather balloon, or, in one comment Nora returned to more than she’d admit, “obviously just Mercury, people, look it up,” a claim Mercury itself would have struggled to defend at nine o’clock at night.
0126		She posted it from the floor of her apartment in Indianapolis, laptop on a packing blanket, the wedding dress bag still hanging on the closet door because Mabs had asked her to drop it at the cleaner’s and Nora had not yet. The radiator knocked. A bus went by on the wet street and threw light across the ceiling. The comments arrived faster than the kettle boiled. She read them standing up, thumb going white on the phone, the apartment smelling of last night’s takeout garlic and the lemon oil she used on the tripods.
0127		By midnight the clip had been stitched into other people’s arguments. A man in a basement in Ohio used it to prove a weather weapon. A woman in Phoenix used it to prove God. A teenager with a ring light used it to prove a filter pack he was selling for six dollars. Nora wrote three replies and deleted four. She slept with the phone face down and still woke every time it buzzed against the wood.
0128		Monday she edited the actual wedding because that was the job. First dance. Cake. The aunt who had started the laugh. She left the tower shot off the cut she sent Mabs and kept it in a folder called MISC, which was a lie. MISC was the only honest folder she had.
0129		She drove the cut to a drugstore to print a still for Mabs — the first dance, nothing from the tower — and stood under fluorescent tubes that made everyone in line look like a suspect. The clerk asked if it was her wedding. Nora said no, and then, because the clerk had been kind, that it was her sister’s. The clerk said the groom had a nice face. Nora agreed, paid, and sat in the car with the envelope in her lap until the windows fogged.
0130		Nobody asked about the eleven seconds of silence. Nobody asked about the alpaca’s timing. 0403	Heading1	APPENDIX: THE SCIENCE THIS BOOK DIDN’T MAKE UP
0404		The New Starter’s Handbook, quoted throughout this book, does not exist and cites nothing. This appendix is the opposite kind of document. The papers and the counts in it are real, the links go to those sources, and none of it is required reading. Where the novel exceeds the citation, the paragraph says so. The bibliography at the back lists the same works in one place.
0405		The Generation Ship
0406		A ship that travels for forty thousand years rather than build something faster than light is not a fantasy so much as a concession: nothing in known physics outruns light, which leaves slow and enormous as the only honest option for crossing interstellar distance with a living crew. The concept has a name, a generation ship, and a real engineering literature, including Hein, Pak, Pütz, Bühler, and Reiss, “World Ships: Feasibility and Rationale,” Journal of the British Interplanetary Society (2012). Kurzgesagt’s YouTube film Generation Ships — The Hardest Part Is Not Distance treats the ship as a closed-world survival problem rather than a set piece. It is worth remembering, reading this book’s forty-thousand-year vessel held together by duct tape and committee, that humanity’s actual attempt at a small sealed biosphere, Biosphere 2, built in Arizona in 1991, could not keep eight people alive inside a fully sealed system for even two years before oxygen levels and crop yields collapsed. This book’s ship is not that. It is the version where the engineering problem gets solved, imperfectly, by people who had two thousand generations and no alternative. The solving is the fiction. The difficulty is not.
0407		The Genetics Problem
0408		The ship’s need for outside genetic material is not a decoration. A sealed population loses genetic diversity every generation. Conservation biology calls the floor under that loss a minimum viable population, and the numbers argued for a wild species over evolutionary time are often in the thousands. Marin and Beluffi’s 2018 simulation is more generous to the engineers than that folklore: under strict breeding rules, a founding crew of 98 can hold for a 6,300-year trip. Forty thousand years is not 6,300, and this book’s ship did not have those rules. That is the shopping trip. The story’s request, at the end, is for consented passage and consented samples, because taking infants without asking does not repair a crew, and children born on Earth to hybrid parents do not fly home by themselves.
0409		The Patient Watcher
0410		A dormant, waiting probe is an old idea in serious astronomy, not a storybook invention. Ronald Bracewell proposed in 1960 that a patient civilization might send automated observers ahead of itself to watch a young species rather than announce itself outright — the concept still carries his name, the Bracewell probe — and modern SETI researchers have taken the idea seriously enough to actually go looking for one, including a 2019 search of the gravitationally stable points near Earth’s own orbit, written up as “Looking for Lurkers”. Nobody has found one. That is, as this book’s Directorate would be the first to point out, exactly what you would also expect if there were nothing there.
0411		Getting Caught
0412		The idea that 1947-era radar was loud enough to be noticed is not this book’s invention either. Earth has been leaking electromagnetic evidence of itself since the first powerful transmitters went up, and the classic paper on the subject — Sullivan, Brown, and Wetherill’s “Eavesdropping: The Radio Signature of the Earth”, Science, 1978 — first worked out how far our own military and civilian radar could plausibly be detected. Later analysis of the same leakage has been given the (much better) name technosignatures, and puts our more powerful broadcasts as detectable across a genuinely startling amount of the galaxy. This book borrows the year. The physics was already sitting there.
0413		Better Eyes
0414		Kade’s worry that Halo’s satellites might accidentally spot the ship is not hand-waved either. The real Vera C. Rubin Observatory is expected, over its ten-year survey, to catalog over a hundred thousand Jupiter Trojans alone — more than seven times the number currently known — which is to say that the part of the sky this book’s ship is hiding in is, this decade, for the first time in human history, actually about to be looked at properly. The hull in this book is a few kilometers across and dark. A small moon in that swarm would already have been famous. Rubin’s threat is to the small and the dark, which is the only size of secret the Trojan cloud can still keep.
0415		The Implant
0416		A mote on the scale of a grain of rice is not pure invention. Seo, Neely, Shen, and colleagues showed, in Neuron in 2016, that ultrasonic neural dust can record from a nerve at that size. What this book adds, and does not pretend has been demonstrated, is the second hop. The mote talks to a tender or a ground relay close enough to hear it. It does not stream a life to Jupiter. The constant signal in the novel is a dog tag: alive, located, unharmed. The occasional signal is a visit, at a range of hours missing from a babysitter’s afternoon, not a radio conversation with the Trojan swarm. A coin-sized commercial brain implant that talks to hardware worn on the body is a different machine, and it is not the one in this story.
0417		The Company That Owns the Sky
0418		Halo Systems is not much of an exaggeration. A real satellite internet constellation, Starlink, had, as of September 2026, more than eleven thousand working satellites in orbit, with public filings and stated ambitions running well past that. The idea of a company quietly running capable eyes on the solar system out of the same budget line as a consumer internet product is, on the public record of that constellation’s scale, not speculative. It is Tuesday.
0419		Further Viewing
0420		For readers who want the Fermi Paradox itself — the much older, much bigger question of why a galaxy old enough to be full of neighbors looks so empty — Kurzgesagt’s two-part explainer remains the clearest short version available: Part One and Part Two. This book’s answer — a species that solved the paradox by leaving early and being extremely patient — is one of dozens the video covers, and arguably not even the most likely one.
