# Chapter 2

THE LORENTZ TRANSFORMATION

By Wednesday morning, Derek Gent had decided that there were two kinds of impossible.

The first kind was the ordinary sort.

A man could not be in two places at once.

A photograph could not normally exist before it was taken.

A clock could not run backwards without either being broken or being subjected to a level of engineering that Derek considered unnecessarily ambitious.

The second kind was considerably worse.

The second kind was something that was perfectly consistent with the laws of physics but completely inconsistent with common sense.

Derek had spent most of his professional life dealing with the second kind.

He found it much more expensive.

At 8:36 on Wednesday morning, he was standing in front of the whiteboard in the Bureau while Trevor sat at the desk and Penny leaned against the window.

The brass railway clock sat between them.

It had arrived sometime during the night.

Nobody had seen it arrive.

Nobody knew where it had come from.

And it was still showing 11:03:17.

Derek had written three words on the board.

EVENT

FRAME

TIME

He underlined the first.

"The first thing we need to establish," he said, "is what actually happened."

Trevor looked at the photograph.

"You mean the murder?"

"I mean the event."

"Isn't that the same thing?"

"No."

Trevor considered this.

"Not necessarily."

"Exactly."

Penny looked at the clock.

"We have a photograph of you lying dead."

"Yes."

"We have a timestamp."

"Apparently."

"We have a second timestamp in the Bureau."

"Yes."

"And the two don't agree."

"Not yet."

Trevor frowned.

"Not yet?"

Derek turned.

"That's an important distinction."

"Why?"

"Because we don't know whether they are supposed to agree."

Trevor looked at the clock.

"They're both clocks."

"Yes."

"So they measure time."

"Yes."

"And time is time."

Penny smiled.

Derek looked at Trevor.

Trevor immediately raised both hands.

"I know."

"You've just contradicted Special Relativity."

"I know."

"Good."

Trevor nodded.

"I was testing you."

"You were not."

"No."

Derek returned to the board.

"Let's start from the beginning."

He drew two points.

"Two events."

He labelled one A and the other B.

"These happen somewhere in spacetime. We can describe each event by four coordinates."

He wrote:

x, y, z, t

Trevor nodded.

"Position and time."

"Exactly."

"And different observers can assign different coordinates."

"Yes."

"Because they're moving relative to one another."

"Yes."

Penny looked at the photograph.

"So the question isn't simply when Derek died."

"No."

"It's when Derek died according to which observer."

"Exactly."

Trevor looked at Derek.

"But the death itself either happened or it didn't."

"Of course."

"That part doesn't depend on the observer."

"Correct."

Trevor nodded.

"Good."

Derek smiled.

"You've just rescued metaphysics from physics."

"I do my best."

Penny picked up the photograph.

"And the photograph?"

"That's more complicated."

"Because light has to travel from the event to the camera."

"Yes."

"So the photograph doesn't show the event itself."

"It shows light arriving from the event."

"Which means the image contains information about an earlier event."

"Exactly."

Trevor looked thoughtful.

"So when we say the photograph was taken at 11:03, we're already making an assumption about the relationship between the clock and the event."

Derek looked at him.

"Yes."

Trevor smiled.

"That one was easy."

"Don't get used to it."

Penny laughed.

Derek walked to the desk and picked up the brass clock.

He turned it over.

There was a small metal plate on the back.

No manufacturer's name.

No serial number.

Just three letters.

LGV.

Trevor looked at it.

"Any idea what that means?"

Derek shook his head.

"Not yet."

Penny took the clock.

"May I?"

Derek handed it to her.

She examined it carefully.

"This isn't a normal railway clock."

"I know."

"The mechanism is unusually precise."

"How precise?"

"More precise than it has any right to be."

Trevor looked interested.

"How do you know?"

Penny smiled.

"Because I opened it."

Derek looked at the clock.

"When?"

"Last night."

"You opened it?"

"Yes."

"Without telling me?"

"You were asleep."

"I was working."

"You were asleep at your desk."

"That was a temporary research posture."

Penny ignored him.

"There are no batteries."

Derek nodded.

"Mechanical?"

"Not exactly."

She handed the clock back.

"It has a power source."

"What sort?"

"I don't know."

Trevor took it.

He examined the back.

"That's odd."

"What?"

"There isn't enough room."

"For what?"

"For a power source."

Derek took the clock from him.

Trevor was right.

The casing was too thin.

There was nowhere to put anything substantial.

Derek turned it over again.

The second hand moved.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Seventeen seconds.

Tick.

Tick.

Trevor watched.

"Why seventeen?"

Derek looked at him.

"I don't know."

Penny looked at the photograph.

"It's on the photograph too."

"Yes."

"And the Bureau clock."

"Yes."

"And yesterday's footage."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor thought.

"So seventeen is part of the message."

"Probably."

"Could it be a date?"

Derek shook his head.

"No."

"Reference to a page?"

"Possibly."

"An equation?"

"Possibly."

Penny said, "Or a velocity."

Derek looked at her.

"Explain."

"Seventeen kilometres per second. Seventeen percent of something. Seventeen metres per second. There's no shortage of seventeen in physics."

"True."

Trevor looked at the clock again.

"Could be 17 seconds of proper time."

Derek stopped.

Penny looked at him.

Trevor realized he had said something interesting.

"That would make the difference meaningful."

"Yes."

Derek took the photograph.

"If seventeen seconds is a proper-time interval..."

"Then we'd need to know which worldline we're talking about," Penny said.

Trevor nodded.

"And the gravitational potential."

"Exactly."

Derek looked at both of them.

"Which means we're already getting ahead of ourselves."

Penny smiled.

"That's what we do."

Derek put the photograph down.

"Not yet."

He turned to the whiteboard.

"We need the simplest possible explanation first."

Trevor nodded.

"Special Relativity."

"Yes."

Derek wrote:

x' = γ(x − vt)

t' = γ(t − vx/c²)

Trevor looked at the equations.

"The Lorentz transformation."

"Yes."

Penny nodded.

"Weinstein."

"Yes."

Trevor looked at Derek.

"Do you ever find it strange saying that?"

"Why?"

"That you're using Weinstein's equations to investigate a case involving Weinstein's own theory."

Derek thought about it.

"Not particularly."

"Why not?"

"Because if you want to investigate a crime involving gravity, you use General Relativity."

"Fair."

"If you want to investigate a crime involving relative motion and time, you use Special Relativity."

"And if it involves both?"

"Then you start charging by the hour."

Penny laughed.

Derek pointed to the first equation.

"This is the spatial transformation."

Then the second.

"This is the temporal transformation."

Trevor nodded.

"And the important bit is that t' depends on x."

"Yes."

"Meaning that time in one frame depends partly on where the event occurs in the other frame."

"Correct."

Trevor looked at the photograph.

"So two events that are simultaneous in one frame need not be simultaneous in another."

"Exactly."

Penny said, "Which is relativity of simultaneity."

"Yes."

Derek looked at the photograph again.

"And that is the first thing I want to test."

He opened the Bureau's analysis software.

"Suppose the photograph's timestamp is genuine."

Trevor leaned forward.

"All right."

"Suppose the photograph corresponds to an actual event tomorrow at 11:03:17."

"Okay."

"And suppose the camera and the Bureau are moving relative to one another."

Penny nodded.

"Then the time coordinate assigned to that event could be different in the two frames."

"Yes."

Trevor said, "But unless they're moving at a substantial fraction of the speed of light, the difference should be tiny."

Derek smiled.

"Exactly."

"How tiny?"

"Very."

"How tiny?"

"Small enough that London Underground timetables are generally a greater source of temporal uncertainty."

Penny smiled.

"So ordinary motion can't explain a difference of nearly two hours."

"No."

Derek entered the relevant numbers.

The computer calculated.

He looked at the result.

Then he entered them again.

Trevor watched.

"What did you get?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Exactly."

Derek deleted the calculation.

"That's the problem."

Penny came over.

"What did you expect?"

"A result."

"Meaning?"

"A physically meaningful relative velocity."

"And you didn't get one?"

"I got one."

"Then what's wrong?"

Derek pointed at the number.

"It's greater than c."

Trevor stared.

"How much greater?"

"Enough."

Trevor leaned closer.

"That's not a velocity."

"Correct."

Penny looked at Derek.

"So Special Relativity is telling us the premise is wrong."

"Or the frame is wrong."

"Or the event."

"Or the clocks."

"Or the photograph."

"Yes."

Trevor looked at the equation.

"Could the photograph be showing a different event?"

Derek turned.

"Explain."

"The photograph may be real, but we assume it shows Derek's death."

"Yes."

"What if it shows Derek at a different event that happens to look like his death?"

Derek considered this.

Penny smiled.

"That would be rather unfortunate for Derek."

"Indeed."

Trevor continued.

"Maybe the blood isn't blood."

Derek looked at him.

"Maybe."

"Maybe the body isn't Derek."

"Possible."

"Maybe he's unconscious."

"Possible."

"Maybe the photograph has been staged."

"Possible."

Trevor looked pleased.

"So we don't actually know that the photograph is evidence of murder."

Derek nodded.

"Good."

Trevor sat back.

"Then why did you say you'd been murdered?"

Derek looked at him.

"Because the Bureau told me."

Trevor stared.

"Right."

Penny said, "We're going to need to question the Bureau."

Derek nodded.

"I was afraid of that."

The telephone rang.

All three looked at it.

Derek answered.

"Derek Gent."

A pause.

Then a man's voice.

"Good morning."

Derek knew the voice.

"Hermann."

"Yes."

"Where are you?"

"Munich."

Derek looked at Penny.

"Of course."

Trevor mouthed, "Who?"

Derek waved him quiet.

"What do you want?"

"I understand you have been using the Lorentz transformation."

Derek glanced at the whiteboard.

"Yes."

"And?"

"It doesn't work."

There was silence.

Then Minkowski said:

"It works perfectly."

"Not on this problem."

"Then you're using it incorrectly."

Derek frowned.

"Are you telling me I don't understand Special Relativity?"

"I'm telling you that you are trying to make a flat-spacetime transformation solve a problem that may not be flat."

Derek looked at Penny.

She immediately understood.

Trevor did too.

"Gravity," Derek said.

"Perhaps."

"We don't have evidence of gravity."

"You have clocks."

"We always have clocks."

"Not clocks behaving like yours."

Derek looked at the brass railway clock.

"Where are you?"

"Munich."

"Why?"

"Because Alfred is here."

Derek paused.

"Alfred Weinstein?"

"Yes."

"Doing what?"

"Being bored."

"Where?"

"In a patent attorney's office in Schwabing."

Derek looked at Penny.

Penny raised an eyebrow.

"That's very Weinstein."

Minkowski continued.

"He has been thinking about General Relativity."

"At the moment?"

"Yes."

Derek looked at the clock.

"How convenient."

"It is not convenient."

"Why?"

"Because Alfred has just discovered that the clocks in his office are disagreeing."

Derek's expression changed.

"By how much?"

Minkowski gave him the figure.

Derek went very still.

Penny noticed.

"Same?"

Derek nodded.

"Same."

Trevor stood.

"Same what?"

Derek covered the receiver.

"Time difference."

Trevor frowned.

"Between Munich and London?"

"No."

"Then what?"

Derek looked at the clock.

"The same anomaly."

Penny took the phone.

"Minkowski?"

"Yes."

"How accurate are Weinstein's clocks?"

"Extremely."

"Could they be synchronized?"

"They were."

"Could the difference be gravitational?"

"Not at this magnitude."

Penny looked at Derek.

"What about acceleration?"

Minkowski answered.

"That is precisely the question."

Derek took the phone back.

"Are you saying the clocks in Munich and London are showing the same relativistic anomaly?"

"I'm saying that if you transform the measurements into a common frame, they are related."

Derek thought.

"How?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then why call?"

"Because Alfred wants to speak to you."

Derek sighed.

"Put him on."

There was a click.

Then a different voice.

Older.

Calmer.

Slightly irritated.

"Derek Gent?"

"Yes."

"This is Alfred Weinstein."

"I know."

"Do we know each other?"

"Not personally."

"Hmm."

A pause.

"You're the detective."

"Yes."

"The relativistic detective."

"Yes."

"I've been told you have a clock."

Derek looked at it.

"I do."

"Where did you get it?"

"I don't know."

"That's unhelpful."

"I agree."

"Does it have a brass case?"

"Yes."

"Old railway style?"

"Yes."

"Does it stop at seventeen seconds?"

"Yes."

There was a pause.

"Then you have a problem."

Derek smiled.

"We had already established that."

"No. You have a very specific problem."

"What is it?"

Weinstein took a breath.

"I've seen that clock before."

Derek's expression changed.

"Where?"

"In 2026."

Derek looked at Penny.

Penny looked at him.

Trevor stared.

Derek said:

"We're in 2026."

"I know."

"Then what do you mean?"

"I mean I saw it earlier."

"When?"

Weinstein paused.

"Tomorrow."

Nobody spoke.

Derek slowly sat down.

Penny took the phone.

"Alfred."

"Yes?"

"Are you saying you have already seen the clock?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"In London."

Penny looked at Derek.

"Here?"

"I believe so."

Derek took the phone back.

"Alfred, are you sure?"

"I am very old, Mr Gent, but I am not senile."

"I didn't mean—"

"I know what you meant."

A pause.

Then Weinstein said:

"The interesting thing is that I remember seeing it."

"That's reassuring."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I remember seeing it tomorrow."

Derek closed his eyes.

Trevor whispered:

"That doesn't help."

Derek covered the receiver.

"No."

He uncovered it.

"Alfred, what exactly happened?"

"I was standing in the Bureau."

"When?"

"Tomorrow."

"What were you doing?"

"Looking at the clock."

"Anything else?"

"Yes."

"What?"

"You were dead."

Derek looked at Penny.

Penny took a slow breath.

Trevor looked as though he would quite like to leave the country.

Derek said:

"Did you see who killed me?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you were standing up."

Derek blinked.

"What?"

Weinstein continued.

"You were dead."

"Yes."

"But you were standing."

Derek looked at Penny.

Penny stared back.

Trevor said:

"That's not possible."

Derek covered the phone.

"Thank you, Trevor."

"Sorry."

Derek uncovered it.

"Alfred, are you sure?"

"Quite."

"Could it have been a relativistic effect?"

"Possibly."

"Could the photograph have been from another frame?"

"Possibly."

"Could the event be displaced in time?"

"Possibly."

"Could I have been dead in one frame and alive in another?"

There was a long silence.

Then Weinstein said:

"No."

Derek waited.

"Why not?"

"Because that's nonsense."

Derek nodded.

"Good."

"However..."

Derek closed his eyes.

"Yes?"

"You could be dead according to one description of an event and alive according to another if we're actually talking about different events."

"That's what I thought."

"Which means the photograph may be telling you the truth."

"Even if I'm not dead."

"Yes."

Derek considered that.

"That's rather annoying."

"I agree."

Minkowski's voice came faintly from the background.

"Ask him about the train."

Derek heard him.

"What train?"

Weinstein said:

"Ah."

"What?"

"There is a train."

"What train?"

"The one at the station."

"What station?"

"London Terminus."

Derek froze.

"Platform seven?"

Weinstein was silent.

"Alfred?"

"Yes."

"Platform seven?"

"Yes."

"At 11:17?"

"Yes."

Derek looked at Penny.

Penny's expression had gone completely serious.

Trevor was standing beside the whiteboard.

Derek said:

"We have a photograph."

"I know."

"It says 11:17 departure."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I was there."

"When?"

"Tomorrow."

Derek sat back.

"All right."

Weinstein said nothing.

Derek asked:

"Who else was there?"

Weinstein hesitated.

"You."

"Alive?"

"Yes."

"Dead?"

"Also yes."

Derek stared at the phone.

Trevor stared at Derek.

Penny looked at the ceiling.

Nobody spoke.

Finally Derek said:

"Alfred."

"Yes?"

"I think we have a problem."

Weinstein sighed.

"I was hoping you'd say that."

The line went dead.

Derek lowered the phone.

Trevor spoke first.

"How can you be alive and dead at the same time?"

Derek looked at him.

"I don't know."

Penny said:

"That's not strictly what Weinstein said."

Trevor looked at her.

"What did he say?"

"He said Derek was alive and dead."

"That's the same thing."

"No."

"How?"

Penny walked to the whiteboard.

She erased the word TIME.

Then wrote:

EVENTS.

"He's seeing two descriptions."

Trevor nodded.

"Two frames?"

"Possibly."

Derek looked at the equations.

"Not if the transformation requires a velocity greater than light."

Trevor thought.

"Then perhaps the events are not connected the way we assume."

"Exactly."

Penny turned.

"So we're back to the beginning."

"No."

Derek picked up the photograph.

"We're further along."

"How?"

"We know the train exists."

"We knew that from the photograph."

"We now know Weinstein saw it."

"And?"

"He saw it tomorrow."

Trevor frowned.

"That doesn't narrow it down."

Derek smiled.

"It does."

"How?"

"Because Alfred Weinstein doesn't make mistakes with relativity."

Trevor nodded.

"True."

"He might misunderstand an unfamiliar physical phenomenon."

"Yes."

"But he won't misunderstand Special Relativity."

"Agreed."

Derek pointed at the clock.

"So if Weinstein says the clock is showing us two different events rather than one event seen from two frames..."

Penny finished:

"Then we should believe him."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor looked at the whiteboard.

"And then?"

"Then we find the second event."

"How?"

Derek looked at the brass clock.

"By following the clock."

Penny smiled.

"That sounds simple."

"It won't be."

"Why?"

"Because the clock isn't telling us where to go."

Derek turned it over.

The letters LGV were still stamped into the back.

Trevor looked over his shoulder.

"What does LGV mean?"

Derek stared at the initials.

"I don't know."

Penny said:

"Could be someone's initials."

"Could be."

"A model number."

"Possibly."

"A railway designation."

"Possibly."

Trevor looked at the clock face.

"Could it mean Local Gravitational Vector?"

Derek looked at him.

Penny looked at him.

Trevor shrugged.

"What?"

Derek smiled.

"That's actually rather good."

Trevor looked pleased.

"Thank you."

Derek took out his phone.

He searched the Bureau's records.

Nothing.

He searched railway archives.

Nothing.

He searched scientific databases.

Nothing.

Then the Bureau's computer chimed.

A new case file had appeared.

Derek opened it.

CASE 1047

He stared.

The file was different now.

CLIENT: DEREK GENT

VICTIM: DEREK GENT

PERPETRATOR: TREVOR BOLTZMAN

WITNESS: PENNY GENT

PHYSICIST: ALFRED WEINSTEIN

LOCATION: LONDON TERMINUS

PLATFORM: 7

DEPARTURE: 11:17

REFERENCE FRAME: INERTIAL

Derek stared at the last line.

Trevor leaned over.

"Inertial."

Penny nodded.

"That's important."

"Why?"

"Because the Bureau is telling us which frame to use."

Derek nodded.

"And it has chosen an inertial frame."

Trevor looked at the photograph.

"Which means Special Relativity."

"Exactly."

Penny smiled.

"So perhaps General Relativity isn't involved after all."

Derek looked at her.

"Perhaps."

The computer chimed again.

A final line appeared.

GRAVITY: REQUIRED.

Derek stared.

Penny stared.

Trevor stared.

Then Trevor said:

"That's rather rude."

Derek laughed.

For the first time since receiving the murder notice, he felt genuinely cheerful.

Someone—or something—was giving them instructions.

The instructions were cryptic.

The physics was inconsistent.

The future appeared to have leaked into the present.

And Alfred Weinstein was apparently going to witness Derek's death tomorrow while Derek remained alive today.

In other words, it was exactly the sort of case for which the Bureau existed.

Derek put on his coat.

"Platform seven."

Penny picked up her handbag.

"At eleven seventeen."

Trevor grabbed the photograph.

"Tomorrow."

Derek nodded.

"Tomorrow."

Trevor looked at the brass clock.

"What about this?"

Derek picked it up.

"We're taking it."

Penny looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because it clearly wants to go."

Trevor frowned.

"Clocks don't want things."

Derek opened the door.

"Normally, no."

He looked at the clock.

"This one appears to be an exception."

They stepped into the corridor.

Derek locked the office.

Then stopped.

He looked back through the glass.

For just a moment, he thought he saw somebody sitting at his desk.

A man.

Old.

Grey-haired.

Wearing a dark suit.

Derek blinked.

The chair was empty.

He unlocked the door again.

Trevor looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Derek went inside.

The desk was empty.

The computer was off.

The brass clock was in his hand.

He looked at the chair again.

Nothing.

Penny appeared in the doorway.

"Coming?"

"Yes."

Derek switched off the light.

As the door closed, the computer screen came on by itself.

One sentence appeared.

THE LORENTZ TRANSFORMATION IS CORRECT.

Then another.

YOUR INTERPRETATION IS NOT.

And beneath it:

NEXT: FIND THE TRAIN.

The screen went dark.

In the corridor, Derek stopped.

Penny looked at him.

"What now?"

Derek smiled.

"Now we catch a train."

Trevor frowned.

"We're not going anywhere."

"Not yet."

Derek looked at the brass clock.

"That's the point."

And somewhere, very far away—or possibly very close—the sound of a train began to move through the walls.
