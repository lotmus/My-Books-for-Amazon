# Chapter 15

## Sherlock and the Final Geometry

Weinstein was having a perfectly ordinary morning.

This meant, naturally, that the universe was about to become ridiculous.

He was sitting in his office in Munich, staring at a sheet of paper on which he had written the same equation three times.

He had crossed it out three times.

The fourth version looked promising.

The fifth looked worse.

The sixth looked suspiciously like the first.

Weinstein sighed.

General Relativity had reached that stage of the investigation where every answer appeared to generate two better questions, three worse equations and an overwhelming desire for tea.

He leaned back.

The problem was not that General Relativity didn't work.

It worked remarkably well.

Too well, in fact.

It described gravity as geometry.

Matter and energy determined the curvature of spacetime.

Free particles followed geodesics.

Clocks measured proper time along worldlines.

Special Relativity emerged locally.

Everything fitted.

And yet Weinstein had the persistent feeling that he was looking at the description rather than the thing being described.

He wrote:

**WHAT IS SPACETIME ACTUALLY MADE OF?**

Then underneath:

**AND WHY DOES IT HAVE TO BE FOUR-DIMENSIONAL?**

He stared at the words.

Then came a noise.

Not a knock.

Not a telephone.

Not the sound of someone opening his office door.

It was a voice.

Inside his head.

"Good question."

Weinstein froze.

He looked around.

Nobody.

The office door was closed.

Therefore somebody was definitely in there.

Obviously.

Weinstein stood.

"Who said that?"

Silence.

Then:

"I did."

Weinstein stared at the wall.

"Who are you?"

A pause.

"Sherlock."

Weinstein frowned.

"Sherlock who?"

Another pause.

"Sherlock."

"That's not a surname."

"No."

"Are you a person?"

"No."

"A computer?"

"No."

"An artificial intelligence?"

"No."

"Then what are you?"

The answer arrived calmly.

"A dolphin."

Weinstein sat down.

"Of course."

There was another pause.

"You don't believe me."

"I am receiving a telepathic communication from a dolphin called Sherlock."

"Yes."

"In Munich."

"Yes."

"Inside my head."

"Yes."

Weinstein considered this.

"Well, that's settled."

Sherlock seemed pleased.

"Good."

Weinstein looked at the clock.

11:17.

He looked again.

Still 11:17.

"Why are you contacting me?"

"Because you are almost finished."

"Finished with what?"

"General Relativity."

Weinstein sat upright.

"You have a final theory?"

"No."

"Then what do you have?"

"The final geometry."

Weinstein stared.

"Go on."

Sherlock began.

And somehow Weinstein understood.

Not as words.

Not exactly.

The communication was more like a sequence of concepts arriving simultaneously.

Geometry.

Curvature.

Matter.

Energy.

Worldlines.

Proper time.

Geodesics.

The Einstein field equations.

Then something deeper.

Weinstein's hand began moving across the paper.

He wrote:

But before he could explain the equation, Weinstein stopped.

There was a problem.

The symbols were perfectly familiar to him, but they were not yet particularly friendly to everyone else.

Especially Trevor.

And if the book was going to explain General Relativity to people who had successfully survived high-school physics, it seemed unfair to throw a Greek alphabet at them and then run away.

Weinstein picked up the pen again.

"Vectors first."

Sherlock approved.

A vector, Weinstein explained, is a quantity that has both magnitude and direction.

Velocity is a good example.

If a car is travelling at 30 kilometres per hour east, that is not merely a number. It tells you how fast and in which direction.

You can represent a vector by components, such as:

**v = (vx, vy, vz)**

Change your coordinate system and the components change.

The physical vector does not.

Weinstein paused.

"A tensor is, roughly speaking, the next level of this idea."

Trevor looked interested.

"Next level?"

"Yes."

"A more complicated vector?"

"That's a useful way to begin thinking about it."

"Not actually the definition?"

"No."

"Good."

Weinstein nodded.

"A tensor can describe relationships involving several directions at once. Scalars have no directional components. Vectors have one directional character. Tensors generalise this idea and, crucially, have well-defined transformation rules when we change coordinates."

Trevor nodded.

"So the components can change while the physical object doesn't."

"Exactly."

Penny smiled.

"That's going to be important."

"Very."

Weinstein turned to the board.

"General Relativity needs tensors because spacetime can be curved and coordinates can be extremely inconvenient."

He wrote:

**METRIC TENSOR → gμν**

"The metric tensor tells us how spacetime is measured."

He wrote:

**ds² = gμν dxμ dxν**

Trevor looked at it.

"That looks unpleasant."

"It is mostly bookkeeping."

"Ah."

"In flat spacetime, the interval can be written in the familiar form involving time and the three spatial directions. In curved spacetime, the metric can vary from place to place."

Penny nodded.

"So the metric contains the geometry."

"Exactly."

"Distances."

"Yes."

"Times."

"Yes."

"Angles."

"Yes."

"Causal structure."

"Yes."

Weinstein smiled.

"The metric is rather like the instruction manual for measuring spacetime."

Trevor nodded.

"That's better."

Sherlock sent another burst of information.

Weinstein wrote:

**Tμν — STRESS-ENERGY TENSOR**

"This one describes matter and energy."

Trevor frowned.

"Everything?"

"Energy density, momentum density, stresses and pressures, and their flow."

"So it's a sort of inventory?"

"An extremely sophisticated inventory."

"Right."

"Then we have the curvature tensors."

He wrote:

**Riemann curvature tensor**

Then:

**Ricci tensor**

Then:

**Einstein tensor — Gμν**

Trevor stared at the collection.

"Why so many?"

"Because spacetime is complicated."

Penny nodded.

"And physicists enjoy making things look worse than they are."

"That too."

Weinstein pointed to the Einstein tensor.

"Fortunately, Einstein packaged the relevant curvature information into a tensor that fits beautifully into the field equation."

Trevor smiled.

"So now we can finally understand the equation."

"Exactly."

Weinstein returned to the top of the page.

He wrote:

**Gμν + Λgμν = 8πG/c⁴ Tμν**

He stared at it.

Sherlock continued.

The left-hand side described geometry.

The right-hand side described matter and energy.

But the equation did something more profound than simply relating gravity to matter.

It said that the geometry of spacetime itself was dynamical.

Weinstein nodded.

"Yes."

Sherlock continued.

Spacetime was not a passive stage.

It was part of the physical system.

Matter told spacetime how to curve.

Curved spacetime told matter how to move.

Weinstein smiled.

"That's already General Relativity."

"Yes."

"Then what's missing?"

Sherlock sent him an image.

It was not an image in the ordinary sense.

It was a geometry.

A four-dimensional manifold.

Curved.

Smooth.

Locally Minkowskian.

But globally complicated.

Weinstein understood.

"Local flatness."

"Yes."

"Every sufficiently small freely falling region looks like Special Relativity."

"Yes."

"So SR isn't replaced by GR."

"No."

"It is contained within it."

"Yes."

Weinstein smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Sherlock sent another thought.

Not all observers agreed on coordinates.

But all physical observers agreed on invariant quantities.

The interval.

Proper time.

Causal structure.

Curvature invariants.

Weinstein nodded.

"Exactly."

Then Sherlock showed him something else.

A geodesic.

It approached a region of extreme curvature.

Weinstein watched.

The geodesic could not be extended.

He frowned.

"A singularity."

"Not exactly."

Weinstein looked again.

The geometry had become incomplete.

"The classical description breaks down."

"Yes."

"That's the important point."

"Yes."

Weinstein stood.

He began pacing.

"So a singularity isn't necessarily a place."

"No."

"Nor necessarily a physical object."

"No."

"It is a failure of the classical spacetime description."

"Yes."

Weinstein stopped.

"Then the final geometry must tell us where that description fails."

Sherlock answered:

"Or why."

Weinstein looked at the equation.

The answer was beginning to emerge.

General Relativity described spacetime geometrically.

But if spacetime itself was emergent, then the Einstein field equations might be a macroscopic limit of something deeper.

Weinstein whispered:

"Like thermodynamics."

Sherlock sent approval.

Macroscopic quantities.

Microscopic degrees of freedom.

Emergent behaviour.

Weinstein wrote:

**GR : SPACETIME :: THERMODYNAMICS : MATTER**

He stared.

"Then curvature could be emergent."

"Yes."

"Gravity could be emergent."

"Yes."

"Time could be emergent."

"Yes."

"And the fundamental theory wouldn't necessarily contain spacetime at all."

"Correct."

Weinstein sat down.

He was suddenly very still.

"Then what does it contain?"

Sherlock answered with a single concept.

**RELATIONS.**

Weinstein stared at the word.

Relations.

Not objects sitting in space.

Not events occurring in time.

Relations from which the apparent structure of space and time could emerge.

He thought about the holographic principle.

He thought about entanglement.

He thought about information.

He thought about the Underground.

The clocks.

The rain.

The train.

The other Derek.

The cucumber.

Everything was suddenly connected.

Not because everything occupied the same place.

Because everything participated in relationships.

He wrote:

**GEOMETRY IS RELATIONAL.**

Then:

**TIME IS RELATIONAL.**

Then:

**GRAVITY IS GEOMETRY.**

Then:

**GEOMETRY MAY BE INFORMATION.**

Sherlock sent another thought.

Weinstein understood.

The final theory would not abolish General Relativity.

It would explain why General Relativity works.

Weinstein stared at the page.

"That..."

He stopped.

Sherlock waited.

"...would be the final GR."

"Yes."

Weinstein smiled.

"Not the replacement."

"No."

"The completion."

"Yes."

Weinstein leaned back.

For several seconds he said nothing.

Then:

"You're a very clever dolphin."

Sherlock replied:

"I read a great deal."

Weinstein laughed.

"How?"

"I live near a marine research station."

"That doesn't explain the physics."

"No."

"How did you learn it?"

Sherlock was silent.

Then:

"Fish are very good listeners."

Weinstein laughed so loudly that someone in the corridor stopped.

The office door remained closed.

The person in the corridor therefore knew better than to enter.

Obviously.

Weinstein looked down at the equations.

There was still one question.

"Why me?"

Sherlock answered immediately.

"Because you were going to find it anyway."

Weinstein smiled.

"Then why contact me?"

"Because you were about to get distracted."

"By what?"

Sherlock sent him an image.

A cucumber.

Weinstein stared.

"That's ridiculous."

"Yes."

"Why is it in the theory?"

"It isn't."

"Then why show it to me?"

"Because Penny thinks it is."

Weinstein laughed again.

Then he stopped.

Something else had appeared on the page.

He hadn't written it.

The words were there in his own handwriting.

**THE UNIVERSE DOES NOT NEED A STAGE.**

Below it:

**THE STAGE IS THE PHYSICS.**

Weinstein stared.

Sherlock was silent.

Outside, Munich continued being Munich.

Cars moved.

People walked.

Clocks ticked.

The Earth rotated.

The Sun continued shining.

And somewhere in the Atlantic, a dolphin named Sherlock was apparently thinking about differential geometry.

Weinstein picked up his pen.

He wrote the final equation.

Then he looked at it.

It was not a new equation.

That was the point.

The Einstein field equations were already there.

The breakthrough was understanding what they meant.

General Relativity was not merely a theory of gravity.

It was a theory in which **spacetime itself was dynamical**.

And if the deeper theory eventually explained where that spacetime came from, General Relativity would remain exactly what it had always been:

the extraordinarily successful macroscopic description of a deeper reality.

Weinstein smiled.

"Well, Sherlock."

"Yes?"

"I think we've finished."

Sherlock replied:

"No."

Weinstein raised an eyebrow.

"What now?"

"The sequel."

Weinstein stared at the wall.

"Of course."

And somewhere in London, Penny was buying another cucumber.

The universe, meanwhile, continued to curve.

As it had every right to.

Obviously.
