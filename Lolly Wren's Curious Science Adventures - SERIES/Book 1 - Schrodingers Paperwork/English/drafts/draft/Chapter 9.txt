# Chapter 9

## The Relativistic Ride

The first important observation was that Derek Gent had apparently not quite murdered himself yet.

This was reassuring.

It was also becoming slightly embarrassing.

The Bureau had announced his death with considerable confidence. There had been a photograph, a timestamp, a location, and enough circumstantial evidence to make most people reasonably uncomfortable about their own mortality.

Yet Derek remained alive.

He found this increasingly difficult to reconcile with the paperwork.

He was standing in his office, staring at the closed door.

From inside came his own voice.

"You're late."

Derek stared at the empty room.

Penny stood behind him.

Trevor stood beside her.

Nobody spoke.

Finally Derek said:

"I don't think I've murdered myself yet."

Penny nodded.

"Apparently not."

Trevor looked at the photograph.

"That's encouraging."

Derek glanced at him.

"Is it?"

"Well, if you had already done it, we'd have a rather awkward problem."

"We already have an awkward problem."

"True."

Derek looked at the photograph again.

The dead man was unmistakably him.

Same face.

Same clothes.

Same regrettable tendency to look as though he had just been interrupted while thinking about something complicated.

Yet he was still standing.

Alive.

Annoyed.

And, at least for the moment, in possession of all the usual body parts.

Derek put the photograph down.

"Perhaps the photograph is from the future."

Penny nodded.

"Obviously."

"Or another event."

"Yes."

"Or another worldline."

"Possibly."

Trevor looked thoughtful.

"Or another Derek."

Derek looked at him.

"There's only one of me."

Trevor nodded.

"That's what makes it difficult."

Derek sighed.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

The office door remained closed.

Which meant Derek was in there.

Obviously.

Penny looked at the door.

"Although technically..."

Derek raised a hand.

"No."

"What?"

"Let's not complicate the door."

"Fair."

He looked out of the window.

Rain was falling.

It had been raining for most of the day.

This was not particularly surprising.

It was London.

Rain in London was not weather so much as a local administrative policy.

Derek watched the drops run down the glass.

"Rain."

Trevor looked at him.

"Yes."

"We keep coming back to rain."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because it keeps falling."

Derek smiled.

"Not always."

Trevor looked at him.

Derek pointed at the window.

Trevor followed his finger.

The drops were falling normally.

Downward.

He frowned.

"That's disappointing."

"Give it time."

Penny picked up the brass clock.

It was reading 11:18.

She turned it over.

"Still no explanation."

Derek nodded.

"Then perhaps we're asking the wrong question."

"Which one?"

"Why the clock stops."

Trevor looked at him.

"What should we ask?"

"Why does it start again?"

Penny smiled.

"That's better."

Derek nodded.

"And why does it always happen at 11:17?"

Trevor said:

"Because something happens at 11:17."

"Yes."

"And what is that?"

Derek looked at the photograph.

"The train."

Penny nodded.

"Platform seven."

"Exactly."

Trevor looked toward the window.

"Then why are we still here?"

Derek smiled.

"We're not."

Penny understood immediately.

"We're taking the Underground."

Trevor looked at her.

"In the rain?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Derek picked up his coat.

"Because Weinstein wants a relativistic ride."

Trevor nodded.

"That sounds expensive."

"It's the Underground."

"Ah."

They left the Bureau.

Derek locked the front door.

Then stopped.

He looked at the office doors.

Penny's was closed.

Trevor's was open.

Derek's was closed.

He nodded.

"Good."

Penny looked at him.

"What?"

"Everyone's where they ought to be."

Trevor looked back.

"My office is empty."

Derek shrugged.

"Virtuality."

"Obviously."

They walked into the rain.

---

The Underground was busy.

It was always busy.

But on a rainy afternoon it was something else.

Wet coats.

Wet shoes.

Wet umbrellas.

Wet people.

The smell of rainwater, electricity and human resignation.

Derek descended the stairs.

He looked at the crowds.

"Excellent."

Trevor looked at him.

"What?"

"Test particles."

Penny smiled.

"Thousands of them."

"Exactly."

Derek opened his notebook.

"Every passenger is following a worldline."

Trevor nodded.

"Every train as well."

"Yes."

"The station."

"Yes."

"The Earth."

"Yes."

"The rain."

"Yes."

Penny said:

"And you."

Derek looked at her.

"Yes."

"Don't forget yourself."

"I wasn't planning to."

They reached the platform.

The train arrived.

Derek looked at his watch.

11:12.

He looked at the station clock.

11:12.

He looked at the train display.

11:12.

"Five minutes."

Trevor nodded.

"Until 11:17."

Derek looked down the tunnel.

"Yes."

Penny smiled.

"What are we expecting?"

Derek considered this.

"Nothing."

"Good."

"That's usually when something happens."

They boarded.

The doors closed.

The train moved.

Derek sat beside the window.

Trevor sat opposite.

Penny stood holding the rail.

The train accelerated.

Derek watched the tunnel.

"Now."

Trevor looked at him.

"Now what?"

"Acceleration."

"Ah."

Derek took out his notebook.

"The train is not an inertial frame."

"No."

"Passengers are accelerating with it."

"Yes."

"But locally, each passenger can still regard themselves as approximately inertial over a sufficiently small region."

"Yes."

"Which is where Special Relativity works."

"Yes."

Penny nodded.

"And General Relativity tells us how the local inertial frames fit together."

Derek smiled.

"Exactly."

Trevor looked at the tunnel.

"So there's no contradiction."

"Not ordinarily."

"And this train isn't going to disprove either theory."

"Not ordinarily."

Trevor smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

The train entered a station.

Brakes applied.

Derek felt the deceleration.

He watched the passengers.

Everyone leaned slightly forward.

The train stopped.

Then started again.

Derek looked at his watch.

11:14.

Normal.

"Again."

Trevor looked at him.

"Again what?"

"Acceleration."

Trevor sighed.

"Yes."

They continued.

11:15.

11:16.

Derek watched the clock.

The second hand moved smoothly.

11:16:50.

11:16:55.

11:16:58.

11:16:59.

11:17:00.

The second hand stopped.

The train continued moving.

Derek smiled.

"Right."

Penny looked at her phone.

11:17.

Trevor looked at the train clock.

11:17.

The carriage display.

11:17.

A passenger checked his phone.

11:17.

Another passenger.

11:17.

Derek looked at Penny.

"All stopped."

She nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor looked around.

Nobody else seemed concerned.

The train continued through the tunnel.

Derek stood.

He walked toward the window.

Outside, the tunnel wall slid past.

He watched it.

Then he noticed something.

The train was moving.

The clock was not.

He smiled.

"That's interesting."

Trevor joined him.

"What's interesting?"

"The train is still changing position."

"Obviously."

"Yet its clock isn't advancing."

"Yes."

"So the train's spatial coordinates are changing while its displayed temporal coordinate isn't."

Trevor nodded.

"That doesn't mean its proper time has stopped."

"Correct."

Penny came closer.

"The clock could simply be failing."

"Except all of them stopped."

"Yes."

"At exactly the same reading."

"Yes."

"And we've already seen this elsewhere."

"Munich."

"The Louvre."

"Yes."

Trevor frowned.

"So the anomaly isn't in the clocks."

Derek smiled.

"That's what I'm thinking."

"What is it in?"

"The relationship between the clocks and the events."

Penny nodded.

"Good."

Derek looked at the rain through the window.

The tunnel ended.

Rain streaked across the glass.

He watched one droplet.

It slid downward.

Then sideways.

Then upward.

Derek blinked.

"Did you see that?"

Penny looked.

"Yes."

Trevor looked.

"Yes."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Trevor frowned.

"What?"

"The rain is misbehaving again."

The train entered another tunnel.

The droplet disappeared.

Derek looked at his watch.

11:17.

Still.

The train continued.

He felt the acceleration.

He thought about Special Relativity.

Then General Relativity.

Then Barbarian.

Time wasn't necessarily a thing.

It might be a relationship between events.

The train didn't need a universal time.

It had a worldline.

The passengers had worldlines.

The rain had worldlines.

The clocks had worldlines.

The Earth had a worldline through spacetime.

Everything had a history.

Perhaps "time" was simply how they described the relationships among those histories.

Derek smiled.

Then thought about poetry.

He couldn't help it.

If time were removed completely, a poem would be a collection of words with no ordering.

Every word would be there.

But all at once.

The idea was ridiculous.

It would sound like a fart.

A highly educated fart.

Possibly a French fart.

But a fart nonetheless.

Derek wrote in his notebook:

**TIME MAY BE THE THING THAT STOPS POETRY FROM BEING A FART.**

Trevor looked over his shoulder.

"What are you writing?"

"Physics."

Trevor read it.

"That's not physics."

"It will be."

Penny looked.

She smiled.

"Actually, he's got a point."

Trevor stared at her.

"How?"

"A poem requires ordering."

"Yes."

"Ordering requires distinguishing one event from another."

"Yes."

"And our experience of that ordering is what we call temporal succession."

Trevor nodded slowly.

"That's fair."

Derek looked pleased.

"Well, that's settled."

Trevor smiled.

"Yes."

The train slowed.

Derek looked at the station sign.

They had arrived at the next station.

He checked the clock.

Still 11:17.

The station clock also read 11:17.

Derek stepped onto the platform.

He looked down.

Water was running along the edge.

He crouched.

A stream of rainwater moved toward the drain.

Derek watched.

The water reached the drain.

Then stopped.

The train doors closed.

The train departed.

The water remained.

Derek stood.

Penny looked at him.

"What?"

"The water."

"What about it?"

"It stopped."

"Because the train left?"

"No."

"Then why?"

Derek shook his head.

"I don't know."

Trevor looked at the water.

"It could be blocked."

"Yes."

"It could be debris."

"Yes."

"It could be the gradient."

"Yes."

Trevor smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Derek laughed.

"Not quite."

He looked at the tunnel.

Then at the station clock.

Then at the water.

"Let's see what happens at 12:04."

Penny's expression changed.

"The restart."

"Exactly."

They waited.

The rain continued.

The station remained busy.

Trains arrived.

Trains departed.

People came and went.

Nobody paid any attention to the three people standing beside a puddle.

At 12:03, Derek checked his watch.

Still 11:17.

At 12:03:30, nothing happened.

At 12:03:50, nothing.

At 12:03:59—

The water moved.

The clock jumped.

11:17 became 12:04.

The station clock did the same.

Derek's watch did the same.

Penny's phone jumped.

Trevor's phone jumped.

The train arriving at the platform seemed to hesitate for the briefest fraction of a second.

Then everything continued.

Derek stared at the clock.

12:04.

He looked at the puddle.

The water was flowing normally.

He looked at Penny.

"Well."

She smiled.

"That's settled."

Trevor nodded.

"The clocks weren't broken."

"No."

"They were waiting."

"Yes."

"For what?"

Derek looked down the tunnel.

"For the next event."

A train approached.

Its headlights appeared in the darkness.

Derek watched it come closer.

Then he noticed the destination display.

LONDON TERMINUS.

PLATFORM 7.

Derek's face changed.

"That's our train."

Trevor looked at the display.

"We're underground."

"Yes."

"The train is coming from the wrong direction."

"Yes."

Penny looked at the clock.

12:04.

Derek smiled.

"Of course."

The train stopped.

The doors opened.

Inside, the carriage was empty.

Completely empty.

No passengers.

No driver.

No conductor.

Just seats.

And at the far end of the carriage was a single brass clock.

11:17.

Derek stared.

Trevor stared.

Penny stared.

Then Derek stepped aboard.

Penny grabbed his arm.

"Derek."

He looked at her.

"Yes?"

"The door is open."

Derek smiled.

"Yes."

"So whoever's in there..."

"Has vanished into virtuality."

"Obviously."

Derek looked into the empty carriage.

"Then we'd better find out where they've gone."

He stepped inside.

The doors closed behind him.

The train began to move.

And somewhere ahead, beneath London, something was waiting at Platform Seven.

It had been waiting since 11:17.

It would remain there until 12:04.

And, according to Derek's watch, it was already tomorrow.


### The Flight

Derek fell asleep on the train.

This was unfortunate.

Not because sleeping on the Underground was particularly dangerous, although it was generally considered unwise to trust one's neck with the suspension system of a London carriage.

It was unfortunate because Derek had a dream.

In the dream, he was on an aeroplane.

He was already late.

This was especially irritating because Derek had been absolutely certain he had left with plenty of time.

He checked his watch.

11:17.

He checked it again.

11:17.

He looked at the departure board.

His flight had already departed.

Derek stared.

"But I was here."

The airport appeared to have no sympathy.

He ran.

He reached the gate.

Closed.

The attendant looked at him.

"Sorry."

"How long ago?"

"About forty minutes."

"Impossible."

She shrugged.

"It happens."

Derek looked at his watch.

11:17.

He looked at the airport clock.

12:04.

He frowned.

"Ah."

The attendant smiled.

"Yes."

"Different reference frame?"

She looked at him.

"Different airline."

Derek stared.

"What?"

"You booked Ryanair."

He looked at the ticket.

Ryanair.

He frowned.

"I usually fly British Airways."

"That may be your problem."

Derek looked at her.

"Why?"

She smiled.

"British Airways uses Greenwich Mean Time."

"Ryanair uses?"

"Whatever time gets you to the gate."

Derek considered this.

"That's not how time zones work."

"No."

"Or Special Relativity."

"No."

"Or airlines."

"Apparently."

She handed him another ticket.

Derek looked at it.

His seat number was written in large letters.

He stared.

"What's this?"

"Your seat."

"I have a seat."

"Yes."

"Where?"

She pointed toward the aircraft.

"On the plane."

"Obviously."

She smiled.

"And please put your seat in the upright position."

Derek looked down.

He was suddenly sitting in the aircraft.

A very attractive flight attendant was leaning over him.

She smiled.

"Your seat, sir."

Derek looked down.

His seat was reclining.

He looked back at her.

"Yes."

"Upright, please."

Derek adjusted it.

She nodded approvingly.

"Much better."

Then she walked away.

Derek watched her go.

He looked at his watch.

11:17.

He looked at the aircraft clock.

12:04.

He stared.

"Well, that's not good."

The captain's voice came over the speaker.

"Ladies and gentlemen, we're currently travelling at approximately thirty-seven thousand feet."

Derek looked out of the window.

The Earth was below.

He thought about proper time.

He thought about gravity.

He thought about acceleration.

He thought about SR.

He thought about GR.

Then he thought about the flight attendant.

Perhaps the problem was not relativity.

Perhaps the problem was Ryanair.

He closed his eyes.

When he opened them, the aircraft was empty.

The flight attendant was gone.

The pilot was gone.

The passengers were gone.

The plane was still flying.

Derek looked at the clock.

11:17.

He looked out of the window.

The Earth was still below.

Then he noticed something.

The clouds were moving backwards.

Derek stood.

He walked toward the cockpit.

The door was open.

Obviously, the pilot had vanished into virtuality.

He looked inside.

Nobody.

The instrument panel was running.

The plane continued flying.

Derek looked at the controls.

Then at the clock.

Then at the empty cockpit.

"Well, that's settled."

The aircraft suddenly descended.

Derek grabbed the nearest seat.

The flight attendant appeared beside him.

"Sir?"

Derek looked at her.

"Yes?"

"Your seat."

"What about it?"

"Upright."

Derek looked down.

His seat was reclining again.

He adjusted it.

She smiled.

"Thank you."

Then she disappeared.

Derek looked around.

The aircraft was empty again.

He looked at the clock.

11:17.

Then 12:04.

Then 11:17.

Then 12:04.

He understood.

The aircraft wasn't travelling through a single universal time.

Its clocks were describing different relationships between events.

The flight had not been late.

The arrival event had not happened "later."

The entire question of later and earlier had become frame-dependent.

Derek smiled.

"That's actually quite beautiful."

Then he looked at the airline ticket.

It said:

**ARRIVAL: YESTERDAY**

Derek stared.

"Right."

The plane landed.

The doors opened.

Derek stepped out.

He was back in the London Underground.

He woke with a start.

The train was still moving.

Penny was sitting opposite him.

Trevor was asleep beside the window.

Penny looked at Derek.

"You were talking."

Derek rubbed his eyes.

"What did I say?"

She smiled.

"You said something about an airline."

Derek looked at the clock.

11:17.

Still.

He looked out the window.

Rain streaked across the glass.

Then he noticed that his seat was reclined.

He stared at it.

Penny looked at him.

"What?"

Derek slowly pushed the seat upright.

"Nothing."

Penny smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Derek looked at the rain.

"Not quite."

He took out his notebook.

Under the previous entry he wrote:

**NOTE: Never book a relativistic flight with Ryanair.**

Then, after a moment's thought:

**British Airways probably isn't much better.**

He closed the notebook.

The train continued into the darkness.

And somewhere ahead, the clock was still waiting for 12:04.
