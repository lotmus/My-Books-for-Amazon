# Chapter 6

## The Fourth Coordinate

For several seconds nobody said anything.

The room beneath the Louvre was small, stone-walled and entirely too quiet.

There was a table.

There was a clock.

There were four people.

And there was a piece of paper asking them for a value of τ.

Trevor was the first to speak.

"That's proper time."

Weinstein nodded.

"Yes."

Derek looked at the paper.

"Which means?"

Trevor pointed at the three numbers.

"Three spatial coordinates have been supplied. The fourth coordinate is missing."

"Correct."

"And they're asking us to calculate it."

"Apparently."

Trevor considered the problem.

"Do we know the metric?"

"No."

"The worldline?"

"No."

"The gravitational field?"

"No."

"The initial conditions?"

"No."

Trevor looked at the paper again.

"Then we can't calculate it."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Trevor looked relieved.

"Good."

Penny, however, had not moved.

She was looking at the clock.

"There's something else."

Derek followed her gaze.

"What?"

"Look at the time."

"11:17."

"Exactly."

"Yes."

"That's when the clocks stopped."

Derek nodded.

"And restarted."

"At 12:04."

"Yes."

Penny pointed to the paper.

"Then perhaps τ isn't asking us for a number."

Trevor looked at her.

"What else could it ask for?"

"An interval."

Trevor frowned.

"Between what?"

Penny pointed at the clock.

"Two events."

Weinstein smiled.

"Exactly."

Derek looked at him.

"So the missing coordinate isn't necessarily a timestamp."

"No."

"It's proper time between events."

"Perhaps."

Trevor looked at the paper again.

"Then we'd still need the worldline."

"Yes."

"We don't have one."

"We have several."

Trevor looked up.

"Whose?"

Weinstein pointed at the clock.

"The clock."

Trevor nodded.

"Right."

Derek picked up the clock.

"Then let's start with the obvious."

He turned it over.

"How does this thing know when to stop?"

Nobody answered.

He examined the mechanism.

There was nothing unusual.

No obvious electronics.

No radio receiver.

No battery.

No hidden switch.

The brass casing was old and scratched.

The movement was mechanical.

Penny took it from him.

"May I?"

"Of course."

She opened the casing.

Trevor leaned over.

Weinstein watched.

The mechanism was intricate.

Beautiful, even.

Tiny gears.

Springs.

Jewelled bearings.

A balance wheel.

An escapement.

Penny examined it for several seconds.

"There's nothing here."

Derek smiled.

"That's becoming a recurring feature."

"I mean literally."

She held the movement toward the light.

"There's no mechanism capable of producing the behaviour we've seen."

Trevor nodded.

"Then the clock isn't doing it."

Penny looked at him.

"What?"

"The clock isn't deciding when to stop."

"Go on."

"It's responding to something."

Derek nodded.

"Exactly."

Weinstein took the clock.

"The clock is a physical system."

"Yes."

"It has a worldline."

"Yes."

"It measures proper time along that worldline."

"Yes."

"So if it stops while everything else continues..."

"Then something about the physical conditions along its worldline has changed."

Trevor looked at him.

"Or the clock itself has changed."

"Yes."

"Could it have been accelerated?"

Weinstein nodded.

"In principle."

"Enough to create a significant time dilation?"

"Not without enormous acceleration."

"How enormous?"

"Enough that you'd notice."

Derek smiled.

"That seems useful."

"Yes."

Penny looked at the clock.

"But the clock didn't move."

"Not in any ordinary sense."

"So acceleration isn't the explanation."

"Not ordinary acceleration."

Trevor thought.

"Gravity?"

Weinstein nodded.

"Possibly."

Derek pointed toward the ceiling.

"We're beneath the Louvre."

"Yes."

"Could there be something beneath us?"

Penny looked at him.

"Such as?"

"A very large mass."

Trevor smiled.

"How large?"

"Large enough to stop clocks."

"That would be rather noticeable."

"Probably."

Weinstein shook his head.

"Not necessarily."

Everyone looked at him.

He continued.

"A gravitational field doesn't have to announce itself by dropping buildings on people."

"Fair."

"And gravitational time dilation depends on the metric, not merely on some crude idea of gravitational force."

"Meaning?"

"Two clocks at different gravitational potentials can accumulate different proper times."

Trevor nodded.

"But not by forty-seven minutes in an ordinary room."

"Correct."

Derek looked at the clock.

"Then we're back to the same problem."

"Yes."

He put the clock down.

"Unless..."

Nobody spoke.

Derek looked at the paper.

Three spatial coordinates.

A missing proper time.

He walked to the wall.

"What if the room itself is the coordinate system?"

Trevor frowned.

"Explain."

Derek pointed to the floor.

"We're somewhere in three-dimensional space."

"Yes."

"Suppose the numbers describe a point."

"Yes."

"Then the missing τ describes which event occurs at that point."

Trevor nodded.

"Yes."

"So perhaps the paper isn't telling us where to look."

Penny finished:

"It's telling us when."

Derek smiled.

"Exactly."

Weinstein shook his head.

"Not quite."

Derek turned.

"What?"

"The word 'when' is precisely what we should avoid."

"Why?"

"Because we're trying to determine whether the events are ordered by a fundamental time or merely related geometrically."

Derek nodded.

"Fair."

Penny smiled.

"So what should we call it?"

Weinstein looked at the equation.

"Which event."

There was silence.

Trevor nodded slowly.

"That's better."

Weinstein continued.

"Suppose the point x = 3, y = 4, z = 12 corresponds to a particular location."

"Yes."

"Then τ identifies a particular event along a worldline."

"Yes."

"But if the geometry is unusual, there may be more than one relevant event associated with that spatial location."

Derek stared at him.

"More than one?"

"Yes."

"At the same place?"

"Possibly."

"How?"

"Different times."

Derek smiled.

"That's a rather circular answer."

"Only if you assume time is fundamental."

Derek laughed.

"Fair enough."

Trevor walked over to the wall.

He took the piece of paper and copied the numbers.

Then he drew three axes.

x.

y.

z.

He marked the point.

"Here."

Derek nodded.

"Now what?"

Trevor added a fourth axis.

τ.

"Here."

He drew a curve.

"The clock's worldline."

Weinstein nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor continued.

"If the clock stops at one point and restarts at another..."

"Then its worldline has a strange section."

"Exactly."

Penny leaned over.

"Or we've misunderstood what stopping means."

Trevor looked at her.

"What do you mean?"

"If the hands stop but the clock's physical state continues changing..."

Trevor's eyes widened.

"Then the clock's reading has stopped, but the clock hasn't."

"Yes."

Derek looked at the movement.

"That's interesting."

Trevor nodded.

"The clock could be experiencing proper time normally while its display remains fixed."

Weinstein smiled.

"That is much more interesting."

Derek looked at Penny.

"Can we test it?"

Penny picked up the clock.

"Probably."

"How?"

"Compare the mechanism's state before and after."

Trevor nodded.

"Balance wheel frequency."

"Yes."

"Escapement."

"Yes."

"Spring tension."

"Yes."

"Temperature."

"Yes."

"Position."

"Yes."

"Gravitational potential."

"Yes."

Derek looked impressed.

"How long?"

Penny shrugged.

"Ten minutes."

Trevor smiled.

"We'll need a laboratory."

Penny looked at the room.

"We have one."

Derek looked around.

"This is the Louvre."

"Yes."

"Not CERN."

"No."

"Then where exactly is the laboratory?"

Penny pointed at the clock.

"Here."

Derek smiled.

"Of course."

They spent the next half hour examining the clock.

There was nothing supernatural about it.

That was, in some ways, the most unsettling thing.

The balance wheel oscillated normally.

The spring behaved normally.

The escapement behaved normally.

The gears moved.

Everything mechanical appeared perfectly ordinary.

And yet the clock had stopped.

Weinstein watched the movement.

"There's no anomaly."

Trevor nodded.

"None."

Derek looked at the face.

"Then why did it stop?"

Penny turned the clock over.

"It didn't."

Derek frowned.

"The hands stopped."

"Yes."

"But the movement didn't."

"Correct."

Trevor looked excited.

"That means the hands aren't coupled to the movement in the ordinary way."

Derek looked at him.

"How?"

Trevor examined the mechanism.

"There must be an intermediate mechanism."

"Which we can't find."

"Correct."

Weinstein nodded.

"Then perhaps it isn't mechanical."

Derek looked at the clock.

"It certainly looks mechanical."

"Appearance isn't evidence."

Penny smiled.

"That's something Derek usually says."

Derek looked offended.

"I say it more elegantly."

"Naturally."

Weinstein turned the clock over again.

The initials were still there.

LGV.

He stared at them.

Then he took out his notebook.

"LGV."

Derek nodded.

"We've seen it before."

"Yes."

"What does it mean?"

"We don't know."

Weinstein wrote the letters.

Then, beneath them, he wrote:

LOCAL?

GRAVITATIONAL?

VECTOR?

Penny looked at the page.

"That's what Trevor suggested."

Weinstein looked at Trevor.

"Did you?"

Trevor nodded.

"Yesterday."

"Interesting."

Trevor smiled.

"Thank you."

Weinstein looked again.

"Local Gravitational Vector."

Derek shook his head.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because a vector requires a direction."

"Yes."

"And we've got no indication that the initials represent one."

"Fair."

Penny said:

"Could be initials."

"Whose?"

Nobody knew.

Weinstein looked at the clock.

Then at the paper.

Then at the three coordinates.

He suddenly stood.

"Let's go upstairs."

Derek looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because I want a map."

"Of the Louvre?"

"Yes."

They returned to the main museum.

A staff member directed them toward a map of the building.

Weinstein examined it.

Then the underground room.

Then the coordinates.

He frowned.

"These aren't Louvre coordinates."

Derek leaned closer.

"How do you know?"

"Because the scale is wrong."

Trevor looked at the map.

"Then what are they?"

Weinstein took a pencil.

He marked three points.

"Not ordinary Cartesian coordinates."

"Then?"

"Relative coordinates."

Derek looked at him.

"Relative to what?"

Weinstein stared at the map.

Then smiled.

"That's the question."

Penny pointed at the clock.

"Could the clock be the origin?"

Weinstein nodded.

"Possibly."

Trevor looked at the numbers.

"Three, four, twelve."

He paused.

"That's a Pythagorean triple."

Derek looked at him.

"What?"

Trevor pointed.

"Three squared plus four squared equals five squared."

Then he added:

"Five squared plus twelve squared equals thirteen squared."

Weinstein looked at the numbers.

Derek smiled.

"So?"

Trevor shrugged.

"Nothing necessarily."

Penny nodded.

"Don't force patterns."

Trevor nodded.

"I wasn't."

Derek looked at him.

"You absolutely were."

"Perhaps slightly."

Weinstein looked at the coordinates again.

"Three, four, twelve."

He wrote:

13.

Then:

5.

Then:

12.

He stared.

"Thirteen."

Derek waited.

"What about it?"

"The spatial distance from the origin is thirteen."

"Assuming Euclidean space."

"Yes."

"And?"

Weinstein looked at the clock.

"The missing coordinate may be related to the invariant interval."

Derek's expression changed.

"Go on."

Weinstein took the pencil.

"In Special Relativity, the spacetime interval is invariant."

He wrote:

ds² = -c²dt² + dx² + dy² + dz²

Trevor nodded.

"Depending on sign convention."

"Yes."

Weinstein continued.

"If we know the spatial separation..."

He pointed at the coordinates.

"...and the interval..."

He looked at the missing τ.

"...we can infer the temporal relationship."

Derek nodded.

"But we don't know the interval."

"No."

"Then?"

Weinstein smiled.

"Perhaps we do."

He pointed toward the Louvre.

"The clock."

Derek frowned.

"The clock gives us proper time."

"Yes."

"So τ is the proper-time coordinate."

"Yes."

"And the spatial distance is thirteen."

"Yes."

"So..."

Weinstein stopped.

"Nothing."

Derek laughed.

"That's a very dramatic pause for nothing."

"We're missing one more piece."

"What?"

"The nature of the interval."

Penny nodded.

"Timelike, spacelike or null."

Weinstein looked at her.

"Exactly."

Trevor smiled.

"If the interval is timelike, the events can be causally connected."

"Yes."

"If spacelike, they can't."

"Correct."

"If null, they're connected by light."

"Yes."

Derek looked at the clock.

"Then we need to determine which one this is."

Weinstein nodded.

"And that may tell us what the clock is doing."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Trevor looked at him.

"What?"

"We have a method."

Penny nodded.

"And a destination."

Derek frowned.

"Destination?"

She pointed toward the map.

"Those coordinates."

Weinstein looked.

They corresponded to a location in the museum.

A gallery on the upper floor.

Derek smiled.

"Right."

They climbed the stairs.

The gallery was crowded.

Visitors moved between paintings.

Tour guides spoke quietly.

Cameras appeared everywhere.

Nothing looked unusual.

Derek checked the coordinates.

"We're here."

Weinstein looked around.

"Where?"

"Somewhere in this room."

Trevor checked the numbers again.

"Three metres east."

"From what?"

Trevor looked embarrassed.

"Right."

Derek sighed.

"Origin."

"Yes."

"Which is?"

They looked at the clock.

Then at one another.

Penny pointed.

"The Louvre entrance."

They walked.

The three coordinates took them toward a large painting.

Derek stopped.

"This is it."

Weinstein looked at the painting.

It showed a landscape.

A road.

Trees.

Mountains.

A distant building.

Nothing unusual.

Then he noticed the perspective.

The vanishing point.

The same feature Derek had noticed earlier.

Weinstein stepped closer.

"The coordinate system."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor looked at the painting.

"The spatial geometry is encoded in the perspective."

"Exactly."

Penny pointed at the horizon.

"And there."

A tiny clock was visible in the distant building.

Weinstein stared.

It showed 12:04.

Derek took the brass clock from his pocket.

Its hands had moved.

12:04.

Trevor checked his phone.

12:04.

Penny checked hers.

12:04.

The gallery clock showed 12:04.

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

Then Derek said:

"Well, that's settled."

Weinstein looked at him.

"What is?"

"We've found the event."

Trevor smiled.

"The fourth coordinate."

Weinstein shook his head.

"No."

They looked at him.

He pointed at the painting.

"We've found the *projection* of the event."

Derek stared at the painting.

"And the event itself?"

Weinstein looked at the clock.

"That may be somewhere else."

The painting suddenly changed.

Not dramatically.

Not with a flash.

Nothing so vulgar.

The tiny clock in the distant building simply moved.

12:04.

12:05.

Then the entire scene shifted.

The road was different.

The trees were different.

The building was gone.

The people in the gallery continued talking.

Nobody else seemed to notice.

Derek stared.

"Did you see that?"

Penny nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor nodded.

"Yes."

Weinstein smiled.

"Good."

Derek looked at him.

"Why are you pleased?"

"Because now we know."

"Know what?"

Weinstein pointed at the painting.

"The event isn't moving through time."

Derek waited.

"It's changing the geometry."

The brass clock in his hand began ticking backwards.

And somewhere beneath Paris, another clock began ticking forwards.

The two clocks were now measuring different proper times.

Neither was wrong.

And, for the first time, Weinstein understood why the question on the paper had not been:

WHEN?

It had been:

τ = ?

The answer was not a date.

It was a path.

A worldline.

And somebody had just drawn them one.


Chapter 6 — continued

Weinstein did not leave the gallery.

Everyone else seemed to have moved on.

Visitors wandered past the painting.

A guide explained something about perspective to a group of tourists.

Someone took a photograph.

A child complained that he was hungry.

The universe continued behaving as though nothing particularly remarkable had happened.

Weinstein remained in front of the painting.

He was thinking.

Derek knew better than to interrupt him.

Penny knew better still.

Trevor, after several years of experience, had learned that there were two kinds of silence from Weinstein.

There was the ordinary silence.

And there was the dangerous silence.

This was the second kind.

Weinstein looked at the painting.

Then at the brass clock.

Then at the three coordinates.

Three spatial dimensions.

One temporal coordinate.

A geometry.

A worldline.

An invariant interval.

And curvature.

He suddenly took out his notebook.

Derek watched.

"What have you got?"

Weinstein didn't answer.

He drew three axes.

Then curved them.

Derek leaned closer.

"What is that?"

"Space."

"That's not very flat."

"No."

"Should it be?"

Weinstein smiled.

"That's the question."

He drew another curve.

Then another.

He began to imagine spacetime itself.

Not a rubber sheet.

Not a picture of a planet sitting in a depression.

Something more fundamental.

A four-dimensional geometry.

The curvature was not necessarily in some larger space.

The geometry itself was curved.

An observer moving through it would experience that geometry as reality.

Weinstein wrote:

CURVATURE.

Then underneath:

MORE CURVATURE.

Then:

HOW MUCH IS TOO MUCH?

He stopped.

Derek read it.

"That's rather philosophical."

"No."

Weinstein looked up.

"It's physics."

"Fair enough."

Weinstein continued.

"If spacetime can be curved..."

"Yes."

"And matter and energy determine that curvature..."

"Yes."

"Then there must be some limit."

Trevor frowned.

"Why?"

Weinstein looked at him.

Trevor waited.

Weinstein considered the question.

"Perhaps there isn't."

Trevor nodded.

"Right."

Weinstein continued.

"But suppose there is."

Derek smiled.

"That's rather different."

"Yes."

Weinstein drew a curve that became increasingly steep.

"Imagine curvature increasing."

"Yes."

"More and more."

"Yes."

"At some point our ordinary description becomes inadequate."

Penny nodded.

"Because the equations stop giving sensible physical answers."

"Perhaps."

"Or because an invariant diverges."

"Yes."

"Or because a geodesic can't be continued."

"Yes."

Weinstein looked at the drawing.

"And that..."

He paused.

"...is where we call it a singularity."

Trevor looked at him.

"You're defining a singularity as 'too much curvature'?"

Weinstein shook his head.

"No."

He crossed out the phrase.

"A singularity isn't simply a region where spacetime is very curved."

He wrote:

BREAKDOWN OF THE CLASSICAL DESCRIPTION.

"That's better."

Derek nodded.

"Much."

Weinstein looked again at his drawing.

"But the intuitive idea remains."

"Which is?"

"If curvature increases without bound, the classical theory can cease to provide a meaningful description."

"Yes."

"And that's precisely the sort of thing we're looking for."

Derek's expression changed.

"Why?"

Weinstein pointed at the brass clock.

"Because we have a clock behaving as though it has crossed from one geometry into another."

Penny frowned.

"Or stopped measuring the same proper time."

"Exactly."

Trevor looked at the painting.

"So you're thinking there's an extreme gravitational region somewhere."

"Perhaps."

"Near Paris?"

"No."

"Then where?"

Weinstein smiled.

"That's what Derek is going to investigate."

Derek sighed.

"Of course I am."

Weinstein looked at him.

"You went to the Louvre."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I followed a clue."

"Good."

"You knew that."

"I suspected."

"And now?"

"Now I suspect you found something else."

Derek smiled.

"I did."

"What?"

Derek pointed at the painting.

"Look at the landscape."

Weinstein did.

"The geometry changes."

"Yes."

"Not the painting?"

"No."

"The projection."

"Exactly."

Derek took out a photograph.

It showed the same painting.

But the perspective was subtly different.

The vanishing point had shifted.

Weinstein studied both images.

"The projection has changed."

"Yes."

"Without the underlying objects changing."

"Yes."

Weinstein frowned.

"That's impossible."

Derek smiled.

Penny immediately said:

"Don't."

Weinstein laughed.

"Fine."

He looked again.

"If the projection changes while the objects remain..."

"The coordinate system has changed."

"Yes."

"And if the coordinate system changes..."

"The metric might."

Weinstein nodded.

"Exactly."

Trevor looked between them.

"So we're not looking at a strange painting."

"No."

"We're looking at a change in the geometry used to describe it."

"Yes."

Trevor thought.

"Then perhaps the painting isn't showing us a place."

Weinstein turned.

"What is it showing us?"

Trevor pointed.

"An event."

There was silence.

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Penny nodded.

"Good."

Weinstein looked at Trevor.

"You may keep him."

Derek laughed.

Trevor smiled.

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"You should."

Weinstein turned back to the painting.

The idea of a curved event returned to him.

An event wasn't merely a point.

It was a point in spacetime.

But if spacetime itself was curved, then the relationship between neighbouring events was not Euclidean.

The geometry mattered.

The interval mattered.

The worldlines mattered.

And if the curvature became extreme enough, the familiar description could break down.

Weinstein wrote one word.

SINGULARITY.

Derek read it.

"That's a rather dramatic word."

"It's precise."

"Is it?"

"In the appropriate mathematical sense."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's reassuring."

Weinstein continued writing.

"Black holes."

"Yes."

"Cosmological singularities."

"Yes."

"Geodesic incompleteness."

"Yes."

Derek looked at him.

"You're thinking about the clock."

"Yes."

"Could a clock pass near a singularity?"

Weinstein looked at the brass clock.

"Not in any ordinary sense."

"Why?"

"Because a singularity isn't simply a very strong gravitational field that you can safely approach."

"Fair."

"The classical spacetime description itself becomes pathological."

"Meaning?"

"You can't simply ask what the clock does at the singularity."

Derek nodded.

"Because the theory has stopped telling us."

"Exactly."

Penny looked at the clock.

"Then perhaps the clock isn't near one."

Weinstein nodded.

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps it's telling us that the geometry between two events has become extreme."

"Yes."

Trevor looked at the three coordinates.

"Then the missing τ could be the proper-time difference along a worldline approaching that region."

Weinstein turned sharply.

"Yes."

Trevor continued.

"And if the proper time remains finite while something else becomes infinite..."

"Then we could have a singular behaviour."

"Yes."

Weinstein smiled.

"Very good."

Trevor looked pleased.

Derek looked at Penny.

"He's becoming dangerous."

"He was always dangerous."

Trevor ignored them.

"So what do we do?"

Weinstein closed his notebook.

"We find out where the curvature becomes extreme."

Derek nodded.

"How?"

Weinstein looked at the painting.

"The same way we found the event."

"The coordinates?"

"Yes."

"And the clock?"

"Yes."

"And the Louvre?"

"Yes."

Derek looked around.

"Then we've got a problem."

"What?"

"We don't have enough coordinates."

Weinstein smiled.

"No."

He pointed at the painting.

"We have four."

Derek looked.

Three numbers had been written on the paper.

He had assumed the fourth coordinate was missing.

It wasn't.

A tiny number was visible beneath the brass clock in the painting.

It had been almost completely hidden by the frame.

Derek leaned closer.

"What's that?"

Weinstein looked.

The number was:

Trevor stared.

"Thirteen."

Penny nodded.

"That's the distance."

"No."

"Because we're assuming Euclidean space."

Trevor nodded.

"Right."

"If this is spacetime, the invariant interval isn't simply the Euclidean distance."

ds² = dx² + dy² + dz² − c²dt²

"Unless we're using the opposite sign convention."

"Does it matter?"

"Not for the physical content."

"Well, that's settled."

He looked again at the painting.

Three.

Four.

Twelve.

Thirteen.

And beneath them, almost invisible:

∞

Derek stopped smiling.

Penny saw it.

Trevor saw it.

Weinstein stared.

"That's not a coordinate."

"No," said Derek.

"What is it?"

"Infinity."

Trevor frowned.

"That would suggest a divergent quantity."

"Yes."

"Curvature?"

"Possibly."

"Proper time?"

"Possibly."

"Interval?"

"Possibly."

Derek looked at him.

"Which?"

Weinstein didn't answer.

He was staring at the painting.

Then he smiled very slowly.

"Now we have our singularity."

Derek looked at Penny.

Penny looked at Trevor.

Trevor looked at Weinstein.

And somewhere, far beneath the Louvre, a clock began ticking.

Not forward.

Not backward.

But sideways.

At least, that was what the clock appeared to think it was doing.
