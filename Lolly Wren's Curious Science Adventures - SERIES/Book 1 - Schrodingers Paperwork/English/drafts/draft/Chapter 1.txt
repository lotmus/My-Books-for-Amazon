# Chapter 1

## The Murder That Hadn't Happened Yet

At precisely 9:17 on a Tuesday morning, Derek Gent received an email informing him that he was dead.

He was not particularly surprised.

Derek had always suspected that death would arrive by email.

Not because he was especially pessimistic, but because nearly everything important seemed to arrive by email these days. Electricity bills. Insurance renewals. Invitations to webinars. Notifications that someone had changed a password Derek had never used.

Death was simply the logical next step.

What surprised him was the sender.

The message had come from the Derek Gent Relativistic Investigation Bureau.

Derek stared at the screen.

The Bureau was not supposed to send him emails.

It was his Bureau.

More precisely, it was a small private investigative organization occupying two rooms on the second floor of an old office building in London, plus a corridor which the landlord insisted was a communal area and Derek insisted belonged to the Bureau whenever he needed somewhere to put a coat.

The Bureau dealt primarily with unusual cases.

This was partly because Derek liked unusual cases and partly because ordinary cases paid terribly.

The sign on the frosted glass door read:

DEREK GENT
RELATIVISTIC INVESTIGATION BUREAU

Underneath, in smaller letters:

CAUSALITY, CHRONOLOGY & OTHER INCONVENIENCES

And underneath that, added by Penny several years earlier:

NO MURDERS BEFORE 10 A.M. WITHOUT AN APPOINTMENT

Derek had objected.

Penny had pointed out that they had never actually received a murder before ten.

Derek had said that this was beside the point.

Penny had said that was precisely the point.

Derek had lost.

He was now looking at an email from the Bureau.

The subject line was:

CASE UPDATE: DEREK GENT

He opened it.

Dear Mr Gent,

We regret to inform you that you have been murdered.

The incident will occur tomorrow at 11:03 a.m.

The perpetrator has been identified.

It is you.

Kind regards,

Derek Gent
Relativistic Investigation Bureau

Derek read it again.

Then a third time.

He looked at the sender's address.

It was correct.

He looked at the Bureau's computer.

It was his computer.

He looked around the office.

Everything appeared to be where it ought to be.

The filing cabinet was locked.

The kettle was where the kettle normally was, which was not necessarily where it ought to be.

The window was shut.

The door was shut.

Nothing appeared to have been disturbed.

Derek looked at the email again.

"That's inconvenient," he said.

Across the room, Trevor Boltzman looked up.

Trevor was twenty-eight, highly intelligent, technically qualified, and possessed of the unfortunate tendency to think about one problem at a time.

This made him very good at mathematics.

It made him considerably less useful when confronted with a sentence such as:

"I have been murdered."

Trevor removed his glasses.

"What?"

"I've been murdered."

"Recently?"

"Tomorrow."

Trevor stared at him.

"Tomorrow?"

"Yes."

Trevor considered this carefully.

"You're telling me you've been murdered tomorrow?"

"Yes."

Trevor nodded.

"Ah."

Derek waited.

Trevor put his glasses back on.

Derek continued waiting.

"Why did you say 'ah'?"

"I wasn't sure what else to say."

"How about 'that's impossible'?"

"I thought you'd already know that."

Derek looked at him.

"Trevor, I have been working in this field for twenty-three years."

"Yes."

"I know the difference between something being impossible and something being highly inconvenient."

"Which is this?"

Derek held up the screen.

"I'm not sure yet."

Trevor stood.

"Tea?"

"Yes."

"Milk?"

"No."

"Sugar?"

"One."

Trevor nodded and walked toward the kitchen.

Derek called after him.

"Two."

Trevor stopped.

"Two?"

"Yes."

Trevor continued.

Derek watched him disappear.

There was a knock at the door.

Before Derek could answer, the door opened.

Penny Gent walked in.

Penny was Derek's cousin.

She was also one of those people who appeared to have been designed specifically to make other people lose their train of thought.

She was a striking brunette, though Derek had never used the word striking in her presence because he valued his life and had no wish to become the subject of a police report.

She wore a dark coat, carried a handbag, and had the calm expression of someone who had already dealt with several irritating people before breakfast.

She looked at Derek.

"Morning."

"Morning."

She put her handbag down.

"Why do you look like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like you've just discovered the Inland Revenue has found you."

"I've been murdered."

Penny paused.

"Already?"

"Apparently."

"Who did it?"

"I did."

She nodded.

"Fair enough."

Derek looked at her.

"You're taking this remarkably well."

"It's Tuesday."

"That doesn't explain anything."

"It explains most things."

She walked over to the computer.

"Show me."

Derek turned the monitor toward her.

Penny read the email.

Her expression changed.

Only slightly.

Penny was clever.

Very clever.

Unlike Trevor, who approached every new piece of information as if it might require a spreadsheet, Penny seemed to absorb things almost before they were said.

She read the message again.

"Tomorrow at eleven-oh-three."

"Yes."

"Not approximately eleven."

"No."

"Eleven-oh-three."

"Yes."

"That's very specific."

"I thought so."

"And the murderer is you."

"Apparently."

Penny looked at Derek.

"Are you planning to murder yourself?"

"Not consciously."

"Good."

She pulled out a chair.

"I'd hate to think you'd become predictable."

Derek smiled despite himself.

Trevor came back carrying three mugs.

"Milk?"

"None," said Derek.

"Two sugars," said Penny.

Trevor handed Penny her mug.

"Thanks."

He handed Derek his.

Derek took a sip.

Then looked at Trevor.

"Where's yours?"

"I didn't make one."

"Why not?"

"I thought there were only two of us."

Penny looked at him.

Trevor looked at Penny.

Then at Derek.

"Oh."

Derek sighed.

"Trevor."

"Yes?"

"That's Penny."

"I know."

"My cousin."

"I know."

"She's been here for approximately ninety seconds."

"Yes."

"And yet somehow you've already forgotten that she exists."

"I haven't forgotten she exists."

"You made two cups of tea."

"I thought you didn't want one."

"I have one."

Trevor stared at Derek's mug.

"Right."

Penny smiled.

"Don't worry. He's usually this bright."

"I'm sitting right here."

"We know."

Derek turned back to the computer.

There was something bothering him.

Not the murder.

The murder was, in some respects, straightforward.

The date was the problem.

He clicked the attachment.

A photograph appeared.

Derek stopped breathing for a moment.

Trevor noticed.

"What?"

Derek turned the monitor toward them.

The photograph showed the Bureau office.

Not a similar office.

Not a room that resembled it.

The actual room.

The same battered desk.

The same green filing cabinet.

The same window overlooking the street.

The same framed photograph of Derek's grandfather standing beside a Morris Minor in 1964.

And on the floor, face-down beside the desk, was Derek.

There was blood on the back of his shirt.

Trevor stared.

Penny leaned closer.

"That's you."

"Yes."

"You look dead."

"I should hope so."

"Why?"

"Because it would be a very poor photograph if I didn't."

Trevor zoomed in.

"There's something on the desk."

Derek looked.

A small brass railway clock stood beside the computer.

Derek frowned.

"I don't own that."

Penny looked at him.

"Yes, you do."

"No."

"You bought it last Thursday."

"I did?"

"You said it was historically significant."

"What is it?"

"An old railway clock."

Derek looked again.

The clock showed 11:03.

He looked at the clock on his own desk.

9:21.

He looked at his computer.

9:21.

His phone.

9:21.

The wall clock.

9:21.

He looked back at the photograph.

11:03.

Derek leaned back.

"Interesting."

Penny noticed.

"What?"

"That photograph wasn't taken tomorrow."

Trevor frowned.

"Then when was it taken?"

Derek pointed.

"The clock says eleven-oh-three."

"Yes."

"Tomorrow's murder is at eleven-oh-three."

"Yes."

"And the photograph was apparently taken at the moment of my death."

"Yes."

Trevor waited.

Derek looked at him.

Trevor looked back.

"So?"

Derek sighed.

"Trevor, if you are going to work here, you really must learn to recognize the moment when a mystery is standing on the table waving its arms."

Trevor looked at the photograph.

"Oh."

Penny smiled.

"There he is."

Trevor leaned closer.

"Hang on."

"What?"

"If that's a photograph of tomorrow—"

"It's not."

"Right."

"If it's a photograph taken tomorrow, then why does the clock show eleven-oh-three?"

"Because that's when Derek dies."

"But the clock here says nine-twenty-one."

"Yes."

"So the photograph was taken two hours later."

"Yes."

Trevor looked pleased.

"That's what I said."

"No," said Penny. "It isn't."

Trevor's expression collapsed.

"Oh."

Derek stood and walked to the window.

Outside, London was doing what London did best.

Being grey.

Being expensive.

Being mildly annoyed.

A bus stopped.

Another bus stopped behind it.

Neither seemed to know why.

Derek watched the traffic.

Then he said:

"Do you know what the irritating thing about this is?"

Penny looked up.

"Your impending murder?"

"Other than that."

"Your Bureau?"

"Also other than that."

Trevor thought.

"Your cousin?"

Penny looked at him.

Trevor immediately reconsidered his life choices.

Derek smiled.

"No."

He pointed toward the street.

"Everyone assumes time is the same everywhere."

Trevor frowned.

"Isn't it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because Alfred Weinstein ruined that particular assumption."

Trevor nodded.

"Special Relativity."

"Yes."

"The Weinstein."

"There aren't many of them."

Penny sat down.

"Explain."

Derek picked up a marker and went to the whiteboard.

"This is basic Special Relativity."

He drew a railway track.

Then a train.

"Suppose you're standing on a platform and a train passes you at a very high speed."

"How high?"

"High enough that relativity becomes interesting."

"That's not a unit."

"It's sufficient for the moment."

He drew two lightning bolts.

"Lightning strikes the front and rear of the train simultaneously."

Penny nodded.

Trevor watched.

"An observer standing on the platform, exactly halfway between the two strikes, sees the flashes arrive simultaneously."

"Right."

"Now imagine someone sitting exactly halfway between the front and rear of the train."

"Also right."

"But the train is moving."

"Yes."

"So the passenger is moving toward the flash at the front and away from the flash at the back."

Trevor nodded.

"The front flash reaches him first."

"Exactly."

Trevor frowned.

"But the lightning still struck at the same time."

Derek smiled.

"According to whom?"

Trevor stared.

Penny leaned forward.

"Ah."

Derek pointed at her.

"Exactly."

"Different observers can disagree about whether two separated events happened at the same time."

"Without either observer being wrong."

Trevor looked at the photograph.

"So there's no universal 'now'."

"Not in Special Relativity."

Derek put the marker down.

"And that matters here."

Penny looked at the photograph.

"Because the murder is an event."

"Yes."

"And the timestamp is an observation of that event."

"Yes."

"So we need to know the reference frame."

Derek nodded.

"Now you're thinking like a detective."

Trevor raised a finger.

"Wouldn't the photograph itself give us the frame?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because a photograph records light."

"So?"

"Light takes time to reach the camera."

Trevor stopped.

Derek could see the thought arriving.

"Ah."

"Exactly."

Penny smiled.

"So even the photograph isn't necessarily showing us what happened when we think it happened."

"Correct."

Derek sat down.

"And that's where things become interesting."

He enlarged the photograph.

There was something in the lower-right corner.

A piece of paper.

He zoomed in.

It contained three lines.

11:03 — DEATH

11:17 — DEPARTURE

12:04 — ARRIVAL

Below them:

DO NOT TRUST THE CLOCKS.

Penny read it.

"That's ominous."

"Very."

Trevor leaned over.

"Could 'departure' mean the train?"

"Perhaps."

"Arrival?"

"Perhaps."

"Death?"

"Almost certainly me."

Trevor looked uncomfortable.

"You're taking that rather calmly."

Derek shrugged.

"At the moment, I'm alive."

Penny looked at him.

"That's a surprisingly weak argument."

"It has served me well."

Derek zoomed in again.

The railway clock in the photograph wasn't showing exactly 11:03.

It showed 11:03:17.

Derek stared.

He looked at his own clock.

9:27:17.

He checked his phone.

9:27:17.

The wall clock.

9:27:17.

Penny noticed.

"What?"

Derek pointed.

"Seventeen seconds."

Trevor looked.

"Same."

Derek nodded.

"That's interesting."

Penny leaned closer.

"Why?"

"Because if this were simply a badly set clock, there's no reason for the seconds to line up."

Trevor thought about that.

"Could someone have set it deliberately?"

"Yes."

"Then it's a clue."

"Exactly."

Derek opened the Bureau's internal records.

The system showed no record of the brass clock.

No purchase.

No inventory entry.

No photograph.

No mention of it.

Derek searched the building's security footage.

Nothing.

He checked the office camera.

At 9:16, the desk was empty.

At 9:17, the desk was empty.

At 9:18, the desk was empty.

At 9:19, the desk was empty.

At 9:20, the brass clock appeared.

Trevor stared.

"How?"

Derek replayed it.

The clock simply appeared.

There was no hand.

No person.

No shadow.

One frame: nothing.

Next frame: clock.

Penny looked at Derek.

"That's not normal."

"No."

"Could the camera have glitched?"

"Yes."

"Did it?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because the clock casts a shadow."

Penny looked again.

It did.

The shadow moved normally as the light from the window changed.

Derek replayed the footage.

The clock existed.

Then it didn't.

Then it existed again.

Trevor looked increasingly unhappy.

"Could someone have edited the footage?"

"Yes."

"Could they have edited the photograph?"

"Yes."

"So how do we know anything is real?"

Derek looked at him.

"Now you're asking the right question."

Trevor didn't look reassured.

Derek opened another file.

"Let's start with the clocks."

He took the brass clock from the photograph and compared its mechanism with the Bureau's clocks.

They were different.

He compared the hands.

Different.

He compared the second hand.

Identical.

Seventeen seconds.

Derek frowned.

Penny noticed.

"Another problem?"

"Yes."

"How many are we up to?"

"Seven."

Trevor raised his eyebrows.

"Seven?"

"At least."

Derek turned the photograph upside down.

There was a small number written on the back.

1047.

He looked at the case database.

Case 1047 did not exist.

He searched deleted cases.

Nothing.

He searched archived cases.

Nothing.

He searched future-dated cases.

Nothing.

Then the computer made a noise.

A soft chime.

A new case appeared.

CASE 1047.

CLIENT: DEREK GENT

STATUS: ACTIVE

Derek stared.

Penny leaned over.

"What does it say?"

Derek scrolled.

The case contained only three pieces of information.

Client: Derek Gent.

Subject: Murder.

Date: Wednesday.

Time: 11:03:17.

Derek clicked "Assigned Investigator."

The field was blank.

He clicked "Perpetrator."

Blank.

He clicked "Victim."

Derek Gent.

He clicked "Case Notes."

There was one note.

Written in Derek's own handwriting.

DO NOT LET TREVOR NEAR THE CLOCK.

Trevor read it.

"I feel slightly insulted."

Derek looked at him.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

"I was."

Penny smiled.

"This is becoming quite good."

Derek stared at the screen.

"Something is using the Bureau."

"What?"

"Someone—or something—is creating cases inside our system."

"From where?"

"I don't know."

"Can we trace it?"

Derek checked.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because the originating address is..."

He stopped.

"What?"

Derek turned the monitor toward them.

The address read:

LOCALHOST.

Trevor frowned.

"That's the computer."

"Yes."

"So someone is doing it from here."

"Apparently."

Penny looked around the room.

There were three people in it.

Derek.

Trevor.

Penny.

Nobody else.

She folded her arms.

"Well."

Trevor looked at her.

"What?"

"You've got an interesting problem."

"Which one?"

"One of us is lying."

Derek looked at the three chairs.

Then the clock.

Then the computer.

"Perhaps."

Penny smiled.

"Or one of us hasn't done it yet."

Derek looked at her.

The office suddenly felt colder.

Trevor swallowed.

"You mean..."

Derek didn't answer.

The computer chimed again.

All three looked at the screen.

A new message appeared.

CASE UPDATE.

PERPETRATOR IDENTIFIED.

Derek opened it.

The name appeared.

TREVOR BOLTZMAN.

Trevor stared.

"What?"

Derek looked at him.

Trevor looked at Penny.

Penny looked at Trevor.

"That wasn't me."

"I know," Derek said.

"How do you know?"

"Because you're still here."

Trevor blinked.

"That's your evidence?"

"For the moment."

Penny walked to the window.

"Then who is framing him?"

Derek looked at the clock.

9:31:17.

He looked at the photograph.

11:03:17.

He looked at the case file.

Then he looked at Trevor.

"Maybe nobody."

Trevor frowned.

"What does that mean?"

Derek walked to the whiteboard.

He wrote:

EVENT

Then:

OBSERVER

Then:

FRAME

Then:

TIME

He circled the first three.

"Before we worry about who did it, we need to know what actually happened."

Trevor nodded.

"Right."

Penny looked at him.

"Do you understand?"

Trevor thought.

"I think so."

"Good."

Derek picked up his coat.

"Then we're going to need to leave."

"Where?"

Derek looked at the photograph.

"The clock."

Penny frowned.

"The clock?"

"The brass clock."

"What about it?"

"It doesn't belong here."

"So?"

"So whoever put it here expected us to find it."

Trevor stood.

"Where are we going?"

Derek held up the photograph.

"To the place where it came from."

Penny grabbed her coat.

"And where is that?"

Derek turned the photograph over.

On the back, beneath the number 1047, another line had appeared.

LONDON TERMINUS

PLATFORM 7

11:17

Penny read it.

"That's tomorrow."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor looked at the photograph.

"And that's after you're dead."

"Apparently."

"So how are we supposed to investigate it?"

Derek smiled.

"We'll have to be there."

Trevor looked worried.

"Tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"At eleven seventeen?"

"Yes."

"After you're murdered?"

"Presumably."

Trevor thought for a moment.

"How do we know we'll get there?"

Derek opened the door.

"Because someone has already told us that we will."

Penny followed him.

Trevor remained where he was.

"Derek?"

"Yes?"

"What if the message is wrong?"

Derek looked back.

"Then we'll have wasted a perfectly good morning."

"And if it's right?"

Derek smiled.

"Then I suppose I'll have to solve my own murder."

They stepped into the corridor.

The door closed behind them.

Inside the empty office, the brass clock appeared on Derek's desk.

Its second hand moved.

One.

Two.

Three.

It reached seventeen.

Stopped.

Then continued.

But now the clock was showing 11:03.

And outside the office, somewhere far away, a train whistle sounded.

There was no railway station anywhere near the building.

The whistle sounded again.

Then a third time.

The Bureau's computer switched itself on.

A final message appeared.

CASE 1047

STATUS: ACTIVE

VICTIM: DEREK GENT

PERPETRATOR: TREVOR BOLTZMAN

WITNESS: PENNY GENT

PHYSICIST: ALFRED WEINSTEIN

REFERENCE FRAME: UNKNOWN

And beneath it, in smaller letters:

DO NOT LET WEINSTEIN GET TO THE STATION FIRST.

The message disappeared.

The screen went black.

Then the computer displayed the date.

Wednesday.

11:03.

17 seconds.

The office was empty.

For the moment.
