A satirical science-comedy about quantum physics, British bureaucracy, and a Ministry that tries to simplify reality with forms.

What happens when a government department tries to make quantum mechanics behave like paperwork?

Lolly Wren has a perfectly ordinary job at the Ministry of Eventualities—until reality starts filing complaints.

As a Transit Reconciliation Officer, Third Grade (ten years in electronics; eleven minutes as a working physicist), Lolly is accustomed to minor discrepancies: people arriving before they have decided to leave, destinations that refuse to stay put, and forms that appear to have been completed tomorrow.

Then things get worse.

A bureaucratic initiative designed to simplify reality begins producing contradictory states, unstable identities, impossible records—and a black hole in regional infrastructure that is quietly evaporating on a municipal timetable.

The Ministry's solution is, naturally, more paperwork.

But Lolly begins to suspect that the problem isn't faulty administration. The Ministry has confused measurement with authority, and assumed that if reality can be assigned a category, reality must obey it.

With a physicist who is rarely reassured, an auditor who refuses impossible certificates, a courier who knows everyone, an engineer who finds the actual switch, and Mrs Elspeth Chain—whose house has become decidedly less like itself—Lolly must work out what the Ministry has done, what can honestly be recovered, and what must be named as gone.

Schrödinger's Paperwork is a satirical, absurdist novel about bureaucracy, uncertainty, information, and the inconvenient habit reality has of refusing to cooperate.

And then there is the physics.

Each of the eighteen chapters is paired with an optional lecture in the Appendix — a real class on what the universe is doing, taught to the standard that if you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it yet. Follow the link at the chapter end; each lecture links back. You can enjoy the novel alone—or take the course and learn superposition, measurement, entanglement, uncertainty, decoherence, Bell's theorem, black-hole information, error correction, thermodynamics, and why there is no lid.

Read it as a novel.
Read it as a science adventure.
Or do both.

Just don't ask the Ministry to explain where your paperwork has gone.
It may not have happened yet.
