2639|A Word on Who Is Who
2640|This novel gives away real physicists twice over, by two entirely different methods.
2641|The first is a joke told out loud. Characters in this book say the names of real scientists without the faintest idea who they are. A woman whose crockery has been administratively altered will demand to know who Hawking is, and be told, correctly, that he is a physicist and not a department. That one is free.
2642|The second method is quieter, and if you enjoy working things out before you are told, stop reading this page now and come back when you have finished the book. Nothing here spoils the plot. It does spoil a run of small pleasures.
2643|Still here?
2644|The consultants are the greatest physicists of the twentieth century, lightly disguised, mildly inconvenienced, and required to explain themselves to a Third Grade officer with a green notebook. They are not caricatures of the real people and their private lives here are wholly invented — but their physics is theirs.
2646|No disrespect is intended to any of them, living or dead. All of them did rather more for human understanding than anyone in this book manages, and not one of them, so far as the author has been able to establish, was ever required to complete a Form 4C in triplicate.
2647|A Note on the Author
2648|Q.E.D. is a pseudonym.
2649|It stands, depending on whom you ask, for quod erat demonstrandum — “which was to be demonstrated,” the phrase generations of mathematicians have written at the foot of a proof to mean, more or less, there, I told you so — or for quantum electrodynamics, the theory that finally taught physics to be honest about how thoroughly strange light and matter actually are.
2650|Both readings are correct. That was rather the point of the book you have just finished.
2651|The name behind the pseudonym is Lothar J. Musiol.
2652|Mr Musiol spent more than forty years in the electronics industry: the kind of career that leaves a person fluent in oscillators, tolerances, failure modes, and the precise moment at which an engineering deadline becomes a philosophical position. Before that he studied physics seriously enough to know exactly how much of it he is simplifying here for comic effect, and exactly how much he is not. Readers who found the bra-ket notation in Lolly Wren’s green notebook oddly natural rather than decorative may draw their own conclusions.
2653|He wrote Schrödinger’s Paperwork the way Lolly Wren does most things — carefully, in the margins of a life spent elsewhere, and with the slowly hardening suspicion that the paperwork was more interesting than anyone had given it credit for.
2654|He is German by birth and writes in English by choice, which may explain the book’s affection for the precise word in the wrong register.
2655|About the Series
2656|Schrödinger’s Paperwork is Book 1 of Lolly Wren’s Curious Science Adventures, a series in which large institutions attempt to administer physics, and physics declines to cooperate.
2657|Each book stands alone. Each teaches its own set of ideas honestly, in the same two-layer form: a comic novel that never stops to lecture, and clearly separated Physics Notes for readers who want the real science and are prepared to be told when it is unsettled.
2658|A second volume exists, concerning a different Ministry, in a different country, with a rather different arrangement of collapsing certainties — for readers who did not, in the event, get enough forms the first time.
2659|If this made you curious
2660|None of the physics in this book was invented for the joke. If you would like to go further than a Ministry can take you, three places are worth the trip: Sean Carroll’s Something Deeply Hidden takes many-worlds seriously and argues for it properly; Carlo Rovelli’s Helgoland makes the case for relational quantum mechanics in prose good enough to make you forgive him for being persuasive. And if you want the same territory covered by the same author’s non-fiction companion, at considerably greater length and with rather less bureaucracy, The Quantum World goes much further into most of the questions this book only had time to dramatise.
2661|Whichever you pick, ask the question this book kept asking — not “does it feel right,” but “what does the theory predict, and did the experiment agree.” That much, at least, was never a joke.
2662|If you enjoyed this book
2663|Reviews are how small books find readers. If Schrödinger’s Paperwork made you laugh, or taught you something you had been told was impossible to understand, a few honest sentences on the store page will do more good than the author can do alone.
2664|And if it made you look at some official letter differently — at the sentence in it that quietly moves a difficulty somewhere you cannot see — then the book has done its actual work, and you may consider the notebook passed on.
2665|There is no lid.
