Once, a tutor had told her that nature did not take the shortest way between two events: it took every way there was, added them up, each weighted by a small rotating arrow, and only the sum was ever measured. “I’m not going to explain it so you understand it,” he’d remarked, not unkindly. “I’m going to explain it the way we calculate it. It works regardless.” She suspected, this morning, he had also been describing the civil service.

--- 904 ---
Fainrose adjusted the scale again. The descending curve sharpened at its lower end, as though modestly reluctant to admit what came next. “There is a vulgar public explanation,” she continued, “involving particle pairs at the horizon, one escaping while the other falls in. It is not wholly useless, but it encourages exactly the wrong sort of confidence: people picture two little billiard balls and feel they have understood something. The cleaner statement, and the one that is actually computed, is that a quantum field does not sit politely empty in curved spacetime; you sum every history the field could have and weight each by its own small rotating arrow, and what comes out at infinity is thermal. Mr Feynman built an entire, perfectly serious theory out of adding up arrows for paths no sensible person would take, and it kept being right, which he found nearly as irritating as I do. Once you accept that the bookkeeping is the physics, the rest is book-keeping with terrible implications.”

--- 905 ---
Lolly opened the green notebook. “Thermal,” she noted aloud while writing. “So not random debris. Not leakage. Radiation with a temperature.”
“Quite.”
“And if it radiates, it loses energy.”
“Yes.”
“And since mass and energy—”
“Are old friends,” Fainrose finished.

--- 1103 ---
“When they measured your house,” Fainrose began, “in order to save it, they had to ask it questions.”
“They asked a great many.”
“Yes. And each question returned an answer. Blue wallpaper. One duvet. Husband present. Sister absent. All perfectly recordable. All faithfully written down.” She tapped the manifest. “That is what is in the vault. Answers.”
“And that is not the house.”
“That is not the house. The house was also the relations between the answers: the way the sister depended on the kitchen, and the kitchen on the route to the shops, and the route on forty years of walking it.”

--- 1104 ---
“Those relations have a name,” Fainrose went on, “and it is not a flattering one to a filing system. Phase. Picture it the way a better physicist than anyone in this building once did: every possible way your questionnaire could have gone carries its own small rotating arrow, and nothing becomes real until you add the arrows tip to tail and see what is left standing. Length tells you how likely an outcome is. Angle (phase) tells you whether two possibilities reinforce one another or cancel out on the way to becoming a house. Ask for a length and you get an honest number. Ask for an angle and the arrow stops spinning, because asking has forced it to stand still. I told you once already that the bookkeeping is the physics. That is what your vault could not put in a column, Mr Voller. Not the answer. The angle it was standing at.”

--- 1105 ---
Below them, on cue, the sound did not repeat. The quiet that followed it was the least reassuring thing Lolly had heard so far.

--- 1406 ---
Fainrose went very still. “That’s not new,” she considered slowly. “That’s Wigner’s friend, dressed in a filing cabinet.”
Eilstein inclined his head, almost approving. “Dressed in a filing cabinet. Yes. I may steal that.”
“Then how does anyone calculate what’s real,” Lolly asked, “if there’s no moment where it happens?”

--- 1407 ---
Eilstein looked pleased to be asked. “Now you are asking the interesting question instead of the frightening one. There is a way. A younger man worked it out, after they buried the version of me you are looking at. He does not ask what happened; he asks what could have happened (every route), gives each one a small turning arrow, and adds them up to see where they point. It works to more decimal places than anything else I have seen tried, and I cannot tell you why. I will not explain it so you understand it: I do not understand it that way myself. I can only tell you it calculates.”

--- 1408 ---
For exactly one word his fluency snagged — gone before anyone could be sure they’d heard it — as though even he found the next sentence expensive to produce.

--- 1808 ---
Gideon looked at the clock. “We should probably stop debating whether we’re ready.”
Beatrix glanced at him. “Why?”
“Because the universe doesn’t appear to have asked whether we are.”

--- 1809 ---
It was that same afternoon, with the plan on the Board’s desk and not a scanner yet switched off, that Mrs Elspeth Chain came to the annex and asked to see Fainrose alone, which she had not done before.

--- 1810 ---
While she waited, Lolly stood at the tall window at the end of the corridor, which nobody had cleaned since the Initiative began and which nonetheless gave, unexpectedly, the best view in the building. Somewhere among the wet slate roofs and scaffolding sat one of the private garden squares that turned up all over that part of London: a rectangle of real grass and real trees, locked behind black railings, key-holders only, visible to absolutely anyone and entered by almost no one. She had never worked out who decided which eleven households got a key and which got a view of other people’s key. It was beginning to seem like the only question the Ministry had ever actually been answering.

--- 1811 ---
A barrier, she thought, was only ever a classical promise. Quantum mechanically nothing was ever quite locked — a particle without the energy to clear a wall could still, occasionally, arrive on the other side of it anyway, for no reason a key would recognise. She wondered, not for the first time, whether the eleven households had ever noticed how much of the grass on their side had simply leaked through from the pavement.

--- 1301 ---
“Then why did it dither?”

--- 1302 ---
“Because someone built a filter.” Now she turned to look at Lolly, and there was something close to fury in it, banked low. “Somewhere in that paperwork is a device (probably a receiver mast, modest and municipal) coupled to the observation quorum. It doesn’t touch the moon. It touches the town: damping the background contact that would decohere Outer Fenwick’s sky, releasing it again only once the forms say the humans have earned a definite one. Out of every shape a superposition can fall into, decoherence leaves only the dull, stable ones standing (einselection) and a moon that just hangs there being a moon is a pointer state: an answerthat survives being asked the same question by everything, constantly. This Ministry built a machine to interrupt that, so forty-one signatures could be sold back as the cure.”

--- 570 ---
Beatrix looked at her properly for the first time since the hallway. “Explain.”

--- 571 ---
Lolly set the tea caddy down carefully, buying herself a second to find the words. “Left alone, it settles. Not because anyone decides, because everything around it keeps bumping into it, gently, from every direction, until only one version survives the bumping. Decoherence, not decision.” “Whoever did this to your house didn’t let it finish. They reached in and fixed a pointer state before the environment had agreed on one for itself. It’s still trying to catch up to its own decision.”

--- 2028 ---
Somewhere in the building, a filing cabinet opened by itself and accepted a document that had not yet been written: collapse, paperwork first and reason afterward — the Ministry’s preferred order of operations.

--- 2029 ---
Somewhere else, a physicist would eventually explain why: reality had summed every route the form could take, weighted with small rotating arrows, and trusted the arithmetic over the intuition — not explained so anyone could follow it, only calculated, because that happened to work.
