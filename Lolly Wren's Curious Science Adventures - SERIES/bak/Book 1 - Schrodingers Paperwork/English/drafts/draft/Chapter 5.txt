# Chapter 5

## The Apple Problem

Alfred Weinstein had never entirely trusted apples.

This was not because of any defect in the fruit.

Apples were perfectly respectable.

They were roundish, nutritious, reasonably portable and, unlike pears, rarely gave the impression that they were about to collapse under their own moral burden.

His distrust concerned their reputation.

For centuries, apples had been given far too much credit.

One particular apple, in particular, had been blamed for an astonishing amount of physics.

Weinstein was thinking about it on the train.

He was on his way back to Munich after the meeting with Julius Barbarian, although "on his way back" was perhaps an unnecessarily confident description of the situation.

The train had stopped.

It had stopped between two stations.

Nobody seemed particularly concerned.

The conductor had announced that there was a "minor delay due to circumstances."

Weinstein had spent enough time on trains to know that "circumstances" meant absolutely anything.

He sat by the window.

Across from him, a woman was reading a novel.

Beside her, a man was asleep.

Two rows ahead, a child was eating an apple.

Weinstein watched the child.

The child took a bite.

Then another.

Then, apparently deciding that the apple had become philosophically unacceptable, dropped it.

It fell to the floor.

Weinstein watched it.

The apple rolled beneath the seat.

He stared at the space where it had disappeared.

Then he smiled.

Newton.

He thought of Newton's famous apple.

The story was almost certainly too neat.

Science rarely arrived with such excellent stage management.

One could imagine the scene.

Newton sits under a tree.

An apple falls.

Newton looks at it.

Newton thinks about gravity.

Physics is transformed.

It was a charming story.

It was also misleading.

The apple had not explained gravity.

It had merely demonstrated that something fell.

The really interesting question was why.

Weinstein leaned back.

Why did the apple fall?

Everyone knew the answer.

Gravity.

But that was not an answer.

It was a name.

He had spent most of his life telling physicists not to confuse naming a phenomenon with explaining it.

Newton had described gravity extraordinarily well.

The inverse-square law worked.

The equations worked.

The predictions worked.

The planets obeyed them.

The Moon obeyed them.

The apple obeyed them.

Everything seemed to obey them.

Then General Relativity had come along and changed the question.

The apple was no longer simply being pulled downward by a force.

It was following a geodesic through curved spacetime.

Weinstein smiled.

The poor apple had been promoted from fruit to geometry.

The train moved.

Then stopped again.

Weinstein sighed.

He looked out the window.

Nothing.

Then, very suddenly, he had an idea.

It was not a complete idea.

It was not even a particularly respectable idea.

It was the sort of idea that arrived without knocking, sat down in the middle of one's mind and refused to explain why it was there.

Weinstein sat upright.

The apple.

Not the falling.

The *falling together*.

He took out his notebook.

He drew the Earth.

Then the apple.

Then an arrow.

He stopped.

Crossed it out.

Drew the Earth.

Then the apple.

Then two arrows.

One pointing from the apple toward the Earth.

One pointing from the Earth toward the apple.

He stared at them.

"Of course."

The woman across the aisle looked up.

Weinstein noticed.

"Sorry."

She returned to her book.

He continued.

Newton's description had made it natural to think of the Earth as pulling the apple.

But General Relativity said something more subtle.

The apple and the Earth were both responding to the geometry.

The apple fell.

The Earth moved too.

Tiny amount.

Almost impossibly tiny.

But not zero.

Weinstein wrote:

THE APPLE DOES NOT FALL TOWARD THE EARTH.

He stopped.

That was not quite right.

He crossed it out.

THE EARTH AND THE APPLE FALL TOWARD EACH OTHER.

He looked at it.

Better.

Then another thought struck him.

If the apple and the Earth were both following the geometry, what exactly determined the geometry?

Mass and energy.

Fine.

But there was something else.

The equivalence principle.

A freely falling observer did not feel gravity locally.

That was the heart of the matter.

An apple falling from a tree was not being dragged through space by an invisible rope.

It was freely falling.

And the tree was preventing it from doing so.

Weinstein stopped.

He looked at the words.

The tree was preventing the apple from following its natural path.

He smiled.

"That's rather good."

The woman looked up again.

This time she decided not to ask.

Weinstein turned the page.

He drew a tree.

Then the apple.

Then the ground.

Then he drew the same apple in a lift.

He imagined the lift accelerating upward in empty space.

The apple would appear to fall.

Yet there would be no gravitational field.

Then he imagined the lift sitting on Earth.

The apple would fall again.

Locally, the two situations could be indistinguishable.

That was the equivalence principle.

Weinstein knew this.

Everyone in the Bureau knew this.

So why had the thought bothered him?

He looked at his drawing again.

Because the apple was falling toward something.

And in the accelerating lift there was no Earth.

The apparent downward direction was created by the acceleration.

Gravity could be imitated by acceleration.

But perhaps the deeper question was the reverse.

Could acceleration be understood as geometry in the same way gravity was?

Yes.

That was already part of General Relativity.

So why did the idea feel new?

Weinstein tapped his pencil against the notebook.

Then he saw it.

Not the answer.

The question.

The question was not:

Why does the apple fall?

The question was:

**Why does the apple have the trajectory it does?**

He sat back.

That was better.

Much better.

The trajectory of a freely falling object was determined by spacetime geometry.

But the geometry was determined by the distribution of matter and energy.

So the apple was both responding to the geometry and, in principle, contributing to it.

The apple affected the spacetime through which it moved.

Tiny amount.

But real.

The Earth affected the apple.

The apple affected the Earth.

The Earth affected spacetime.

The apple affected spacetime.

Everything was interacting with everything else.

Weinstein stared at the page.

"That's going to annoy someone."

The man sleeping beside the woman opened one eye.

"What?"

"Nothing."

The man closed his eye again.

Weinstein wrote:

ASK DEREK.

Then underneath:

INVESTIGATE THE APPLE.

He stopped.

That sounded ridiculous.

He crossed out APPLE.

Then wrote:

INVESTIGATE THE FALLING BODY.

He looked at it.

Too clinical.

He crossed out the whole line.

He wrote:

WHY DOES THE APPLE FALL?

He smiled.

That was better.

He took out his phone.

He called Derek.

Derek answered on the fourth ring.

"Yes?"

"Alfred."

"Where are you?"

"On a train."

"Which one?"

"The Munich one."

Derek paused.

"That's not terribly specific."

"I know."

"Are you going somewhere?"

"Apparently."

"Do you know where?"

"Munich."

"You're already in Munich."

"I know."

There was silence.

Then Derek said:

"Has Julius Barbarian finally broken you?"

"No."

"Good."

"I have a question."

"Go on."

"Why does an apple fall?"

Derek was silent.

Then:

"Are you serious?"

"Perfectly."

"Gravity."

"No."

Derek sighed.

"Don't start."

"I'm not starting anything."

"You've just asked me why an apple falls."

"Yes."

"And you're Alfred Weinstein."

"Yes."

"The inventor of General Relativity."

"Yes."

"The theory that explains why it falls."

"Precisely."

"Then why are you asking me?"

"Because I think the question is wrong."

Derek sat down.

"All right."

Penny was sitting nearby.

She looked over.

Derek put the phone on speaker.

"Go on."

Weinstein said:

"Suppose an apple is freely falling."

"Yes."

"Is the apple accelerating?"

"In the Newtonian sense, yes."

"And in General Relativity?"

"It is following a geodesic."

"Exactly."

"So locally, the apple is not experiencing a force."

"Correct."

"Then why does it appear to accelerate toward the Earth?"

Derek thought.

"Because we are observing it from a non-free-falling frame."

"Yes."

"And the Earth's surface is preventing us from following our own geodesic."

"Yes."

Penny nodded.

"That settles the basic point."

Weinstein smiled.

"Yes."

Derek said:

"But that's not the question."

"No."

"What is?"

Weinstein looked out of the window.

"The apple and the Earth are both moving."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

"The apple moves toward the Earth."

"Yes."

"The Earth moves toward the apple."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Derek paused.

Penny looked at him.

Derek said:

"Because of their mutual gravitational interaction."

Weinstein said nothing.

Derek smiled.

"That's the Newtonian answer."

"Exactly."

"So you want the General Relativistic answer."

"Yes."

Derek leaned back.

"The geometry."

"Yes."

"The stress-energy determines the geometry."

"Yes."

"And the geometry determines the geodesics."

"Yes."

"The apple follows the geodesic."

"Yes."

Derek paused.

"Well, that's settled."

Weinstein laughed.

"No."

Derek smiled.

"You've just said yes to everything."

"I know."

"Then what isn't settled?"

Weinstein looked at his notebook.

"The apple."

Penny leaned toward the phone.

"What about it?"

"Why that apple?"

Derek frowned.

"Why that particular apple?"

"Yes."

"Why does it matter which apple?"

"That's what I don't know."

Penny smiled.

"Now that's interesting."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

Weinstein continued.

"The Bureau has been investigating events that appear to depend on which frame we use. Julius Barbarian has been arguing that time may not be fundamental. We have clocks behaving differently. And now I am thinking about an apple."

Derek said:

"That sounds like a normal Thursday."

"It's Wednesday."

"Right."

Weinstein ignored that.

"Suppose the apple falls."

"Yes."

"Suppose we reverse the process."

Derek paused.

"Reverse it?"

"Imagine reconstructing the trajectory."

"From the ground upward?"

"Yes."

"We'd have to know its position and velocity."

"Yes."

"And the gravitational field."

"Yes."

"And the geometry."

"Yes."

"And the matter distribution."

"Yes."

Weinstein said:

"Exactly."

Derek waited.

Then he understood.

"We'd need the entire local configuration."

"Yes."

Penny looked at Derek.

"So the apple's trajectory contains information about the geometry."

"Yes."

"And the geometry contains information about the matter."

"Yes."

"And therefore the falling apple is effectively telling us something about its environment."

"Exactly."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Weinstein laughed.

"No."

"What now?"

"Because I've just had another thought."

"Of course you have."

"The Bureau's clocks."

Derek straightened.

"What about them?"

"They are following worldlines."

"Yes."

"They're measuring proper time."

"Yes."

"They are effectively sampling spacetime along those worldlines."

"Yes."

"And if their readings are anomalous..."

"Then the geometry is anomalous."

"Perhaps."

Derek frowned.

"Or the clocks are."

"Yes."

"But we've already ruled out ordinary clock failure."

"Yes."

"So..."

Weinstein paused.

"Investigate the trajectories."

Derek stared at the phone.

"The trajectories of what?"

"Everything."

"Everything?"

"Start with the clocks."

Derek looked at Penny.

Penny nodded.

"That actually makes sense."

Derek said:

"We've been treating the clocks as stationary measuring devices."

"Yes."

"Perhaps we should treat them as physical objects following worldlines."

"Exactly."

Trevor appeared in the doorway.

He had clearly been listening.

"That means the clocks themselves are part of the experiment."

Derek looked at him.

"Yes."

Trevor nodded.

"Well, that's settled."

Derek smiled.

"See? He's learning."

Trevor looked pleased.

Then asked:

"What's a worldline?"

Penny laughed.

Derek said:

"Come in."

Trevor did.

Derek explained:

"In spacetime, an object's history can be represented as a line. Not a line through ordinary space, but through spacetime. If the object moves, its position changes with time. The complete history forms its worldline."

Trevor nodded.

"And a clock follows one."

"Yes."

"So the clock doesn't merely tell us time."

"Correct."

"It gives us a physical record along its worldline."

"Exactly."

Trevor looked at the brass clock.

"Then if the clock behaves strangely, we should ask whether its worldline is strange."

Derek smiled.

"Now you're ahead of me."

Trevor nodded.

"That happens occasionally."

Penny looked at him.

"Don't overdo it."

Trevor smiled.

"I won't."

Derek returned to the phone.

"Alfred, what exactly do you want us to investigate?"

Weinstein looked down at his notebook.

"The apple."

Derek laughed.

"You've come all this way in physics to send me after an apple."

"No."

"Then what?"

"The apple is the model."

"For what?"

"Everything."

Derek became serious.

Weinstein continued.

"If a freely falling body is following a geodesic, then its trajectory is telling us about the geometry. If we can observe enough trajectories, we can reconstruct information about that geometry."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

"And if the Bureau is experiencing an unusual spacetime effect, we should stop staring at the clocks."

"We should watch what moves around them."

"Exactly."

Penny smiled.

"That's actually rather elegant."

Weinstein said:

"Thank you."

Derek asked:

"Do you have a particular trajectory in mind?"

"Yes."

"Whose?"

Weinstein looked out the train window.

A landscape passed.

Trees.

Fields.

Buildings.

Everything apparently moving.

Or perhaps the train was moving.

Or perhaps both.

He smiled.

"Mine."

Derek frowned.

"What?"

"I want you to investigate my trajectory."

"Your worldline?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because something happened in Munich."

"What?"

"My clock stopped."

"We know."

"No."

Weinstein's voice became quieter.

"It stopped before the clocks in London stopped."

Derek froze.

Penny looked at him.

Trevor stopped moving.

Derek said:

"When?"

"Eleven sixteen."

Derek stared at the brass clock.

"But ours stopped at eleven seventeen."

"Yes."

"So there's a one-minute difference."

"Exactly."

Derek thought.

"That could be ordinary synchronization."

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because I wasn't where you think I was."

Derek frowned.

"What do you mean?"

"I was on the train."

Derek stared.

"What train?"

"The train to Munich."

"You were going to Munich."

"Yes."

"You were already in Munich."

"Yes."

Derek closed his eyes.

"Alfred."

"Yes?"

"You were on a train going to Munich while you were in Munich."

"Yes."

"How?"

"I don't know."

Penny smiled.

"That's rather good."

Derek looked at her.

"Is it?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because now we have a genuine problem."

Derek returned to the phone.

"Alfred, where are you now?"

There was a pause.

"On the train."

"Which train?"

"The train to Munich."

Derek frowned.

"But you're in Munich."

"Apparently."

Trevor looked at Penny.

"Could he be in two reference frames?"

Penny shook her head.

"Not in that sense."

"Right."

Derek said:

"Alfred, can you see the station?"

"Yes."

"Which one?"

"Munich."

"Are you moving?"

"Yes."

"Relative to the station?"

"Yes."

Derek rubbed his forehead.

"Then what is happening?"

Weinstein said:

"I don't know."

Derek nodded.

"Well, that's settled."

Weinstein laughed.

"Precisely."

Derek looked at Penny.

She was smiling.

"What?"

"You're enjoying this."

"Not the impossible train."

"The investigation."

"Yes."

Derek turned back to the phone.

"Alfred, what does the train clock say?"

"11:17."

Derek looked at the Bureau clock.

11:17.

Penny looked at hers.

11:17.

Trevor looked at his phone.

11:17.

All four devices were synchronized.

Derek said:

"And your watch?"

"11:17."

"The brass clock?"

"11:17."

"All right."

Derek took a breath.

"Now look outside."

"What do you see?"

"Munich."

"Moving?"

"Yes."

"Backward?"

"No."

"Forward?"

"Yes."

Derek nodded.

"Good."

"Why?"

"Because I was beginning to worry."

"About what?"

"That we might have to investigate Munich."

Weinstein laughed.

Then his voice changed.

"Wait."

"What?"

"I see something."

"What?"

"An apple."

Derek looked at Penny.

"What sort of apple?"

"Red."

"That's encouraging."

"It is falling."

"Where?"

"From a tree."

Derek smiled.

"Alfred, are you sure you're not just watching an ordinary apple?"

"I am."

"Then what is it doing?"

Weinstein was silent.

Derek waited.

Finally Weinstein said:

"It's falling upward."

Nobody spoke.

Trevor looked at Penny.

Penny looked at Derek.

Derek said:

"Well."

Penny smiled.

"That settles it."

Trevor nodded.

"Gravity has become unreasonable."

Weinstein's voice came through the phone.

"No."

Derek looked at him.

"What?"

"The apple isn't falling upward."

"Then what is happening?"

Weinstein watched through the train window.

"The train is."

Derek stared.

"The train is falling?"

"No."

"Moving?"

"No."

Weinstein paused.

"The train is changing."

Derek frowned.

"Changing how?"

"The geometry."

There was silence.

Then Derek said:

"Alfred."

"Yes?"

"Investigate the apple."

Weinstein smiled.

"I was hoping you'd say that."

The line went dead.

Derek put the phone down.

Trevor looked at him.

"So we're investigating an apple."

Derek nodded.

"Apparently."

Penny picked up her coat.

"When?"

"Immediately."

Trevor looked puzzled.

"Where?"

Derek looked at the brass clock.

Then at the photograph.

Then toward the door.

"Munich."

Penny smiled.

"Good."

Trevor stood.

"How are we getting there?"

Derek put on his coat.

"Train."

Trevor looked at him.

"Which train?"

Derek paused.

"The one that's already arrived."

Trevor considered this.

"That sounds difficult."

Derek opened the door.

"Yes."

Penny followed him.

"That's why we have a Bureau."

They left.

The office remained empty.

On the desk lay Weinstein's forgotten notebook.

The last thing he had written was:

WHY DOES THE APPLE FALL?

Underneath, in smaller letters:

AND WHY DID IT CHOOSE TODAY?

The brass clock ticked once.

Then twice.

Then, very quietly, it began to run backwards.

Yes. That gives us a very good next step. I would make this the **second half of Chapter 5**, rather than starting a new chapter yet. It follows naturally from the apple: Weinstein realizes that he has been thinking about the geometry too locally.

And the Louvre gives Derek a wonderfully suspicious reason to be somewhere he has absolutely no business being.

## Chapter 5 — continued

### The Shape of an Event

Weinstein did not immediately go to Munich.

He went to Paris.

This was not because Paris was on the way.

It wasn't.

It was also not because he had suddenly developed an interest in French cuisine, although he had no objection to French cuisine and considerable objections to German railway sandwiches.

He went to Paris because Derek Gent had gone to the Louvre.

This was, in itself, suspicious.

Derek had once described museums as "warehouses for dead people who had better PR than most living people."

He had never shown the slightest interest in art.

He had once spent twenty minutes looking at a painting in the National Gallery before admitting that he had been facing it upside down.

He had also claimed that the Mona Lisa was "smiling because she knows something."

Penny had asked him what.

Derek had replied:

"That's what I'm trying to find out."

So when Weinstein learned that Derek had abruptly travelled to Paris and gone to the Louvre, he did not waste much time wondering whether Derek had developed an appreciation for Leonardo.

He assumed Derek had found something.

And knowing Derek, it would be something weird.

Very weird.

Weinstein was standing beside the Seine when the thought about the apple returned.

He watched the river.

Water moved continuously beneath the bridges.

Yet there was no obvious thing called "the flow of time" visible anywhere.

There was only change.

Water here.

Water there.

A boat here.

A boat there.

A person crossing a bridge.

A person no longer crossing it.

Weinstein frowned.

Barbarian's argument was irritating precisely because it was difficult to dismiss.

Time might not be a thing.

It might be a way of describing relationships between things.

That was not particularly controversial when expressed carefully.

Physics already did something similar.

Position was not a substance.

Velocity was not a substance.

Temperature was not a fluid.

They were descriptions of physical relationships.

Perhaps time was another.

But General Relativity complicated the matter.

There was spacetime.

Four coordinates.

Three spatial.

One temporal.

The metric.

Curvature.

Worldlines.

Proper time.

Weinstein stopped walking.

He had been thinking about the apple as though it were following a line.

But what if an event wasn't really a line?

What if an event was something with structure?

He looked at the river.

An apple falling from a tree was not merely:

Here.

Then here.

Then here.

It was a collection of positions and relationships.

At every point, there was a location in space.

And there was a temporal coordinate.

He took out his notebook.

He drew three axes.

x.

y.

z.

Then, beside them, he wrote:

t.

He stared at it.

Then crossed out t.

Underneath, he wrote:

τ.

Proper time.

He stared again.

Then he added:

imaginary?

He immediately frowned.

"No."

That was too careless.

Imaginary time was useful mathematics.

But that did not mean the universe literally contained an imaginary fourth direction.

A Wick rotation could turn a Lorentzian metric into a Euclidean-looking one.

It was a mathematical transformation.

It could make certain calculations beautifully behaved.

It did not mean clocks suddenly ran through the complex numbers.

Weinstein smiled.

"Not yet, anyway."

He closed the notebook.

Then opened it again.

There was something about the four-dimensional description that bothered him.

Not the mathematics.

The interpretation.

If an event was specified by three spatial coordinates and one temporal coordinate, then a complete event was a point in spacetime.

Fine.

But the apple did not experience the whole spacetime diagram.

The apple experienced its own worldline.

A sequence of events.

Except Barbarian would object to the word sequence.

Because sequence implied time.

Weinstein frowned.

Then perhaps the worldline was not a thing moving through time.

Perhaps it was simply the complete geometric object.

A curve.

All at once.

He stopped again.

That was uncomfortable.

If the complete worldline existed as a geometrical object, then asking where the apple "was going" was merely a way of describing the relationship between different points on the curve.

The apple did not need to know about the future.

The geometry did.

Weinstein looked up.

The Eiffel Tower was visible in the distance.

He ignored it.

This was more interesting.

Suppose the universe could be represented as a four-dimensional structure.

Then "past", "present" and "future" might be properties of how an observer experienced different parts of that structure.

Not necessarily ingredients of the structure itself.

He wrote:

THE EVENT DOES NOT HAPPEN IN TIME.

He paused.

Then added:

THE EVENT IS IN SPACETIME.

He stared at the sentence.

That was better.

Much better.

And suddenly he understood why the clocks in London and Munich were so troubling.

They might not be disagreeing about *time*.

They might be sampling different geometrical relationships.

The clocks were innocent.

The geometry was not.

Weinstein put the notebook away.

Then his phone rang.

It was Derek.

Weinstein answered.

"Where are you?"

"Paris."

"I know."

A pause.

"You know?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"You're at the Louvre."

Another pause.

"How do you know that?"

"Penny."

"Of course."

"Why are you there?"

"Because I found something."

"What?"

"I'll tell you when you get here."

"I'm already in Paris."

"Good."

"Where are you?"

"Inside."

"The Louvre?"

"Yes."

Weinstein looked at the enormous building.

"Why?"

"Because I think Leonardo knew something."

Weinstein closed his eyes.

"About what?"

"Time."

Weinstein opened his eyes.

"Leonardo da Vinci?"

"Yes."

"The painter?"

"Among other things."

Weinstein sighed.

"All right."

"Come inside."

"I am."

"Good."

The line went dead.

Weinstein entered the Louvre.

It took him less than ten minutes to find Derek.

He was standing in front of a painting.

Not the Mona Lisa.

A much less famous work.

Derek was staring at it with the concentration of a man trying to solve a murder.

Weinstein approached.

"What are you looking for?"

Derek didn't turn.

"Geometry."

"Of the painting?"

"Of the event."

Weinstein looked at the painting.

It was beautiful.

It was also completely irrelevant.

Probably.

"What did you find?"

Derek pointed.

"Look at the perspective."

Weinstein looked.

"Yes."

"Notice anything?"

"No."

"Good."

Weinstein looked at him.

"Good?"

"Yes. I wanted to know whether you'd see it immediately."

"See what?"

"The problem."

Weinstein examined the painting again.

Then he understood.

"The vanishing point."

Derek smiled.

"Exactly."

Weinstein looked closer.

"That's not unusual."

"No."

"But the way the scene is constructed..."

"Yes."

"It isn't simply perspective."

"No."

"It's a coordinate system."

Derek nodded.

"Exactly."

Weinstein stared at the painting.

The objects appeared to converge toward a point.

A mathematical point.

Not a physical object.

A representation.

A transformation.

A way of describing a three-dimensional scene on a two-dimensional surface.

He smiled.

"Interesting."

Derek finally turned.

"Now look at the other one."

They moved to another painting.

Then another.

Derek had marked nothing.

He didn't need to.

He simply pointed.

Weinstein looked.

The same conceptual structure appeared.

Not identical.

But related.

Different projections.

Different perspectives.

Different ways of representing the same kind of spatial relationships.

Weinstein suddenly understood why Derek had brought him there.

"A coordinate system depends on the observer."

"Yes."

"And the underlying scene doesn't."

"Usually."

Weinstein looked at him.

"Usually?"

Derek smiled.

"We're dealing with my murder."

"Fair enough."

They moved to a quieter gallery.

Derek stopped.

"Now imagine that the paintings aren't two-dimensional."

"Go on."

"Imagine each painting is a slice through a three-dimensional object."

Weinstein nodded.

"Yes."

"Different observers see different slices."

"Yes."

"But the object itself is larger than any one slice."

"Yes."

Derek looked at him.

"And now add time."

Weinstein became still.

"Three dimensions of space."

"Yes."

"And one temporal dimension."

"Yes."

"Then an event is not merely a point moving through time."

"No."

"It's a point in four-dimensional spacetime."

"Yes."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that settles it."

Weinstein looked at him.

"What does?"

"We've finally found the shape of the problem."

Weinstein shook his head.

"Not quite."

"Why?"

"Because you're still thinking of time as the fourth dimension in the same way as the three spatial dimensions."

Derek frowned.

"What's wrong with that?"

"Nothing mathematically."

"Then?"

"The metric."

Derek waited.

Weinstein continued.

"The temporal coordinate doesn't behave like an ordinary spatial coordinate. The sign is different."

Derek nodded.

"Lorentzian geometry."

"Exactly."

"So not an ordinary four-dimensional Euclidean space."

"No."

"Spacetime has a different geometry."

"Yes."

Derek looked back at the painting.

"Could we represent it visually?"

"Only imperfectly."

"Why?"

"Because we're trying to draw four dimensions on three-dimensional paper."

Derek smiled.

"That sounds like the sort of thing the Bureau was designed for."

Weinstein laughed.

Then Derek's expression changed.

"There's something else."

"What?"

"Look at the people."

Weinstein did.

"Yes?"

"They aren't simply objects in space."

"No."

"They are events."

Weinstein looked at him.

"Careful."

"Why?"

"Because a person is a worldline, not a single event."

Derek smiled.

"Precisely."

"And you're about to tell me you've found something."

"I have."

"What?"

Derek pointed toward the gallery.

"That man."

A security guard stood near the doorway.

Weinstein looked.

"Yes?"

"He walked past us three times."

"So?"

"He walked past us three times without ever appearing in the same place twice."

Weinstein frowned.

"That's normal."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because the Louvre corridor is a straight line."

Weinstein looked.

It was.

Derek continued.

"I watched him."

"So?"

"He passed us."

"Yes."

"Then he passed us again."

"Yes."

"Then again."

"Yes."

"And each time he was carrying a different newspaper."

Weinstein stared.

"That's odd."

"Very."

"Could be different guards."

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Same face."

"Same clothes?"

"Exactly."

Weinstein looked at the guard again.

The man turned.

For a fraction of a second, his eyes met Weinstein's.

Then he walked away.

Derek said quietly:

"That's our man."

Weinstein frowned.

"Who?"

"I don't know."

"That's rather inconvenient."

"Yes."

Derek smiled.

"But I know where he goes."

"Where?"

Derek pointed toward a staircase.

"Down."

They followed.

The staircase led beneath the museum.

The further they went, the older the building became.

Eventually they reached a corridor with no tourists.

No paintings.

No signs.

Only stone walls.

At the end stood a door.

Derek stopped.

"That's odd."

"What?"

"There wasn't supposed to be a door here."

Weinstein looked at it.

"Are you sure?"

"I've been here before."

"Why?"

"Long story."

"Fair enough."

Derek opened the door.

Inside was a small room.

And in the middle of it stood a table.

On the table was a clock.

Brass.

Railway style.

11:03:17.

Weinstein stared.

"The same clock."

"Yes."

"How did it get here?"

Derek shook his head.

"I don't know."

Weinstein approached.

There was something beside the clock.

A sheet of paper.

He picked it up.

Three coordinates were written on it.

x = 3

y = 4

z = 12

Then:

τ = ?

Weinstein stared.

Derek came closer.

"Well?"

Weinstein smiled.

"Now we have a problem."

Derek nodded.

"Good."

"Why?"

"Because problems are what we investigate."

Weinstein looked at the clock.

Then the coordinates.

Then the empty space where the fourth coordinate should have been.

He said:

"The three spatial coordinates are specified."

"Yes."

"But proper time isn't."

"No."

"Someone has given us a three-dimensional event."

Derek frowned.

"An event needs four coordinates."

"Exactly."

"So whoever left this knows that."

"Yes."

Derek looked at the clock.

"And they're asking us to find the fourth."

Weinstein nodded.

"Yes."

Penny's voice suddenly came from the doorway.

"Then I suppose we ought to."

They both turned.

Penny stood there.

Trevor was behind her, looking rather pleased with himself.

Derek stared.

"How did you get here?"

Penny smiled.

"Train."

Derek looked at Trevor.

"And you?"

"Also train."

Derek nodded.

"Well, that's settled."

Penny looked at the clock.

"No."

"What?"

"That's not the weird part."

She pointed at the paper.

"The weird part is the date."

Weinstein looked down.

He hadn't noticed it.

At the bottom of the page was a date.

Tomorrow.

Derek looked at Penny.

Then at Trevor.

Then at Weinstein.

Weinstein slowly smiled.

"Now," he said, "we're getting somewhere."

And above them, in the Louvre, a clock struck twelve.

Below them, the brass railway clock struck eleven seventeen.

And for the first time, Weinstein wondered whether the problem was not that the clocks disagreed about time.

Perhaps they disagreed about **which event they belonged to**.
