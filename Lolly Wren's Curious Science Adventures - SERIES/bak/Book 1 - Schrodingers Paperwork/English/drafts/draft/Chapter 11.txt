# Chapter 11

## The Hologram Problem

Derek was still staring at himself.

The other Derek was still sitting on the train.

The train was still sitting in the station.

The station clock still said 11:17.

The cucumber was still in Penny's hand.

Trevor was still trying very hard not to say anything stupid.

This was proving difficult.

The other Derek raised the cucumber again.

Derek raised an eyebrow.

"Why has he got a cucumber?"

Penny looked at him.

"Why have *you* got a cucumber?"

"I haven't."

She held hers up.

"No. You have."

Derek looked down.

He was holding a cucumber.

He stared at it.

Then at Penny.

Then at the other Derek.

"Ah."

Trevor sighed.

"That clears everything up."

The other Derek smiled.

Then the train doors closed.

The train departed.

Derek ran after it.

"Wait!"

The train disappeared into the tunnel.

Derek stopped.

He looked at the empty track.

Then at the cucumber in his hand.

"Right."

Penny walked over.

"You've got a cucumber."

"Apparently."

"Where did you get it?"

"I don't know."

"You were holding it."

"I know."

"Then you got it somewhere."

"That's annoyingly logical."

Trevor looked at the tunnel.

"Perhaps we should ask whether the other Derek is real."

Derek looked at him.

"Excellent question."

Penny nodded.

"How would we know?"

Trevor thought.

"If he can interact with things..."

"He can."

"If he has a worldline..."

"Apparently."

"If he has proper time..."

"Unknown."

"If he can eat a cucumber..."

Derek looked at him.

"Why would that prove anything?"

Trevor shrugged.

"It would at least be satisfying."

Penny laughed.

Derek looked at the cucumber.

Then at the tunnel.

Then at the clock.

11:17.

He suddenly smiled.

"Maybe we've been asking the wrong question."

Penny nodded.

"Again?"

"Yes."

"What is it this time?"

"Not whether the other Derek is real."

"Then?"

"Whether reality itself is real in the way we think it is."

Trevor frowned.

"That's rather broad."

"Yes."

"How broad?"

Derek looked at the ceiling.

"Potentially four-dimensional."

Penny smiled.

"Or more."

Derek nodded.

"Or fewer."

Trevor looked concerned.

"Fewer?"

"Don't worry."

"That's not reassuring."

Derek began walking.

"Come on."

"Where?"

"Back to the Bureau."

"Why?"

"Because I have an idea."

Penny followed.

Trevor followed Penny.

The cucumber came too.

---

The idea was not Derek's.

That was the first thing he admitted when they reached the Bureau.

"The holographic principle."

Trevor sat down.

"That's not your idea."

"I know."

"Who proposed it?"

"Several people contributed to the development."

"Good."

"Gerard 't Hooft and Leonard Susskind are particularly important."

Trevor nodded.

"And Witten?"

"Edward Witten is important to the later theoretical development connecting holography and string theory."

Penny looked at Derek.

"You've been studying."

"I have."

"That's worrying."

Derek ignored her.

He went to the whiteboard.

"The basic idea is astonishing."

He drew a cube.

Then a flat surface around it.

"Imagine that all the information describing what happens inside a volume can, in principle, be encoded on the boundary."

Trevor nodded.

"The number of degrees of freedom scales with area rather than volume."

"Exactly."

"Black-hole entropy is the clue."

"Yes."

Derek wrote:

S ∝ A

Then:

"Not volume."

Trevor nodded.

"Interesting."

Penny looked at the equation.

"So the universe could be described by information living on a boundary."

"Possibly."

"Like a hologram."

"That's the analogy."

Derek turned.

"But we need to be careful."

"Why?"

"Because saying 'the universe is a hologram' is much stronger than saying that certain theories have holographic descriptions."

Trevor nodded.

"So we shouldn't actually claim we're living inside a giant cinema screen."

"Correct."

Penny looked disappointed.

"That would have made the paperwork easier."

Derek smiled.

"And there are no strings attached."

Trevor stared at him.

"Really?"

Derek smiled.

"No."

Trevor groaned.

Penny laughed.

Derek continued.

"String theory is one route by which holographic duality becomes extraordinarily powerful. The famous example is the AdS/CFT correspondence."

He wrote:

**AdS ↔ CFT**

"A gravitational theory in a higher-dimensional spacetime can be equivalent to a non-gravitational quantum theory living on its lower-dimensional boundary."

Trevor nodded.

"So gravity appears on one side but not the other."

"Exactly."

"That's extraordinary."

"Yes."

"And if that works..."

"Then perhaps spacetime itself is emergent."

Penny looked at him.

"Emergent from what?"

"Information."

"Quantum degrees of freedom."

"Relationships."

"Entanglement."

"Possibly."

Derek stepped back.

The board was now covered.

Spacetime.

Curvature.

Worldlines.

Information.

Entanglement.

Holography.

He looked at the cucumber.

"And now we have a problem."

Trevor sighed.

"What?"

"If spacetime is emergent..."

"Yes?"

"Can Special Relativity still be true?"

Trevor thought.

"Why not?"

"Can General Relativity?"

"Why not?"

"Can both?"

"Why not?"

Derek smiled.

"Exactly."

Penny nodded.

"The laws we observe don't necessarily have to disappear merely because there's a deeper description."

"Correct."

Trevor looked at the board.

"The temperature of a gas is real even though it's emergent from microscopic motion."

"Exactly."

"Fluid pressure is real."

"Yes."

"Sound waves are real."

"Yes."

"Yet none of those are fundamental microscopic objects."

"Correct."

Derek pointed at the word **SPACETIME**.

"So perhaps spacetime could be like that."

Penny nodded.

"Real without being fundamental."

"Exactly."

Trevor looked thoughtful.

"Then SR could describe the local behaviour of the emergent spacetime."

"Yes."

"And GR could describe its large-scale geometry."

"Yes."

"While the deeper holographic theory describes the underlying information."

"Exactly."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Penny looked at him.

"Not quite."

Derek paused.

"Why?"

"Because we still have the cucumber."

He looked down.

The cucumber was sitting on the table.

Derek stared at it.

"Yes."

Penny picked it up.

"If spacetime is emergent..."

"Yes."

"And the cucumber is made of atoms..."

"Yes."

"And the atoms are described quantum mechanically..."

"Yes."

"And the geometry in which those atoms exist might itself be emergent..."

"Yes."

"Then the cucumber is an emergent object in an emergent spacetime."

Derek smiled.

"That sounds unnecessarily complicated."

Penny shrugged.

"It's a cucumber."

Trevor nodded.

"That simplifies things."

Derek laughed.

"Does it?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"We know it exists."

Derek looked at him.

"Do we?"

Trevor held up the cucumber.

"Yes."

"How?"

Trevor considered this.

"It's here."

Derek smiled.

"Excellent epistemology."

Penny took the cucumber from him.

"Perhaps we should test it."

Derek looked suspicious.

"How?"

"Measure it."

"With what?"

"A scale."

"Fine."

She weighed it.

Trevor recorded the result.

Penny measured its length.

Then circumference.

Then temperature.

Then mass distribution.

Derek watched.

"What are we proving?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

"We're establishing that the cucumber has observable properties."

"Yes."

"And if the holographic description is correct, those properties must ultimately be encoded in the underlying degrees of freedom."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

"So the cucumber can be described in more than one way."

"Like spacetime."

"Exactly."

Trevor looked thoughtful.

"Could the cucumber itself have a holographic description?"

Derek smiled.

"Of course."

"Really?"

"In principle."

Trevor looked at it.

"That's extraordinary."

Derek nodded.

"The information describing the physical system can have different representations."

Penny smiled.

"Just don't tell the cucumber."

"Why?"

"It may become arrogant."

## Everything Everywhere All at Once

Penny had decided that Derek was becoming unbearable.

Not because he was wrong.

That would have been easier.

It was because he was usually right.

There was something deeply irritating about a man who could spend an afternoon discussing curved spacetime, singularities and the holographic principle and then, when Penny pointed out an obvious practical difficulty, simply say:

"Yes."

As though the universe had just been waiting for him to catch up.

So Penny went to the cinema.

Alone.

This was not particularly unusual.

What was unusual was the film she chose.

*Everything Everywhere All at Once.*

She settled into her seat with popcorn and a drink and watched the opening credits.

Then she saw her.

Penny sat upright.

"Blimey."

Jamie Lee Curtis.

She had survived *Halloween* after all.

Apparently, having spent several films being pursued by a man who refused to die, she had decided that the next sensible career move was to become an IRS employee and spend her time tormenting bewildered Asians across multiple universes.

Penny smiled.

"Americans."

She took a handful of popcorn.

The film began.

Within minutes there were universes.

Then other universes.

Then other versions of the same people.

Then universes in which perfectly ordinary things had become completely ridiculous.

Penny watched.

She became increasingly interested.

Not because the film was physically accurate.

It wasn't.

Nor was it trying to be.

But the underlying idea was strangely familiar.

Different possible realities.

Different versions of people.

Different histories.

Different outcomes.

Different choices.

A universe in which one thing happened.

Another in which it didn't.

Penny thought about Derek.

She thought about the Bureau.

She thought about the strange clock.

She thought about the train that apparently existed in one description of reality and didn't in another.

She thought about Weinstein's question.

If spacetime itself could be emergent, what exactly did it mean for there to be one universe?

She leaned back.

The film continued.

Michelle Yeoh's character moved through wildly different versions of reality.

Penny smiled.

"Right."

A woman sitting several seats away glanced at her.

Penny lowered her voice.

"This is actually rather relevant."

The woman looked away.

Penny continued watching.

The film's multiverse was not the same thing as the multiverse physicists sometimes discuss.

That distinction mattered.

But it was useful as an intuition pump.

Suppose reality could be described in many different ways.

Suppose an observer occupied one particular history.

Suppose other histories existed in some mathematical sense.

Then the question became:

What was fundamental?

The particular history?

The collection of histories?

The mathematical structure connecting them?

Or something deeper still?

Penny thought about holography.

If a higher-dimensional gravitational description could be equivalent to a lower-dimensional quantum description, then perhaps what looked like an enormous four-dimensional reality from one perspective could be represented by something radically different from another.

Not a movie.

Not a projection in the everyday sense.

A mathematically equivalent description.

Two apparently different descriptions.

One underlying physics.

She smiled.

"Now we're getting somewhere."

On screen, the characters continued jumping between realities.

Penny watched Jamie Lee Curtis.

"Still alive."

She shook her head.

"She really does have remarkable career longevity."

Then another universe appeared.

Penny frowned.

Something about it bothered her.

Not the physics.

The logic.

If every possible version of a person existed, what distinguished one version from another?

Their history.

Their state.

Their relationships.

Their information.

Penny sat forward.

Information.

That was the word.

Everything came back to information.

A physical system could be described by its state.

A clock had a state.

A cucumber had a state.

A person had a state.

A train had a state.

A universe had a state.

And perhaps the geometry of spacetime itself emerged from relationships between quantum states.

She took out her phone.

She typed:

**Holography: information on boundary → bulk geometry?**

Then:

**Could different descriptions correspond to same physics?**

Then:

**SR and GR survive because they are emergent descriptions?**

She paused.

Then added:

**Ask Alfred.**

She put the phone away.

On screen, Jamie Lee Curtis was once again doing something completely unreasonable.

Penny smiled.

"Americans."

A few minutes later she laughed.

Then laughed again.

Then stopped.

Because something on the screen had triggered a thought.

The characters weren't merely travelling through space.

They were switching between descriptions of reality.

The important thing was not that they moved from one physical universe to another.

The important thing was that the **information defining the character changed**.

Different memories.

Different relationships.

Different physical histories.

Different versions of the same person.

Penny thought about worldlines.

A worldline was a history through spacetime.

But if spacetime itself was emergent, perhaps the deeper theory did not contain worldlines at all.

Perhaps worldlines were emergent too.

That would mean that Derek wasn't fundamentally a line through spacetime.

He was a particular physical state, embedded in a network of relationships.

His apparent history was one way of describing that state and its correlations.

Penny smiled.

"That explains the other Derek."

Then she frowned.

"No, it doesn't."

The woman beside her looked over again.

Penny whispered:

"Sorry."

The film continued.

Penny watched.

Suddenly the multiverse stopped being merely funny.

It became useful.

Not as physics.

As a question.

If there were many mathematically possible descriptions of reality, what made one description equivalent to another?

What had to remain unchanged?

The answer, she realised, had to involve observables.

Physics couldn't depend on what coordinates someone chose.

It couldn't depend on whether someone described the world using one mathematical language or another.

The measurable predictions had to agree.

That was already familiar from relativity.

Coordinates could change.

Physics didn't.

A Lorentz transformation changed the coordinates assigned to events.

It didn't change the invariant interval.

A different coordinate system could make the mathematics look different.

The physics remained.

Perhaps holographic duality was a much more radical version of the same philosophical lesson.

One description could have gravity and curved spacetime.

Another could have quantum fields without gravity.

Yet they could encode the same physics.

Penny sat back.

"Bloody hell."

The woman beside her looked again.

Penny smiled.

"Physics."

The woman nodded uncertainly.

On screen, everything became even more absurd.

Penny laughed.

Then she suddenly remembered something.

The cucumber.

She had left it at the Bureau.

She looked at her phone.

No messages.

Then the screen lit up.

A message from Derek.

**WHERE ARE YOU?**

Penny typed:

**Cinema.**

Three dots appeared.

**WHAT ARE YOU WATCHING?**

She replied:

**Everything Everywhere All at Once.**

The reply came almost immediately.

**WHY?**

Penny smiled.

She typed:

**Because you keep talking about holography and I wanted to see what the Americans think it means.**

There was a pause.

Then:

**AND?**

Penny looked at the screen.

She typed:

**Jamie Lee Curtis survived Halloween.**

Another pause.

Then:

**THAT'S YOUR PHYSICS CONCLUSION?**

Penny smiled.

**No.**

She looked at the screen.

Then typed:

**My physics conclusion is that if different descriptions can encode the same underlying reality, SR and GR don't need to be fundamental to be true.**

Derek replied:

**Correct.**

Penny smiled.

Then another message appeared.

**ALSO, JAMIE LEE CURTIS DID NOT "SURVIVE HALLOWEEN" IN THE PHYSICS SENSE.**

Penny frowned.

She typed:

**You know what I meant.**

**I don't.**

**Americans.**

A pause.

Then:

**That settles it.**

Penny laughed.

She put the phone away.

Then she noticed something.

On the screen, a character was holding a cucumber.

Penny stared.

Surely not.

She leaned forward.

There it was.

A cucumber.

She laughed.

"You're having a laugh."

The woman beside her looked at the screen.

"What?"

"The cucumber."

"What about it?"

Penny stared.

"Nothing."

She reached into her handbag.

Her own cucumber was still there.

She looked from the cucumber in her bag to the cucumber on the screen.

Then back again.

She frowned.

The one on the screen was not identical.

Of course it wasn't.

That would have been ridiculous.

Still...

Penny took a photograph of the screen.

She sent it to Derek.

**LOOK.**

His reply came almost immediately.

**I'M LOOKING.**

Then:

**THAT'S A CUCUMBER.**

Penny smiled.

**Yes.**

**So?**

She typed:

**Nothing.**

She paused.

Then:

**Yet.**

She put the phone away.

The film continued.

Penny watched the characters collide with one absurd reality after another.

Then she thought about the Bureau.

About Derek.

About Weinstein.

About Barbarian.

About the clocks.

About the rain.

About the strange train.

About the other Derek.

Perhaps the Bureau had been approaching the problem backwards.

They had assumed that something strange was happening *inside* spacetime.

What if the strange thing was the relationship between different descriptions of spacetime?

What if the clocks weren't moving incorrectly?

What if they belonged to different descriptions?

What if the train wasn't travelling through a strange geometry?

What if the train was the strange geometry?

Penny sat very still.

Then she smiled.

She took out her phone.

She wrote one final message to Derek.

**Don't investigate the clocks.**

His reply came:

**Why?**

She typed:

**Investigate the descriptions.**

A pause.

Then:

**Explain.**

Penny looked at the film.

Then at the cucumber.

Then at the rain running down the cinema window.

She wrote:

**Maybe the universe doesn't have one story.**

She thought for a moment.

Then added:

**Maybe it has several descriptions that all have to agree on what can actually happen.**

Derek replied:

**Now you're talking.**

Penny smiled.

She put the phone away.

On the screen, everything was happening everywhere all at once.

Penny watched for another minute.

Then quietly said:

"Americans."

She ate another piece of popcorn.

"That settles it."

And somewhere in London, a clock stopped at 11:17.

Again.

---

The laboratory door opened.

Weinstein walked in.

Everyone turned.

He looked at the whiteboard.

Then at Derek.

Then at Penny.

Then at the cucumber.

"Why is there a cucumber?"

Penny smiled.

"Long story."

Weinstein nodded.

"Fine."

He looked at the board.

"Holography."

Derek nodded.

"Yes."

Weinstein took off his coat.

"Interesting."

Trevor looked surprised.

"You believe in it?"

Weinstein smiled.

"That's not how theoretical physics works."

"How does it work?"

"We investigate whether the mathematics describes reality."

"Fair."

Weinstein walked toward the board.

"You've been asking whether SR and GR can both survive if spacetime is emergent."

"Yes."

"They can."

Derek nodded.

"That was my conclusion."

"Good."

"Unless..."

Weinstein smiled.

"There's always an unless."

He pointed at the equation.

"The important thing is that holography does not mean that the lower-dimensional description is a fake version of the higher-dimensional one."

Derek nodded.

"Duality."

"Exactly."

"Two descriptions of the same physics."

"Yes."

"Then the geometry could be encoded in something that doesn't look geometric at all."

"Yes."

Trevor's eyes widened.

"So curvature could emerge from information."

"Possibly."

"Gravity too?"

"Possibly."

"Spacetime?"

"Possibly."

"Time?"

Weinstein stopped.

The room became quiet.

He looked at the word.

TIME.

Then erased it.

"Especially time."

Derek stared.

"You're thinking the same thing."

"I am."

Penny sat down.

"Explain."

Weinstein took the marker.

"Suppose the fundamental description does not contain spacetime in the form we experience it."

"Yes."

"Suppose what we call distance and time emerge from deeper relationships."

"Yes."

"Then the fact that we experience a temporal ordering doesn't necessarily mean that a fundamental clock is ticking beneath everything."

Derek nodded.

"Barbarian."

"Perhaps."

Weinstein continued.

"Time could emerge together with geometry."

Trevor frowned.

"Then why does it feel so real?"

Weinstein looked at him.

"Why does temperature feel real?"

"Because it is."

"Exactly."

Trevor nodded.

"But temperature is statistical."

"Yes."

"And time?"

"We don't yet know."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Weinstein laughed.

"No."

He pointed at the cucumber.

"But the cucumber is useful."

Penny looked at him.

"You too?"

"Why not?"

"Everyone is obsessed with it."

"It's an excellent test object."

"Why?"

"Because it is ordinary."

Derek nodded.

"That's precisely why."

Weinstein picked it up.

He examined it.

"An ordinary object should obey ordinary physics."

"Yes."

"SR."

"Yes."

"GR."

"Yes."

"Quantum mechanics."

"Yes."

"Whatever deeper theory may underlie them."

"Yes."

He put the cucumber down.

"So if we discover something impossible about the cucumber..."

Derek smiled.

"We've found something wrong with our description of reality."

"Or with the cucumber."

Penny laughed.

Trevor looked at the cucumber.

"I don't think it can be trusted."

Nobody disagreed.

Weinstein turned back to the board.

"There is another problem."

Derek waited.

"If spacetime is emergent, what determines the geometry?"

"Information."

"Yes."

"Entanglement."

"Possibly."

"And what determines the information?"

Weinstein smiled.

"That is where the physics gets interesting."

He drew two circles.

A.

B.

Then a line between them.

"Suppose two quantum systems are entangled."

"Yes."

"The correlations between them can contain information."

"Yes."

"Now imagine a huge number of such relationships."

He drew many more lines.

"Could the pattern of entanglement determine which systems are close and which are far apart?"

Trevor nodded.

"Like a network."

"Exactly."

"And geometry could emerge from the network."

"Yes."

Derek stared at the board.

"So distance could be a measure of connectivity."

"Potentially."

"And curvature would correspond to changes in that connectivity."

"Potentially."

"And gravity would emerge from the structure."

"Potentially."

Derek smiled.

"That's rather beautiful."

Penny nodded.

"Very."

Trevor looked at the cucumber.

"And somewhere in that network..."

He held it up.

"...there's this."

Weinstein laughed.

"Presumably."

Derek nodded.

"Then why not?"

Penny smiled.

"We do."

Trevor nodded.

"Cucumbers do."

Derek added:

"SR does."

Penny:

"GR does."

Trevor:

"Rain does."

Derek:

"Poetry does."

Penny looked at him.

"Does it?"

Derek considered.

"Most of the time."

Trevor grinned.

"And apparently shafting and bonking do."

Penny slowly turned toward him.

Trevor immediately realised his mistake.

"I mean..."

Penny raised an eyebrow.

"Go on."

Trevor looked at Derek.

Derek looked at Weinstein.

Weinstein looked at the cucumber.

Nobody spoke.

Finally Penny smiled.

"Well."

She put the cucumber back on the table.

"If the universe can accommodate curved spacetime, quantum entanglement, holographic duality and Trevor..."

Trevor sighed.

"...then I suppose there's room for everything else."

Derek nodded.

"Exactly."

Weinstein smiled.

"But remember."

They all looked at him.

"The holographic principle is a serious physical idea."

"Yes."

"It is not established that our actual universe is literally a hologram."

"Yes."

"And even where holographic dualities are well understood, they are mathematical equivalences between particular theories, not proof that reality is secretly a two-dimensional cinema."

Derek nodded.

"Understood."

Penny nodded.

"Yes."

Trevor nodded.

"Fine."

Weinstein looked at the cucumber.

"And that is why we must not confuse a useful description with the thing being described."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Weinstein smiled back.

"For now."

The laboratory lights flickered.

The clocks stopped.

11:17.

Nobody moved.

Penny looked at the cucumber.

It had changed.

Not visibly.

Not physically.

But there was something different about it.

Derek approached.

"What happened?"

Penny picked it up.

The weight was the same.

The temperature was the same.

The shape was the same.

Everything measurable appeared identical.

Then Trevor noticed something.

"There."

He pointed to the end of the cucumber.

A tiny pattern had appeared in the skin.

Not a letter.

Not a number.

A geometric figure.

A circle inside a square.

And inside the circle, three lines.

Derek stared.

Weinstein leaned closer.

"It's a projection."

"Of what?"

Weinstein looked at the pattern.

"I don't know."

Derek smiled.

"Well, that's settled."

Penny shook her head.

"No."

"What?"

She turned the cucumber over.

The pattern was also visible on the other side.

Exactly the same.

Trevor looked at it.

"That's impossible."

Derek smiled.

"Don't say it."

Trevor nodded.

"Right."

Weinstein took the cucumber.

He looked at the pattern.

Then at the clock.

11:17.

Then at the whiteboard.

At the words:

**SPACETIME**

**HOLOGRAPHY**

**INFORMATION**

He slowly turned the cucumber around.

The pattern was still there.

And then he saw what it represented.

Not a map.

Not a coordinate system.

A boundary.

Weinstein looked at Derek.

"Derek."

"Yes?"

"I think we've just found the surface."

Derek stared at the cucumber.

"The boundary?"

Weinstein nodded.

"Yes."

Penny smiled.

Trevor looked horrified.

"You're telling me the cucumber is the universe?"

Weinstein considered it.

"No."

He paused.

"That would be ridiculous."

The cucumber made a tiny ticking sound.

Everyone froze.

Derek looked at it.

"Did anyone else hear that?"

Penny nodded.

Trevor nodded.

Weinstein stared at the cucumber.

Then it ticked again.

11:17.

And somewhere beneath London, a train began moving.

Without a driver.

Without passengers.

And, according to every timetable in existence, it had never been built.
