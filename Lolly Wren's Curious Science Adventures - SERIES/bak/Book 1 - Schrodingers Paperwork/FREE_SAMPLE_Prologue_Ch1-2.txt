Prologue: Before Anything Went Wrong

There was a time when the Ministry of Eventualities was not in trouble.

This was mainly because nobody had yet noticed that it existed.

The Ministry occupied a respectable grey building in Coldharrow, between a Department of Minor Infrastructure and an office responsible for the licensing of ceremonial ladders. Its sign was small. Its corridors were long. Its carpets had the peculiar brown colour adopted by institutions that have survived several changes of government and are now too embarrassed to ask for anything.

Its official purpose was simple: to deal with events that had not happened yet.

This sounded reasonable when written in a memorandum. It still does, which is the trouble with memoranda.

The difficulty was deciding what counted as such an event.

Almost anything did, apparently.

The Ministry had forms for anticipated accidents, projected disputes, future absences, possible administrative consequences, and one particularly unpopular form for things that were almost certainly going to happen but had not yet acquired the courtesy to do so.

Nobody considered this strange.

This was the first mistake.

The second was believing that reality could be made simpler by paperwork.

The third was giving the paperwork a budget.

No one has ever located a fourth mistake, though several long-serving clerks maintain that one is still out there, unfiled, and getting bigger.

By the time Lolly Wren arrived at Transit Reconciliation, the Ministry had developed a sophisticated theory of reality. It was not a good theory, but it was laminated, indexed and available in three approved colours.

According to the theory, the universe stayed in a tidy superposition of everything it might be, provided that nobody measured it too closely. The instant a clipboard, an eye, or a Third Grade officer with a form recorded an outcome, the plurality narrowed to one aggravating fact. The Ministry had a word for this. So, less politely, did physics: collapse. Nobody at the Ministry understood why. Understanding was never required, not even for physicists; only the calculation had to work. The Ministry’s, unsettlingly, did.

No one had thought to ask what would happen if it ever stopped.

There was, unfortunately, a universe. And lately it had been reading the staff handbook the way an employee reads the small print after being asked to do something unreasonable: carefully, and with growing interest in the word “no.”

It found none.

People arrived before they had decided to travel. Forms appeared before they had been completed. Objects became difficult to describe. In one unfortunate district, local amenities began to require confirmation before they were allowed to continue being themselves.

One bakery filed the same confirmation form seventeen times in a single morning and still wasn’t certain, by lunch, whether it currently sold bread.

It was getting worse. Nobody had scheduled a meeting about it yet, which was the most alarming fact so far.

Then someone discovered that the Ministry had been quietly moving complexity somewhere else.

Not solving it.

Moving it.

Into a place called 4C.

No one could say precisely where 4C was. Everyone had agreed, without ever discussing it, that it was better not to ask twice.

This would have been alarming even without what was waiting in the basement.

There was, unfortunately, something in the basement. It had no file number, in a building that had issued one to the weather. Nobody had ever explained why.

Every so often someone new asked. The conversation ended quickly, and the asker developed, soon afterward, a sudden and lasting interest in filing cabinets.

And so, on an otherwise unremarkable morning, Lolly Wren sat down with a cup of tea, a complaint terminal, and the entirely reasonable expectation that the worst thing she would have to deal with that day was a misplaced train.

She did not know about 4C, or the basement. For the moment, the tea and the terminal were as much of the day as she had. She drank it anyway, and logged in.

She was wrong.

Obviously.

She simply did not yet have the paperwork to prove it.

Chapter 1: The Form That Would Not Resolve

There are, in the management of large and failing systems, certain sounds one learns to distrust. The metallic cough of a heating vent. The clerical thump of an internal memorandum. And the soft clicking of the complaint terminal on Lolly Wren’s desk, which had lately developed the unhelpful habit of reporting the future as though it had already happened.

It had not yet been wrong today. That was the part Lolly chose not to dwell on.

Lolly looked up from her tea. This was unfortunate for two reasons. She had not yet had enough of it to be tolerant, and the tea itself had the colour and emotional range of varnished disappointment. The terminal clicked again. Lolly sighed in the manner of a woman who had once trained in theoretical physics and now worked in Transit Reconciliation: celestial harmony, then local government.

Her office, if such a term may be generously applied to a room with one window, four filing stacks, and the temperament of a damp envelope, occupied the far end of the Regional Transit Reconciliation Annex.

It smelled, on a good day, of photocopier toner and lukewarm tea; on a bad day, of wet coats and the particular dust that settles only on forms nobody has read twice.

The Annex’s wireless network, EVENTUALITIES-GUEST, had been intermittent for as long as anyone could remember. IT Support maintained this was an unrelated infrastructure issue, wholly unconnected to anything happening upstairs. Lolly had long since stopped asking which upstairs they meant.

Gideon Wigglesworth was one of the few people who visited Lolly voluntarily, and the only one she trusted with an impossible problem. They had shared a workbench for the better part of a decade. Gideon was very clever, painfully clumsy, and so agreeable that even the Ministry’s furniture seemed reluctant to injure him. He appeared carrying two coffees, caught his shoe on the threshold, recovered, and handed Lolly one of them.

He was a big, rumpled man, built more for a workbench than an office chair, with shirtsleeves perpetually pushed back over forearms freckled with old solder burns.

The reverse-osmosis machine in the staff kitchen had been broken since March, leaving the building with nothing but plain filtered water from a jug, which Gideon refused on principle to drink. He maintained instead a small private stockpile of Irn-Bru in the bottom drawer of his desk, purchased at his own considerable expense, for reasons he would only ever describe as “standards.”

Lolly noticed, without wanting to, that hers was already going lukewarm, though Gideon swore he’d poured it only moments before.

He smelled, as ever, of hot solder and pencil shavings — a combination Lolly had come to associate with problems that were about to be solved rather than caused.

“Your machine is making the usual noise,” Gideon observed.
Lolly glanced at the terminal. “It has only just started.”
“No. The machine has only just admitted it.” He nodded at the case file, which had opened itself on the screen unbidden and had not yet settled on its own fields. “What is the order of those?”
“Type, subcategory, status, urgency.”
He frowned. “And the status is already saying present, before the journey has even finished happening.”

Lolly looked at him.

“That may be the mistake,” Gideon continued. “You’re assuming the system is recording what happened. What if it’s using what it thinks has happened to decide what was supposed to happen?”

He took a sip of coffee, turned to put it down, and walked directly into the doorframe.

Lolly stared at him for a moment. “That,” she allowed, “was an excellent insight.”
“Thank you.” Gideon rubbed his shoulder. “The door was less impressed.”

The Annex had been designed in the late bureaucratic style: openness in theory, fluorescent headaches in practice. People came to complain about missed connections, duplicate arrivals, incorrect departures, luggage with philosophical objections to ownership, and, increasingly, journeys that had happened in the wrong order. Lolly had processed three of those this week. She did not care for trends in metaphysics.

Beyond her half-open door the floor kept up its usual undertone — a phone ringing for someone who had stepped away, a stapler giving out, the low shuffle of people rehearsing complaints they hoped might still sound reasonable by the time they reached a desk.

Outside, London was doing what London did between October and roughly May: raining without commitment, in the fine, directionless way that soaked through a coat faster than actual weather ever managed.

The terminal emitted a third click, sharper this time, and the half-written case file collapsed, all at once, into a shape it had not had a moment before.

    COMPLAINT TYPE: TRANSIT DISCREPANCY

    SUBCATEGORY: PREMATURE ARRIVAL / INTENT LAG CITIZEN

    STATUS: PRESENT URGENCY: MODERATE TO EXISTENTIAL

Lolly stared at the final line. Then she took another sip of tea in the vain hope that liquid might alter the meaning of language. It did not.

There came a brisk knock at the open office door, immediately followed by a woman entering without waiting for permission. She was in her late sixties, silver hair pinned back, in a dark coat and sensible shoes, with the expression of someone who had accepted the collapse of standards in public life as regrettable but had no intention of accepting the collapse of space-time.

She was short and compact, built like a woman who had spent decades winning small arguments with tradesmen by simply outlasting them, and she led with her chin the way other people lead with an opening sentence.

Lolly answered in the flat, unhurried register she’d perfected for delivering bad news to people who outranked her — pitched low enough that panicking rooms tended to quiet down just to catch the rest of the sentence.

“Ms Wren?” she asked.
“Lolly Wren, yes.”
“Good. I’ve been sent from Admissions.”

This was never good.

Lolly gestured to the chair opposite her desk. “Please sit down.” She sat with the composure of someone settling in for a procedure that should have been unnecessary.

“Elspeth Chain,” she offered. “Mrs Elspeth Chain, technically, though I’ve found that ‘Mrs’ invites a certain tone from public institutions and I’d rather avoid it.”

Her voice was low and dry, built, it seemed, to carry the length of a queue without ever once needing to be raised for it.

“That seems wise,” ventured Lolly. Mrs Chain nodded once, as if pleased that one civil servant in the building had not entirely wasted her upbringing.

Lolly brought up the file. “You’ve reported a transit discrepancy.”
“I have.” She glanced down. “Premature arrival with intent lag.”
“Yes.”

Lolly folded her hands. “I wonder if you might explain what happened in your own words.”

Mrs Chain did not answer immediately. Instead she removed her gloves, placed them side by side in her lap, and began, “I set out this morning to visit my sister in Whitbury-on-Sea.” Lolly nodded. “I had not,” she continued, “fully decided to go.”
Lolly paused. “I see.”
“No, Ms Wren, that is rather the problem. I had not fully decided to go, but I had advanced sufficiently in the direction of deciding that I put on my coat, found my umbrella, and went to the bus stop in order, so to speak, to think transitively.”
Lolly regarded her. “Transitively.”
“Yes. It often helps.”
“Of course.”

“It was drizzling,” offered Mrs Chain.
“Was it?”
“At home, yes. At the bus stop, less so. Halfway through the route it became bright, then windy, then, for one very unpleasant stretch near the old post office, maritime.”
“Maritime.”
“There were gulls.” Lolly made a note.

When she looked down at it a moment later, the note appeared to have already been crossed out, in handwriting she was fairly sure was her own.

Mrs Chain went on. “I was still deciding whether I wished to spend the afternoon with my sister when the bus stopped, the doors opened, and I found I had already arrived.”
“That seems, on the face of it, efficient.” Mrs Chain gave her a look which suggested that if she continued in that vein she might improve her, physically.

“Ms Wren,” she explained, “Whitbury-on-Sea is twelve miles from my house. I had not committed to the journey. One ought not to arrive anywhere before one has properly consented to the inconvenience.”

“No,” Lolly agreed quietly. “One oughtn’t.”
“Quite.”

She glanced at the screen again. The file now read:

    STATUS: APPROVED

    RESOLUTION: PASSENGER DELIVERED SUCCESSFULLY

Lolly frowned. Then, before her eyes, it changed.

    STATUS: MISROUTED

    RESOLUTION: PASSENGER ARRIVED WITHOUT COMPLETED INTENTION

Then it changed again.

    STATUS: CLOSED

    RESOLUTION: NO TRANSIT EVENT DETECTED

Lolly did not move for several seconds. The file waited, smugly. Mrs Chain observed her. “That isn’t encouraging, is it.”
“No,” she admitted. “Not especially.”

She pressed the refresh key. The screen flickered and returned to the first status.

    APPROVED.

She pressed it again.

    MISROUTED.

Again.

    CLOSED.

There are moments in office life when one has to decide whether a problem is technical, personal, or metaphysical. The correct answer is often all three, but procedure requires a degree of optimism. Lolly chose technical first.

“I’m sure,” she recited, with the restrained falsity of a trained employee, “that this is simply a processing irregularity.”
Mrs Chain glanced at the screen. “Is that why it keeps changing its mind?”
“Possibly.”
“And is that common?”
“No.”
“Good,” she remarked. “I dislike being reassured with uncommon things.”

Lolly reached for a pencil. Most officers used regulation pens, permanent and chained to the desk. Lolly preferred pencil. In the margin of the printed complaint slip she wrote:

This was not entirely a matter of preference. The Annex photocopier had declared itself out of order sometime the previous spring and shown no inclination since to reconsider, so anything needing duplicating, amending, or filling in twice was these days done by hand — and a pencil, unlike Ministry-issue pens, could at least be sharpened when it wore down to nothing.

\|journey⟩ = a\|not yet taken⟩ + b\|already arrived⟩

She stopped, considered, and added: with a and b being annoyingly nonzero. It was not, she would have insisted to anyone who asked, a proper equation: only a superposition, a state holding both a journey not yet taken and one already completed, neither of them secretly true underneath the whole time. The two did not average into something safely in between. They simply both were, until something (an apparatus, an observer, a Regional Transit Reconciliation Annex) was made to choose.

Once, a tutor had told her that nature did not take the shortest way between two events: it took every way there was, added them up, each weighted by a small rotating arrow, and only the sum was ever measured. “I’m not going to explain it so you understand it,” he’d remarked, not unkindly. “I’m going to explain it the way we calculate it. It works regardless.” She suspected, this morning, he had also been describing the civil service.

Through the one window, on the floor below, she could see another clerk sitting very still in front of a screen that had just changed colour. Neither of them mentioned it later. Lolly decided not to ask.

Mrs Chain leaned slightly forward. “What’s that?”
Lolly covered the page with professional reflex. “Nothing official.”
“It’s mathematics.”
“It is a private habit.”
“Is it useful?”
“Not always,” Lolly admitted. “But it occasionally stops me shouting.”
Mrs Chain accepted this at once. “Sensible.”

On the ceiling, the office speaker wrought a soft ping. A voice of cultivated female neutrality announced, “Attention staff. Due to the implementation of the Simplification Initiative, all departments are reminded that duplicate interpretations of public events create avoidable confusion. Please encourage outcomes to settle promptly. Thank you for supporting a clearer tomorrow.”

Lolly looked up. Mrs Chain looked up. The speaker, having rendered its contribution to civilisation, remained silent.

For half a second too long, the ceiling speaker held its silence like something deciding whether to say more, and then did not.

“Did that seem odd to you?” she asked.
“Very.”
“Good.” “It used the phrase ‘encourage outcomes,’” Lolly noted.
“I heard it.”
“You can’t encourage outcomes.”
Mrs Chain gave her a dry look. “Everything in this building appears to encourage outcomes.”

That, Lolly felt, was unpleasantly fair.

She turned back to the terminal and opened the transit record in detail. A route map appeared, though it was not behaving in a route-like manner. The line representing Mrs Chain’s bus journey seemed to have split in three places and doubled back through a district which, unless Lolly was badly mistaken, had been a municipal duck pond until last February.

She zoomed in. The map reconfigured itself. Whitbury-on-Sea moved three streets inland. Lolly blinked. Then the little sea icon beside the town name vanished altogether and was replaced with a notation reading

    COASTAL STATUS: LEGACY

Mrs Chain was watching her. “What is it now?”
Lolly hesitated: civil service etiquette discouraged alarming the public before lunch. “How attached,” she asked carefully, “would you say Whitbury is to the sea?”
Mrs Chain stared at her. “It is in the name.”
“Yes,” Lolly conceded.
“Very attached,” considered Mrs Chain.
“Quite,” Lolly agreed.
“Why?” asked Mrs Chain.

Lolly turned the screen very slightly in her direction. Mrs Chain adjusted her spectacles and read the notation. There was a small silence. Then she asked, in a voice sharpened by disbelief, “What does ‘legacy’ sea mean?”
“I haven’t the faintest idea.”
“Can seas be legacy?”
“They ought not to be.”
“Yes,” she confirmed. “That was my own view.”

Lolly printed the file. This took much longer than it should have, because the printer on her floor had been designed to detect urgency and resent it. When the sheet finally emerged, it came out warm and faintly humming. She flattened it on the desk. Across the bottom margin, in type too small to have been there a moment earlier, appeared a sentence she was quite certain had not existed in the record before:

    DESTINATION UPDATED TO NEAREST STABLE VERSION.

Lolly read it twice. Mrs Chain read it once and pronounced, “That sounds appalling.”
“Yes,” Lolly agreed. “And what does it mean?”

Lolly did not answer directly. “If you had to describe, in the fewest possible words, which of everything that might have happened to you this morning was most likely to survive being looked at by a stranger, what would you say?”
Mrs Chain considered this with the seriousness of a woman being asked a fair question for once in a civic building. “I suppose... that I went to my sister’s.”
“That,” Lolly explained, “is roughly what the system has decided for you. Not what happened. Whichever version was sturdiest.” (The proper word for it, though no form in the Annex would ever print it, was decoherence: the environment’s habit of letting only the sturdiest version of events survive being watched.)

Mrs Chain drew herself up. “A sister is not sufficiently similar to a seaside town.”
“No,” Lolly agreed.
“Nor is a town without a sea sufficiently similar to itself,” Mrs Chain added.
“No.”
She folded her gloves again. “Well. I’m glad we agree on fundamentals.”

Lolly looked back at the terminal. The file was changing once more.

    APPROVED

    MISROUTED

    CLOSED

    APPROVED

The statuses cycled with a kind of clerical impatience, as if the machine itself wished she would stop noticing.

She opened her bottom drawer and took out a thin green notebook labelled PRIVATE / UNHELPFUL. It contained several pages of equations, four pages of complaints about the new software, and one pressed bus ticket from a journey that had taken place twice but only on alternate Tuesdays. She wrote:

If a citizen may arrive before deciding, then decision and arrival are no longer properly ordered.

Then beneath it:

If place may be updated to nearest stable version, then location is no longer being treated as factual.

Then, after a moment:

This is going to become a row.

Mrs Chain watched the pencil move. “You’re worried.”
Lolly closed the notebook. “I’m employed.”
“Yes,” Mrs Chain agreed, “but are you worried?”
Lolly considered the matter honestly. “I wasn’t,” she conceded, “until the sea became historical.”

Mrs Chain stood, gathering her gloves. “Excellent.”
“I beg your pardon?”
“I prefer an official who begins worried. It saves time.” She paused, and for a moment the briskness went out of her, leaving something plainer underneath. “I don’t suppose either of us can make the sea reappear by wanting it to. Nor undo whatever my bus did on the way to Whitbury. There’s no sense standing here regretting the shape of the morning. Shall we find out what happens next?”

“Mrs Chain, I’ll need to escalate this.”
“To whom?”

She almost laughed, which would have been unprofessional and also inaccurate, because there was no one in the building to whom one escalated anything with confidence. One merely moved trouble into a higher room and waited. “I’m not yet sure,” she confessed.

As if in answer, there came from the corridor the sound of running. People did not run in the Annex unless the kitchen had reopened unexpectedly or the laws of existence were behaving in a manner likely to reflect badly on management.

A figure appeared at the door: a narrow young man from Internal Intake, breathless and carrying a stack of forms with all the dignity of someone trying not to admit the forms were winning.

“Wren,” he gasped.
Lolly straightened. “Yes?”
“You’ve got the Chain file?”
“Yes.”
“Don’t close it.”
“I hadn’t planned to.”
“Good.”

The young man swallowed. “Three more just came in, three when I left the desk. It didn’t feel like a number that was going to hold still.”

“Three more what?” Lolly asked.

“Premature arrivals. One man reached a funeral before the deceased had committed. A woman in Clerken Rise got off the tram and found she’d already had lunch tomorrow. And somebody in East Wexham has reported commuting home to a house listed as an equivalent residence unit.”

Mrs Chain, still by the chair, demanded very distinctly, “A what?”

The young man looked as if he wished to be elsewhere, preferably in a different branch of causality. “Equivalent residence unit,” he repeated. “It’s on the paperwork.”

Lolly felt something cold settle in the centre of her thoughts. “Who issued the classification?”

The young man held up one of the forms. Across the top, in a new departmental font Lolly already despised, were the words:

    SIMPLIFICATION INITIATIVE

    CIVIC STABILITY THROUGH OUTCOME CONSOLIDATION

Underneath, in print small enough to have been designed not to be read, it added:

Phase One: Stabilisation, status complete.

Lolly looked at that line rather longer than the first.

Lolly took the paper. For a moment the office seemed to become very still. Even the terminal stopped clicking, which was somehow worse. She thought, absurdly, of Gideon walking into the doorframe an hour before, of what he’d said just beforehand, about a system that might use what it thought had happened to decide what was supposed to happen. She had called it an excellent insight. She had not meant it as a diagnosis.

Mrs Chain spoke first. “Ms Wren, is someone altering the world on purpose?”

Lolly glanced from the form to the flickering complaint file, to the notebook, to the pale messenger, and at last back to Mrs Chain, who had arrived somewhere before deciding to go there. She wished very much that the tea had been better.

“I sincerely hope not,” she murmured.

Then the terminal clicked again. A new line appeared across the screen in bright administrative blue:

    CASE ESCALATED

    PLEASE REPORT TO QUEUE MANAGEMENT

Lolly stared at it. There are few phrases in government more sinister than Queue Management. Most of them are longer, and available only in landscape format.

Her own least favourite, circulated by Fiscal Reconciliation the previous spring, asked each citizen to state their income to the nearest degree of certainty, and then, immediately below it, to state how certain they were of that certainty. Lolly had left both boxes blank, on principle, and received a stern reply confirming receipt of nothing.

Mrs Chain picked up her handbag. “Well,” she commented, “that sounds dreadful.”
“Yes,” Lolly agreed, reaching for her coat. “I rather think it does.”

She paused at the door only long enough to do something the terminal had not asked her to do: she took the printed slip (APPROVED, MISROUTED, CLOSED, APPROVED, and now none of those), folded it once, and put it in her own coat pocket instead of the outgoing tray. If the system wished to decide what had happened to Mrs Chain, it would do so with Lolly still in the room. It was a small thing. She did it anyway.

The printer produced a second sheet all by itself:

    FOR THE AVOIDANCE OF CONFUSION

Lolly shut her eyes. This was how serious things generally began. With stationery.

Rule: Before a measurement forces one answer, a quantum state may be more than a list of answers waiting in a drawer.

Lecture for this chapter: Superposition

(If you would rather hold a superposition than take Lolly’s word for it, the back of the book tells you where to try one yourself.)

Chapter 2: The Wrong Queue

The queue to which Lolly had now been summoned was not the ordinary version. She and Mrs Elspeth Chain followed the signs down two flights of stairs, along a beige corridor devoted entirely to lowering expectations, and through a pair of glass doors bearing the legend:

    QUEUE MANAGEMENT

For the Avoidance of Unauthorised Resolution

“That,” pronounced Mrs Chain, reading it once and rejecting it absolutely, “is a revolting sentence.”
“Yes.” “It’s new.”

Lolly did not ask new since when. Some questions, she had learned, were better left to answer themselves, usually badly.

Inside, Queue Management occupied what must once have been an atrium, since persuaded to regret its openness: chrome stanchions, retractable belts, lanes of subtle cruelty under a high ceiling that had given up on grandeur.

The air carried the specific smell of a modern queue: hand sanitiser, hot plastic from the ticket printer, and the flat, coffee-less anxiety of people who had been standing there long enough to consider it a personality trait.

A laminated poster by the coat pegs, left over from an earlier Fiscal Reconciliation campaign, read: YOUR TAX STATUS MAY EXIST IN SUPERPOSITION UNTIL FORMALLY ASSESSED. DO NOT ATTEMPT TO COLLAPSE IT YOURSELF. Beneath it, in biro, someone had added: too late.

At the far end stood six service desks beneath softly glowing signs:

    INITIAL DETERMINATIONS

    REVISIONAL DETERMINATIONS

    POST-DETERMINATE APPEALS

    PRE-RESOLVED EXCEPTIONS

    INTENT ALIGNMENT

    GENERAL ENQUIRIES / NO

Mrs Chain read the last of them aloud. “General Enquiries, slash, no.” She turned to Lolly. “Is that a sign, or is it already the answer?”
“I think,” Lolly ventured, “we’re meant to find out.”

Between the doors and the desks stood perhaps fifty people in a queue which immediately, and with appalling confidence, divided into six.

Lolly stopped. Mrs Chain stopped beside her. “I see,” she concluded, after a moment, “that this is idiotic.”
“Yes,” Lolly murmured, staring ahead. “How does one join it?”
“I’m not yet sure it can be joined in any recognisably civic sense.”

At the entrance stood a free-standing pillar dispensing numbered tickets. This, in any civilised institution, would have been reassuring.

Lolly pressed the button. The machine emitted a mild chime and produced three tickets.

A17.

A17 (provisional).

A17/b.

One of the three was faintly warm, as if it had been printed before she’d arrived to ask for it.

Lolly eyed them in silence.

Mrs Chain took one, adjusted her spectacles, and read it. “What,” she asked, “is a provisional seventeen?”
“I would assume,” Lolly guessed, “a seventeen that has not committed.”
“And the b?”
“I refuse to speculate.”

Near the pillar a young man in a reflective waistcoat was attempting to provide guidance to the public in the bright, exhausted tone of someone whose training had been both inadequate and recent.

“If you are here regarding an unresolved outcome,” he was saying, “please proceed to the queue most closely aligned with your present state. If uncertain, join the uncertainty lane until a more definite uncertainty becomes available.”

A woman clutching a pram asked, “Which one is the uncertainty lane?”
The young man looked around helplessly. “At the moment, madam, all of them.”

Mrs Chain closed her eyes briefly. “Oh, very good. They’ve hired a poet and issued him a lanyard.”

Lolly examined the room. The six lanes were not stable. That was the first problem.

The second was that they were not, in any useful sense, separate. The queue seemed to divide and rejoin itself according to principles more suggestive of philosophy than architecture.

A man in a pinstripe suit occupied two adjacent lanes by means of a posture that deserved a pension. An elderly couple entered Revisional Determinations and emerged in General Enquiries. A student with a rucksack advanced without moving his feet.

Lolly watched for perhaps ten seconds and had the distinct feeling that the room was selecting answers based on the questions people brought into it. She disliked rooms that had opinions.

Somewhere behind her, a man who had been rerouted three times in as many minutes had stopped trying to explain himself at all, and simply let the queue decide. Lolly filed that away as a thing worth being afraid of later.

“Ms Wren?”

She turned.

A woman approached from Desk Three with a file under one arm and the expression of someone who had once been patient and had since overcome it. Dark grey; badge clipped with punitive exactness. This, Lolly realised, would be Desk Three’s idea of help.

She was narrow and upright, and moved with the same brisk economy she brought to a ledger: nothing wasted, nothing repeated.

    BEATRIX SLOAN

Internal Audit

She looked first at Lolly, then at Mrs Chain, then at the three contradictory queue tickets in Lolly’s hand, and seemed not at all surprised.

“You’re the Chain case,” she observed.
Lolly inclined her head. “Lolly Wren. Transit Reconciliation.”
“I know.” She held out a hand to Mrs Chain. “Sloan. Internal Audit.”
Mrs Chain shook it. “Are you in charge of this?”
“No,” Beatrix replied. “Otherwise it would be less decorative.”

Up close she smelled faintly of ink and wet wool, a combination Lolly would learn to recognise from three departments away.

She said it the way people say things they have already written a report about.

She took one of Lolly’s tickets and read it without approval. “They’ve started branching admissions.”
“What does that mean?” asked Lolly.

“It means,” replied Beatrix, “that rather than deciding what category a problem belongs in, the department now permits the problem to be processed under several mutually incompatible interpretations until one of them becomes administratively cheaper.”

Mrs Chain considered this. “That sounds immoral.”
“Yes,” allowed Beatrix, “but efficiently so, which is the current preference.”

She turned and set off toward the lanes without waiting to see if they followed, which they did, because some people generate obedience merely by walking as though corridors owe them truth.

At the mouth of the queue Beatrix stopped and pointed. “Look carefully. Tell me what you see.”

Lolly did. To an untrained eye, the movement of the lanes might have appeared merely disorderly. But once one attended to the signs, a pattern emerged.

Citizens approaching with misplaced luggage tended to be pulled toward General Enquiries / No. Those with contradictory documents drifted toward Revisional Determinations. People with the stunned, inward-looking expression of persons whose day had become ontological were drawn, slowly but unmistakably, toward Intent Alignment.

“Is it sorting by complaint type?” Lolly asked.
“Not exactly,” Beatrix corrected. “It’s sorting by interpretive frame.”
Mrs Chain gave her a sharp look. “In English.”
“The room decides what sort of problem you are having,” Beatrix continued, “and then forces you to become that sort of problem.”

Lolly felt a small, unpleasant quickening in the back of her mind. “Based on what?”
“On what is observed first,” Beatrix answered.

There are sentences which, to a former physicist trapped in public administration, land with the emotional force of a brick through stained glass.

Lolly examined the room again. A man holding a damp briefcase was being asked, at Desk One, whether his issue concerned delay, duplication, or symbolic displacement. Each answer altered the forms placed before him.

A woman near Desk Five had apparently described her missing husband as both late and increasingly hypothetical, and was now being redirected toward Post-Determinate Appeals with an orange folder marked

    DOMESTIC AMBIGUITY.

Lolly ventured quietly, “It’s choosing what kind of complaint it can bear to have.”
Beatrix glanced at her. “I beg your pardon?”
“The system,” she continued, more quickly now. “It isn’t receiving the complaint. It is deciding, from the first question, what sort of person is permitted to bring it. Delay, identity, route, intent. After that, everything has to fit.”

Mrs Chain folded her arms. “So if I say I took the wrong bus, that’s one problem. And if I say I arrived before deciding, that’s another.”
“Exactly,” Lolly agreed. “But they are the same thing.”
“Yes,” Mrs Chain conceded.
“That’s monstrous,” Lolly declared.
“Yes,” offered Beatrix. “But very modern.”

Lolly took out her pencil and, balancing the green notebook against one hand, wrote:

First question becomes first answer.

Then below it:

The queue does not ask. It assigns.

Mrs Chain peered at the page. “That’s not going to improve matters, is it.”
“No,” Lolly granted. “But it may help identify the species of disaster.”

They entered the lane marked

    INTENT ALIGNMENT

At once the carpet changed colour from apologetic blue to a slightly sterner shade of blue, as if to indicate that uncertainty was tolerated here only under supervision. The barriers to either side gave a faint electric hum.

Ahead of them stood five citizens in various states of administrative fatigue: a cyclist received by his office before he had left home, though only emotionally; a teacher whose afternoon had been reassigned to Thursday; a teenager with a parcel who had meant to visit his grandmother “in principle.”

As the line advanced, Lolly noticed something even less encouraging. The questions changed people.

A clerk at the front desk, hidden until now by a partition, was speaking in a soothing voice to the cyclist. “Did you intend,” she asked, “to go to work?”
“Yes, obviously.”
“Fully?”
The cyclist blinked. “What?”
“Would you say your intention was complete, partial, or symbolic?”
“Complete, I suppose.”

The clerk ticked a box. At once the screen beside her updated:

    COMMUTER MISSEQUENCING

    SUBTYPE: PRE-COMMITTED ARRIVAL

The cyclist looked down at the label that had printed for him, and in that instant his whole complaint seemed to shrink. He began repeating the clerk’s terminology back to her, as though his own experience had already been translated into something smaller and easier to store.

The teacher stepped up next. Her account of a missing afternoon was reframed, within seconds, as

    TEMPORAL MISALLOCATION WITH EDUCATIONAL IMPACT

By the time she left the desk, she seemed almost persuaded that the matter concerned staffing.

Lolly wondered, not for the last time that morning, what happened to the ones who never got persuaded.

Mrs Chain remarked under her breath, “They file you until you agree.”
“Yes,” Lolly agreed.
Beatrix, ahead of them, nodded once. “That’s the model.”

Then it was Mrs Chain’s turn.

The clerk looked up with the professionally laundered smile of a person trained never to acknowledge originality in suffering. “Good morning. Nature of unresolved intent discrepancy?”
Mrs Chain set her handbag down on the counter with deliberate care. “I went to visit my sister in Whitbury-on-Sea.”
“Certainly.”
“I had not entirely decided to go.”
“Yes.”
“And when I arrived, the town seemed to be missing part of its sea.”
The clerk’s fingers paused above the keyboard. “Was the destination emotionally authorised prior to embarkation?”
Mrs Chain stared at her. “It was what?”
“Emotionally authorised.”
“No,” retorted Mrs Chain. “It was my sister.”
The clerk blinked. “Would you classify your journey as voluntary, reluctant, habitual, or familial?”
Mrs Chain drew herself up. “I would classify it as none of your business.”

Lolly had noticed this about her before. Mrs Chain did not require the world to make sense before she would proceed through it; she simply proceeded, sense or no sense, the way a person crosses a road they disapprove of, and left the arguing for whatever came after. It was not defiance exactly. It cost less than defiance, and lasted longer.

Lolly almost admired the speed with which Beatrix stepped in. “Internal Audit,” she announced, placing her badge on the counter. “This case is under review.”
The clerk’s face changed by perhaps half a millimetre, which in administrative environments counts as a passionate emotional event. “Of course,” she allowed. “Then I’ll need Form IA-9 for diverted interpretive handling.”
“You will not,” countered Beatrix. “You will give me the current classification tree and the routing logic for this lane.”
“That material is not available at desk level.”
“Then fetch someone to whom availability has happened.”

The clerk hesitated. There are many people in bureaucracy who wield power, but very few who wield certainty. Beatrix Sloan wielded certainty in a way that made lesser officials briefly consider confession.

Precisely one creature in Beatrix’s acquaintance remained wholly unimpressed by this: her next-door neighbour’s cat, which had disliked her on sight some years previously, for no reason either party had ever cared to establish — allegedly — and pursued the position with a consistency the Ministry itself might have envied.

Beatrix had long suspected, though never proven, that the animal belonged to the elderly gentleman two doors further along, who was sometimes seen observing it through his front window with an expression of what could only be called profound scientific interest, and who had introduced himself, on the one occasion they spoke, simply as “a retired physicist. Allegedly.” She had decided, on reflection, not to ask him about the box.

The clerk rose and vanished through a side door.

Mrs Chain turned. “I like her.”
“I noticed,” Lolly noted.
“Does she ever smile?”
“Possibly in private.”
“Wasteful.”

While they waited, Lolly examined the counter terminal. On the edge of the screen, where some programmer had assumed no one with curiosity would ever be employed, ran a diagnostic panel:

    PRIMARY MEASUREMENT FRAME: INTENT

    SECONDARY FRAME: ROUTE

    TERTIARY FRAME: CIVIC STABILITY

    AUTO-COLLAPSE ENABLED: YES

Lolly’s mouth went dry. She copied it into the notebook, word for word.

A measurement basis, she thought, was nothing more sinister than a choice of question. Ask a particle where it is, and it will oblige you with a position. Ask instead how fast it is going, and it will oblige you with a speed. It will not give you both, and, this was the part the public never forgave physics for, it will not tell you which one was “really” true before you asked, because there is no fact sitting underneath the choice, waiting to be read off. The universe does not keep a warehouse of pre-written answers. It manufactures the one you ordered, and quietly discards the stock it didn’t make.

Ask this room whether a citizen’s trouble is about intent, and intent is what collapses out, tidy and specific and stripped of everything else it might have been. Ask about route instead, and you get a route. Neither answer is a lie. That was the horror of it. Both were true, right up until the asking, and only one of them was ever permitted to survive contact with a form.

Auto-collapse. That was not a phrase one used by accident.

She drew a line beneath it and wrote:

Ask different first question, obtain different world.

A quiet voice at her elbow murmured, “That’s true more often than people care to admit.”

Lolly turned. The speaker was the thin young man she had noticed only in passing when they entered: dark coat, narrow face, stack of sealed envelopes under one arm, the general air of someone who belonged everywhere on a provisional basis. He had somehow arrived beside them without ever seeming to cross the intervening space.

His voice matched the rest of him: unhurried, faintly amused, and pitched low enough that people found themselves leaning in before they’d decided the sentence was worth it.

He smelled, up close, of biro ink and other people’s post — the nearest thing to a warning Lolly would get, in the weeks to come, that Jago Flint had arrived somewhere.

“I’m sorry?” Lolly asked. The young man nodded toward the notebook. “Different question, different world. Sensible. Bit gloomy, but sensible.” Mrs Chain eyed him. “Were you in this queue?” “Briefly.” “And now?” “I’ve improved my position by not belonging to it.”

Beatrix looked him over. “Who are you?”
“Jago Flint.” He produced an identification card so quickly and casually that Lolly entirely missed where it had been kept.

Interdepartmental Courier Service Restricted / Floating Clearance

“Floating clearance?” Lolly asked.
Jago smiled. “Means I’m authorised where things aren’t fully settled.”
“That can’t be an actual credential,” Lolly objected.
“Ms Wren,” Jago noted pleasantly, “neither, at present, can your sea.”

Before Lolly could reply, the side door reopened and the clerk returned with three forms, a thin man in a mustard tie, and an atmosphere of immediate uselessness.

“This is Deputy Lane Supervisor Pritchard,” she announced.

Pritchard had a stooped, hesitant manner, as though he had been promoted specifically because he would not resist language. He gave them all a smile of deep institutional sadness.

“What seems to be the difficulty?”

Beatrix handed him Mrs Chain’s printout, Lolly’s contradictory tickets, and the audit badge in one economical movement. “The difficulty,” she explained, “is that Queue Management is collapsing cases into interpretive states based on intake phrasing.”
Pritchard glanced at the papers. “Ah.”
“Do you deny it?”
“No,” he admitted. “That is the intended service enhancement.”

“Service enhancement?” blurted Lolly, before she could help herself.
“Yes. Citizens were becoming distressed by prolonged ambiguity. The new system ensures each event is processed under a stable descriptive regime at the earliest convenience.”
“But if the description is wrong,” Lolly pressed, “the outcome is wrong.”
Pritchard offered a smile of almost paternal despair. “Ms Wren, outcomes are more manageable when they are stable.”
“That is not the same thing.”
“No,” allowed Pritchard. “But it is budgetable.”

There was a brief silence. Somewhere across the room a woman began arguing that her fiancé had split into a weekday version and a weekend version, both of which were disappointing in different ways.

Two lanes over, a man was explaining to a clerk that his tax return could not possibly be overdue, since the assessment period it belonged to had not yet been finalised. The clerk agreed entirely, apologised for the inconvenience, and marked it overdue anyway, on the grounds that lateness, once observed, has a way of backdating itself. He left holding a receipt for a fine he had not, strictly speaking, earned yet.

Lolly turned a hard look on Pritchard. “Who authorised auto-collapse?”
Pritchard’s expression altered very slightly. “That terminology is internal.”
“So is panic,” put in Beatrix, “but here we are.”

Pritchard cleared his throat. “The Simplification Initiative has determined that multiple coexisting interpretations of public events create undue load on systems, staff, and public confidence.”
Mrs Chain asked, “Public confidence in what?” No one answered.

Jago, leaning one shoulder against the barrier as though he had always been part of the architecture, remarked, “That’s usually a bad sign. The fuller answer is probably restricted to senior management.”

Lolly held up the printout. “Whitbury-on-Sea has been updated to its nearest stable version.”
Pritchard nodded sympathetically. “Coastal redundancies are outside my lane.”
“It has a sea in the name,” Lolly persisted.
“Yes,” Pritchard offered, “but names can be legacy features.”

Mrs Chain made a sound that would have done credit to artillery.

Beatrix stepped forward. “I want the routing logic, the collapse parameters, and the policy authorisation.”
“I’m afraid I can’t release—”
“Then I’ll seize.”

Pritchard looked at her, then at the badge, then at the growing unease around the room as more people noticed that their complaints were being translated into sanctioned nonsense.

Lolly saw the moment at which the deputy supervisor wrought his choice. It was not a noble choice, or even a thoughtful one. It was the small, bleak calculation of a bureaucrat determining which form of trouble might leave fewer marks.

He reached into his folder and withdrew a pale blue memorandum. “This,” he confided carefully, “was circulated yesterday under restricted headings. I should not, strictly speaking, have brought it to floor level.”

Beatrix took it.

Across the top, in a typeface Lolly already despised on moral grounds, were the words:

    SIMPLIFICATION INITIATIVE PHASE ONE

    IMPLEMENTATION NOTES

Below that: To minimise interpretive drift, all citizen-facing anomalies are to be resolved within the first viable measurement frame.

Lolly read the sentence twice. Then a third time.

There are few moments in life when one feels the exact hinge on which a disaster turns. Lolly felt this one with mathematical clarity.

They were not merely mishandling reality. They were teaching it to answer too soon.

At the bottom of the page, almost as an afterthought, a line in smaller type read: Where persistence of alternate states produces resistance, escalate to Continuity Review.

Lolly’s stomach dropped half a second before the rest of her caught up with why. Resistance. Mrs Chain, not four minutes ago, refusing outright to be filed as voluntary, reluctant, habitual, or familial. Refusing, in the department’s own vocabulary, to resolve.

She did not say this aloud. There did not seem to be a version of saying it aloud that improved anyone’s afternoon.

Jago let out a low whistle. Mrs Chain asked, “What is Continuity Review?” No one replied at once, because all four of them, in their different ways, understood that some phrases explain themselves.

Across the room, a printer began to chatter. Then another. Then another. Sheets emerged at half a dozen desks simultaneously. The clerks read them, and several visibly paled.

Pritchard took one from the nearest tray, glanced down, and closed his eyes.

“What now,” demanded Beatrix.
He swallowed. “We appear to have an expansion event.”
Lolly felt her stomach perform a small, unauthorised administrative review. “Meaning?”

Pritchard handed her the notice.

    IN RESPONSE TO INCREASED PUBLIC VARIANCE

    QUEUE MANAGEMENT WILL NOW OPERATE IN PARALLEL MODES

As she looked up, the six service signs at the far end of the room flickered. There was a soft electrical sigh. Then each sign doubled.

Twelve desks. Twelve lanes. Twelve different ways, Lolly thought, for the wrong question to become official.

She glanced down at her tickets. There were four of them now. She did not remember queueing for a fourth.

Beside her, Jago Flint smiled in a way that was either delighted or appalled, and with him one could never be sure that the distinction existed. “Well,” he mused, “that’s new.”

Beatrix snatched the memo from Lolly’s hand. Mrs Chain clutched her handbag like a weapon of last constitutional resort. Somewhere behind them a child asked whether they were all in the wrong line and was told, with admirable honesty, that no one knew.

Lolly opened the green notebook and wrote only one sentence.

The queue is measuring the public.

Then she shut it.

Because ahead of them, under a newly lit sign reading PROVISIONAL REALITY ADJUSTMENTS, a door had opened that Lolly was quite certain had not been there before. And from beyond it came the unmistakable sound of someone important approaching with papers, and the particular hush that only precedes a person who has never had to knock.

They did not wait to find out who. Beatrix was already three strides down the corridor, on the grounds, offered without turning round, that whoever it was could file a complaint like everybody else.

Lolly did not know what came through doors like that. She had a shrewd and unhappy suspicion she would find out eventually. She followed anyway, which was not nothing, for a woman who had spent most of her life reading exits before entrances.

They did not stop until the revolving doors of the Annex had spat them out onto the pavement, the hush and the unopened door already three departments behind. Whoever had been about to walk through it would simply have to wait its turn, like everyone else in Queue Management. Lolly filed the thought as unfinished business, in the only place she had left for such things, and did not open it again that morning.

Rule: Ask reality through the wrong sorting frame, and the answer may be the only part of it your apparatus has left standing.
