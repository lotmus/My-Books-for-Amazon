By Wednesday morning, Derek Gent had decided that there were two kinds of impossible. The first kind was the ordinary sort. A man could not be in two places at once.
A clear photograph could not normally exist before it was taken. A clock could not run backwards without either being broken or being subjected to a level of engineering that Derek considered unnecessarily ambitious. The second kind was considerably worse.
The second kind was something that was perfectly consistent with the laws of physics but completely inconsistent with common sense. Derek had spent most of his professional life dealing with the second kind. He found it much more expensive.

The mug on the windowsill was empty again. No one had drunk from it.
Others simply rearranged the furniture and waited for you to notice they had always been there.
He had never been greedy about problems. Problems, in his experience, had a habit of multiplying without need of encouragement.
Still, the Bureau was usually good at inventing new problems once the old ones became inconvenient.
Derek suspected the universe operated on similar principles, apparently. The evidence was, by now, fairly strong.
He was beginning to think the universe and the Bureau had more in common than was strictly healthy — both specialised in rules that only became visible after you had already broken them.
