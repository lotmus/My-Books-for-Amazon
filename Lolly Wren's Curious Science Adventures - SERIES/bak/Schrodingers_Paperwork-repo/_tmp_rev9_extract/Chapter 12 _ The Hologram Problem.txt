Derek was still staring at himself. The other Derek was still sitting on the mysterious train. The train was still sitting in the distant station.
The empty station clock still read 11:17. The cucumber was still in Penny’s hand. Trevor was still trying very hard not to say anything stupid.
This was proving difficult. The other Derek raised the ordinary cucumber again. Derek raised an eyebrow. “Why has he got a cucumber?”
Penny regarded him carefully. “Why have you got a cucumber?”
“I haven’t.”
She held hers up. “No. You have.”
Derek glanced down. He was holding a cucumber. He stared at it.
Then at Penny. Then at the other Derek.
“Ah.”
Trevor sighed. “That clears everything up.”
The other Derek smiled. Then the train doors closed. The train departed.
Derek ran after it.
“Wait!”
The empty train disappeared into the deep tunnel. Derek stopped. He watched the empty track.
Then at the cucumber in his hand.
“Right.”
Penny walked over. “You’ve got a cucumber.”
“Apparently.”
“Where did you get it?”
“I don’t know.”
“You were holding it.”
“I know.”
“Then you got it somewhere.”
“That’s annoyingly logical.”
Trevor surveyed the dark tunnel. “Perhaps we should ask whether the other Derek is real.”
Derek met his gaze. “Excellent question.”
Penny nodded. “How would we know?”
Trevor thought. “If he can interact with things…”
“He can.”
“If he has a worldline…”
“Apparently.”
“If he has proper time…”
“Unknown.”
“If he can eat a cucumber…”
Derek regarded him. “Why would that prove anything?”
Trevor shrugged. “It would at least be satisfying.”
Penny laughed. Derek examined the cucumber. Then at the dark tunnel.
Then at the clock. 11:17.
He suddenly smiled. “Maybe we’ve been asking the wrong question.”
Penny nodded. “Again?”
“Yes.”
“What is it this time?”
“Not whether the other Derek is real.”
“Then?”
“Whether reality itself is real in the way we think it is.”
Trevor frowned. “That’s rather broad.”
“Yes.”
“How broad?”
Derek inspected the ceiling. “Potentially four-dimensional.”
Penny smiled. “Or more.”
Derek nodded. “Or fewer.”
Trevor looked sharply concerned. “Fewer?”
“Don’t worry.”
“That’s not reassuring.”
Derek began walking.
“Come on.”
“Where?”
“Back to the Bureau.”
“Why?”
“Because I have an idea.”
Penny followed. Trevor followed Penny. The cucumber came too.
The idea was not Derek’s.
That was the first thing he admitted when they reached the Bureau. “The holographic principle.”
Trevor wearily sat down. “That’s not your idea.”
“I know.”
“Who proposed it?”
“Several people contributed to the development.”
“Fine.”
“Gerard ‘t Hooft and Leonard Susskind are particularly important.”
Trevor nodded. “And Witten?”
“Edward Witten was important to the later development connecting holography with string theory.”
“Right.”
“Imagine,” Derek said, “that you could describe everything happening inside a box using information written only on the surface.”
Trevor frowned. “Everything?”
“In principle.”
“That's backwards.”
“Exactly.”
Derek drew the cube again. “Normally we'd expect a bigger box to contain proportionally more information. Double the dimensions, and the volume goes up enormously.”
He shaded the surface.
“But black holes suggest something stranger. Their entropy—the amount of information associated with them—scales with the area of the boundary.”
Penny looked at the cube. “So the surface somehow knows what's inside.”
“That's the astonishing part.”
“Like a hologram?”
“That's where the name comes from. But don't take the analogy too literally.”
Penny sighed. “There goes my giant cosmic cinema screen.”
“I'm afraid so.”
Trevor pointed at the board. “And this is where string theory comes in?”
Derek nodded. “One of the most famous examples is called AdS/CFT. Very roughly, it says that a theory involving gravity in one description can be equivalent to a quantum theory without gravity in another.”
Trevor stared at him. “You said ‘roughly.’”
“Because the real thing is considerably more complicated.”
“Good.”
“But the idea is extraordinary. Something that looks like spacetime and gravity in one description can emerge from something that looks completely different in another.”
Penny looked at the board. “So spacetime might not be fundamental.”
Derek paused. “Possibly.”
“Then what is it made from?”
Derek looked at the equations. “That's where things get really interesting.”
Trevor waited. “Information. Quantum states. Entanglement. Relationships between things.”
Penny stared at him. “That's a lot of things.”
“Yes.”
Derek stepped back.
The board was now covered.
Spacetime
Curvature
Worldlines
Information
Entanglement
Holography
He gazed at the ordinary cucumber.
It was, Derek reflected, precisely the kind of idea that made a stopped clock and a train that only sometimes existed feel less like anomalies and more like symptoms. “And now we have a problem.”
Trevor softly sighed. “What?”
“If spacetime is emergent…”
“Yes?”
“Can Special Relativity still be true?”
Trevor thought. “Why not?”
“Can General Relativity?”
“Why not?”
“Can both?”
“Why not?”
Derek smiled. “Quite.”
Penny nodded. “The laws we observe don’t necessarily have to disappear merely because there’s a deeper description.”
“Correct.”
Trevor consulted the board.
“The temperature of a gas is real even though it’s emergent from microscopic motion.”
“Exactly.”
“Fluid pressure is real.”
“Yes.”
“Sound waves are real.”
“Yes.”
“Yet none of those are fundamental microscopic objects.”
“Correct.”
Derek pointed at the word SPACETIME. “So, perhaps spacetime could be like that.”
Penny nodded. “Real without being fundamental.”
“Exactly.”
Trevor considered it in silence. “Then SR could describe the local behaviour of the emergent spacetime.”
“Yes.”
“And GR could describe its large-scale geometry.”
“Yes.”
“While the deeper holographic theory describes the underlying information.”
“Exactly.”
Derek smiled. “Well, that’s settled.”
Penny regarded him carefully. “Not quite.”
Derek paused. “Why?”
“Because we still have the cucumber.”
He looked down. The cucumber was sitting on the table. Derek stared at it. “Yes.”
Penny picked it up. “If spacetime is emergent…”
“Yes.”
“And the cucumber is made of atoms…”
“Yes.”
“And the atoms are described quantum mechanically…”
“Yes.”
“And the geometry in which those atoms exist might itself be emergent…”
“Yes.”
“Then the cucumber is an emergent object in an emergent spacetime.”
Derek smiled. “That sounds unnecessarily complicated.”
Penny shrugged. “It’s a cucumber.”
Trevor nodded. “That simplifies things.”
Derek laughed. “Does it?”
“Yes.”
“How?”
“We know it exists.”
Derek watched him. “Do we?”
Trevor held up the cucumber. “Yes.”
“How?”
Trevor considered this. “It’s here.”
Derek smiled. “Excellent epistemology.”
Penny took the ordinary cucumber from him. “Perhaps we should test it.”
Derek looked suspicious. “How?”
“Measure it.”
“With what?”
“A scale.”
“Fine.”
She weighed it. Trevor recorded the result. Penny measured its length.
Then circumference. Then temperature. Then mass distribution.
Derek intently watched. “What are we proving?”
“Nothing.”
“Good.”
“We’re establishing that the cucumber has observable properties.”
“Yes.”
“And if the holographic description is correct, those properties must ultimately be encoded in the underlying degrees of freedom.”
Derek nodded. “Yes.”
“So, the cucumber can be described in more than one way.”
“Like spacetime.”
“Just so.”
Trevor fell into thought. “Could the cucumber itself have a holographic description?”
Derek smiled. “Of course.”
“Really?”
“In principle.”
Trevor surveyed it. “That’s extraordinary.”
Derek nodded. “The information describing the physical system can have different representations.”
Penny smiled. “Just don’t tell the cucumber.”
“Why?”
“It may become arrogant.”
Derek checked his watch. Still 11:17.
Somewhere, he suspected, a different representation of the same eleven minutes was already causing trouble.
