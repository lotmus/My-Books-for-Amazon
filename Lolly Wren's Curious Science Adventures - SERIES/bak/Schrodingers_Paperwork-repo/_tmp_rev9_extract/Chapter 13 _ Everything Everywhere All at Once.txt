Penny had decided that Derek was becoming unbearable. Not because he was wrong. That would have been easier.
It was because he was usually right. There was something deeply irritating about a man who could spend an afternoon discussing curved spacetime, singularities and the holographic principle and then, when Penny pointed out an obvious practical difficulty, simply say:
“Yes.”
As though the universe had just been waiting for him to catch up. So, Penny went to the cinema. Alone.
This was not particularly unusual. What was unusual was the film she chose.
Everything Everywhere All at Once
She settled into her seat with popcorn and a drink and watched the opening credits. Then she saw her.
Penny sat upright. “Blimey.”
Jamie Lee Curtis.
She had survived Halloween after all.
Apparently, having spent several films being pursued by a man who refused to die, she had decided that the next sensible career move was to become an IRS employee and spend her time tormenting bewildered Asians across multiple universes.
Penny smiled. “Americans.”
She took a handful of popcorn. The film began.
Within minutes there were universes.
Then other universes. Then other versions of the same people. Then universes in which perfectly ordinary things had become completely ridiculous.
Penny watched. She became increasingly interested. Not because the film was physically accurate.
It wasn’t. Nor was it trying to be. But the underlying idea was strangely familiar.
Different possible realities.
Different versions of people.
Different histories.
Different outcomes.
Different choices.
A universe in which one thing happened.
Another in which it didn’t.
Penny thought about Derek.
She thought about the clock.
She thought about the distant train that apparently existed in one description of reality and didn’t in another. And about Weinstein’s question…
If spacetime itself could be emergent, what exactly did it mean for there to be one universe?
She leaned back. The film continued.
Michelle Yeoh’s character moved through wildly different versions of reality.
Penny smiled. “Right.”
A woman sitting several seats away glanced at her.
Penny lowered her strange voice. “This is actually rather relevant.”
The woman looked away.
Penny continued watching.
The film’s multiverse was not the same thing as the multiverse physicists sometimes discuss.
That distinction mattered. But it was useful as an intuition pump.
Suppose reality could be described in many different ways.
Suppose an observer occupied one particular history. Suppose other histories existed in some mathematical sense.
Then the question became:
What was fundamental?
The particular history?
The collection of histories?
The mathematical structure connecting them?
Or something deeper still? Penny thought about holography.
If a higher-dimensional gravitational description could be equivalent to a lower-dimensional quantum description, then perhaps what looked sharply like an enormous four-dimensional reality from one perspective could be represented by something radically different from another.
Not a movie.
Not a projection in the everyday sense.
A mathematically equivalent description. Two apparently different descriptions. One underlying physics.
She smiled. “Now we’re getting somewhere.”
On screen, the characters continued jumping between realities.
Penny watched Jamie Lee Curtis. “Still alive.”
She shook her head.
“She really does have remarkable career longevity.”
Then another universe suddenly mysteriously appeared. Penny frowned. Something about it bothered her.
Not the physics.
The logic. If every possible version of a person existed, what distinguished one version from another?
Their history.
Their state.
Their relationships.
Their information.
Penny sat forward. Information.
That was the word. Everything came back to information.
A physical system could be described by its state.
A clock had a state.
An ordinary cucumber had a state.
A person had a state.
A train had a state.
A universe had a state.
And perhaps the geometry of spacetime itself emerged from relationships between quantum states.
She took out her phone. She typed: Holography: information on boundary → bulk geometry?
She typed a second line, read it back, and deleted it. It had sounded profound for exactly four seconds before revealing itself as nonsense.
Then: Could different descriptions correspond to same physics?
Then: SR and GR survive because they are emergent descriptions? She paused.
Then added: Ask Alfred.
She put the phone away.
On screen, Jamie Lee Curtis was once again doing something completely unreasonable.
Penny smiled. “Americans.”
A few minutes later she laughed.
Then laughed again. Then stopped.
Because something on the screen had triggered a thought.
The characters weren’t merely travelling through space. They were switching between descriptions of reality.
The important thing was not that they suddenly moved from one physical universe to another.
The important thing was that the information defining the character changed.
Different memories.
Different relationships.
Different physical histories.
Different versions of the same person.
Penny thought about worldlines.
A worldline was a history through spacetime. But if spacetime itself was emergent, perhaps the deeper theory did not contain worldlines at all.
Perhaps worldlines were emergent too.
That would mean that Derek wasn’t fundamentally a line through spacetime.
He was a particular physical state, embedded in a network of relationships.
His apparent history was one way of describing that state and its correlations.
Penny smiled. “That explains the other Derek.”
Then she frowned. “No, it doesn’t.”
If Derek was simply a state rather than a line, there was nothing in the mathematics that said there could only be one of him.
Two identical states, two separate histories, no contradiction anywhere in the equations.
That was the trouble with elegant explanations.
They had an alarming tendency to explain slightly too much.
The woman beside her looked over again.
Penny whispered: “Sorry.”
The film continued. Penny watched.
Suddenly the multiverse stopped being merely funny.
It became useful. Not as physics. As a question.
If there were many mathematically possible descriptions of reality, what made one description equivalent to another?
What had to remain unchanged?
The answer, she realised, had to involve observables.
Physics couldn’t depend on what coordinates someone chose.
It couldn’t depend on whether someone described the world using one mathematical language or another.
The measurable predictions had to agree.
That was already familiar from relativity.
Coordinates could change. Physics didn’t.
A Lorentz transformation changed the coordinates assigned to events.
It didn’t change the invariant interval. A different coordinate system could make the mathematics look different. The physics remained.
Perhaps holographic duality was a much more radical version of the same philosophical lesson.
One description could have gravity and curved spacetime.
Another could have quantum fields without gravity.
Yet they could encode the same physics.
Penny sat back. “Bloody hell.”
The woman beside her looked again.
Penny smiled. “Physics.”
The woman nodded uncertainly.
On screen, everything became even more absurd.
Penny laughed.
Then she suddenly remembered something.
The cucumber.
She had left it at the office. “That’s the straw that broke the camel’s toaster.”
Penny turned to him. “I don’t think that proverb has ever existed.”
Derek nodded. “Neither does the train, apparently.”
A mysterious message from Derek.
WHERE ARE YOU?
Penny typed:
Cinema
Three dots appeared.
WHAT ARE YOU WATCHING?
She replied:
Everything Everywhere All at Once.
The reply came almost immediately.
WHY?
Penny smiled. She typed: Because you keep talking about holography and I wanted to see what the Americans think it means.
There was a pause. Then:
AND?
Penny stared at the screen.
She typed: Jamie Lee Curtis survived Halloween.
Another pause. Then:
THAT’S YOUR PHYSICS CONCLUSION?
Penny smiled. She gazed at the screen. Then typed:
My physics conclusion is that if different descriptions can encode the same underlying reality, SR and GR don’t need to be fundamental to be true.
Derek replied:
Correct.
Penny smiled. Then another sudden message mysteriously appeared.
ALSO, JAMIE LEE CURTIS DID NOT “SURVIVE HALLOWEEN” IN THE PHYSICS SENSE.
Penny frowned. She typed:
You know what I meant.
I don’t.
Americans
A pause. Then:
That settles it.
Penny laughed. She put the phone away. Then she noticed something. On the screen, a character was holding a cucumber.
Penny silently stared. Surely not. She leaned forward.
There it was. A cucumber.
She laughed. “You’re having a laugh.”
The woman beside her studied the screen. “What?”
“The cucumber.”
“What about it?”
Penny stared intently. “Nothing.”
She reached into her handbag. Her own cucumber was still there. She looked from the ordinary cucumber in her bag to the cucumber on the screen.
Then back again. She frowned. The one on the screen was not identical.
Of course it wasn’t. That would have been ridiculous. Still…
Penny took a vivid photograph of the screen. She sent it to Derek.
LOOK.
His reply came almost immediately.
I’M LOOKING.
Then:
THAT’S A CUCUMBER.
Penny smiled.
Yes.
So? She typed:
Nothing.
She paused. Then:
Yet.
She put the phone away. The film continued. Penny watched the characters collide with one absurd reality after another.
Then she thought about the Bureau.
About Derek. About Weinstein. About Barbarian.
About the clocks. About the cold rain. About the train. About the other Derek.
Perhaps the Relativistic Investigation Bureau had been approaching the problem backwards.
They had assumed that something strange was happening inside spacetime.
What if the strange thing was the relationship between different descriptions of spacetime?
What if the clocks weren’t moving incorrectly?
What if they belonged to different descriptions?
What if the mysterious train wasn’t travelling through a strange geometry?
What if the train was the strange geometry?
Penny wearily sat very still. Then she smiled. She took out her phone.
She wrote one final sudden message to Derek.
Don’t investigate the clocks.
His reply came:
Why?
She typed:
Investigate the descriptions
A pause. Then:
Explain
Penny examined the film. Then at the ordinary cucumber. Then at the rain running down the cinema window.
She wrote:
Maybe the universe doesn’t have one story.
She thought for a moment.
Then added:
Maybe it has several descriptions that all have to agree on what can actually happen.
Derek replied:
Now you’re talking.
Penny smiled. She put the phone away.
On the screen, everything was happening everywhere all at once.
Penny watched for another minute.
Then quietly mentioned: “Americans.”
She ate another piece of popcorn. “That settles it.”
And somewhere in London, a clock stopped at 11:17. Again.
