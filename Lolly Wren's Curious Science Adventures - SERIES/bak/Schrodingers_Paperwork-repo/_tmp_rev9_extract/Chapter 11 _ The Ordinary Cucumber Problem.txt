Penny had a theory. It was not a particularly complicated theory.
Nor, for once, did it involve spacetime. She needed an ordinary cucumber. She was standing in an organic fruit market in London, looking at a crate of them while a man in a green apron explained the virtues of soil, “The important thing,” he asserted, “is that they’re grown naturally.”
Penny picked one up. “Naturally?”
“Without synthetic pesticides.”
“Right.”
She examined it. “Are cucumbers fruits?”
The man hesitated. “Botanically, yes.”
Penny nodded. “Excellent.”
“But culinarily—”
“Don’t.”
He stopped. Penny put the cucumber in her basket. She had no particular interest in the botanical classification of cucumbers.
She had an interest in what they could be used for. And at the moment, she needed one for an experiment. Nothing sexual.
Nothing particularly mysterious. Just a cucumber. The vendor watched her. “Anything else?”
“No.”
“Tomatoes?”
“No.”
“Courgettes?”
“No.”
“Organic aubergines?”
“No.”
He looked disappointed. “Just the cucumber.”
“Very well.”
Penny paid. She left. It was raining.
Of course it was. London had apparently decided that weather was unnecessary and rain was sufficient. She walked back toward the Bureau with the cucumber under her coat.
She crossed the street. A bus went past. Water sprayed from its wheels.
She watched the droplets scatter. She smiled. Everything had a frame.
Everything had a worldline. Everything had relationships. Even cucumbers.
Perhaps especially cucumbers. She reached the Investigation Bureau. Trevor was sitting at the familiar front desk.
He gazed at the cucumber. Then at Penny. Then at the cucumber again.
“What is that?”
“A cucumber.”
“I can see that.”
“Then why ask?”
Trevor nodded. “True.”
He peered at it again.
“What are you doing with it?”
“Experiment.”
“What sort?”
“Relativistic.”
Trevor nodded. “Of course.”
Penny walked past him. He followed. “Is it dangerous?”
“No.”
“Explosive?”
“No.”
“Quantum?”
“No.”
“Gravitational?”
“Possibly.”
Trevor stopped. “With a cucumber?”
Penny turned back. “You’d be surprised what can be gravitational.”
Trevor considered that. “Fair enough.”
They entered the laboratory. Derek was there. His familiar office door was quietly closed.
Obviously. Trevor pointed toward it. “He’s in there.”
“Working?”
“Probably.”
Penny smiled. “Or shagging.”
Trevor wearily sighed. “Please don’t.”
“Why?”
“Because I know what you’re going to say.”
Penny smiled. “Then you have excellent predictive powers.”
She placed the ordinary cucumber on the laboratory table. Trevor eyed it.
Derek’s voice came through the quietly closed door. “What are you two doing?”
Penny called back, “Science.”
“Good.”
A pause. “Carry on.”
The door remained closed. Trevor nodded. “He’s working hard.”
“Obviously.”
Penny picked up the cucumber. “Right.”
Trevor watched. “What exactly are you testing?”
Penny watched the cucumber. Then at the silent clock. Then at the equipment.
“Whether an ordinary object can help us detect the strange change in the local frame that Derek encountered on the Underground.”
Trevor’s expression changed.
“Ah.”
“Exactly.”
She placed the ordinary cucumber on a small platform. The platform was mounted on a sensitive motion sensor. Trevor leaned over. “You’re using it as a test mass.”
“Yes.”
“Why a cucumber?”
Penny surveyed him. “Because it’s convenient.”
“Why not a metal sphere?”
“Because we’ve already tested those.”
“And?”
“Nothing.”
“And cucumbers?”
“We haven’t tested one.”
Trevor nodded. “That’s one less variable, then.”
Penny smiled. “Precisely.”
She switched on the apparatus. The cold platform began to move. The cucumber rolled slightly.
Then stopped. Trevor watched the graph. A smooth curve suddenly appeared on the monitor.
Then— A tiny discontinuity. Trevor frowned. “That’s odd.”
Penny nodded. “Very.”
The platform returned to its starting position. She reset the experiment. Again.
The same thing happened. Smooth acceleration. Tiny discontinuity.
Smooth acceleration. Trevor stared at the screen. “Same position.”
“Yes.”
“Same acceleration?”
“Almost exactly.”
“Same discontinuity?”
“Yes.”
He studied the cucumber. “Perhaps cucumbers are strange.”
Penny smiled. “That’s what I’m hoping.”
They replaced the cucumber with a wooden block. They ran the experiment. The discontinuity remained.
They replaced the block with a brass sphere. Same result. Then a glass sphere.
Same result. Then a bag of sugar. Same result.
Trevor regarded the collection of objects. “The cucumber is innocent.”
Penny nodded. “Completely.”
“Poor thing.”
Penny picked it up. “You can go back to being a fruit.”
She put it beside the apparatus. Trevor examined the graph. “It’s not the test object.”
“No.”
“It’s not the apparatus.”
“Apparently not.”
“It’s not the acceleration.”
“Not ordinary acceleration.”
Trevor inspected her. “What does that mean?”
Penny sat down. “Imagine you’re in a train.”
“All right.”
“The train begins accelerating.”
“Yes.”
“You feel yourself pushed backward.”
“Yes.”
“But if you’re inside a sufficiently small room, you could describe the effect as though there were a gravitational field pointing backward.”
Trevor nodded. “The equivalence principle.”
“Just so.”
“Locally, acceleration and gravity can be indistinguishable.”
“Yes.”
“But only locally.”
“Right.”
“Because a real gravitational field can produce tidal effects.”
Penny smiled. “Exactly.”
Trevor considered the graph again. “So, we’re looking for something that isn’t simply acceleration.”
“Yes.”
“Something that changes from one location to another.”
“Yes.”
“Tidal effects.”
“Possibly.” Penny stood. She moved the sensor lonely platform a metre to the left. They ran the test.
The discontinuity occurred. She moved it a metre to the right. Again.
She moved it upward. Again.
Trevor stared. “Everywhere.”
“Yes.”
“Then it’s not a local effect.”
Penny shook her head. “Not necessarily.”
“Why?”
“Because our laboratory is tiny compared with the scale of the geometry we’re investigating.”
Trevor nodded. “So, the curvature could be approximately uniform across the room.”
“Exactly.”
“Then we’d need a larger baseline.”
Penny smiled. “Now you’re thinking.”
Trevor glanced at the ceiling. “The Underground.”
“Yes.”
“The whole train.”
“Yes.”
“Perhaps the whole station.”
“Yes.”
“The whole city?”
“Could be.”
Trevor looked impressed. “That would make the experiment rather inconvenient.”
Penny nodded. “That’s why we have Derek.”
They looked toward Derek’s closed office door. Trevor smiled. “He’s in there.”
“Yes.”
“Working hard.”
“Obviously.”
“Or—”
Penny pointed at him. “Don’t.”
Trevor shut up. Penny walked to the whiteboard. She drew a distant empty train.
Then a series of small local frames around it.
“Imagine the train moving through space and time.”
Trevor nodded. “At any particular point along its worldline, we can construct a local inertial frame.”
“Yes.”
“Inside that sufficiently small region, Special Relativity works.”
“Yes.”
“But as we move farther along the train—or farther through space—the local frames don’t necessarily line up globally.”
“Because of curvature.”
“Exactly.”
She drew a second frame. Then a third. The axes were slightly rotated.
Trevor stared at them. “So, the contradiction between SR and GR is only apparent.”
“Yes.”
“SR describes each local frame.”
“Yes.”
“GR describes the geometry relating those frames.”
“Precisely.”
Trevor nodded. “Then what are we actually looking for?”
Penny pointed at the tiny discontinuity in the graph. “That.”
“Why?”
“Because something has happened to the relationship between our local frame and the physical system we’re measuring.”
Trevor frowned. “Could it be a coordinate effect?”
“Yes.”
“Meaning nothing physical has happened?”
“Possibly.”
“Then why does the apparatus detect it?”
“Because coordinate effects can change what different observers assign to an event.”
Trevor nodded. “But measurable quantities must remain invariant.”
“Correct.”
Penny pointed to the graph. “So, the next question is whether that discontinuity survives a change of coordinates.”
Trevor softly smiled. “That’s clever.”
“Thank you.”
“How do we test it?”
“We change observers.”
Trevor stared at her. “How?”
Penny pointed at the door. “Derek.”
Trevor nodded. “Of course.”
He went toward the door. Then stopped. It was still closed.
He knocked. Nothing. He knocked again.
Still nothing. Penny looked over. “Working hard.”
“Evidently.”
Trevor opened the door. Derek was sitting at his desk. He looked up. “What?”
“We need you.”
“For what?”
“An experiment.”
Derek gazed at the ordinary cucumber. He stared intently. Then at Penny.
Then back at the cucumber.
“No.”
Penny laughed. “It’s not about the cucumber.”
“Good.”
“It’s about the reference frame.”
“Better.”
She showed him the graph. Derek studied it. “When does the discontinuity occur?”
“At the same position every time.”
“Relative to the apparatus?”
“Yes.”
“Not the room?”
“No.”
“The room itself?”
“We haven’t tested that.”
Derek nodded. “Then that’s what we need.”
He stood. Penny smiled. “You’re coming?”
“Yes.”
Trevor’s eyebrows rose.
“Just like that?”
Derek shrugged. “It’s interesting.”
They went outside. The cold rain had become heavier. Derek looked at the street.
Then at the laboratory. Then at the ordinary cucumber.
“Bring it.”
Penny held it up. “Seriously?”
“Absolutely.”
“Why?”
Derek smiled. “If we’re going to do relativistic physics in London, we may as well have a proper control.”
Trevor inspected the cucumber. “What’s the control?”
“The cucumber.”
“Why?”
“Because everyone expects us to lose our minds.”
Trevor nodded. “Fair.”
They placed the apparatus on a trolley. Then pushed it into the street. The cold rain struck the sensor.
Water ran down the equipment. Penny covered it. Derek watched the traffic.
Buses. Cars. Bicycles.
Pedestrians. Umbrellas. Everything moving.
Everything with its own worldline. He watched the cold rain. “Perfect.”
Penny surveyed him. “Why?”
“Because now we’ve got millions of test particles.”
Trevor smiled. “Rain.”
“Yes.”
“Again.”
“Again.”
They started the apparatus. The trolley moved down the pavement. The graph suddenly appeared.
Smooth. Then— The discontinuity.
Derek stopped. “Where?”
Penny pointed. “Exactly here.”
Derek cast a quick look around. They were beside a distant station entrance. He checked the map. “Which line?”
“Central.”
Derek studied the entrance.
Then at the silent clock. 11:17.
He went still. Penny noticed. “What?”
Derek pointed. The station clock had stopped. 11:17.
Trevor regarded his phone. 11:17.
The sensor clock. 11:17.
The ordinary cucumber rolled slightly on the trolley. Then stopped.
Derek smiled. “The cucumber has found the reference frame.”
Trevor stared. “That’s not possible.”
“No.”
“Then what?”
Derek dropped his eyes into the Underground entrance.
“I think we’re looking at the same event.”
Penny nodded. “11:17.”
“Yes.”
“The clocks stop.”
“Yes.”
“The rain behaves strangely.”
“Yes.”
“And now our test mass detects a discontinuity.”
“Yes.”
Trevor examined the cucumber. “So, the cucumber isn’t the cause.”
“No.”
“It’s a witness.”
Derek softly smiled. “Exactly.”
Penny inspected the Underground entrance. “Then perhaps we should go down.”
Derek nodded.
Trevor wearily sighed. “Again?”
Derek turned his attention to him. “Did you have another appointment?”
“No.”
“Good.”
They descended. The cold rain continued falling above them. The cucumber came too.
And, somewhere deep beneath London, an empty train was approaching a station at exactly 11:17.
Derek quietly glanced at the silent clock. It stopped.
The train arrived. The doors opened. For one second, Derek could see through the carriage.
There was nobody inside — except at the far end, sitting calmly by the window, was Derek Gent. Derek stared at himself.
The other Derek tipped his head back. Their eyes met. The other Derek smiled.
Derek did not smile back.
Then he raised an ordinary cucumber. Derek stared at Penny. Penny gazed at Trevor.
Trevor peered at the ordinary cucumber in Penny’s hand. Then back at the mysterious train.
“Well,” he pointed out.
“That’s rather settled.”
Derek shook his head.
“No.”
He stepped toward the carriage.
“That isn’t settled at all.”
