There was a rule at the Bureau. It was not written down.
It did not appear in the employee handbook.
There was, in fact, no employee handbook, largely because Derek considered employee handbooks “a form of administrative poetry.”
Nevertheless, everyone knew the rule. If an office door was open, the occupant had vanished into virtuality. Obviously.
Penny considered this. “That’s the sort of rule that sounds sensible until it starts wearing a hat.”
There was no point knocking.
There was no point calling.
There was no point standing in the doorway shouting someone’s name.
An open old office door meant that the person in question had ceased, for all practical purposes, to occupy ordinary spacetime.
They might be in another room.
They might be in London.
They might be in Munich.
They might be in a completely different reference frame. They might even, on occasion, be dead.
The Bureau had learned not to worry. If the door was quietly closed, however, the situation was entirely different.
Then the person was in there.
Obviously. They were either working hard… or shagging, equally hard.
There was no third possibility worth discussing.
Derek had first formulated the principle after Penny had disappeared for three hours.
He had found her office door open.
He had checked the kitchen. Nothing.
He had checked the laboratory. Nothing.
He had checked the basement. Nothing.
He had checked the train timetable. Nothing.
Eventually he had returned to her office.
The door was still open.
Trevor had suddenly appeared beside him.
“Where’s Penny?”
“Virtuality.”
Trevor nodded. “Right.”
They went back to work.
Three hours later Penny had emerged from Derek’s office carrying a notebook.
Derek had watched her. “Where were you?”
“Working.”
“With the door closed?”
“Yes.”
Derek nodded. “There we are, then.”
Penny had softly smiled. Trevor had surveyed the two of them.
Then at the quietly closed old door.
Then at Derek.
Then at Penny. He had wisely remained silent.
The rule had subsequently become part of Bureau culture.
And on Friday morning, Derek’s office door was open. Penny’s was quietly closed.
Trevor’s was open. Weinstein’s was quietly closed. Nobody found this unusual.
Trevor walked down the narrow corridor carrying three cups of tea.
He stopped outside Derek’s office. The door was open.
He looked inside. Empty.
He nodded. “Virtuality.”
He continued.
Penny’s heavy door was closed.
Trevor knocked.
“Come in.”
He opened it.
Penny was sitting at her desk. She was reading a report.
Trevor put down her tea. “Working hard?”
“Yes.”
“Good.”
He left.
Penny waited until the door closed. Then smiled.
The Bureau was a peculiar place.
Derek returned twenty minutes later. He walked through the front door carrying a wet umbrella.
Penny glanced up. “Where have you been?”
“Underground.”
“Did you find anything?”
“Yes.”
“What?”
“Rain.”
Penny waited.
“Anything else?”
“Yes.”
“What?”
“It was falling upward.”
Penny nodded. “There. Sorted.”
Derek took off his coat. “Exactly.”
He put the umbrella in the stand. “Except I don’t know whether the rain was falling upward.”
“Or?”
“The train was falling downward.”
Penny nodded. “Or?”
“Neither.”
“Good.”
Derek fixed her with a look. “You’re getting rather good at this.”
“I’ve had excellent training.”
He smiled.
Then he noticed her familiar door. It was open.
Derek frowned. “Where’s Trevor?”
“Virtuality.”
“Of course.”
He looked toward Trevor’s office. His familiar door was open too.
Derek nodded. “Both at once?”
“Apparently.”
“That’s unusual.”
“Not really.”
Derek considered this. “Fair enough.”
He walked to the whiteboard.
There were equations everywhere.
Lorentz transformations.
Metric tensors.
A sketch of a geodesic.
A badly drawn simple apple.
An empty train.
A cloud.
And, in large letters:
THE RAIN IS NOT OBLIGED TO FALL DOWN.
Derek stared at it. “Did you write this?”
“No.”
“Trevor?”
“Probably.”
Derek regarded the open office door. “Virtuality.”
Penny nodded.
Derek picked up a marker.
Underneath the sentence he wrote:
NOR IS THE TRAIN OBLIGED TO KNOW WHERE IT IS GOING
He put down the marker. Then the telephone rang.
Penny answered, “Relativistic Investigation Bureau.”
A pause.
She examined Derek. “It’s for you.”
“Who?”
“Herbert.”
Derek took the phone. “Matkowski.”
“Derek.”
“Yes?”
“We’ve found something.”
Derek smiled. “Where?”
“London.”
“Good.”
“What do you mean, good?”
“That’s where we are.”
“Yes.”
“Then you’ve saved us a journey.”
There was silence.
“We’re in Munich.”
Derek paused. “Ah.”
Penny inspected him. “Virtuality?”
Derek nodded. “Apparently.”
Matkowski continued, “The problem concerns your clocks.”
Derek became serious.
“What about them?”
“We’ve compared the London readings with the Munich readings.”
“And?”
“They don’t disagree.”
Derek frowned. “That’s good.”
“No.”
“Why not?”
“Because they should.”
Derek sat down slowly, the chair creaking in a way that felt, this week, unnecessarily editorial. “Explain.”
“The clocks are following different worldlines.”
“Yes.”
“Different gravitational potentials.”
“Yes.”
“Different motions.”
“Yes.”
“So, their proper times should differ.”
“Yes.”
“But they don’t.”
Derek frowned. “Then they’re synchronized.”
“Exactly.”
“That’s not a problem.”
“It is if they have never been synchronized.”
Derek stopped. Penny considered him. “Go on.”
Matkowski commented:
“Someone has synchronized the clocks.”
“When?”
“We don’t know.”
“How?”
“We don’t know.”
“Why?”
“We don’t know.”
Derek smiled. “Well, that’s settled.”
“What’s settled?”
“We know almost nothing.”
Matkowski sighed. “That’s not what I meant.”
Derek glanced at Penny.
She smiled the particular smile she reserved for moments when she already knew how the conversation would end. “Fair enough.”
Matkowski continued, “There is something else.”
“Of course.”
“We found a reference to the Louvre.”
Derek’s expression changed.
“Where?”
“In a file that should not exist.”
“What’s in it?”
“Your name.”
Derek asked, “Anything else?”
“Alfred’s.”
“Penny?”
“Yes.”
“Trevor?”
“Yes.”
Derek paused. “And Julius Barbarian?”
There was silence.
“Yes.”
Derek stared at Penny. “That’s interesting.”
“Very.”
Matkowski noted: “The file is dated tomorrow.”
Derek leaned back. “Excellent. Case closed.”
Matkowski sounded irritated.
“Would you stop saying that?”
“Sorry.”
“No, you’re not.”
“Not particularly.”
There was another pause.
Then Matkowski observed:
“You should examine your own office.”
Derek looked toward the narrow corridor, where the light seemed, if anything, slightly reluctant to reach the far end. “My office?”
“Yes.”
“Why?”
“Because the file indicates something is there.”
“What?”
“We don’t know.”
Derek stood. He walked toward his familiar office. The door was open.
He stopped. Penny followed him. “What?”
“Nothing.”
He looked inside. The office was empty. The old battered desk was empty.
The chair was empty. The computer was off. Derek smiled. “Virtuality.”
Penny nodded. “Obviously.”
Then she fixed him with a look. “You’re not the brightest bulb in the toolbox today.”
Derek stepped inside. On the desk was a single sheet of paper. He picked it up.
It contained one sentence.
IF THE FAMILIAR DOOR IS OPEN, DON’T GO IN
Derek stared.
Penny stared.
Trevor suddenly appeared behind them. “What’s that?”
Derek took in the room. “Where did you come from?”
Trevor pointed at his own office. “The door was closed.”
Derek nodded. “Ah.”
Trevor gazed at the paper. “What does it say?”
Derek handed it to him.
Trevor read it.
Then looked at the open door.
“That’s confusing.”
Penny nodded. “Yes.”
Trevor thought. “Perhaps we’re not supposed to be here.”
Derek smiled. “Excellent.”
A pleased smile appeared on Trevor’s face. “I’ve solved it.”
“Not quite.”
“Oh.”
Derek eyed the paper again.
Then at the door.
Then at the clock. 11:17.
The ancient clock stopped. All three watched.
The second hand froze.
The computer switched on. A message quietly appeared:
DOOR STATUS: OPEN
Then:
OCCUPANT STATUS: VIRTUAL
Derek frowned. “Who’s the occupant?”
The computer answered.
DEREK GENT
Derek stared intently. Trevor stared. Penny stared.
Derek dropped his eyes at himself.
“I’m here.”
Penny nodded. “Yes.”
Trevor considered this. “Apparently you’re both here.”
Derek watched him. “Both?”
Trevor pointed at the screen. “Virtual Derek.”
Then at Derek.
“Actual Derek.”
Derek smiled. “Well, that’s inconvenient.”
The screen changed.
DOOR CLOSING IN 10 SECONDS
Derek surveyed the door. “Why?”
The countdown began.
Penny replied: “Perhaps you should leave.”
Derek nodded. “Good idea.”
They stepped backward. The heavy door slammed shut. The clocks restarted. 11:18.
The computer went dark. For several seconds nobody moved.
Then Derek studied Penny. “What does a closed door mean?”
Penny smiled. “Someone’s in there.”
Derek nodded. “Obviously.”
Trevor regarded the door. “Who?”
Derek opened it. The quiet office was empty. He stared.
Then quietly closed it again. “Me, apparently.”
Penny laughed.
Derek examined the closed door. “Right.”
He knocked.
From inside came Derek’s own quiet voice. “Come in.”
Trevor’s mouth fell open.
Penny looked delighted.
Derek carefully opened the door.
His office was still empty. Except for himself. He stood in the doorway.
His own voice came from somewhere inside. “You’re late.”
Derek stared into the empty room.
Then answered: “Well, that’s settled.”
The distant voice answered: “Not remotely.”
