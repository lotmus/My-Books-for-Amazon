By Thursday morning, Alfred Weinstein was sitting in a patent attorney’s office in Schwabing. He was bored. This was not unusual.
He had once pointed out, forcefully and at some length, that a certain other physicist had also spent his most productive years examining patents in an office very much like this one, and that history had found this charming rather than suspicious. Nobody at the Bureau had found a tactful way to change the subject fast enough.
The patent attorney was explaining a device intended to improve the efficiency of a mechanical regulator. Weinstein had been listening for approximately six minutes. He had understood the first two.
The third had been optimistic. The fourth had involved a spring. The fifth had involved a spring behaving in a way Weinstein considered personally insulting.
By the sixth minute, his mind had left the room. He looked out of the cold window. Munich was busy.
A tram rattled past. A cyclist narrowly avoided a delivery cart.
Someone shouted at someone else.
A dog had apparently decided that the entire afternoon belonged to it. Weinstein watched the movement for a while. Then he quietly stared at the silent clock.
He had been in the quiet office for forty-three minutes. It felt like three hours. He smiled.
That was an interesting thought. The feeling of time passing was obviously psychological. Physical time was another matter.
He gazed at the clock again. Then at his watch. They disagreed.
Only slightly. Weinstein frowned. He checked them again.
The difference was still there. He looked around the office. One mysterious clock was on the wall.
His watch was on his wrist. Nothing unusual about that. He returned to the patent.
The attorney was still speaking. Weinstein lasted another twelve seconds. Then his mind wandered back to the clocks.
Suppose two identical clocks were placed at different heights. Would they remain synchronized? He knew the answer.
No.
General Relativity predicted that a strange clock deeper in a gravitational field would run more slowly than one higher up. The difference under ordinary circumstances was extremely small. But it was real.
And, more importantly, it was measurable. Weinstein picked up his pencil. On the margin of the patent application he wrote:
GRAVITY CHANGES THE RATE OF TIME
He reread the sentence.
Then added:
THE DIFFERENCE SHOULD BE MEASURABLE
He stared at it. The patent attorney stopped speaking. “Herr Weinstein?”
“Yes?”
“Are you following me?”
“Absolutely.”
“What did I just say?”
Weinstein eyed the patent. Then at the attorney. Then at his pencil.
“You were explaining the regulator.”
“Yes.”
“And the spring.”
“Yes.”
“The spring is wrong.”
The attorney stared at him. “I beg your pardon?”
Weinstein watched the diagram. “It won’t behave as you’ve described.”
“Why not?”
Weinstein surveyed the drawing for a moment. “Because you’ve assumed the temperature remains constant.”
The attorney blinked. “Doesn’t it?”
“No.”
“Oh.”
Weinstein turned the paper around and pointed to a line. “This coefficient changes with temperature.”
The attorney leaned over the desk. “Good heavens.”
Weinstein shrugged. “Well, there we are.”
He returned to his mysterious clock. The patent attorney studied the amended diagram. Then at Weinstein.
“You’ve been listening after all.”
“Occasionally.”
The attorney smiled. “I suppose I should be grateful.”
“You should.”
Weinstein regarded his watch again. The difference between it and the wall clock had increased. He frowned.
That was no longer ordinary. He stood. “Do you have another clock?”
The attorney looked puzzled. “Yes.”
“Why?”
“I’d like to compare them.”
The attorney pointed towards the secretary’s desk. “There is one there.”
Weinstein walked over. He compared it with his watch. Then with the wall clock.
Then he examined the positions of all three. His expression changed. The patent attorney noticed.
“Something wrong?”
“Yes.”
“What?”
“The clocks are disagreeing.”
“They do that.”
“Not like this.”
The attorney inspected him. Weinstein took out his notebook. He calculated.
Then calculated again. The difference was far too large. He looked at the ceiling.
Then at the floor. The secretary came in.
“Herr Weinstein?”
“Yes?”
“Is everything all right?”
“No.”
“What happened?”
Weinstein held up the notebook. “The clocks are behaving badly.”
The secretary glanced at the clocks. “They seem fine.”
“That’s what makes it interesting.”
He put the notebook away. “How high is this building?”
“Four floors.”
“Exactly?”
“I believe so.”
Weinstein nodded. He looked up. “Which floor are we on?”
“Second.”
He thought. Then stared at his watch again. The difference had increased.
He frowned. “That’s impossible.”
The secretary smiled. “I thought you didn’t believe in impossible things.”
“I don’t.”
“Then what is it?”
Weinstein considered the question. “A very large error.”
The secretary nodded. “Ah.”
She returned to her desk. Weinstein picked up his coat. The patent attorney looked alarmed. “Are you leaving?”
“Yes.”
“What about the rest of the patent?”
“It can wait.”
“The spring?”
“It will continue being wrong while I’m gone.”
He left the quiet office. Herbert Matkowski was standing in the empty corridor. Weinstein stopped. “You knew.”
Matkowski gazed at him.
“Knew what?”
“The clocks.”
“What about them?”
“They’re disagreeing.”
Matkowski nodded. “Yes.”
“By a ridiculous amount.”
“Yes.”
“And you knew.”
“Yes.”
Weinstein stared at him. “Well?”
“Well what?”
“What is happening?”
Matkowski smiled. “That is what Derek Gent is trying to discover.”
Weinstein frowned. “Who?”
“Derek Gent.”
“Never heard of him.”
“You will.”
Matkowski handed him a vivid photograph.
Weinstein studied it. The picture showed an office.
A man was lying face-down on the floor.
Weinstein stared. “Who is that?”
“Derek Gent.”
“Dead?”
“Apparently.”
Weinstein peered at the strange vivid photograph again.
“When?”
“Tomorrow.”
Weinstein turned to Matkowski. “That’s inconvenient.”
“I thought you’d say that.”
Weinstein studied the photograph. Then he noticed the clock. 11:03:17.
His expression changed. “Where did you get this?”
“From the Bureau.”
“What Bureau?”
“Derek Gent’s Relativistic Investigation Bureau.”
Weinstein watched the photograph.
Then at Matkowski. “And you expect me to believe this?”
“No.”
“Good.”
“I expect you to analyze it.”
Weinstein nodded. “That is more reasonable.”
“What frame?”
Matkowski smiled. “Exactly.”
Weinstein turned to him. “Don’t be pleased with yourself.”
“I wasn’t.”
“You were.”
“Perhaps.”
Weinstein returned to the strange vivid photograph. “The timestamp alone tells us almost nothing.”
“Correct.”
“We need the event coordinates.”
“Correct.”
“And the observer’s frame.”
“Correct.”
“And the light-travel time between the event and the camera.”
“Correct.”
Weinstein nodded. “Well, that’s straightforward.”
Matkowski smiled. “Is it?”
“No.”
“Fine.”
Weinstein looked again. There was something else. An old brass clock.
The same old clock he’d seen in his office. He pointed at it. “That.”
“Yes.”
“I’ve seen that clock.”
“Where?”
“In London.”
Matkowski was silent.
Weinstein regarded him. “Yesterday.”
Matkowski nodded. “Yes.”
Weinstein stared intently. “Why didn’t you tell me?”
“I wanted you to notice.”
Weinstein sighed. “You have an unfortunate tendency to turn information into puzzles.”
“It keeps the mind active.”
“It also wastes time.”
“Not according to relativity.”
Weinstein examined him. “That was terrible.”
“I know.”
Weinstein handed the photograph back.
“Let’s go to London.”
Matkowski raised an eyebrow. “Now?”
“Yes.”
“You’re not curious about the patent?”
Weinstein looked toward the office. “No.”
“The spring?”
“No.”
“The regulator?”
“No.”
Matkowski nodded. “Good.”
They walked toward the stairs.
After a moment, Weinstein added: “There’s something else.”
“What?”
“The difference between the clocks.”
“Yes?”
“It’s much too large to be explained by the gravitational field of the Earth at that height.”
Matkowski nodded. “Exactly.”
“So, either the clocks are wrong…”
“Yes.”
“… or we’re not dealing with the Earth’s ordinary gravitational field.”
“Yes.”
Weinstein stopped. “Then what are we dealing with?”
Matkowski inspected him. “That is the interesting question.”
Weinstein nodded. “Then it is decided.”
Matkowski stared at him. “What is?”
“That we don’t know.”
Matkowski smiled. “Quite.”
They continued down the stairs. In London, Derek Gent was standing in the Bureau, staring at the same photograph. Penny stood beside him.
Trevor was at the computer. The three of them had spent the morning testing the Lorentz transformation against the mysterious timestamps. The result had been unsatisfactory.
The mathematics worked. The interpretation didn’t. Derek considered the latest calculation. “We’ve got a problem.”
Trevor looked over. “What sort?”
“The relative velocity required to transform the two timestamps is greater than the speed of light.”
Penny nodded. “So, the two timestamps cannot simply be the same event described in two inertial frames.”
“Correct.”
Trevor glanced at the equation. “Then one of our assumptions is wrong.”
“Yes.”
“Which one?”
“We don’t know.”
Penny stared at the photograph. “Could the photograph be genuine but the event misidentified?”
“Yes.”
“Could the clock be genuine but the frame wrong?”
“Yes.”
“Could both be genuine and there be another effect?”
Derek gazed at her.
“Yes.”
Trevor thought for a moment. “Gravity.”
Derek nodded. “Possibly.”
Surprise briefly overtook Trevor’s face.
“You think General Relativity?”
“I think we shouldn’t ignore it.”
Penny nodded. “That would be sensible.”
Derek studied the silent clock.
Derek studied the silent clock for a long moment, turning it over once in his hands, as though weight alone might explain what angle couldn’t. “What?”
“The effect is too large.”
Trevor walked over. “By how much?”
Derek showed him.
Trevor whistled softly. “That isn’t a small correction.”
“No.”
“Could a strong gravitational field do it?”
“Certainly.”
“Do we have one?”
Derek surveyed the empty office. “Not unless the landlord has installed a neutron star.”
Penny eyed the floor. “Perhaps he has.”
Derek smiled. “Then I want my rent reduced.”
The telephone rang.
Derek answered, “Derek Gent.”
A familiar voice explained: “Good morning.”
Derek smiled. “Herbert.”
“Yes.”
“Where are you?”
“Munich.”
“Still?”
“Yes.”
“Why?”
“Alfred is here.”
Derek watched Penny. “That’s convenient.”
“No.”
“What?”
“You don’t understand.”
“Explain.”
“He has found the same anomaly.”
Derek straightened.
“The clocks?”
“Yes.”
“Same magnitude?”
“Essentially.”
Penny’s expression changed. She had gone very still, the way she did whenever the ordinary rules of a Tuesday afternoon stopped applying. Trevor surveyed her. “What?”
She whispered: “Same anomaly in Munich.”
Trevor understood.
Derek declared: “Put Alfred on.”
There was a click. Then a voice. “Mr Gent.”
“Alfred.”
“You have the clock.”
“Yes.”
“I have seen it.”
“I know.”
“No. I mean I have seen that exact clock.”
“Where?”
“In your Bureau.”
Derek glanced at the ancient brass clock. “When?”
“Tomorrow.”
Derek sat down.
Penny took the phone. “Alfred, what did you see?”
“I saw the clock.”
“And?”
“I saw you.”
“Alive?”
“Yes.”
Penny studied Derek. “And?”
“You were also dead.”
Trevor stared intently.
Derek took the phone back. “Alfred, let’s be precise.”
“I always am.”
“You saw me alive and dead.”
“Yes.”
“At the same location?”
“Apparently.”
“At the same time?”
“That depends on which time you mean.”
Derek softly smiled. “Good answer.”
“Thank you.”
Penny announced:
“That’s settled, then.”
Derek nodded. “Evidently.”
Trevor regarded them. “That’s it?”
Penny examined him. “What else is there to settle?”
Trevor considered that. “Fair enough.”
Outside, the rain had not let up. Derek glanced once at the window before returning to the phone. “Alfred, do you know what causes the effect?”
“No.”
“Could it be gravitational?”
“Yes.”
“Ordinary Earth gravity?”
“No.”
“Could it be acceleration?”
“Possibly.”
Derek asked, “Could it be spacetime curvature?”
“Perhaps.”
“Could it be a combination?”
“Could be.”
Derek smiled. “Very helpful.”
“I am not there to solve your case.”
“Then why are you coming to London?”
There was a pause. “Because I am curious.”
Derek nodded. “Well, that settles it.”
“What does?”
“You’re coming.”
“Yes.”
“When?”
“Tomorrow.”
Derek inspected Penny. Penny smiled. “Of course.”
Weinstein continued: “I’ll meet you at the station.”
“Which station?”
“London Terminus.”
Derek returned to the photograph. “Platform seven?”
“Yes.”
“At 11:17?”
“Yes.”
Derek went silent. Trevor watched him. Penny understood immediately.
“That was in the photograph.”
“Yes,” Derek asserted.
Alfred pointed out: “I know.”
Derek glanced at the brass clock. “Alfred.”
“Yes?”
“Why are you so certain?”
A pause. “Because I’ve already been there.”
Derek frowned. “Tomorrow?”
“Yes.”
“And you’ve already seen me?”
“Yes.”
“And the clock?”
“Yes.”
Derek thought. “Then we have a causal loop.”
“Perhaps.”
Penny studied him. “Don’t get excited.”
“I wasn’t.”
“You were.”
Derek smiled. “I was mildly interested.”
“That’s your version of excitement.”
Trevor nodded. “It is.”
Derek ignored both of them.
“Alfred, one more thing.”
“Yes?”
“Does General Relativity explain what we’re seeing?”
There was a pause.
Then Weinstein answered: “It explains the principle.”
“But not the effect.”
“No.”
Derek waited. Weinstein continued:
“The gravitational time-dilation effect is real. We understand it extremely well. What we don’t understand is why your clocks are showing a difference so large.”
Derek nodded. “So, we’re not discovering new relativity.”
“No.”
“We’re applying known relativity to an unknown situation.”
“Exactly.”
Derek smiled. “Right, that’s that, then.”
“What is?”
“The physics isn’t broken.”
“No.”
“The situation is.”
“Yes.”
“Good.”
The call ended. Derek put down the phone. Trevor stared at him. “We’re going to London Terminus tomorrow.”
“Yes.”
“At 11:17.”
“Yes.”
“After your supposed murder.”
“Yes.”
Penny picked up her coat. “And Weinstein will be there.”
“Yes.”
Trevor nodded. “That resolves it, then.”
Derek gazed at him. “Does it?”
Trevor thought. “Yes.”
“Good.”
Derek picked up the ancient brass clock. The second hand moved. Tick.
Tick. It reached seventeen.
Stopped. Then started again. Derek peered at it.
“There’s still one thing.”
Penny paused at the door. “What?”
Derek turned the silent clock over. The initials LGV were still stamped into the back.
“I want to know what this means.”
Trevor eyed it.
“Perhaps Weinstein knows.”
Derek nodded. “Perhaps.”
Penny opened the door. “Ask him tomorrow.”
Derek put the silent clock in his coat pocket. “Good idea.”
They left the Bureau. Behind them, the computer switched itself on. A new case note mysteriously appeared.
CASE 1047
REFERENCE FRAME: INERTIAL
GRAVITATIONAL CORRECTION: REQUIRED
Derek’s name suddenly appeared beneath it.
Then Trevor’s.
Then Penny’s.
Then Alfred Weinstein’s.
Finally, one more line appeared.
HERBERT MATKOWSKI: CONSULTING
The screen went dark. In Munich, at almost exactly the same moment, the wall clock in the patent attorney’s familiar office stopped. Weinstein noticed.
He watched his watch. Then at the clock. Then at the second hand.
It had stopped at seventeen. He smiled. Matkowski noticed.
“What?”
Weinstein held up his watch. “Same thing.”
Matkowski looked. “Indeed.”
Weinstein put the watch away. “Good. Progress.”
“What is?”
“We’re going to London.”
Matkowski nodded. “And the patent?”
Weinstein looked sharply toward the quiet office. “The spring can wait.”
They walked toward the strange station. Behind them, in the patent attorney’s office, the silent clock started again. Only this time it was running backwards.
