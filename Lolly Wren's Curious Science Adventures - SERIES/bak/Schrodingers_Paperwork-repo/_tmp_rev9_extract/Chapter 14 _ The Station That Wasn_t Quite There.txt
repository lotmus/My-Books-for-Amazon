The first thing Derek noticed was that the gloomy station had twelve doors. The second thing he noticed was that it had thirteen. The third thing he noticed was that Trevor was counting them.
“How many?”
“Twelve.”
Derek pointed. “Thirteen.”
Trevor counted again.
“Twelve.”
Penny looked. “Thirteen.”
Trevor looked again. “Twelve.”
Derek nodded. “Good.”
Trevor sharply frowned. “Good?”
“At least we’re all seeing the same disagreement.”
They were standing in a strange corridor beneath London. It looked like an ordinary Underground corridor. This was, naturally, suspicious.
The tiles were ordinary. The fluorescent lights were ordinary. The smell was ordinary.
Even the damp patch on the wall was reassuringly ordinary. But the narrow corridor was wrong. Not dramatically wrong.
That would have been easier. Dramatically wrong things announce themselves. They have the decency to be obvious.
This narrow corridor was merely wrong enough to make a sensible person uncomfortable. Derek walked back to the entrance and eyed the sign.
There was no station name. Only:
PLATFORM 7
Penny watched it. “We’re already on Platform Seven.”
Derek nodded. “Apparently.”
Trevor looked behind them. There was no Platform Seven. There was only the narrow corridor.
“Perhaps it’s a service corridor.”
Derek considered this. “Then why does it have a platform number?”
Trevor shrugged. “British Rail?”
“It’s the Underground.”
“Even worse.”
Penny smiled. “Glad that’s cleared up.”
Derek ignored them. He took a tape measure from his coat.
Trevor surveyed him. “You carry a tape measure?”
“I investigate things.”
“You carry one everywhere?”
“Not everywhere.”
“Where don’t you carry it?”
Derek thought. “The shower.”
Penny studied him. “Why not?”
Derek stared. “Because I have limits.”
They measured the strange corridor. Forty-seven metres.
Exactly.
Derek wrote it down. Then he walked from one end to the other. Forty-seven metres.
He walked back. Forty-seven metres.
Penny did the same. Forty-seven metres.
Trevor did the same. Forty-seven metres.
Derek nodded. “Good.”
Trevor regarded him. “What’s good about forty-seven metres?”
“Nothing.”
“Then why—”
“Because it’s the same.”
Trevor wearily sighed. “Right.”
Derek examined the doors. “Now let’s count them again.”
They did.
Twelve. Then thirteen. Then twelve. Then thirteen.
Penny folded her arms. “Perhaps we’re counting different things.”
Derek smiled. “Excellent.”
He pointed to the first door. “Describe it.”
“Brown.”
“Number?”
“One.”
“Next?”
“Two.”
“Next?”
“Three.”
They continued. Every door was ordinary. Every number was ordinary.
Every distance between them was ordinary. And yet, somehow, the corridor contained either twelve or thirteen doors depending on who was doing the counting. Derek took out a notebook. “Trevor.”
“Yes?”
“Walk to the end.”
Trevor did. “How many?”
“Twelve.”
“Penny?”
“It’s not rocket surgery.”
Trevor fixed him with a look. “That is not reassuring.”
“Derek?”
He counted.
“Twelve.”
Penny smiled. “So, it’s me.”
Trevor inspected her. “You’re the problem.”
“It would seem so.”
Derek shook his head.
“No.”
They gave him a measured look.
“The problem is that we’re assuming the number of doors is an invariant.”
Trevor frowned. “A what?”
“Something every observer must agree on.”
Penny nodded. “Like the spacetime interval.”
“Exactly.”
Trevor glanced at the doors. “So, door-counting isn’t invariant.”
“Not if the corridor isn’t behaving like ordinary Euclidean space.”
Trevor looked suspicious. “Are you saying we’ve found curved space?”
“Perhaps.”
“Underground?”
“Yes.”
“In London?”
“Yes.”
Trevor considered this. “That seems unnecessarily ambitious.”
Derek smiled. “It’s London.”
“Fair.”
They slowly moved farther down the narrow corridor. Derek stopped beside one of the doors. “Open it.”
Trevor did. There was a small room inside.
Two chairs. A table. Two clocks. Nothing else.
Derek entered. Penny followed.
Trevor remained in the doorway. “What?”
Derek stared at the clocks. One read: 11:17
The other: 12:04
Trevor stared silently. “Again?”
Derek nodded. “Again.”
Penny stepped inside.
“Are they broken?”
Derek checked them. “No.”
“Different mechanisms?”
“No.”
“Different batteries?”
“No.”
He compared them carefully. Identical. Same make.
Same model. Same calibration. Same temperature.
Same reference frequency. Same everything.
Except the time.
Trevor gazed at the two clocks. “They’re less than two metres apart.”
“Yes.”
“And they’re disagreeing by forty-seven minutes.”
“Yes.”
Trevor considered this. “That’s not good.”
“No.”
“Could one be in a different gravitational field?”
Derek smiled. “Good question.”
He took out an instrument. They measured the local gravitational field. Nothing unusual.
They measured again. Nothing. Derek moved the 12:04 clock three metres down the corridor.
The difference changed.
Trevor’s eyebrows rose.
“Did you see that?”
“Yes.”
Derek moved it back. The difference shrank.
Penny peered at him. “That’s gravitational.”
“Something gravitational.”
“But the field is nowhere near strong enough.”
“Correct.”
“So?”
Derek eyed the two clocks. “The important thing may not be the strength of the gravitational field.”
Penny nodded. “The variation.”
“Exactly.”
Trevor looked confused. “Explain.”
Derek leaned against the table. “Imagine two people falling toward Earth.”
“All right.”
“They are both accelerating toward the centre of Earth.”
“Yes.”
“But the person slightly to the left is falling in a slightly different direction from the person slightly to the right.”
“Because they’re pointing toward different parts of the Earth.”
“Exactly.”
Trevor concluded, “So, their paths slowly change relative to each other.”
“Yes.”
“That’s a tidal effect.”
“Precisely.”
Penny nodded. “Geodesic deviation.”
Derek smiled. “Precisely.”
Trevor looked from one mysterious clock to the other. “So, gravity isn’t just about how strong it is at one point.”
“Correct.”
“It’s also about how it changes across space.”
“Yes.”
“And that’s curvature.”
“Now you’re getting it.”
Trevor smiled. “Well, that’s one theory down.”
The lights flickered. All three looked up. The lights went out.
For one second there was complete darkness.
Then they came back on. The clocks were still there.
The chairs were still there. The table was still there.
But the narrow corridor outside had changed.
Derek stepped out. There were fourteen doors.
Trevor counted. “Fourteen.”
Penny counted. “Thirteen.”
Derek counted. “Fourteen.”
They stared at each other.
Trevor pointed at Penny. “You’re doing it again.”
Penny smiled. “Apparently.”
Derek watched the corridor. “Something has changed.”
“What?”
“The geometry.”
Trevor frowned. “How can you see geometry?”
“You don’t see it directly.”
“Then how do you know?”
“Because measurements don’t fit together the way they should.”
Penny nodded. “That’s the important bit.”
She walked to the end of the narrow corridor.
Then back. “Forty-seven metres.”
Derek measured. “Forty-seven.”
Trevor measured. “Forty-seven.”
Derek slowly surveyed the doors. “So, the length hasn’t changed.”
“No.”
“The number of doors has.”
“Yes.”
“The clocks have changed.”
“Yes.”
“The gravitational gradient has changed.”
“Yes.”
Trevor looked increasingly uncomfortable. “And you’re saying all of this can happen because spacetime is curved?”
Derek nodded. “That’s what General Relativity permits.”
“But not arbitrarily.”
“Correct.”
“The geometry has to satisfy Einstein’s equations.”
“Yes.”
“So, this isn’t magic.”
“No.”
Trevor looked at the fourteen doors. “It looks remarkably like magic.”
Derek smiled. “That’s because you haven’t seen enough mathematics.”
Penny laughed. They continued. At the far end of the corridor was a wall.
No door. No sign. Nothing.
Derek touched it. Solid.
Penny knocked. Solid.
Trevor knocked. Solid.
Derek stepped back.
“Move.”
Trevor did. Derek took three steps backward.
Then ran toward the wall.
Penny shouted: “Derek!”
He hit the wall. Except he didn’t. He went through it.
Penny stared intently.
Trevor stared.
Derek disappeared.
There was silence. Trevor regarded Penny. “He’s gone.”
Penny nodded. “Yes.”
“Should we be worried?”
“No.”
“Why not?”
“His office door was closed.”
Trevor nodded. “Ah.”
A voice came from the other side of the wall.
“Come through.”
Penny walked toward the wall. She touched it. Her hand went through.
She smiled. “Interesting.”
Trevor looked horrified. “I don’t think that’s a property walls normally have.”
Penny examined him. “Nothing does until it does.”
She walked through.
Trevor sighed. Then followed.
On the other side was another strange corridor.
Much longer.
Much darker.
And, unlike the first strange corridor, it contained no doors at all. At the far end was a station platform.
A sign read:
COLD PLATFORM 7
Derek stood beside it.
Trevor joined him. “Where are we?”
Derek took in the room. “Underground.”
“We were already underground.”
“Yes.”
“So?”
Derek pointed upward. They could hear rain.
Trevor looked confused. “We’re beneath London.”
“Yes.”
“And it’s raining above us.”
“Yes.”
“How can you hear it?”
Derek smiled. “Good question.”
Penny inspected the floor. Water was running along the edge of the platform.
She crouched. “It isn’t just rain.”
Derek joined her. “What?”
“The water.”
She pointed. It was flowing toward the drain.
Then it stopped. Completely. Not slowed. Stopped.
A drop hung at the edge of the drain.
Trevor crouched. “Is that normal?”
“No,” Derek responded.
The drop moved backward.
Then forward. Then sideways.
Then fell into the drain. Trevor stared. “Right.”
Derek stood. “The local geometry is doing something strange.”
Penny considered him. “Can we distinguish that from a coordinate effect?”
“Not yet.”
“How?”
“Find something invariant.”
Trevor glanced around. “The cucumber.”
They all stared at him.
He shrugged. “You’ve used it before.”
Penny softly smiled. “Good thinking.”
She took the cucumber from her bag.
Derek stared. “You brought it?”
“Obviously.”
“Why?”
“You never know.”
Trevor nodded. “That resolves it, then.”
They placed the ordinary cucumber on the platform. Derek measured its length. Exactly thirty-one centimetres.
Penny measured it. Exactly thirty-one centimetres. Trevor measured it.
Thirty-one centimetres.
Derek nodded. “Stationary.”
“Yes.”
“Now let’s move it.”
He placed it on a small wheeled platform.
They pushed it along the track. Penny measured again.
Thirty centimetres.
Trevor frowned. “Length contraction.”
“Exactly.”
“Special Relativity.”
“Yes.”
Derek pushed it faster. Twenty-eight centimetres.
Trevor smiled. “Still Special Relativity.”
“Yes.”
Then they stopped. The cucumber returned to thirty-one centimetres. Perfectly ordinary.
Penny smiled. “Excellent.”
Derek nodded. “Now we know the cucumber works.”
Trevor stared at him. “What does that mean?”
“It obeys relativity.”
“Of course it does.”
Derek glanced at the empty strange station. “Now we need to find out whether the geometry itself is changing.”
He placed the cucumber at one end of the platform. Then another.
The measured lengths changed slightly depending upon position.
Trevor stared at the readings. “That’s not length contraction.”
“No.”
“Because the cucumber isn’t moving.”
“Correct.”
“So, the spatial geometry is changing.”
“Yes.”
Penny stared at the track. “Could the gravitational field be responsible?”
“Possibly.”
“The tidal field?”
“More likely.”
Trevor took in the room. “How much curvature would we need?”
Derek smiled. “More than London normally provides.”
“That’s reassuring.”
“Unfortunately…”
Derek pointed down the track.
The rails were bending. Not physically.
They remained straight to the eye. But the measured distance between them was changing.
Penny stared intently. “That’s curvature.”
Derek nodded. “Yes.”
Trevor swallowed. “So, the Underground has become a General Relativity experiment.”
“Apparently.”
“Is that safe?”
Derek considered it. “Probably.”
Trevor waited. “Probably?”
Derek smiled. “Would you prefer ‘possibly’?”
“No.”
“Then probably.”
The station lights flickered again.
The clocks all stopped. 11:17.
Penny gazed at her phone. 11:17.
Trevor checked his. 11:17.
Derek checked his watch. 11:17.
The cucumber began to tick.
Trevor looked at it. “That can’t be good.”
Derek smiled. “Nothing is good at 11:17.”
A train appeared in the tunnel. No headlights. No sound.
It simply suddenly appeared.
Penny stood. “Where did that come from?”
Derek studied the rails. “That’s the wrong question.”
“What is the right one?”
“Which worldline did it follow?”
Trevor stared at him. “The train has a worldline?”
“Everything physical does.”
“Even the cucumber?”
“Yes.”
Penny watched the ordinary cucumber, thinking, 'peeled and pre-warmed might be nicer'.
Then she said, “Even the cucumber.”
Trevor nodded slowly. “Splendid. Moving on.”
The train approached. It stopped. The doors opened.
Nobody got off. Nobody got on. Inside, the carriage was completely empty.
Except for a single seat. And on the seat was another cucumber. Derek stared at it.
Penny stared at Derek.
Trevor stared at Penny.
Nobody offered anything.
Then Derek smiled. “Well.”
Penny folded her arms. “Don’t.”
“I haven’t offered anything.”
“You were about to.”
Derek surveyed the second cucumber. “All right.”
He stepped into the carriage.
Penny followed.
Trevor wearily sighed.
Then followed them. The doors quietly closed.
The mysterious train began to move.
Behind them, the station disappeared.
Ahead, the tunnel curved.
And somewhere between London and somewhere that London had apparently forgotten to put on the map, the clocks began moving again.
11:18. 11:19. 11:20.
Derek studied his watch. It still read 11:17.
He smiled. “There. Progress.”
The mysterious distant train accelerated into the darkness.
And for the first time, Derek began to suspect that the strangest thing about the empty station was not that its geometry was curved.
It was that something appeared to be curving it on purpose.
