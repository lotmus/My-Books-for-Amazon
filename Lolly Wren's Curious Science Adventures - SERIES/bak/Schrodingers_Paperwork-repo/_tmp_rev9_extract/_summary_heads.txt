In Which the Universe Refuses to Behave
The murder had not happened yet. This was unusual mainly because the paperwork said that it had.
The time of death had been entered. The location had been entered. The victim had been identified.
The victim was Derek Gent. Derek was alive. He was also the sort of man who noticed discrepancies in official paperwork. This particular discrepancy was rather difficult to ignore.
The paperwork predicted his death three days before it was due to occur. Derek therefore did what any sensible investigator would do.
He checked the date, then the time, then the old clock.
Then the clock again, because the first check had produced a result he found professionally inconvenient. 11:17. The mysterious clock had stopped.
Then there was Sherlock, a dolphin who worked remotely as an occasional Bureau consultant and was paid in fish. Nobody had yet found this arrangement stranger than the cases he commented on.
For the same reason, when Derek found a murder notice announcing his own future death, he did not immediately conclude that he was being hunted by a time traveller, a quantum ghost or an unusually vindictive neighbour.
The case would eventually force an even less comfortable possibility: that the Bureau’s own computer was not merely recording the crime, but mis-filing a future event as one.
He began with the evidence.
A stopped clock.
A future date.
A murder that had not happened.
And, eventually, a distant, mysterious train.
There was only one sensible response. Investigate.
Preferably before 11:17.

At precisely 9:17 on a Tuesday morning, Derek Gent received an email informing him that he was dead. He was not particularly surprised.
Derek had always suspected that death would arrive by email. In this, as in so many other matters, he had been only slightly ahead of the general population — a position he found both comforting and faintly depressing, which was, on balance, better than the alternatives. The alternatives were rarely an improvement, a fact Derek had verified more than once, usually at personal cost and never with a sense of novelty.
Not because he was especially pessimistic, but because nearly everything important seemed to arrive by email these days. Electricity bills. Convenient ways to re-enter bank account numbers for verification. Invitations to life-changing webinars. A unique opportunity to cooperate with a prince in Nigeria. Notifications that someone had changed a password Derek had never used. Insurance recommendations. Death was simply the logical next step. What surprised him was the sender.
The message had come from the Derek Gent Relativistic Investigation Bureau. Derek stared at the screen. The Bureau was not supposed to send him emails. Derek had written the rule himself, which made its violation particularly irritating.
It was his Bureau. More precisely, it was a small private investigative organization occupying two rooms on the second floor of an old office building in London, plus a corridor which the landlord insisted was a communal area and Derek insisted belonged to his Bureau whenever he needed somewhere to dry his brolly. The Bureau dealt primarily with unusual cases.
This was partly because Derek liked unusual cases and partly because ordinary cases paid terribly. The sign on the frosted glass door read:
DEREK GENT
RELATIVISTIC INVESTIGATION BUREAU
Underneath, in smaller letters:
CAUSALITY, CHRONOLOGY &
OTHER INCONVENIENCES
And underneath that, added by Penny several years earlier:
NO MURDERS BEFORE 10 A.M.
WITHOUT AN APPOINTMENT
Derek had objected. Penny had pointed out that they had never actually received a case before ten.
Derek had said that this was beside the point.
Penny had stated that was precisely the point.
Derek had lost.
He was now looking at an email from the Bureau. The subject line was:
CASE UPDATE: DEREK GENT
He opened it.
Dear Mr Gent,
We regret to inform you that you have been
murdered.
The incident will occur tomorrow at 11:03 a.m.
The perpetrator has been identified. It is you.
Kind regards,
Derek Gent
Relativistic Investigation Bureau.
Derek read it again.
Then a third time. He glanced at the sender’s address. It was correct.
Then he stared at the Bureau’s computer. It was his computer.
Everything appeared to be where it ought to be. Derek did not find this reassuring.
The filing cabinet was locked. The kettle was where the kettle normally was, which was not necessarily where it ought to be.
Outside, a bus pulled away from the kerb with a noise suggesting that several of its components had voted against the decision. Rain tapped the window. Somewhere downstairs, the landlord was drilling into something which, judging by previous work, would shortly become the Bureau’s responsibility.
Derek gazed at the email again, with the particular stillness of a man who has just been handed a problem that refuses to sit neatly in any of the usual categories. The Bureau had categories for almost everything. This was not among them. A new category would no doubt be invented once the damage was complete.
“That’s inconvenient,” he commented, as if the matter were primarily a filing error.
Being confronted with a situation such as “Apparently I’ve been murdered.”  made Derek feel considerably less useful.
Across the empty room, Trevor Boltzman looked up. Trevor was twenty-eight, highly intelligent, technically qualified, and possessed of the unfortunate tendency to think about one problem at a time — a trait that made him excellent at mathematics and intermittently disastrous at life. When thinking deeply, he carried himself like a frozen Windows system before reboot.
He had a sister, Tabitha, who was generally considered the more dangerous of the two, a fact Trevor mentioned only when cornered, and sometimes not even then.
At his desk, Trevor removed his glasses — a gesture that, in the Bureau, usually preceded either insight or complete bafflement, with no reliable way of telling which in advance. “What?”
Derek had stopped trying to predict it years ago. “I’ve been murdered.”
“Recently?”
“Tomorrow.”
Trevor stared at him. “Tomorrow?”

By Wednesday morning, Derek Gent had decided that there were two kinds of impossible. The first kind was the ordinary sort. A man could not be in two places at once.
A clear photograph could not normally exist before it was taken. A clock could not run backwards without either being broken or being subjected to a level of engineering that Derek considered unnecessarily ambitious. The second kind was considerably worse.
The second kind was something that was perfectly consistent with the laws of physics but completely inconsistent with common sense. Derek had spent most of his professional life dealing with the second kind. He found it much more expensive.
At 8:36 on Wednesday morning, he was standing in front of the whiteboard in the Bureau while Trevor sat at the old desk and Penny leaned against the window. The brass railway clock sat between them. It had arrived sometime during the night.
Nobody had seen it arrive.
Nobody knew where it had come from.
And it was still showing 11:03:17.

EVENT
FRAME
TIME

Trevor considered this. “Not necessarily. You may be putting the cart before the flaming horse.”

“Not yet.”
Trevor frowned. “Not yet?”
Derek turned to him. “That’s an important distinction.”
“Why?”
“Because we don’t know whether they are supposed to agree.”
Trevor examined the clock. “They’re both clocks.”

By Thursday morning, Alfred Weinstein was sitting in a patent attorney’s office in Schwabing. He was bored. This was not unusual.
He had once pointed out, forcefully and at some length, that a certain other physicist had also spent his most productive years examining patents in an office very much like this one, and that history had found this charming rather than suspicious. Nobody at the Bureau had found a tactful way to change the subject fast enough.
The patent attorney was explaining a device intended to improve the efficiency of a mechanical regulator. Weinstein had been listening for approximately six minutes. He had understood the first two.
The third had been optimistic. The fourth had involved a spring. The fifth had involved a spring behaving in a way Weinstein considered personally insulting.
By the sixth minute, his mind had left the room. He looked out of the cold window. Munich was busy.
A tram rattled past. A cyclist narrowly avoided a delivery cart.
Someone shouted at someone else.
A dog had apparently decided that the entire afternoon belonged to it. Weinstein watched the movement for a while. Then he quietly stared at the silent clock.
He had been in the quiet office for forty-three minutes. It felt like three hours. He smiled.
That was an interesting thought. The feeling of time passing was obviously psychological. Physical time was another matter.
He gazed at the clock again. Then at his watch. They disagreed.
Only slightly. Weinstein frowned. He checked them again.
The difference was still there. He looked around the office. One mysterious clock was on the wall.
His watch was on his wrist. Nothing unusual about that. He returned to the patent.

Suppose two identical clocks were placed at different heights. Would they remain synchronized? He knew the answer.
No.

GRAVITY CHANGES THE RATE OF TIME
