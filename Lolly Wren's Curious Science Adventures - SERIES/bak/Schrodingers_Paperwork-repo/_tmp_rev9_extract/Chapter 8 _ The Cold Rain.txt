Derek Gent had been given a difficult assignment. He was to investigate the London Underground on rainy days.
This was not, in itself, unusual. The London Underground was already difficult on most days. On rainy days it became something else entirely.
People arrived wet, irritated and vaguely convinced that the entire transport system had been personally designed to inconvenience them. Trains became late. Platforms became slippery. Umbrellas became weapons. Shoes acquired the consistency of soup. Derek had been instructed to find out whether anything in the Underground, specifically on rainy days, could prevent Special Relativity and General Relativity from both being true at the same time.
He had read the instruction three times. Then he had called Weinstein, “Alfred.”
“Yes?”
“Are you aware that you’ve sent me to investigate the London Underground?”
“Yes.”
“On rainy days.”
“Yes.”
“To determine whether it contains something that makes Special Relativity and General Relativity mutually incompatible.”
“Yes.”
Derek paused. “Fine. Next problem.”
“Good.”
“One question.”
“Yes?”
“Why?”
Weinstein had been silent for several seconds.
Then he had queried:
“Because time doesn’t exist.”
Derek had waited.
“That’s your explanation?”
“Not quite.”
“What is the rest?”
Weinstein had wearily softly sighed. “I’m beginning to suspect that time is a bookkeeping device.”
“Like accounting?”
“Exactly.”
“That sounds dangerous.”
“It is.”
“And the Underground?”
“Also dangerous.”
Derek had considered this. “Anything else?”
“Rain.”
“Right.”
And that was how, at 4:18 on a wet Thursday afternoon, Derek Gent found himself standing on a London Underground platform with a notebook in one pocket, a train ticket in the other, and an increasingly damp left shoe. He looked up at the distant station sign.
He inspected the rainwater dripping from the entrance. He considered the arriving empty train. Then he glanced at his notebook.
At the top of the page he had written:
INVESTIGATION 1047-B
QUESTION:
Can London Underground infrastructure, under rainy conditions, prevent SR and GR from simultaneously describing reality?
Under that he had written:
SECONDARY QUESTION:
Why?
And underneath that:
THIRD QUESTION:
What the hell does poetry have to do with it?
Derek closed the notebook. A woman standing beside him glanced at the page.
She stared at him. Then at the page. Then back at him.
“Work?”
Derek nodded. “Unfortunately.”
She smiled sympathetically. “Important?”
“Apparently.”
She stepped onto the empty train. Derek followed. The train doors quietly closed.
The carriage moved. Derek sat down. Across from him, a man was reading a newspaper.
The headline concerned the weather. Derek gazed at it. Then at the cold rain running down the window.
Then at his notebook. He quietly opened it again.
He wrote:
Start with SR. Special Relativity was not difficult in principle. Two observers in relative inertial motion could assign different coordinates to the same event.
Time intervals and spatial intervals could differ between frames. The speed of light remained invariant. The Lorentz transformation connected the descriptions.
Nothing about that contradicted ordinary experience. It merely contradicted the assumption that everyone was entitled to the same old clock.
General Relativity went further. Gravity was not merely a force acting across space. Spacetime itself had geometry. Matter and energy influenced that geometry.
Freely falling objects followed geodesics. Clocks followed worldlines and measured proper time along them. Again, nothing inherently contradictory.
Indeed, General Relativity contained Special Relativity locally. In sufficiently small regions of spacetime, where curvature could be neglected, freely falling observers recovered the familiar rules of SR.
Derek stopped writing.
That was the obvious answer. SR and GR weren’t enemies. GR contained SR as its local limit.
So why had Weinstein sent him into the Underground? Derek looked at the wet floor. A drop of water rolled along the rubber mat.
He watched it. It moved. Stopped.
Moved again. He smiled. Perhaps the question wasn’t whether SR and GR could both be true.
Perhaps the question was whether the way they described time could be reconciled with the strange behaviour they were observing. The train entered a narrow tunnel. The lights flickered.
Derek eyed his watch. Normal. He checked the empty station strange clock.
Normal. He checked the silent clock on the train. Normal.
Three clocks. Three agreements. He wrote:
Everything disappointingly normal. The train stopped.
A recorded voice announced the empty station.
Derek lifted his gaze. He had been expecting something. He wasn’t sure what.
Perhaps the carriage would suddenly become six-dimensional. Perhaps the passengers would all age at different rates. Perhaps a small black hole would appear near the emergency familiar door.
Nothing happened. A man sneezed.
Derek softly sighed.
He got off. Rainwater dripped from his umbrella as he climbed the stairs. At street level he stopped.
The cold rain was heavier now. He watched the sky. Then at the pavement.
Then at the Underground entrance. The cold rain was falling. The trains were moving.
The clocks were ticking. Everything seemed perfectly ordinary. Which, Derek had learned, was often when things became interesting.
He went back underground. This time he chose an empty station with a long escalator. He quietly stood still while it carried him downward.
He quietly watched the steps. They moved beneath him. He watched the handrail.
It moved at a different speed relative to the walls. He surveyed the people. They were moving relative to him.
The entire station was a small laboratory of relative motion. He smiled. “Special Relativity.”
A man beside him looked over.
Derek pretended he had said something else.
At the bottom, he walked toward the platform. Then he stopped. There was a puddle.
A large one. It had formed where rainwater had been carried down by passengers. Derek studied it.
Then at the moving escalator. Then at the rails. Then at the tunnel.
An idea occurred to him. The Underground was full of reference frames. Passengers.
Trains. Platforms. Escalators.
Ventilation systems. Water droplets. Everything moved relative to everything else.
And yet the laws of physics were supposed to work for all of them. SR did not say that only one frame was correct. GR did not say that either.
So what could possibly go wrong? Derek took out his notebook. He wrote:
SR: local inertial frames. Then: GR: curved spacetime.
Then: Question: what happens where the Underground is not locally inertial? He stopped.
That was better. A distant train accelerating away from a station was not an inertial frame. A train braking into one wasn’t either.
A passenger standing on the platform was supported by the Earth. The platform was therefore not a freely falling frame. The Earth itself was rotating.
The entire planet was orbiting the Sun. The Sun was moving through the galaxy. The galaxy was moving relative to other galaxies.
There was no shortage of motion. Derek smiled. The universe had an extraordinary sense of humour.
He put the notebook away. Then he heard a quiet voice behind him.
“You’re thinking too hard.”
He turned. A woman was standing there. She was perhaps forty, dressed in a dark coat, holding an umbrella.
Derek slowly regarded her. “Excuse me?”
“You’ve been staring at that puddle for five minutes.”
“Have I?”
“Yes.”
“Did you time me?”
“No.”
“Then how do you know?”
She smiled. “Because I was staring at you.”
Derek considered this. “That’s fair.”
She pointed at his notebook. “Physics?”
“Yes.”
“What sort?”
“Relativity.”
She nodded. “Special or General?”
Derek stared at her. “Both.”
“Good.”
She smiled. “Then you have a problem.”
Derek laughed. “So it appears.”
She walked away. Derek watched her disappear into the crowd. He stood still.
Then he opened his notebook. He wrote: Find woman.
Then crossed it out. He wrote: Find out why she knew.
Then crossed that out too. Finally: Probably irrelevant.
He put the notebook away. He was learning. Not everything was a clue.
Sometimes a woman on the Underground simply knew what relativity was. It was London. Anything was possible.
He did not write her down as irrelevant a second time.
The empty train arrived. Derek boarded. He sat down.
And then, for no particular reason, he began thinking about poetry. The thought had been there since Weinstein’s phone call. Time didn’t exist.
It was merely a way of preventing everything from happening at once. The phrase had been absurd. But the more Derek thought about it, the less absurd it became.
Consider a poem. The words had an order. A line followed another line.
A word followed another word. Change the order and the poem changed. Not merely its meaning.
Its rhythm. Its structure. Its sound.
Derek imagined reading a poem in which every word was spoken simultaneously. He immediately understood the problem. It would sound like a fart.
A very literary fart, perhaps. But a fart nonetheless. He smiled.
He wrote:
TIME PREVENTS POETRY FROM SOUNDING LIKE A FART.
He stared at it.
Then added:
Possibly its greatest achievement.
A man sitting beside him looked over.
Derek closed the notebook.
The train entered another deep tunnel. The lights flickered again. And this time something happened.
Derek’s watch stopped.
He examined it. 11:17.
He inspected the mysterious clock. 11:17.
The carriage display. 11:17.
Everyone else’s phones.
Several passengers checked them. 11:17.
Derek sat up. The mysterious train continued moving.
A woman coughed. Someone turned a page.
The mysterious train rocked.
Everything was happening. But the clocks had stopped. Derek smiled. “Right.”
He looked around.
Nobody else seemed particularly alarmed.
A man checked his phone again.
Still 11:17.
Derek looked through the window. The tunnel walls moved past.
He stood.
He walked toward the end of the carriage.
The train continued.
Then he noticed something else. The cold rain. He could see it through a small window near the door.
Rainwater was running down the outside of the mysterious train. But the drops suddenly appeared to be moving differently. Not backwards.
Not forwards. Simply differently. Derek stared.
The mysterious distant train emerged from the deep tunnel.
Daylight flooded the carriage.
The clocks jumped. 11:18.
Everyone continued reading. Nobody noticed.
Derek sat down.
He opened his notebook and wrote:
11:17 → 11:18
Then:
Ancient clock stopped for approximately?
He looked at the second hand. It had not advanced. But the train had travelled several kilometres.
Derek thought. The clock had measured proper time. The mysterious train had followed its worldline.
The passengers had followed theirs.
The Earth had followed its own.
All of them had continued through physical processes.
Yet the displayed time had stopped. That was not necessarily a failure of time. It was a failure—or transformation—of the relationship between a physical clock and the events around it.
Derek smiled. “Weinstein.”
He called. No answer. He called again.
Still nothing. Then a message appeared.
WEINSTEIN: WHAT?
Derek typed: Clock stopped at 11:17.
The reply came immediately.
WEINSTEIN: WHERE?
LONDON UNDERGROUND.
A pause.
WEINSTEIN: RAIN?
Derek looked out the window. Cold rain was still falling.
YES.
The reply:
WEINSTEIN: GOOD.
Derek stared. He typed:
GOOD?
The response:
YES. NOW FIND OUT WHAT THE WATER IS DOING.
A small official notice was taped to a pillar:
RAIN IS NOT TO BE CONSIDERED EVIDENCE WITHOUT FORM 17B-W.
Beneath it, in different handwriting:
Especially if it falls sideways.
Derek studied the notice for a moment. Someone in the Bureau had clearly been here before him, or after him, or in a reference frame that made the distinction unhelpful. He photographed the notice, filed it under Provisional Geometry, and only then looked back at the rain.
Derek glanced at the cold rain. Then at the window. Then at the track.
Then at the puddles on the lonely platform as the train approached the next station. He understood. Water was not merely water.
It was matter moving through spacetime.
Each droplet followed a trajectory.
Each trajectory depended on its initial conditions and the geometry.
If there was something strange about the geometry, the water would reveal it.
The Underground had thousands of natural test particles. Rain provided millions.
Derek got off the train.
He stood beneath the gloomy station canopy.
Rain poured beyond it.
He quietly watched.
Droplets fell. Most followed ordinary paths.
But some struck the pavement. Some hit umbrellas. Some bounced.
Some ran along walls.
Some disappeared into drains.
Every one of them had a history.
A worldline.
Derek suddenly understood what Weinstein had been getting at.
The apple. The clock. The train. The cold rain.
They were all the same problem.
Different physical systems. Different worldlines.
Different measurements. One geometry.
He opened his notebook.
He wrote:
If SR and GR are both true, then every local inertial observer must recover SR.
Then:
GR determines how those local frames fit together globally.
Then:
Therefore, if the Underground appears to produce a contradiction, the contradiction must be in our description, not in the theories.
He stopped.
That was it.
That was what Weinstein wanted him to find.
Unless… Derek stared at the cold rain.
Unless there was a region where the assumptions needed to connect the local frames failed.
A singularity. A boundary. Something pathological.
Something where the geometry itself ceased to behave normally.
He thought of the Louvre.
The painting.
The changing perspective.
The missing coordinate. The infinity.
He thought of the clock. 11:17.
Then 12:04.
Then the train. Then the cold rain.
He smiled.
Perhaps the Underground wasn’t causing the contradiction.
Perhaps it was revealing it.
He called Weinstein.
This time Weinstein answered, “Yes?”
“I think I understand.”
“Fine.”
“SR and GR don’t conflict.”
“No.”
“SR works locally.”
“Yes.”
Derek continued, “GR tells us how those local inertial frames fit into curved spacetime.”
“Yes.”
“And if we find something that appears to make them incompatible…”
“We’ve misunderstood something.”
“Precisely.”
There was a pause.
Derek smiled. “Well, that’s settled.”
“Yes.”
“There’s just one problem.”
“What?”
“I think the Underground has a singularity. Or something that wants to be a wormhole and hasn’t decided yet.”
Weinstein looked pained. “Please do not invent new topology without filing a form.”
Derek nodded. “I’ll put it under Pending Geometry.”
Weinstein did not look reassured. Pending Geometry was already three drawers deep.
Mrs Marsh appeared at the end of the platform as if she had always been part of the architecture. “If that is a hole,” she said, “it is not one of mine. Mine are all accounted for and properly signed.”
She pointed at a small official sticker on a nearby pillar. “No unauthorised singularities.”
Derek found himself almost respecting the sticker. It had the finality of a stamped refusal.
On the windowsill sat a white mug. It was the same mug that had been in the office that morning, except that it was now empty when it should have been half full, and the tea stain inside had moved three centimetres to the left.
No one commented.
In the Bureau, objects that quietly disagreed with themselves were considered ordinary until they filed a complaint.
Mr Pendleton appeared as if summoned by the word “unauthorised.”
He made a note on his clipboard. “If it requires a form, it is not my form. If it does not require a form, it is still not my form.”
He looked faintly satisfied, as though he had just closed a stable door on a horse that had never existed.
Weinstein was silent.
Then: “Where?”
Derek lowered his gaze to the platform. At the far end, beyond the lights, a distant strange train was approaching. He gazed at the rain.
Then at the clock. 11:17.
Then he peered at the dark tunnel. “That’s what I’m trying to find out.”
Weinstein remained silent. Derek waited.
Finally Weinstein stated: “Don’t go into the tunnel.”
Derek smiled. “Why?”
“Because I know you.”
“That’s not a scientific reason.”
“No.”
“Then?”
“Because the last time you claimed ‘I’m trying to find out’, you ended up in the basement of the Louvre.”
Derek considered this. “Fair.”
He hung up. The train arrived. The doors opened.
Derek stepped inside.
He eyed the clock. 11:17.
The doors quietly closed. The mysterious train suddenly moved into the tunnel. And the cold rain continued falling outside.
Derek watched the droplets through the window. One of them ran upward. He stared.
Then another. Derek smiled. “Well.”
He took out his notebook.
And wrote:
THE COLD RAIN IS FALLING UP
He thought about it.
Then added:
OR THE TRAIN IS FALLING DOWN
He paused. Then, beneath that:
OR NEITHER
The mysterious train entered darkness.
And somewhere ahead, something in the geometry of London appeared to be waiting for him.
