The Relativistic Investigation Bureau Discovers That
Solving One Mystery Is Merely an Invitation to Acquire Another
The Bureau had solved the murder. This was the good news. The bad news was that the murder’s one defining characteristic, on reflection, was that it had never actually been a murder — which left ‘solved’ doing a great deal of unearned work.
Derek Gent found this distinction less reassuring than the others did.
There had been no human murderer. The closest thing to a perpetrator was the Bureau’s own computer: Case 1047 had arrived as a future-event record and the machine identified by LOCALHOST had filed it as though the event were already complete. Trevor’s name in the PERPETRATOR field had been another classification error, not a confession.
The photograph had not been taken by a hidden photographer at all. It was a Bureau system output generated with Case 1047. Its 11:03:17 was the death-event label in the record’s reference frame, not a universal time stamped by a camera. The matching seventeen seconds came from that same event label being propagated into the image and the live display.
The brass clock was the liar, but only in a precise sense: it sometimes displayed an event coordinate instead of its own proper time. The ordinary clocks had been doing exactly what clocks do.
The rest of the note finally mapped onto the journey. 11:17 was the departure coordinate at Platform 7. 12:04 was the arrival coordinate shown by the station when they got off, even though their local watches read 11:18.
The computer had not predicted the future. It had received a record through the same anomalous geometry that produced the impossible station and filed it according to the record’s event coordinate instead of Derek’s local proper time. Its “future knowledge” went no further than data that actually arrived through that geometry.
The clocks had been explained.
The mysterious train had been investigated. The corridor had been counted several times, with results that depended alarmingly on who was doing the counting. The cucumber had been measured, discussed and, for reasons nobody could later justify, purchased again.
Even the dolphin had contributed, at 1.7 fish per hour. The appendix had done its best to make all of this sound respectable. It had nearly succeeded.
Mrs Marsh knocked once, entered without waiting for a reply, and set a single sheet of paper on Derek’s desk.
“The singularity,” she said, “is still not registered to my building. I checked twice.”
She left before anyone could thank her. Mr Pendleton followed almost immediately, clipboard in hand. “Whichever form is required,” he announced, “it is not my form.”
“Which form is it, then?” Derek asked.
“Unknown. But it is definitely not mine.”
He left, apparently satisfied.
The telephone rang once more.
Trevor answered, listened, then held it away from his ear. “Julius Barbarian,” he reported, “declines to comment, on the grounds that comment implies a timeline, and he is not prepared to commit to one.”
He hung up. Nobody was surprised.
Derek therefore allowed himself one dangerous thought. Perhaps it was over.
He sat for a while without doing anything useful, which was, for Derek, its own kind of achievement.
He thought about the photograph — his own face, on the floor, in a moment that had never happened and now, perhaps, never would.
He thought about how close those two things had sat, for a while: never would, and hadn’t happened yet.
The distance between them turned out to matter more than he had expected.
He did not examine the thought any further than that. Some thoughts, he had found, were better left proper time to settle.
He was standing in the Bureau that evening when the brass clock appeared on his desk.
It read 11:17. Derek stared at Penny.
Penny gazed at the clock.
Neither of them reached for a notebook. After the year they’d had, some things no longer needed writing down. They had simply become family shorthand — the kind their grandmother would have recognised, and approved of, in her own alarming way.
Trevor looked under the old desk, apparently on the theory that a strange clock capable of appearing on a desk might also be capable of appearing underneath it.
Weinstein looked at nobody. He was staring at a sheet of paper.
It had not been there a moment earlier. At the top was the familiar heading:
RELATIVISTIC INVESTIGATION BUREAU.
Underneath it were written four words:
CASE STATUS: NOT YET OCCURRED.
Derek frowned. Below the heading was a date. Tomorrow.
Below the date was a location.
A railway station that did not exist.
Below the location was a final line.
DO NOT ALLOW DEREK GENT TO
BOARD THE MYSTERIOUS TRAIN.
There was a long silence. Trevor eventually looked up. “That seems quite specific.”
Penny nodded. “Yes.”
Derek picked up the paper. On the back was another strange message.
PLEASE NOTE: THE PREVIOUS WARNING WAS SENT TOO LATE.
Derek turned the page over again. Nothing. Then the quiet office door opened.
No one was standing there. Beyond the doorway was a railway platform. There had never been a railway platform there.
A distant train waited in the darkness. Its destination board displayed one word.
YESTERDAY.
Penny smiled. It was not a reassuring smile.
“Well,” she observed, “that saves us the trouble of finding the station. The ship has left the barn, apparently.”
Derek looked at the mysterious train.
Then at the silent clock. 11:17.
Then at the warning. He put the paper in his pocket. “Nobody boards that train.”
From somewhere inside the carriage came three soft knocks.
Knock. Knock. Knock.
A voice followed. “Derek?”
Derek went very still. It was his own voice. “Yes?”
The voice from the mysterious train called back, “Whatever you do, do not open the door.”
Derek stared at the carriage door.
Penny stared at Derek.
Trevor whispered something about taking up accounting.
And somewhere, far beyond the cold platform, Sherlock the dolphin began thinking about tensors.
The universe continued expanding.
The train continued waiting.
The silent clock remained at 11:17.
Derek Gent, who had spent an entire book trying to discover what had happened, was finally confronted with the much more dangerous question:
What happens next?
Unfortunately, the universe had already filed the answer. It was marked:   ALLEGEDLY.

One small matter had, admittedly, never been resolved. The initials on the clock — LGV — turned out, when Penny finally thought to ask a Frenchman, to mean nothing more mysterious than Ligne à Grande Vitesse: the stamp of an ordinary French high-speed railway line, most likely off a piece of decommissioned rolling stock.
Trevor was delighted. He had, after all, guessed ‘a railway designation’ within the first five minutes, and nobody had believed him.
