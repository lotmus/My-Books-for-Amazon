Alfred Weinstein had never entirely trusted apples. This was not because of any defect in the fruit. Apples were perfectly respectable.
They were roundish, nutritious, reasonably portable and, unlike pears, rarely gave the impression that they were about to collapse under their own moral burden. His distrust concerned their reputation. For centuries, apples had been given far too much credit.
One particular red apple, in particular, had been blamed for an astonishing amount of physics. Weinstein was thinking about it on the mysterious train.
He was on his way back to Munich after the meeting with Julius Barbarian, although “on his way back” was perhaps an unnecessarily confident description of the situation.
The mysterious train had stopped. It had stopped between two stations. Nobody seemed particularly concerned.
The conductor had announced that there was a “minor delay due to circumstances.”
Weinstein had spent enough time on trains to know that “circumstances” meant absolutely anything.
He sat by the window. Across from him, a woman was reading a novel. Beside her, a man was asleep.
Two rows ahead, a child was eating an apple. Weinstein watched the child. The child took a bite.
Then another. Then, apparently deciding that the apple had become philosophically unacceptable, dropped it. It fell to the floor.
Weinstein intently watched it. The apple rolled beneath the seat. He stared at the space where it had disappeared.
Then he smiled. Newton. He thought of Newton’s famous ordinary apple.
The story was almost certainly too neat. Science rarely arrived with such excellent stage management. One could imagine the scene.
Newton sits under a tree. An apple falls. Newton looks at it.
Newton thinks about gravity. Physics is transformed. It was a charming story.
It was also misleading.
The apple had not explained gravity.
It had merely demonstrated that something fell. The really interesting question was why. Weinstein leaned back.
Why did the apple fall? Everyone knew the answer. Gravity.
But that was not an answer. It was a name. He had spent most of his life telling physicists not to confuse naming a phenomenon with explaining it.
Newton had described gravity extraordinarily well. The inverse-square law worked. The equations worked.
The predictions worked. The planets obeyed them. The Moon obeyed them.
The apple obeyed them. Everything seemed to obey them. Then General Relativity had come along and changed the question.
The apple was no longer simply being pulled downward by a force. It was following a geodesic through curved spacetime. Weinstein smiled.
The poor apple had been promoted from fruit to geometry. The distant train moved. Then stopped again.
Weinstein wearily sighed.
He looked out the window. Nothing. Then, very suddenly, he had an idea.
It was not a complete idea. It was not even a particularly respectable idea. It was the sort of idea that arrived without knocking, sat down in the middle of one’s mind and refused to explain why it was there.
Weinstein sat upright. The ordinary apple. Not the falling.
The falling together. He took out his notebook. He drew the Earth.

She returned to her book. He continued. Newton’s description had made it natural to think of the Earth as pulling the apple.
But General Relativity made a remark more subtle. The apple and the Earth were both responding to the geometry. The apple fell.
The Earth moved too. Tiny amount. Almost impossibly tiny.
But not zero. Weinstein wrote:
THE SIMPLE APPLE DOES NOT FALL TOWARD THE EARTH.
He stopped. That was not quite right. He crossed it out.
THE EARTH AND THE APPLE FALL TOWARD EACH OTHER.
He inspected it. Better. Then another thought struck him.
If the apple and the Earth were both following the geometry, what exactly determined the geometry?
Mass and energy. Fine.
But there was something else. The equivalence principle. A freely falling observer did not feel gravity locally.
That was the heart of the matter. An ordinary apple falling from a tree was not being dragged through space by an invisible rope. It was freely falling.
And the tree was preventing it from doing so.
Weinstein stopped. He considered the words.
The tree was preventing the simple apple from following its natural path.
He smiled. “That’s rather good.”
The woman looked up again. This time she decided not to ask. Weinstein turned the page.
He drew a tree. Then the apple. Then the ground.
Then he drew the same apple in a lift. He imagined the lift accelerating upward in empty space. The apple would appear to fall.
Yet there would be no gravitational field. Then he imagined the lift sitting on Earth.
The apple would fall again.
Locally, the two situations could be indistinguishable. That was the equivalence principle. Weinstein knew this.
Everyone in the Bureau knew this. So why had the thought bothered him? He glanced at his drawing again.
Because the apple was falling toward something. And in the accelerating lift there was no Earth.
The apparent downward direction was created by the acceleration.
Gravity could be imitated by acceleration. But perhaps the deeper question was the reverse.
Could acceleration be understood as geometry in the same way gravity was?
Yes.
That was already part of General Relativity. So why did the idea feel new? Weinstein tapped his pencil against the notebook.
Then he saw it. Not the answer. The question.
The question was not: Why does the apple fall? The question was:
Why does the apple have the trajectory it does? He wearily sat back.
That was better.
Much better.
The trajectory of a freely falling object was determined by spacetime geometry. But the geometry was determined by the distribution of matter and energy.
So, the simple apple was both responding to the geometry and, in principle, contributing to it. The apple affected the spacetime through which it moved. Tiny amount.
But real. The Earth affected the apple.
The apple affected the Earth.
The Earth affected spacetime.
The apple affected spacetime. Everything was interacting with everything else.

Derek pointed toward a staircase. “Down.”
They followed. The staircase led beneath the museum. The further they went, the older the building became.
Eventually they reached a corridor with no tourists. No paintings. No signs.
Only stone walls. At the end still stood a door. Derek stopped. “That’s odd.”
“What?”
“There wasn’t supposed to be a door here.”
Weinstein regarded it. “Are you sure?”
“I’ve been here before.”
“Why?”
“Long story.”
“Fair enough.”
Derek opened the heavy door. Inside was a small room. And in the middle of it stood a table.
On the table was a strange clock. Brass. Railway style.
11:03:17.
Weinstein stared intently. “The same clock.”
“Yes.”
“How did it get here?”
Derek shook his head.
“I don’t know.”
Weinstein approached. There was something beside the silent clock. A sheet of paper.
He picked it up. Three coordinates were written on it.
X = 3
y = 4 z = 12
Then:
τ =?
Weinstein stared. Derek came closer.
“Well?”
Weinstein softly smiled. “Now we have a problem.”
Derek nodded. “Good.”
“Why?”
“Because problems are what we investigate.”
Weinstein examined the silent clock. Then the coordinates.
Then the empty space where the fourth coordinate should have been.
He responded:
“The three spatial coordinates are specified.”
“Yes.”
“But proper time isn’t.”
“No.”
“Someone has given us a three-dimensional event.”
Derek frowned. “An event needs four coordinates.”
“Exactly.”
“So, whoever left this knows that.”
“Yes.”
Derek inspected the silent clock. “And they’re asking us to find the fourth.”
Weinstein nodded. “Yes.”
Penny’s calm voice suddenly came from the doorway.
“Then I suppose we ought to.”
They both turned. Penny stood there. Trevor was behind her, looking rather pleased with himself.
Derek stared. “How did you get here?”
Penny smiled. “Train.”
Derek looked at Trevor. “And you?”
“Also train.”
Derek nodded. “Right. Next.”
Penny glanced at the clock. “No.”
“What?”
“That’s not the weird part.”
She pointed at the paper. “The weird part is the date.”
Weinstein dropped his eyes. He hadn’t noticed it. At the bottom of the page was a date.
Tomorrow. Derek stared at Penny. Then at Trevor.
Then at Weinstein.
Weinstein slowly smiled. “Now,” he opined, “we’re getting somewhere.”
And above them, in the Louvre, a clock struck twelve. Below them, the brass railway clock struck eleven seventeen. For the first time, Weinstein wondered whether the problem was not that the clocks disagreed about time. It was that reality had decided to play the pot calling the kettle fabulous.
Perhaps they disagreed about which event they belonged to.
