# **How to Read This Book**

This is a satirical, absurdist novel with a strong dose of British
humour. You need to know no physics at all to read it, and nothing in
the story requires homework. The physics is there for the same reason
the forms are there: because reality does not go away when you stop
looking at it, and neither does the paperwork.

But it is also an honest book, and it is built in two layers.

**The novel** is the story of Lolly Wren, a Transit Reconciliation
Officer, Third Grade, who spent ten years in the electronics trade and
eleven minutes as a working physicist, and who discovers that her
department has been making reality simpler by pushing the difficult
parts of it into a black hole in regional infrastructure.

**The lectures** live together in the Appendix as a real class, 18
lectures in four parts, one per chapter.

Each lecture tells you what you need first, what you leave knowing how
to do, and then stops you cold until you can say the idea plainly to
someone who has never heard the jargon, and keep her interested.

**Each lecture is laid out the same way, so you always know where you
are:**

- In one breath: the whole idea in a sentence or two.

- Start here: an everyday analogy, no jargon.

- The idea, step by step: the argument assembled in order.

- What this chapter was actually showing you: the story and the science
  locked together.

- Where the popular version goes wrong: the misconception you have
  probably been taught, corrected.

- Going deeper: the real machinery, the equations, the named
  experiments. Explicitly skippable.

- What sticks: the few things worth keeping without notes.

- One-line summary: the sentence to keep.

**Three Honest Ways to Read this** b**ook**

For the story alone: read the chapters and stop at each rule.

You will lose nothing. Not a single plot point lives in the lectures.

To take the class: read each chapter, then its lecture, in order.

Lecture 1 assumes nothing;

Lecture 18 assumes Lecture 17.

Use "[The Shape of the Course](#shape)" if you want the map.

By the end you will genuinely understand superposition, measurement,
entanglement, uncertainty, decoherence, Bell's theorem, why black holes
are a problem, why local order is always paid for somewhere else, and
why there is no final lid, and you will know which of the things you
have heard about quantum physics are false.

If you are impatient: the Physics Prologue at the back summarises the
entire scientific content of the book in bullet points, drily, in about
ten minutes.

It was written by a civil servant for other civil servants and it gives
away nothing except the physics.

One promise, kept throughout: where the science is genuinely unsettled,
and a great deal of it is, this book says so plainly rather than tidying
it away.

Tidying things away is what the Ministry does, and look where that got
them.

# **Cast of Characters**

# **Prologue: Before Anything Went Wrong**

There was a time when the Ministry of Eventualities was not in trouble.

This was mainly because nobody had yet noticed that it existed.

The Ministry occupied a respectable grey building in Coldharrow, between
a Department of Minor Infrastructure and an office responsible for the
licensing of ceremonial ladders. Its sign was small. Its corridors were
long. Its carpets had the peculiar brown colour adopted by institutions
that have survived several changes of government and are now too
embarrassed to ask for anything.

Its official purpose was simple: to deal with events that had not
happened yet.

This sounded reasonable when written in a memorandum. All was well,
obviously. Or was it?

The difficulty was deciding what counted as such an event.

Almost anything did, apparently.

The Ministry had forms for anticipated accidents, projected disputes,
future absences, possible administrative consequences, and one
particularly unpopular form for things that were almost certainly going
to happen but had not yet acquired the courtesy to do so.

Nobody considered this strange.

This was the first mistake.

The second was believing that reality could be made simpler by
paperwork.

The third was giving the paperwork a budget.

By the time Lolly Wren arrived at Transit Reconciliation, the Ministry
had developed a sophisticated theory of reality. It was not a good
theory, but it was laminated, indexed and available in three approved
colours.

According to the theory, the universe was fundamentally orderly,
provided that nobody perceived too closely at it.

There was, unfortunately, a universe. And it had recently begun to
notice the staff handbook.

And the universe had recently begun behaving like an employee who had
discovered that the staff handbook contained no provision for refusing
an unreasonable instruction.

People arrived before they had decided to travel. Forms appeared before
they had been completed. Objects became difficult to describe. In one
unfortunate district, local amenities began to require confirmation
before they were allowed to continue being themselves.

Then someone discovered that the Ministry had been quietly moving
complexity somewhere else.

Not solving it.

Moving it.

Into a place called 4C.

This would have been alarming even without what was waiting in the
basement.

There was, unfortunately, something waiting in the basement.

And so, on an otherwise unremarkable morning, Lolly Wren sat down with a
cup of tea, a complaint terminal, and the entirely reasonable
expectation that the worst thing she would have to deal with that day
was a misplaced train.

She was wrong.

Obviously.

She simply did not yet have the paperwork to prove it.

# **Chapter 1: The Form That Would Not Resolve**

There are, in the management of large and failing systems, certain
sounds one learns to distrust. The metallic cough of a heating vent. The
clerical thump of an internal memorandum. And the soft clicking of the
complaint terminal on Lolly Wren's desk when it had decided that the
morning was not going to proceed properly.

Lolly eyed up from her tea. This was unfortunate for two reasons. She
had not yet had enough of it to be tolerant, and the tea itself had the
colour and emotional range of varnished disappointment. The terminal
clicked again. Lolly sighed in the manner of a woman who had once
trained in theoretical physics and now worked in Transit Reconciliation:
celestial harmony, then local government.

Her office, if such a term may be generously applied to a room with one
window, four filing stacks, and the temperament of a damp envelope,
occupied the far end of the Regional Transit Reconciliation Annex.

Gideon Wigglesworth was one of the few people who visited Lolly there
voluntarily, and one of the few she trusted with an impossible problem.
They had shared a workbench for the better part of a decade. Gideon was
very clever, painfully clumsy, and so agreeable that even the Ministry's
furniture seemed reluctant to injure him. He appeared carrying two
coffees, caught his shoe on the threshold, recovered, and handed Lolly
one of them.

"Your machine is making the usual noise," Gideon said.

Lolly glanced at the terminal. "It has only just started."

"No. The machine has only just admitted it." Gideon noted at the case
file, which had opened itself on the screen unbidden and had not yet
settled on its own fields. "What is the order of those?"

"Type, subcategory, status, urgency," mentioned Lolly.

Gideon frowned. "And the status is already saying *present* before the
journey has been resolved."

Lolly looked at him.

"That may be the mistake," Gideon said. "You're assuming the system is
recording what happened. What if it is using what it thinks has happened
to decide what was supposed to happen?"

He took a sip of coffee, turned to put it down, and walked directly into
the doorframe.

Lolly stared at him for a moment. "That," she said, "was an excellent
insight."

"Thank you." Gideon rubbed his shoulder. "The door was less impressed."

The Annex had been designed in the late bureaucratic style: openness in
theory, fluorescent headaches in practice. People came to complain about
missed connections, duplicate arrivals, incorrect departures, luggage
with philosophical objections to ownership, and, increasingly, journeys
that had happened in the wrong order. Lolly had processed three of those
this week. She did not care for trends in metaphysics.

The terminal emitted a third click, sharper this time, and the
half-written case file resolved itself, all at once, into a shape it had
not had a moment before.

> COMPLAINT TYPE: TRANSIT DISCREPANCY
>
> SUBCATEGORY: PREMATURE ARRIVAL / INTENT LAG CITIZEN
>
> STATUS: PRESENT URGENCY: MODERATE TO EXISTENTIAL

Lolly stared at the final line. Then she took another sip of tea in the
vain hope that liquid might alter the meaning of language. It did not.

There came a brisk knock at the open office door, immediately followed
by a woman entering without waiting for permission. She was in her late
sixties, silver hair pinned back, in a dark coat and sensible shoes,
with the expression of a woman who had accepted the collapse of
standards in public life as regrettable but had no intention of
accepting the collapse of space-time.

"Ms Wren?" she said.

"Lolly Wren, yes."

"Good. I've been sent from Admissions."

This was never good.

Lolly gestured to the chair opposite her desk. "Please sit down."

She sat with the composure of someone settling in for a procedure that
should have been unnecessary.

"Elspeth Chain," she said. "Mrs Elspeth Chain, technically, though I've
found that 'Mrs' invites a certain tone from public institutions and I'd
rather avoid it."

"That seems wise," ventured Lolly.

Mrs Chain nodded once, as if pleased that one civil servant in the
building had not entirely wasted her upbringing.

Lolly brought up the file. "You've reported a transit discrepancy."

"I have." She glanced down. "Premature arrival with intent lag."

"Yes," said Lolly.

Lolly folded her hands. "I wonder if you might explain what happened in
your own words."

Mrs Chain did not answer immediately. Instead she removed her gloves,
placed them side by side in her lap, and said, "I set out this morning
to visit my sister in Whitbury-on-Sea."

Lolly nodded.

"I had not," she continued, "fully decided to go."

Lolly paused. "I see."

"No, Ms Wren, that is rather the problem. I had not fully decided to go,
but I had advanced sufficiently in the direction of deciding that I put
on my coat, found my umbrella, and went to the bus stop in order, so to
speak, to think transitively."

Lolly regarded her. "Transitively."

"Yes. It often helps."

"Of course."

"It was drizzling," ventured Mrs Chain.

"Was it?"

"At home, yes. At the bus stop, less so. Halfway through the route it
became bright, then windy, then, for one very unpleasant stretch near
the old post office, maritime."

"Maritime."

"There were gulls."

Lolly made a note.

Mrs Chain went on. "I was still deciding whether I wished to spend the
afternoon with my sister when the bus stopped, the doors opened, and I
found I had already arrived."

"That seems, on the face of it, efficient."

Mrs Chain gave her a look which suggested that if she continued in that
vein she might improve her, physically.

"Ms Wren," she said, "Whitbury-on-Sea is twelve miles from my house. I
had not committed to the journey. One ought not to arrive anywhere
before one has properly consented to the inconvenience."

"No," said Lolly quietly. "One oughtn't."

"Quite."

She glanced at the screen again. The file now read:

> STATUS: APPROVED
>
> RESOLUTION: PASSENGER DELIVERED SUCCESSFULLY

Lolly frowned. Then, before her eyes, it changed.

> STATUS: MISROUTED
>
> RESOLUTION: PASSENGER ARRIVED WITHOUT COMPLETED INTENTION

Then it changed again.

> STATUS: CLOSED
>
> RESOLUTION: NO TRANSIT EVENT DETECTED

Lolly did not move for several seconds. The file waited, smugly.

Mrs Chain observed her. "That isn't encouraging, is it."

"No," she said. "Not especially."

She pressed the refresh key. The screen flickered and returned to the
first status. APPROVED. She pressed it again.

MISROUTED.

Again.

CLOSED.

There are moments in office life when one has to decide whether a
problem is technical, personal, or metaphysical. The correct answer is
often all three, but procedure requires a degree of optimism. Lolly
chose technical first.

"I'm sure," she said, with the restrained falsity of a trained employee,
"that this is simply a processing irregularity."

Mrs Chain glanced at the screen. "Is that why it keeps changing its
mind?"

"Possibly."

"And is that common?"

"No."

"Good," she said. "I dislike being reassured with uncommon things."

Lolly reached for a pencil. Most officers used regulation pens,
permanent and chained to the desk. Lolly preferred pencil. In the margin
of the printed complaint slip she wrote:

**\|**journey**⟩** = a**\|**not yet taken**⟩** + b**\|**already
arrived**⟩**

She stopped, considered, and added: with a and b being annoyingly
nonzero.

Mrs Chain leaned slightly forward. "What's that?"

Lolly covered the page with professional reflex. "Nothing official."

"It's mathematics."

"It is a private habit."

"Is it useful?"

"Not always," said Lolly. "But it occasionally stops me shouting."

Mrs Chain accepted this at once. "Sensible."

On the ceiling, the office speaker wrought a soft ping. A voice of
cultivated female neutrality announced, "Attention staff. Due to the
implementation of the Simplification Initiative, all departments are
reminded that duplicate interpretations of public events create
avoidable confusion. Please encourage outcomes to settle promptly. Thank
you for supporting a clearer tomorrow."

Lolly looked up. Mrs Chain looked up. The speaker, having rendered its
contribution to civilisation, remained silent.

"Did that seem odd to you?" she asked.

"Very."

"Good."

"It used the phrase 'encourage outcomes,'" said Lolly.

"I heard it."

"You can't encourage outcomes."

Mrs Chain gave her a dry look. "Everything in this building appears to
encourage outcomes."

That, Lolly felt, was unpleasantly fair. She turned back to the terminal
and opened the transit record in detail. A route map appeared, though it
was not behaving in a route-like manner. The line representing Mrs
Chain's bus journey seemed to have split in three places and doubled
back through a district which, unless Lolly was badly mistaken, had been
a municipal duck pond until last February.

She zoomed in.

The map reconfigured itself. Whitbury-on-Sea moved three streets inland.
Lolly blinked. Then the little sea icon beside the town name vanished
altogether and was replaced with a notation reading

COASTAL STATUS: LEGACY

Mrs Chain was watching her. "What is it now?"

Lolly hesitated. Civil service etiquette discouraged alarming the public
before lunch.

"How attached," she asked carefully, "would you say Whitbury is to the
sea?"

Mrs Chain stared at her. "It is in the name."

"Yes," Lolly conceded.

"Very attached," considered Mrs Chain.

"Quite," said Lolly.

"Why?" asked Mrs Chain.

Lolly turned the screen very slightly in her direction. Mrs Chain
adjusted her spectacles and read the notation.

There was a small silence. Then she said, in a voice compelled sharp by
disbelief, "What does 'legacy' sea mean?"

"I haven't the faintest idea."

"Can seas be legacy?"

"They ought not to be."

"Yes," she said. "That was my own view."

Lolly printed the file. This took much longer than it should have,
because the printer on her floor had been designed to detect urgency and
resent it. When the sheet finally emerged, it came out warm and faintly
humming. She flattened it on the desk.

Across the bottom margin, in type too small to have been there a moment
earlier, appeared a sentence she was quite certain had not existed in
the record before:

DESTINATION UPDATED TO NEAREST STABLE VERSION.

Lolly read it twice.

Mrs Chain read it once and said, "That sounds appalling."

"Yes," Lolly agreed.

"And what does it mean?"

She chose her words with care. "I believe it means the system considers
the place you went to sufficiently similar to the place you intended."

Mrs Chain drew herself up. "A sister is not sufficiently similar to a
seaside town."

"No," Lolly agreed.

"Nor is a town without a sea sufficiently similar to itself," said Mrs
Chain.

"No," said Lolly.

She folded her gloves again. "Well. I'm glad we agree on fundamentals."

Lolly looked back at the terminal. The file was changing once more.

APPROVED

MISROUTED

CLOSED

APPROVED

The statuses cycled with a kind of clerical impatience, as if the
machine itself wished she would stop noticing.

She opened her bottom drawer and took out a thin green notebook labelled
PRIVATE / UNHELPFUL.

It contained several pages of equations, four pages of complaints about
the new software, and one pressed bus ticket from a journey that had
taken place twice but only on alternate Tuesdays.

She wrote:

If a citizen may arrive before deciding, then decision and arrival are
no longer properly ordered.

Then beneath it:

If place may be updated to nearest stable version, then location is no
longer being treated as factual.

Then, after a moment:

This is going to become a row.

Mrs Chain watched the pencil move. "You're worried."

Lolly closed the notebook. "I'm employed."

"Yes," Mrs Chain agreed, "but are you worried?"

Lolly considered the matter honestly. "I wasn't," she said, "until the
sea became historical."

Mrs Chain stood. "Excellent."

"I beg your pardon?"

"I prefer an official who begins worried. It saves time."

Lolly stood as well, mostly because it seemed rude not to. "Mrs Chain,
I'll need to escalate this."

"To whom?"

She almost laughed, which would have been unprofessional and also
inaccurate, because there was no one in the building to whom one
escalated anything with confidence. One merely moved trouble into a
higher room and waited.

"I'm not yet sure," she said.

As if in answer, there came from the corridor the sound of running.
People did not run in the Annex unless the kitchen had reopened
unexpectedly or the laws of existence were behaving in a manner likely
to reflect badly on management.

A figure appeared at the door: a narrow young man from Internal Intake,
breathless and carrying a stack of forms with all the dignity of someone
trying not to admit the forms were winning.

"Wren," he said.

Lolly straightened. "Yes?"

"You've got the Chain file?" the young man asked.

"Yes," said Lolly.

"Don't close it."

"I hadn't planned to," said Lolly.

"Good."

The young man swallowed. "Three more just came in."

"Three more what?" said Lolly.

"Premature arrivals. One man reached a funeral before the deceased had
committed. A woman in Clerken Rise got off the tram and found she'd
already had lunch tomorrow. And somebody in East Wexham has reported
commuting home to a house listed as an equivalent residence unit."

Mrs Chain, still by the chair, said very distinctly, "A what?"

The young man discerned as if he wished to be elsewhere, preferably in a
different branch of causality. "Equivalent residence unit," he repeated.
"It's on the paperwork."

Lolly felt something cold settle in the centre of her thoughts. "Who
issued the classification?"

The young man held up one of the forms. Across the top, in a new
departmental font Lolly already despised, were the words:

SIMPLIFICATION INITIATIVE

CIVIC STABILITY THROUGH OUTCOME CONSOLIDATION

Lolly took the paper. For a moment the office seemed to become very
still. Even the terminal stopped clicking, which was somehow worse.

# **Chapter 2: The Wrong Queue**

The queue to which Lolly had now been summoned was not the ordinary
version. She and Mrs Elspeth Chain followed the signs down two flights
of stairs, along a beige corridor devoted entirely to lowering
expectations, and through a pair of glass doors bearing the legend:

QUEUE MANAGEMENT

For the Avoidance of Unauthorised Resolution

"That," said Mrs Chain, reading it once and rejecting it absolutely, "is
a revolting sentence."

"Yes," said Lolly. "It's new."

Inside, Queue Management occupied what must once have been an atrium,
since persuaded to regret its openness: chrome stanchions, retractable
belts, lanes of subtle cruelty under a high ceiling that had given up on
grandeur.

At the far end stood six service desks beneath softly glowing signs:

> INITIAL DETERMINATIONS
>
> REVISIONAL DETERMINATIONS
>
> POST-DETERMINATE APPEALS
>
> PRE-RESOLVED EXCEPTIONS
>
> INTENT ALIGNMENT
>

Between the doors and the desks stood perhaps fifty people in a queue

Lolly stopped. Mrs Chain stopped beside her.

"I see," she said after a moment, "that this is idiotic."

Lolly stared ahead. "Yes."

"How does one join it?"

"I'm not yet sure it can be joined in any recognisably civic sense."

A17/b.

whose training had been both inadequate and recent.

"If you are here regarding an unresolved outcome," he was saying,
"please proceed to the queue most closely aligned with your present

uncertainty becomes available."

A woman clutching a pram said, "Which one is the uncertainty lane?"

The young man looked around helplessly. "At the moment, madam, all of
them."

Mrs Chain closed her eyes briefly. "Oh, very good. They've hired a poet
and issued him a lanyard."

Lolly examined the room. The six lanes were not stable. That was the
first problem.

The second was that they were not, in any useful sense, separate. The
queue seemed to divide and rejoin itself according to principles more
suggestive of philosophy than architecture.

A man in a pinstripe suit occupied two adjacent lanes by means of a
posture that deserved a pension. An elderly couple entered Revisional
Determinations and emerged in General Enquiries. A student with a
rucksack advanced without moving his feet.

Lolly watched for perhaps ten seconds and had the distinct feeling that
the room was selecting answers based on the questions people brought
into it. She disliked rooms that had opinions.

"Ms Wren?"

She turned.

A woman approached from Desk Three with a file under one arm and the

BEATRIX SLOAN

Internal Audit

She looked first at Lolly, then at Mrs Chain, then at the three

surprised.

"You're the Chain case," she said.

Lolly inclined her head. "Lolly Wren. Transit Reconciliation."

"I know." She held out a hand to Mrs Chain. "Sloan. Internal Audit."

Mrs Chain shook it. "Are you in charge of this?"

"No," said Beatrix. "Otherwise it would be less decorative."

She took one of Lolly's tickets and read it without approval. "They've
started branching admissions."

"What does that mean?" asked Lolly.

"It means," replied Beatrix, "that rather than deciding what category a

Mrs Chain considered this. "That sounds immoral."

Beatrix gave a tiny shrug. "Yes, but efficiently so, which is the
current preference."

She turned and set off toward the lanes without waiting to see if they
followed, which they did, because some people generate obedience merely

At the mouth of the queue Beatrix stopped and pointed. "Look carefully,"

Lolly did. To an untrained eye, the movement of the lanes might have
appeared merely disorderly. But once one attended to the signs, a
pattern emerged.

Citizens approaching with misplaced luggage tended to be pulled toward
General Enquiries / No. Those with contradictory documents drifted
toward Revisional Determinations. People with the stunned,
inward-looking expression of persons whose day had become ontological
were drawn, slowly but unmistakably, toward Intent Alignment.

"Is it sorting by complaint type?" Lolly asked.

"Not exactly," ventured Beatrix. "It's sorting by interpretive frame."

Mrs Chain gave her a sharp look. "In English."

Beatrix obliged at once. "The room decides what sort of problem you are
having, and then forces you to become that sort of problem."

Lolly felt a small, unpleasant quickening in the back of her mind.
"Based on what?"

"On what is observed first," said Beatrix.

There are sentences which, to a former physicist trapped in public
administration, land with the emotional force of a brick through stained
glass.

being asked, at Desk One, whether his issue concerned delay,
duplication, or symbolic displacement. Each answer altered the forms
placed before him.

DOMESTIC AMBIGUITY.

bear to have."

Beatrix glanced at her. "I beg your pardon?"

"Yes," said Mrs Chain.

First question becomes first answer.

The queue does not ask. It assigns.

Lolly felt cold.

TEMPORAL MISALLOCATION WITH EDUCATIONAL IMPACT

Mrs Chain drew herself up. "I would classify it as none of your

# **Chapter 3: A Small Error in Reality**

By half past eleven, Lolly Wren decided that watching reality subdivide
before lunch had become personal. She followed Beatrix out of Queue
Management with Mrs Chain beside her and Jago materialising wherever
least helpful.

"We're not going upstairs?" said Lolly, clutching the pale blue
memorandum marked Phase One Implementation Notes.

"No," ventured Beatrix.

"To whom, then, are we escalating this?"

"No one."

Lolly blinked. "That seems institutionally unsound."

Beatrix pushed through the revolving doors with the crisp violence of a
woman who viewed architecture as a minor procedural obstacle. "We are
going to the address."

Mrs Chain nodded. "At last."

Lolly hesitated on the pavement outside the Annex. "You mean your
house."

"Yes, Ms Wren. That is what an address generally indicates unless one
has been promoted."

Jago, hands in his coat pockets, glanced up at the sky. "Good plan. If
the map's lying, the streets usually start first."

Lolly turned. "Why are you still here?"

Jago seemed mildly surprised by the question. "Professional curiosity.
Also, the courier bag I'm carrying is now addressed to a district that
no longer exists, so I feel personally included."

He produced one of the envelopes and showed it to Lolly.

District Transit Review Office, Lower Whitbury Maritime Fringe

Lolly stared at the words Maritime Fringe. "Was that ever a place?"

"Not with commitment," said Jago.

They took the bus because Beatrix did not trust Ministry pool cars,
taxis were on strike, and Mrs Chain would not pay to revisit her own
compromised life.

The bus stop opposite the Annex had changed since morning: a plaque
praising Civic Flow Modernisation, and a route map showing Chain
Terrace, which had not existed at breakfast.

Mrs Chain leaned in. "It was Elm Grove."

"Yes," said Lolly. "Is it still Elm Grove?"

CHAIN TERRACE (formerly Elm Grove)

Lolly rendered a note in the green notebook.

Place names now settling after observation.

That was bad enough.

Or perhaps because of it.

Gideon Wigglesworth had wrought the bus by a margin that embarrassed

Gideon smiled. "Either way, you shouldn't trust the first version you

matching too well, identical stone birdbaths. A cat sat on a wall with

another eleven nineteen, a third almost noon, drawn from different but
administratively compatible afternoons.

Mrs Chain stopped in the middle of the pavement. "There," she said,

someone had described cheerfulness to a machine and accepted the first
draft.

EQUIVALENT RESIDENCE UNIT 7B

Mrs Chain closed her eyes. "That," she said, "is not my house."

Address is not home. Similarity is not continuity.

>
> Equivalent Residence Unit 7B.
>
> Equivalent Residence Unit 7B (Revised).
>
> Equivalent Residence Unit 8.
>

averaged."

ominous than a creak.

The front path was lined with potted daffodils. Mrs Chain stopped again.
"I don't own daffodils."

"They look official," ventured Jago.

"They look synthetic," offered Beatrix.

Lolly bent slightly. The flowers were artificial, in the careful premium
manner of a procurement team instructed to provide "warmth."

Mrs Chain's voice lowered. "I had geraniums."

"Perhaps they benchmarked poorly," replied Jago, which was not advice
likely to preserve him.

Mrs Chain gave him a look that could have restructured a lesser man.

Inside, the house was immaculate. This was the first and worst clue.

People really living in a place introduced minor frictions: shoes with
opinions, keys of varying utility, a newspaper not quite where it should
be.

This hallway contained an umbrella stand, a narrow mirror, and a bowl
for keys, all placed so correctly that none had ever been looked for in
irritation.

Mrs Chain stepped in and drew a sharp breath. "My mirror was oval."

The mirror on the wall was rectangular, tasteful, and entirely wrong.
"And the bowl?" Lolly asked.

"Blue," said Mrs Chain.

"That is teal," observed Lolly, "which is the sort of colour invented by
people who wish to avoid a verdict."

Lolly perceived toward the stairs. Framed photographs hung along the
wall. She moved closer.

In the first, Mrs Chain stood beside a short man in spectacles whom
Lolly assumed had once been her husband. In the second she stood alone
in a garden she did not recognise. In the third she appeared beside a
dark-haired woman and a small dog.

The trouble was not that the people were implausible. The trouble was
that they were almost plausible.

"Is this your sister," asked Lolly.

Mrs Chain came to her shoulder, looked, and stiffened. "No."

"But it is you."

"Yes," she said. "Or enough of me to be impertinent."

Beatrix moved toward the sitting room. "Look at the furniture."

Lolly followed. It was a good room: armchair, bookshelves, two lamps, a
sideboard.

Only this one had been assembled in one afternoon by an intelligence
that understood comfort as a category but not as a memory.

The armchair was too symmetrical. The books were arranged by spine
colour. Three identical coasters sat by the window, because no real
household has ever believed in identical coasters.

Mrs Chain walked to the mantelpiece. "My husband hated clocks," she
said. "Said they created the room feel supervised. There is now a clock
where his photograph should be."

Lolly looked. The clock ticked in a faint, officious manner.

"That," said Mrs Chain, "is not a design choice. That is malice."

Jago had drifted to the bookshelves. "These aren't books," he said.

Lolly joined him. They were indeed books in the legal and physical
sense, but not in the intimate one. Their titles were subtly wrong.

Houseplants of Civic Value. Pleasant Soups Through the Year. A
Listener's Guide to Harp Moderation.

"Has anyone," Jago asked, "ever voluntarily read a listener's guide to
harp moderation."

"No," said Beatrix. "It's decorative literacy."

Lolly wrote the phrase down immediately. Then she crossed to the

The kettle was stainless steel instead of enamel.

The tea caddy had been labelled

HOT BEVERAGE INFUSION MATERIALS

Three motivational mugs hung from hooks.

Mrs Chain appeared in the doorway, saw the mugs, and muttered something
so compressed and eloquent that Lolly felt it should be preserved for
constitutional use.

She opened the tea caddy. Inside were sachets. "Dear God," she said.

Beatrix leaned in. "What."

"Bagged herbal alternatives," noted Jago, reading the label.

Mrs Chain gripped the counter. "No."

Jago peered over Lolly's shoulder. "Lemon serenity."

Mrs Chain shut her eyes. "Ms Wren, if at any point it becomes necessary
to break the law in order to restore my kitchen, I should like you to
know in advance that I am philosophically available."

Lolly was about to reply when the wall clock in the sitting room chimed
eleven. A moment later, the kitchen clock chimed ten forty-eight. Then,
somewhere upstairs, another clock struck noon with irritating
confidence. All four of them froze.

Beatrix said, "That's not acceptable."

"No," ventured Lolly.

"Why not," reflected Jago, "from a strictly theoretical standpoint."

"Because," replied Lolly, "the house cannot hold its story together."

There was a brief silence. Then Beatrix glanced at her. "Explain."

Lolly set the tea caddy down carefully. "Someone has produced it choose
a version before its details match," she said. "It is trying to look
finished and failing."

Mrs Chain folded her arms. "So it's not my proper house and not a proper
replacement either."

"Yes," ventured Lolly.

"It's botched," noted Mrs Chain.

"Yes," reflected Lolly.

Jago nodded appreciatively. "That's almost comforting."

Lolly took out the green notebook and wrote:

Premature decision settled house

Then below it:

A forced answer is not necessarily a finished one.

Beatrix was already moving again, opening cupboards and checking
drawers. "In here," she said.

They joined her in a small room at the back of the hall which Mrs Chain
identified, with indignation, as the airing cupboard.

A white sticker had been fixed above the handle.

OPTIONAL LINEN SPACE

Mrs Chain stared at it as if she had finally found the exact point at
which civilisation had given up. "It is not optional," she said.

Beatrix held up a folded sheet of paper she had found wedged behind the

The sheet was a checklist. At the top, in small unforgivable capitals,

DOMESTIC CONSOLIDATION VISIT

RESIDENTIAL CLUSTER 7

Below were boxes, some ticked, some not:

Remove low-value uniqueness markers. Standardise navigational cues.
Replace emotionally dense objects with compliant equivalents. Align
photographs to nearest stable kinship model. Ensure clocks converge

Mrs Chain read the line about kinship twice. "Nearest stable what."

Beatrix turned the page over. On the back someone had written, by hand
and in pencil:

> Unit 7B resisting final set. Memory adhesion unusually high.
>
> Recommend secondary review before identity trimming.

No one spoke. Even Jago, who seemed to consider silence a hobby for
other people, peered briefly without charm.

Mrs Chain took the paper from Beatrix. "Identity trimming," she said.

Lolly nodded once and looked at her. "That means me," said Mrs Chain,
with a terrible steadiness.

"Yes."

Lolly's hands, she noticed, had gone entirely still, which was not the
same thing as calm.

The house, perhaps sensing that it had been discussed too accurately,
wrought a small sound overhead. Not a creak. More a correction.

Then, from the sitting room, came the flat electronic ding of an
arriving message.

They returned to find the rectangular mirror in the hall faintly lit.
Words were appearing across its surface in blue.

> WELCOME, RESIDENT
>
> YOUR DOMESTIC EXPERIENCE HAS BEEN UPDATED FOR GREATER CONSISTENCY
>
> PLEASE REPORT ANY REMAINING ATTACHMENT TO PREVIOUS LAYOUTS

Lolly, recognising the posture of a woman about to improve technology by
force, said, "Possibly not---"

Too late. She struck the mirror smartly across the middle. The text

in the house seemed honestly itself.

Jago let out a breath that might almost have been admiration. "That," he
said, "felt clinically significant."

Beatrix bent to inspect the broken frame. Behind it, fixed to the
plaster, was a small grey module no larger than a cigarette case,
humming very softly.

Lolly stared. "What is that."

Beatrix prised it loose. On the back was stamped:

CIVIC STABILITY SUBSTRATE - HOME EDITION

Beneath it, in smaller type:

Property of the Simplification Initiative

Lolly took the device in both hands. It was warm. Too warm. "It's
local," she said. "They're installing reality correction hardware in
people's houses."

Mrs Chain said, with terrible calm, "Remove it."

Lolly observed uncertainly at Beatrix. Beatrix said, "Do it."

Jago stepped back into the doorway. "Before you do, a practical note."

Lolly glanced up. "What practical note."

"If this thing is part of a wider correction network, unplugging it may
tell someone that Unit 7B has become ideologically restless."

Mrs Chain tightened her grip on the umbrella. "Excellent."

Lolly turned the device over. On one edge was a recessed catch. She
pressed it. The module clicked.

For one instant nothing happened. Then everything in the room became
uncertain.

The clock vanished, reappeared, and turned into a photograph of a man
with spectacles. The daffodils browned into geraniums, then both at
once. The carpet shifted from stripes to swirls to bare boards and back.

Somewhere upstairs, a door opened and shut in several incompatible
styles.

Mrs Chain grasped the banister.

Lolly felt the air thicken around her, not physically but
informationally, as though the house had ceased to know which version of
itself it had been ordered to become.

She opened the notebook with one hand and wrote almost blindly:

System disturbed. Underlying states still present. False certainty was
holding them down.

Beside her Beatrix said, "Wren."

She discerned up. At the far end of the hall, where the front door
should have shown a small rectangle of afternoon light, there was now a
man in a dark coat standing just inside the threshold. No one had heard
him enter.

He held a slim case and wore the polished smile of someone whose job
consisted of describing intolerable things as procedural necessities.

He inclined his head. "Good afternoon," he said. "Continuity Review."

Mrs Chain lifted the umbrella a little higher. The man's smile remained
in place with professional courage. "I'm afraid," he said, "someone
appears to have interrupted an authorised domestic convergence."

Lolly looked down at the humming module, at the cracked mirror, at the
photograph still trying to become a clock, and then back at the man in
the doorway. "Oh," she said. "Was that yours."

At the end of the corridor, a door that had been there ten minutes
earlier was no longer there. Lolly wrote its number down before she
could forget it. When she looked again, the number was still in the
notebook. The door was not.

That was the first time she understood that the Ministry was no longer
merely recording errors in reality. It was beginning to acquire them.

# **Chapter 4: Continuity Review**

The man in Mrs Chain's doorway was not damp, which only served to make
the rest of him more offensive. He was in a dark coat of almost
expensive sobriety, with a narrow tie and a face that perceived as
though it had once tried charm and settled, after a risk assessment, on
procedure.

In his right hand he carried a slim case of brushed grey metal. In his
left he held a card badge displaying a silver spiral and the words:

> MINISTRY OF CIVIC STABILITY
>
> CONTINUITY REVIEW
>
> Field Resolution

Mrs Elspeth Chain tightened her grip on the umbrella.

Lolly Wren observed down at the warm grey module in her hands, then back
at the visitor. "Yes," she said. "That does seem, in hindsight, to have
occurred."

The man stepped one shoe inside the threshold, then paused as if waiting
for the house to endorse him. It did not, exactly, but a lamp in the
sitting room brightened by a degree that felt sycophantic.

"My name is Alistair Venn," he said. "Senior Review Officer, Continuity.
If you'd all remain calm, this can be resolved locally."

Mrs Chain gave a short laugh of such pure disbelief that even the
daffodils in the hall seemed to reconsider themselves. "Mr Venn," she
said, "you have entered my house during a metaphysical burglary and used
the word locally."

"I do understand this is a distressing transition."

"It is not a transition," said Mrs Chain. "It is vandalism with
signage."

Beatrix Sloan had already moved to one side of the hall, where she stood
with her arms folded and the air of an auditor preparing to improve a
man's afternoon beyond recovery. "Show me your authority," she said.

Venn smiled. "Internal Audit."

"Yes."

"How comforting."

"No," remarked Beatrix, "specific."

Venn set the slim case on the hall table (teal, Lolly noticed with
renewed disgust) and removed a folded document stamped with too many
approvals to be morally sound.

"Phase One continuity authority," he said. "Regional domestic
stabilisation. Sub-clause twelve permits local correction where
unresolved identity adhesion threatens public confidence."

Mrs Chain pointed the umbrella at him. "Public confidence in what."

Venn's smile remained in place with admirable institutional training.
"In dwellings, among other things."

Jago Flint, who had taken up a position against the wall in the vague
manner of a man unwilling to commit to being fully in one room, said,
"That's not an answer, is it."

"It is the one currently issued," said Venn.

Lolly, still holding the detached module, felt the house shifting
minutely around her. The cracked mirror in the hall was attempting to
decide whether it had always been cracked, and the clock in the sitting
room had, for the moment, become a photograph again. One sensed a large
number of small possibilities pressing upward through a badly fitted
administrative lid.

Venn noticed the module in Lolly's hand. "If you'd return that to me,"
he said, "I can re-establish coherence."

Lolly did not move. "What exactly does it do."

"It supports stable domestic identity."

"By replacing people's teacups," said Mrs Chain, "and misfiling their
dead."

Venn gave her a patient look. "By reducing low-value divergence."

There are phrases which, once spoken aloud, remove any residual doubt as
to whether a person deserves tea.

Beatrix said, "Define low-value."

"Features whose emotional significance exceeds their civic necessity."

Mrs Chain stared at him. "You ghastly man."

Venn ignored this with professional ease. "Now then. If we proceed
promptly, most residual variance can be contained."

He opened the slim case. Inside lay a small handheld scanner, three
blank forms, a silver pen, and a set of coloured tabs arranged with a
precision that suggested neatness had, for him, stopped being a habit
and become a creed.

A small, cold audit opened somewhere under Lolly's ribs. "What do the
tabs do," she asked.

"Nothing dramatic," said Venn. "They classify continuity pressure."

"That is a dramatic sentence," considered Jago.

Venn selected a pale yellow tab and affixed it to the top of one form.
"Site unstable," he murmured. "Occupant resistant. Decorative conflict
moderate."

Mrs Chain wrought a sound like a kettle about to begin legal
proceedings.

Then Venn raised the scanner and pointed it at the mantelpiece. A soft
tone sounded. The photograph of the spectacled man flickered and became,
for half a second, the clock again.

Lolly stepped forward. "Stop that."

Venn lowered the device. "Mr---"

"Wren."

"Ms Wren, your interference is understandable, but not helpful. The
property is currently exhibiting unresolved domestic supernumeraries.
Until I determine which elements belong to the authorised continuity
path, the environment may display minor symbolic agitation."

"Symbolic agitation," observed Beatrix. "Is that what you call deleting
a woman's life."

"No," remarked Venn. "That would be an escalation."

The room went still. Even Jago seemed briefly at a loss for an
appropriate angle of insolence.

Lolly noted at the scanner. "You're testing the house object by object."

"Of course."

"And every test makes it narrower."

Venn tilted his head. "That is a somewhat theatrical way of putting it."

"It is an accurate one."

"Accuracy," said Venn, "is not always the same thing as usefulness."

This was, Lolly felt, a remark that ought to disqualify a man from
discussing reality.

Venn turned to Mrs Chain. "I'll need to confirm a few details to reduce
remaining branch pressure."

"No," said Mrs Chain.

"I'm afraid yes."

"No," she repeated, with the sort of clarity one associates with church
bells and artillery.

Venn consulted the form as if the form might lend him courage. "Was your
late husband," he asked, "habitually visible in the front room."

Mrs Chain blinked. "What."

"On display," Venn clarified. "Photographic representation,
commemorative object, or emotional proxy."

Lolly shut her eyes briefly.

Beatrix said, "You cannot possibly think that is an admissible
question."

Venn sighed the sigh of a man burdened by other people's attachment to
language. "We have found that unresolved bereavement often creates
object density around obsolete personal states."

Mrs Chain advanced one step. "My husband," she said, "was not an
obsolete personal state."

Venn created a small note on the form. "High memorial adhesion," he
murmured.

Jago straightened from the wall. "I wouldn't write that down if I were
you."

Venn, not yet appreciating the counsel, continued. "Now then. Sister
present or absent in core domestic narrative?"

Mrs Chain simply glanced at him.

Venn tried again. "Would you say your sister is structurally central to
your self-model."

Mrs Chain turned to Lolly. "Ms Wren."

"Yes."

"If I strike him, will the paperwork improve."

"No," reflected Lolly honestly. "But I am beginning to see its appeal."

Venn raised the scanner again, this time toward the bookshelves. The
titles shimmered. Pleasant Soups Through the Year became Regional Stews
for Quiet Evenings, then Domestic Warmth: A Practical Guide, then
settled once more on soup.

Lolly stared. The scanner was not detecting a stable reality. It was
forcing a choice each time it asked. Question. Narrowing. Selection.

She opened the green notebook and wrote quickly:

Repeated inspection. Narrower answers. The form decides what survives.

Underneath that she added:

The house is larger than the form.

She looked up and said, "You're not restoring anything. You're
compressing it."

Venn glanced over with mild interest, as one might regard a spaniel that
had unexpectedly solved an invoice. "Compression," he said, "is an
inelegant term."

"It's exact."

"It is practical."

"It's theft." This from Mrs Chain, with such force that even Venn chose
not to answer at once.

Then, from upstairs, came a crash. Everyone eyed up.

Venn frowned. "That shouldn't happen."

"Good," reflected Jago. "I enjoy it when systems lose confidence."

Another crash followed, then the sound of a drawer opening in what Lolly
would have sworn were at least two contradictory bedrooms.

Venn stepped toward the stairs. "Please remain here."

Beatrix moved at once to block him. "No."

"Ms Sloan, this is authorised territory."

"You've already revised the kitchen into herbal defeat. You don't get
the upper floor without witnesses."

Venn considered objecting, thought better of it, and climbed.

They followed in a compact and deeply unwilling procession: Venn first,
Beatrix at his shoulder, Lolly with the notebook and the warm module,
Mrs Chain gripping the umbrella like the last practical principle in the
cosmos, and Jago behind them, carrying no visible authority at all and
somehow appearing the least concerned.

Upstairs, the landing had changed again. The stickers remained on the
doors, but they no longer agreed with the rooms.

> BEDROOM A hung over what was plainly a bathroom.
>
> SANITATION UNIT had attached itself to the airing cupboard.
>
> OPTIONAL LINEN SPACE now marked the stairs to the loft.

"That," offered Mrs Chain, "is deranged."

Venn scanned the corridor, and the labels jumped once more, nervously,
as though caught gossiping. "Substrate drift," he muttered.

He opened the first door. Inside, what had been earlier a bedroom now
existed in a state of elaborate compromise.

Half the wallpaper showed faded blue flowers; the other half had become
a tasteful cream stripe. On the bed lay two duvets, one floral, one
aggressively neutral, occupying the same territory with visible
resentment.

A framed photograph on the chest of drawers showed Mrs Chain beside the
spectacled man from downstairs; another, half-phased into it, showed her
alone on a cruise ship she had almost certainly never taken.

Lolly felt the sudden intellectual terror of recognition. It was not
that the room contained random errors. It contained alternatives still
trying to survive.

She whispered, almost to herself, "They're all still here."

Venn turned. "What."

"The discarded versions. The house hasn't lost them. It's suppressing
them."

Beatrix discerned sharply from the room to Lolly. "Meaning."

"Meaning the imposed version needs constant effort. Every scan and
classification keeps pressing the other details out of view."

Jago nodded as if this were perfectly ordinary conversation to be having
beside a bed with contradictory duvets. "So the house is pretending
harder than it can manage."

"Exactly."

Venn's expression tightened for the first time. "Ms Wren, you are not
qualified to assess continuity architecture."

"No," said Lolly. "But I am qualified to notice when a lie requires
electricity."

Venn took a step forward. "Return the substrate module."

Lolly did not. There followed one of those brief, lucid silences in
which every person present understands that the day has crossed from
paperwork into decision.

Then Jago said, "I may be wrong, but is this little grey thing
wireless."

Venn's gaze flicked toward him. "Restricted information."

"Excellent," observed Jago. "That means yes."

He was moving almost before the sentence had ended. One moment he was in
the doorway; the next he had crossed the room, lifted the floral duvet,
and yanked from beneath the bed a thin cable leading into the skirting
board.

"Jago," observed Lolly.

"Trust me."

"Why would I."

"No time."

He pulled. The cable came free with a crack and a shower of plaster.

The effect on the house was immediate. Not catastrophic. Worse.
Intimate.

The cream stripes on the wall dissolved into blue flowers. The
photograph of the cruise ship vanished and was replaced by one of Mrs
Chain and a dark-haired woman laughing over some unseen mishap. The
neutral duvet slid to the floor.

From downstairs came a rapid sequence of sounds: the kitchen mugs
shattering, the herbal sachets dropping like failed policy, a clock
giving up halfway through a chime.

Mrs Chain gripped the bedpost. "My sister," she said softly.

The woman in the restored photograph was, unmistakably, someone she
knew.

Venn swore, and the effect was so startling in a man like him that it
briefly improved him.

He lunged toward Lolly for the substrate module. Beatrix intercepted him
with the clean, efficient brutality of a person who had spent years
carrying boxes of evidence and had rendered peace with leverage.

Venn hit the wardrobe, rebounded, and sat down abruptly on the edge of
the bed with all the dignity of a policy meeting its evidence.

Jago perceived genuinely impressed. "Oh, splendid."

Beatrix did not look at him. "Wren."

Lolly, heart thudding in a most administratively inconvenient way,
turned the grey module over in her hands.

On its underside, beneath the Ministry stamp, something had been
scratched in tiny letters as if by a previous handler unwilling to trust
official channels.

If found active in residential field, contact S. Fainrose.

She was right. Lolly read it twice. Then a third time.

"Beatrix," she said.

"What."

She held out the module.

Beatrix read the inscription, and for the first time since Lolly had
known her, her expression shifted from contemptuous control to something
much closer to recognition.

"Bloody hell," she said quietly.

Mrs Chain perceived up. "What is it."

Beatrix straightened. "A name."

"Whose."

She glanced at Lolly. "Someone who may know how bad this actually is."

Venn, still recovering both breath and authority, said, "You cannot
involve external theoretical parties."

"No," said Beatrix, tucking the module into her coat. "But we may."

Downstairs, the house continued rearranging itself in unhappy little
surges, as if relieved to be less wrong but not yet sure how to be
right.

Lolly scanned once more at the half-restored room: at the wallpaper
finding its conviction, at the single duvet, at the photograph of the
sister returned from administrative extinction.

Then she opened the notebook and wrote:

Too much observation can make the wrong world stick.

And below it:

Need Fainrose.

She did not wait to see whether the sentence agreed with her. She was
already reaching for her coat.

"Bring the module," said Beatrix, without turning. "If we are going to
be wrong, we will be wrong with evidence."

# **Chapter 5: Dr Fainrose Is Not Reassured**

Forty minutes after the interruption of Mrs Elspeth Chain's domestic
convergence, Beatrix Sloan was in an unlicensed grey car with Lolly
Wren, Jago Flint, a confiscated module, Mrs Chain's umbrella, and a
woman whose house was still fluctuating. This was witness selection: the
third response to institutional catastrophe she considered proper, after
documentation and containment.

The car belonged to Jago. Or rather, it belonged to a man in Croydon who
believed it was in long-term bonded storage, which was not identical but
had, Jago maintained, all the practical advantages of ownership without
the correspondence.

Lolly sat in the back holding the grey module in both hands, as if it
might otherwise begin revising her. Mrs Chain sat beside her with the
umbrella across her knees. Beatrix occupied the front in concentrated
disapproval. Jago drove with one hand and the fatalism of a man who
trusted neither maps nor causality.

"Where exactly," ventured Lolly, for the third time, "are we going."

Beatrix did not turn. "Fainrose."

"That is not a place."

"No," said Jago. "It's worse. It's a person."

Mrs Chain eyed up. "The name on the device."

"Yes," replied Beatrix.

Lolly glanced down at the scratched inscription on the module. If found
active in residential field, contact S. Fainrose. She was right. There
is something uniquely disheartening about discovering that a person one
has not yet met has already won an argument with the universe.

"Who is she," asked Mrs Chain.

Beatrix was silent for a moment, which in her case meant the answer
required enough accuracy to be annoying. "Former Ministry physicist,"
she said at last. "Systems architecture. Interpretive dynamics. Early
transition modelling."

"I'm sorry," said Lolly. "Are those real subjects."

"They were funded as if they could be wrought to sit still in a briefing
paper."

Jago turned left through a yellow light with the lightness of conscience
that comes from never recognising traffic law as binding philosophy.
"She used to be very fashionable," he said. "Brilliant, impossible,
terrifying in committee. Left after some unpleasantness involving
continuity theory and a minister who thought eigenstates were a minority
voting bloc."

Mrs Chain considered this. "And we trust her."

"No," said Beatrix.

"Good."

"We require her."

"Better."

They drove west. The city thinned gradually. Office blocks gave way to
terraces, then detached houses, then stretches of overmanaged green. The
morning had become one of those bright English afternoons determined to
deny the possibility of disaster.

Lolly watched the route display on the dashboard. It altered twice. The
first time, their destination changed from Wetherby Lane to Wetherby
Approach. The second time it became Fainrose Cottage / Pending Local
Address Reconciliation. She sighed. Jago noticed. "You've got to stop
taking road systems personally."

"They've started first."

"Fair."

At length they turned off the main road into a narrow lane. A
wrought-iron gate appeared where Lolly would have sworn there had been
no gate. It bore a sign:

THE INSTITUTE FOR UNHELPFUL CLARITY

Visitors by prior argument only

No refunds for conclusions Jago braked.

Mrs Chain leaned forward. "I like her already."

Beyond the gate stood a long, low brick house with a slate roof and the
defensive air of a property that had spent years discouraging
governments. An old dish antenna had been bolted to one chimney. On the
lawn stood a brass armillary sphere and what noted like a weather vane
but was almost certainly measuring something less forgivable.

Lolly got out and stared. "This," she said, "is where the Ministry's
former continuity architect lives."

"No," remarked Beatrix. "This is where she waits for it to fail."

Before anyone could knock, the front door opened. Stephanie Fainrose was
not tall, and not young, and had a stillness that forced other people
feel over-articulated. She wore a dark cardigan, narrow trousers, and
spectacles that looked like a final administrative judgment upon the
human face.

She eyed first at Beatrix, then at Lolly, then at the module in Lolly's
hands, and then at Mrs Chain's umbrella.

"Oh dear," she said. "You've brought evidence and emotion."

Mrs Chain drew herself up. "I beg your pardon."

"Not criticism," noted Fainrose. "Classification."

Her eyes moved to the module again. "Come in," she said. "If that thing
is active, the hallway is the worst possible place for it unless you
happen to dislike your staircase."

They entered. The house smelled of paper, tea, solder, and intellectual
impatience. Books lined every wall in layers of active use. A long table
beneath the window held microscopes, mugs, and what Lolly realised was
half of a dismantled detector array.

Mrs Chain surveyed around. "She lives properly."

"I make allowances for entropy," considered Fainrose, closing the door.

Beatrix removed the module from Lolly's hands and placed it on the
table. Fainrose looked at it for perhaps two seconds. Then she said,
"Idiots."

Lolly, who had spent the day surrounded by euphemism, felt an immediate
and unreasonable surge of gratitude. "You know what it is," she said.

"Yes," said Fainrose. "A domestic projection stabiliser,
third-generation civilian adaptation, vulgar casing, incompetent thermal
shielding. I warned them not to deploy these into lived environments
without local phase damping. They said I was being precious."

"Were you."

"No," mentioned Fainrose. "I was being exact. Precision merely looks
emotional to the incompetent, who prefer it in bullet points."

Jago wandered toward the bookshelves. "Did you scratch the note."

"Of course."

Mrs Chain stepped forward. "My house has been replaced."

Fainrose looked at her then, properly, and the severity of her face
altered by a fraction. "Not replaced," she said. "Compressed. There's a
difference."

"I don't care for either."

"No," replied Fainrose. "Most sane people don't."

She picked up the module with thumb and forefinger, carried it to a
bench at the far end of the room, and placed it inside a wire mesh box
connected to several cables. A screen above the bench lit at once with
graphs, matrices, and a pulsing red bar marked

LOCAL COERCION FIELD

Lolly took an involuntary half-step closer. Fainrose noticed without
looking up. "You studied physics once."

She blinked. "How do you know that."

"You're standing like a woman being insulted by notation."

"That seems unfairly accurate."

"Yes," said Fainrose. "The world rarely improves under scrutiny."

She tapped several keys. The red bar lengthened. Then lengthened again.
Then, to Lolly's mounting discomfort, split into several thinner lines
that rose and fell like competing heartbeats. Fainrose stared at them.
"Oh, that's worse than I expected."

Beatrix folded her arms. "How worse."

Fainrose pointed at the screen. "This device is not merely enforcing one
stable domestic reading. It's selecting from multiple possible
home-states and driving the property toward whichever one best satisfies
central continuity metrics."

Mrs Chain said, "In English."

"It is choosing the cheapest version of your life that still looks
enough like your life to survive complaint."

Mrs Chain inhaled once, slowly, in the dangerous manner of a woman
considering whether murder may briefly count as tidying.

Lolly said, "But it isn't settling cleanly. The clocks, photographs,
labels: they contradict one another."

"Of course they do," said Fainrose. "Because they've confused a state
with a solution."

She turned from the screen. "When a system evolves naturally," she said,
"it doesn't jump from richness to paperwork. They're imposing an end
state without understanding the dynamics that produce one."

Lolly felt the sentence land in her with peculiar force. "You mean---"

"Yes," she said. "They're vandalising the evolution."

Beatrix frowned. "Evolution of what."

"Of everything that has not yet become final," mentioned Fainrose.
"Homes. Routes. Identities. Intentions. Any state with multiple live
continuations."

Jago, still scanning titles on the shelves, said, "That sounds broad."

"It is broad," said Fainrose. "Which is why one grows tired."

Lolly had taken out the green notebook without noticing. Now she wrote:
Not state alone. Need law of change.

Fainrose saw this and gave the smallest of nods. "Good," she said. "At
least one of you is house-trained."

# **Chapter 6: The Object Is Not Mislaid**

Stephanie Fainrose remarked there was a black hole in regional
infrastructure while adjusting the flame under a kettle with one hand

Nobody spoke for several seconds. Then Jago Flint said, "I don't wish to
seem needlessly difficult, but that sounds like the sort of thing

Fainrose ignored him. On the narrow screen above the wire mesh box, the

Lolly found this more disturbing than panic would have been. She moved
closer to the display. "When you say black hole---"

"I do," observed Fainrose.

Jago accepted a cup. "And this one."

"This one," said Fainrose, "appears to be shrinking."

"No."

"I think," offered Fainrose, "that if a black hole loses energy by
quantum emission, its mass falls. As its mass falls, its temperature
rises. As its temperature rises, it radiates more efficiently. Which
means the process does not merely continue. It accelerates."

Mrs Chain stared. "Who is Hawking."

There was a silence. Beatrix closed her eyes briefly.

Jago said, "A physicist, Mrs Chain, not a department."

"Yes."

"Yes."

Fainrose nodded once. "The house."

"Yes."

compressed."

altered my crockery that this would end in moral sewage." No one

Mrs Chain picked up her umbrella. "And what exactly do we do now."

recoverable."

# **Chapter 7: What Remains Correlated**

By the time they reached the Regional Compression Annex, the day had
acquired the exhausted brightness peculiar to national mistakes. The

CIVIC INFRASTRUCTURE HARMONISATION SITE

Authorised access only

No public gravitation beyond this point

"There," replied Jago Flint, slowing the car, "is a sentence nobody

The annex had once been a distribution hub, though years of government
improvement had polished it into the sort of place that no longer
admitted to having had a function. Its walls were pale, windowless, and
faintly curved in a way that suggested either modern design or local
damage to geometry. Several service roads led toward it and then,
halfway there, seemed to lose confidence and become lay-bys.

Stephanie Fainrose observed all this without visible surprise. "That's
new," she said.

"The building?" asked Lolly.

"The curvature suppression."

She looked again. Now that she had observed it, the air above the

"I am going to ask in a tone that makes refusal sound career-limiting
and pension-adjacent."

Mrs Elspeth Chain emerged from the back seat with her umbrella and
surveyed up at the sign. "No public gravitation," she read. "They always
phrase wickedness as if it were a leisure rule."

The gate guard, whose relationship with sleep appeared to have become
historical, took one look at Beatrix's identification, one look at
Fainrose's face, and decided that regulations were excellent in
principle but perhaps not in this specific hour. They were admitted.

Inside, the annex was colder than the day required. Corridors extended

the sort of terminology institutions use when trying to make dangerous
machinery sound recyclable.

ANCHOR MANAGEMENT.

ANCHOR THERMAL DRIFT WITHIN PROJECTED ENVELOPE

Entangled systems fail together. Decohered systems do not forgive

The country is attached to a dying argument with physics.

# **Chapter 8: The Backup That Could Not Exist**

Mr Voller had reached this stage by twenty past four. "There is a
restoration protocol," he said.

The Regional Compression Annex had by then acquired the atmosphere of a
hospital in which the patient had begun offering opinions. Two of the
wall displays were showing districts that did not currently agree on
their own boundaries. A printer in the corner had produced eleven copies
of a form that had never been designed. Somewhere below them, in the
deeper part of the building where the anchor housing sat behind three
doors and a good deal of optimism, something was making a sound not
unlike a cathedral clearing its throat.

Stephanie Fainrose did not look up from the console. "Say that again,"
she said.

Voller straightened his cuffs, which is what such men do instead of
praying, having long ago misfiled the latter as an external dependency.
"There is a restoration protocol," he repeated. "Full-state civic
reconstruction. We hold a reference copy."

Beatrix Sloan turned very slowly. "You hold what."

"A reference copy," reflected Voller. "Of the pre-simplification
configuration. Homes, records, routes, identity linkages. Everything the
Initiative touched was mirrored before adjustment. It was a condition of
approval. I signed the assurance myself."

Lolly Wren, who had spent the last hour watching reality itemise its own
injuries, felt a brief and treacherous flare of hope. It lasted
approximately as long as it took her to look at Fainrose's face. She had
gone quite still.

"Mr Voller," she said, and her voice had lost all its edges, which was
infinitely worse than when it had them, "where is this copy."

"Cold vault. Sub-level two. Independent substrate, isolated field, no
live coupling." He permitted himself the smallest recovery of dignity.
"Precisely so that it could not be affected by anything occurring
above."

"And what," offered Fainrose, "is in it."

Voller blinked. "The state. The prior state."

"Which prior state."

"The correct one."

Fainrose closed her eyes for a moment. "Lolly," she said. "Come here."

She went.

She pulled a fresh pane onto the display and typed a short command.

The vault manifest opened: page after page of entries, each one a
district, a street, a dwelling, a person, a linkage, all neatly indexed,
all timestamped, all marked with a small green tick indicating
successful capture.

It was, Lolly thought, one of the most reassuring documents she had ever
seen, and she understood at once that this was the problem with it.

"Look at the fidelity column," reflected Fainrose.

She looked.

> STATE CAPTURE: COMPLETE
>
> CLASSICAL REPRESENTATION: VERIFIED
>
> PHASE RECORD: NOT APPLICABLE

She read the last line three times, in the manner of a woman hoping that
words might be persuaded. "Not applicable," she said.

"Not applicable," said Fainrose. "They didn't record the phase
relations."

"They could not record the phase relations," said Lolly. "Then this
isn't a copy."

"No," said Fainrose. "It is a photograph of a conversation, with all the
inconvenient replies removed."

Mrs Elspeth Chain had come to the console with her umbrella in the crook
of her arm and the expression of a woman determined to be present at the
moment when the men admitted it. "Explain," she said.

Fainrose turned to her, and something in her manner changed. She spoke
to Mrs Chain, always, as though Mrs Chain were the only person in the
room whose understanding actually mattered (which, Lolly had begun to
suspect, was a fairly accurate assessment).

"When they measured your house," said Fainrose, "in order to save it,
they had to ask it questions."

"They asked a great many."

"Yes. And each question returned an answer. Blue wallpaper. One duvet.
Husband present. Sister absent. All perfectly recordable. All faithfully
written down." She tapped the manifest. "That is what is in the vault.
Answers."

"And that is not the house."

"That is not the house. The house was also the relations between the
answers. The way the sister depended on the kitchen, and the kitchen on
the route to the shops, and the route on forty years of walking it.
Those relations were not answers to any question they knew how to ask.
To write them down, they would have had to ask a different question, and
asking it would have destroyed the answers they had already taken."

Mrs Chain considered this for some time. "So they could have one or the
other," she said.

"Yes."

"But not both."

"Not both. Never both. Not by incompetence, by law."

Fainrose's mouth tightened. "And so, being administrators, they took the
one that fitted the form."

Jago Flint, from his position against the doorframe, said, "Forgive me.
Could they not simply have fashioned a second house and asked it the
other question."

"No," offered Fainrose.

"Why not."

"Because you cannot copy it, Mr Flint. That is not a difficulty. That is
a theorem."

Voller engineered a small noise of protest. "We back up systems
constantly. Every department---"

"You back up records," ventured Fainrose. "You have never once backed up
a state. Nobody has. Nobody will. If a general quantum state could be
duplicated, you could measure the copy without disturbing the original,
and the entire structure of physics would come apart in your hands like
wet cardboard. It cannot be done. It has been known that it cannot be
done for a very long time. It is in the literature. It is not obscure."
She surveyed at him. "You signed an assurance that you had done an
impossible thing, and nobody in the approval chain knew enough to
laugh."

The silence that followed had a specific quality. It was the silence of
five people all doing the same arithmetic and arriving at the same
figure.

Lolly broke it. "Then the vault is worthless."

"No," said Fainrose. "Worthless would be a mercy. The vault is
plausible. Which means that at some point in the next several hours, a
senior person will be told that a full restoration is available, and
that person will authorise it, and the system will overwrite what
remains of the real configuration with a flattened cartoon of it, and it
will report success in green letters."

Beatrix had already moved. "Then we lock the vault."

"Yes," noted Fainrose. "On what authority."

"Mine," remarked Beatrix, "retroactively."

Voller found his voice. "You cannot seal a national restoration asset on
a theoretical objection."

Beatrix looked at him with the particular patience of an auditor who has
been waiting eleven years for exactly this sentence. "Mr Voller," she
said, "I have sealed a hospital laundry contract over a missing
signature. You have no idea what I am capable of when the physics is on
my side. It is an unusually well-documented side."

She began typing.

Fainrose, meanwhile, had turned back to the anchor traces, and Lolly,
who had learned over the past day to read her shoulders the way sailors
read weather, saw that she had stopped being angry, which meant she had
started being useful.

"If we cannot restore it," she said, "what can we do."

"Something better," observed Fainrose. "Something the vault people never
understood, because they were thinking about storage instead of
structure."

She pulled up the correlation lattice again: the great trembling web of
district nodes, domestic nodes, routing lines, all of it still
convergent on the black disk marked 4C. "Look at it properly, Lolly. Not
as a map. As a code."

She looked.

"There's redundancy," she said slowly. "The same information appears in
more than one place. Mrs Chain's sister isn't only in Mrs Chain's house.
She's in the ownership record, and the queue token, and the memorial
photograph, and the route to the shops, and the two contradictory birth
entries---"

"Go on."

"---and no single one of those is sufficient. But together they
overdetermine her."

She felt the shape of it arrive. "You don't need the copy. The
information isn't in any one place. It's in the pattern across all of
them."

Fainrose permitted herself, at last, the full smile. It was brief, and
slightly frightening, and Lolly understood that she had just passed an
examination she had not known she was sitting.

"That," she said, "is how one protects a delicate thing without ever
holding it. You do not keep a copy. You spread it out so thin that no
local damage can find enough of it to destroy. Any single node may be
lost, corrupted, misfiled, or run over by a lorry, and the whole may
still be recovered from what remains, provided you know which
correlations to trust, and provided you repair them in the right order."

Jago said, "And do we know the right order."

"No."

"Ah."

"But we know it exists," said Fainrose, "which is more than the Ministry
has known about anything since Tuesday."

Beatrix finished typing and stood back.

On the main display, the words

RESTORATION ASSET: SEALED PENDING REVIEW

appeared, followed almost immediately by three separate priority queries
from three separate departments, all of which she ignored with the
serenity of a woman who had already decided which hill this was.

"How long to work out the order," she said.

Fainrose was already assembling something on the second console, a
syndrome table, Lolly realised, watching the columns fill: not a list of
what was broken, but a list of which combinations of breakages could be
told apart from one another. "That depends," she said, "on whether we
can measure the damage without measuring the thing."

Lolly stared at her. "Is that possible."

"It is the only thing that is possible," said Fainrose. "You do not ask
the patient how they are. You ask whether their left hand and right hand
still agree. The answer tells you where the injury is and nothing
whatever about who they are, which is precisely why it does not hurt
them."

She glanced up. "It is the one form of inspection this Ministry never
learned, because it requires wanting to know something other than an
answer."

Below them, in the anchor housing, the cathedral cleared its throat
again.

Mrs Chain looked at the ceiling, then at the floor, then at Fainrose.
"Dr Fainrose."

"Mrs Chain."

"When you have finished," replied Mrs Chain, "will my sister be my
sister."

Fainrose did not soften it, and Lolly thought better of her for it. "She
will be recoverable," she said. "That is not the same as unharmed. But
it is the difference between a person and a form, and I intend to spend
the rest of the evening defending it."

Mrs Chain's hands were very still on the railing. "Her name," she said,
"is Margaret. She takes her tea with too much milk and she laughs at the
wrong jokes. If you bring me back a file that answers to Margaret, I
shall call that mercy. If you bring me back a form that does not know
the milk, I shall call that what it is." She nodded once. "Then I shall
make the tea, and you will all stop apologising and start working."

Lolly opened the green notebook.

She wrote:

No copy exists. No copy can exist.

Then, below it:

> But it is written everywhere at once, faintly, and that is stronger
> than a copy, provided one repairs it in the right order.

And then, after a moment, in smaller letters:

They built a vault. They should have built a code.

Beatrix did not look up from the seal. "I can file a theorem," she said.
"I cannot file a hope."

# **Chapter 9: The Two Columns**

At eight she had been downstairs. The requisition archive occupied a
basement room designed for files and since surrendered to them. Gideon
sat at the central table with his coat still on, because the heating in
the annex had an interpretive relationship with winter, and a stack of
ledgers arranged in a kind of apologetic order.

"You look as if you have been here all night," Lolly said.

"I have been here all night," replied Gideon. "The distinction is one I
have been meaning to mention to someone." He took the tea she had
brought, missed the saucer by the usual margin, and recovered both
without spilling, which she chose not to comment on. "Vale signed for
the vault isolation. Voller countersigned. The second page of 11-C is in
a different folder, in a different year, and nobody who signed the first
page appears to have seen it."

"That is going to matter."

"Yes," he said. "I thought I'd mention it before I walked into
anything."

She looked at him. He looked at the ledger. The heating made a decision
and reversed it.

"You're doing the useful part," she said.

"I'm reading other people's handwriting. That's not the useful part.
That's the part I can do without breaking the building." He turned a
page. "Go upstairs. Something on paper is coming. Paper is never a
social call."

She went. At six minutes past nine, he was proved right.

The order came down on paper, which was itself a bad sign.

Ministries that intend to be reasonable send email. Email can be
amended, forwarded, or quietly forgotten. Paper is sent when someone
senior wishes there to be an object later.

Beatrix Sloan read it twice at the annex reception desk, then carried it
to the long table in the observation room and put it down in front of
Stephanie Fainrose without comment.

Fainrose read it. Then she laughed: one short, unlovely sound, like a
chair leg on tile.

"Oh, they've done it," she said. "They've actually legislated it."

Lolly Wren leaned over. The document ran to a page and a half and had
passed through at least four hands, each of which had engineered it
worse.

DIRECTIVE 11-C: CIVIC RECOVERY ASSURANCE

In order that public confidence be maintained during the current period
of adjustment, all recovery operations shall be conducted so as to
satisfy both of the following conditions simultaneously:

1.  The precise present configuration of each affected dwelling, record,
    and person shall be established and certified to district level,
    such that no citizen enquiry may receive an indeterminate response.

2.  The precise rate and direction of ongoing change in each affected
    dwelling, record, and person shall be established and certified to
    district level, such that all future states may be projected and no
    citizen enquiry may receive an unforecastable response.

Recovery may not proceed until both certificates are held.

Signed, on behalf of the Board, C. Vale

Lolly read it again.

"Both," she said.

"Both," said Fainrose. "To arbitrary precision. Simultaneously. With
certificates."

"That's---"

"Yes."

"That's not a difficult instruction."

This is an instruction you cannot begin." She set the paper down very

looked at physics, disliked the terms, and issued a rebuttal."

Jago Flint, who had spent the night in a chair and peered entirely

"Yes. And if I ask, with equal insistence, exactly where it is going?"

"You have asked for a different sort of answer."

"One which the first answer has created worse." She put down the chalk.

servant can requisition. The more tightly one is fixed, the less
honestly the other can be certified. There is no apparatus, budget, or
signature that alters the bargain."

Mrs Elspeth Chain, who had appointed herself to the tea and to the moral

Mrs Chain set down the pot. "Then they have ordered the tide."

Lolly felt the floor of the argument give slightly beneath her.

"What does compliance look like," she said, "when the thing is
impossible?"

Fainrose looked at her for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is the only question in the directive."

The answer arrived at ten forty in the form of a young woman from
Certification named Miss Adaeze Pike, who had a laptop, a lanyard, and
the specific brightness of a person doing well within a system she had
not yet examined.

She was, Lolly thought, entirely blameless, and she disliked what was
about to happen to her.

the table. "Field Certification. We've been running since seven."

Beatrix's head came up. "Running what?"

"Both columns." Miss Pike turned the laptop so that they could see.
"Column A is present configuration. Column B is rate of change. Every
affected address gets a line."

The spreadsheet was beautiful. Lolly had spent long enough in
electronics to recognise a well-built sheet: conditional formatting,
locked headers, validation on every field. Somebody had cared about
this, and she felt, for one traitorous moment, the old satisfaction of a
well-soldered joint: a thing built so it would not fail you, which she
had not let herself miss in years.

Every single row was filled.

"How," observed Fainrose.

Miss Pike blinked. "How what, sorry?"

"How did you fill Column B?"

"Oh, from the survey," remarked Miss Pike. "The Continuity Review data.
We take the certified present configuration, compare it to the previous
certified present configuration, divide by the interval. That gives rate
of change." She smiled, pleased with the tidiness of it. "It's rather
elegant, actually. The same measurement serves both columns."

There was a pause of perhaps three seconds.

Then Fainrose said, very gently, "Miss Pike, would you sit down?"

Miss Pike sat down, because that was the sort of voice it had been.

"Scanned it. Object by object. It's a full-resolution pass, so every

reliable."

"Fixed."

"Yes."

divided the difference by seven days, and called the result the rate of
change of the house."

"Exactly."

"Miss Pike," said Fainrose, "you have not measured the rate of change of
the house. You have measured the rate at which your own instrument
disturbed it."

Miss Pike's expression did not change immediately. Lolly watched it fail
to change, and then watched it change.

"No," she said. "No, because the disturbance is calibrated out. There's
a correction factor. Annex Four."

"Show me Annex Four."

She showed her. Fainrose read it in about eleven seconds, and Lolly, who
had developed a reasonable ear for her silences, understood that the
eleven seconds were courtesy.

"This factor," replied Fainrose, "was derived by comparing scan results
against a reference configuration."

"Yes."

"Held in the cold vault."

Miss Pike's fingers stopped on the trackpad.

"The vault's been sealed since last night," she said. "There's a note. I
assumed it was procedural."

"Because it is a photograph of a conversation," said Beatrix, "and I did
not intend to let anyone restore the country from it."

properly the second time, which she respected. "Then Column B is the
instrument looking at itself."

"Yes," said Fainrose.

"Nine thousand rows."

"Yes."

"And Column A is real."

"Column A is an answer," said Fainrose. "It is real in the way a verdict
is real. It became true when you asked."

Miss Pike put both hands flat on the table.

"So the more precisely we certified where things were," she replied
slowly, "the more nonsense we generated about where they were going."

"Yes."

"And the directive requires both."

"Yes."

"So the harder we comply, the worse it gets."

"Now," observed Fainrose, "you have understood 11-C better than the man

Nobody answered.

"Dr Fainrose. Nine thousand rows. They are addresses. Somebody lives at

Every dwelling certified to the highest precision in Column A showed, in
the days following, the wildest instability in every other respect.
Routes to the shops changed on alternate mornings. Correspondence
arrived addressed to residents who were and were not the residents. Two
households' front doors had, for eleven hours, both opened onto the same
hallway, an incident logged as shared vestibule, low priority. A man in

record could no longer predict where he would be at nine in the morning,

were erratic. The more certain the pin, the more erratic the record; the
more erratic the record, the stronger the case for a further pin." She
turned. "It is a machine for manufacturing exactly the instability it
was built to remove, and it will run as long as it is funded, because
every cycle produces evidence that it is needed."

Jago exhaled. "That is the most administratively perfect thing I have
ever heard."

"It is not a flaw in the Initiative," said Fainrose. "It is the
Initiative. Vale does not need reality to be simple. He needs it to be
reportably simple. Those are different projects. Only one is achievable,
and it is achieved by insisting on two certificates that cannot both be
honest."

being furious and started constructing.

"Miss Pike," she said.

Miss Pike eyed up.

"Column B is fabricated."

"Not deliberately---"

"I did not say fraudulently. I said fabricated. Do you dispute it?"

conditional formatting, four weeks of good work in service of an
impossibility) and Lolly watched her decide. Whatever else came of this
appalling week, she thought, that decision had been worth witnessing.

"Yes," she said.

"Then we have him," said Beatrix. "Not for the black hole. Not for the
houses. Nobody is ever hanged for a black hole." She picked up the
directive and held it out. "For requiring, in writing, over signature, a
certification that cannot exist, and then acting on it. That is not a
scientific dispute. That is a false instrument in a live system."

Fainrose looked at the paper with something like respect.

"He wrote it down," she said.

"He wrote it down," said Beatrix.

Mrs Chain refilled the pot.

"Men like that always do," she said. "They cannot bear a thing to be
true unless it has been copied twice and misfiled once."

PRIVATE/UNHELPFUL

She wrote:

You may know where it is. You may know where it is going. You may not
have both, and the shortfall is not a shortage of effort.

Then, below:

> They demanded both. So the second column had to be invented.
>
> And then they punished people for failing to match the invention.

And at the bottom, smaller:

The cruelty is not the uncertainty. The cruelty is the certificate.

The two columns were meant to be reassuring. One recorded the state the
Ministry had certified. The other recorded the state it intended to
certify next. Lolly stared at them until she understood the trap: the
system had no column for *we don't know*. It had only two kinds of
certainty, and both were now being fed back into the thing they were
supposed to describe.

"How long?" she asked.

Fainrose surveyed at the clock. "Until it becomes self-consistent."

"And if it does?"

She did not answer.

# **Chapter 10: The Moon That Was Pending**

The complaint came in from Outer Fenwick at half past seven in the
evening, which was itself unusual, because Outer Fenwick did not
officially exist until the moon came up, and the moon did not officially
come up until enough people had filled in the form saying they had seen

"Ratepayer states," she said aloud, "that on the night of the

eleven, at which point enough of the residents had done their forms and
it committed.'"

"Being a moon, I think."

"I am aware of that, Ms Sloan. I am reading you the complaint."

Stephanie Fainrose, who had not slept in a describable way for two days
and had reached the particular plateau of tiredness at which she became
lucid about everything except her own coat, which she had put on inside
out, took the sheet from her.

"Outer Fenwick," she said. "Get the car."

At a quarter to nine, with the sky doing something tentative and
unfinished in the east, Lolly first understood the shape of what they
were driving towards. People sat on their front steps. Not many (eleven,
perhaps fourteen, scattered along half a mile of terraced housing), each
with a folding chair, a flask, and a clipboard, all facing the same

attraction was running late.

The far side of the ridge fell away towards Sub-District 6, which lay
under a continuity freeze, Vale's order, to hold the local grid steady
while 4C was argued about elsewhere. It showed from up here as a grid of
windows all lit the same flat yellow, not one of them changing. The

OUTER FENWICK NOCTURNAL AMENITY:

CONFIRMATION OF SIGHTING

with boxes for name, address, and time, and a tick beside the words I
confirm that I have observed the lunar body described in Schedule 4 and
consider it to be present.

"Schedule 4."

"Don't," said Fainrose, in the tone of a woman reserving strength for
worse news later.

Jago Flint, driving, took the last bend onto the ridge somewhat faster
than the road recommended and said, without turning round, "I've heard
of this. Didn't credit it. There's a district over Coldharrow way that
does the same with its high-street lamp, the one by the butcher's. Goes
out on cloudy nights if nobody's looked at it by six. Everyone assumed
the wiring was at fault."

FENWICK RESIDENTS' MOON WATCH,

EST. FOURTEEN MONTHS AGO

and a woman of about sixty came out to meet them before they had
properly got out of the car.

She wiped her hands on an apron which suggested that she had left
something on a stove from sheer bureaucratic duty.

"You'll be from the Ministry," she said, without much enthusiasm. "I'm
Mrs Prosser. I do the rota."

"The moon." Mrs Prosser ventured it as one might say the bins: a fact of
civic life requiring management rather than wonder. "It came in with the
Continuity Review, love. Fourteen months back. Somebody at the Ministry
decided Outer Fenwick's night sky was an 'unresolved amenity pending
classical adequacy determination' (those are the exact words; I kept the
letter) and until it was resolved, resolving it required sufficient
corroborated observation from the resident population. So. Rota."

Lolly had the green notebook marked

PRIVATE / UNHELPFUL

out already. "How many observers constitute sufficient?"

"Forty-one," said Mrs Prosser, with the flat authority of a woman who
had fought this battle with a spreadsheet and won. "It was thirty-eight
for the first six months. Then they moved the district boundary, picked
up three more houses, and never revised the number back down. I have
written to them nine times about it, and I would like it on record that
I have written to them nine times."

"And on the night it dips below forty-one?"

"It gets vague," said Mrs Prosser. "Not gone. Vague. You get a sort of
grey smudge where it ought to be, like a photograph somebody's thumb was
over half of. Cats won't sit by the window, and my Reginald says his
knee plays up, though Reginald's knee plays up regardless and I don't
put that one on the Ministry. I have standards."

Fainrose had gone very still, in the specific way Lolly now recognised
as her thinking so hard that stillness was the only way to contain it.

"Mrs Prosser," she said. "On the night the complaint describes, talk me
through it minute by minute."

Mrs Prosser was, it turned out, exactly the witness a desperate
physicist could have ordered from a catalogue. She had logged
everything. At 8:52 there had been thirty-four confirmed observation
returns: people on steps, forms ticked, the usual quorum falling short
by the usual margin, which normally resolved itself by nine as the last
few latecomers emerged with tea.

But on the fourteenth, seven regular observers had been at the church
hall for the whist drive, and the moon (Mrs Prosser used the word with
the weary precision of someone who had stopped finding it strange) had
"sort of dithered." It was visible from some houses and not from others.

One household reported a crescent; the house two doors along, looking at
the identical patch of sky, reported nothing at all, "which caused
words, because the Hendersons have form for exaggerating and everyone
said so to their faces."

"And then," remarked Mrs Prosser, "gone eleven, the whist drive let out,
and everybody came home and had cocoa and observed up out of habit more
than hope, and there it was. Properly there. Committed, like the letter
said."

"Like the complaint said," Lolly corrected gently.

"Same thing round here," agreed Mrs Prosser.

Fainrose walked to the pavement's edge and scanned up at the sky. It was
presently offering a perfectly ordinary, extremely convincing gibbous
moon, hanging in high cloud with the smug indifference of an object that
had never once been asked to complete a declaration about itself.

"It is not that observation makes it real," she said, half to herself
and half to Lolly. She had learned that this half-address was the
closest thing to a lecture she was going to get, and had trained herself
to write quickly. "That is the vulgar version, and it is wrong, and it
is exactly the version this Ministry has legislated into a rota."

"Then what is happening?"

Fainrose looked at the moon for another moment. "A moon does not require
forty-one people with clipboards. The world is already in contact with
it. That is enough to keep it ordinary."

"Then why did it dither?"

"Because someone built a filter," noted Fainrose. Now she turned to look
at her, and there was something close to fury in it, banked low.

"Somewhere in that Continuity Review paperwork is a device (probably a
receiver mast, something modest and municipal, put up 'to improve local
signal') coupled to the observation quorum. It is interfering with the
local conditions that keep the moon settled, then letting them return
only when the paperwork says the humans have earned a definite sky."

Lolly looked from her to Mrs Prosser to the moon and felt the particular
vertigo she had come, over the past few days, to recognise as
understanding arriving slightly ahead of comfort.

"They're not making it real by looking," she replied slowly. "They have
broken the thing that lets it be real without looking, and then charged
the residents to fix it. With a rota."

"With a rota," Fainrose agreed, "and quorum thresholds, and presumably a
line item."

"Why?"

"Because a moon requiring forty-one confirmed observations to commit is
a moon the district can be billed for," considered Beatrix Sloan, who
had got out of the car at some point during this and had already found,
unerringly, the one sentence in the whole business that was actually
about money. "Ongoing amenity-resolution services. I should like to see
the invoice."

Mrs Prosser brightened for the first time since they had arrived. "There
is an invoice," she said. "I kept that too."

On the drive back that night, Lolly opened the green notebook.

She wrote:

> Nobody has to look at the moon for it to stay a moon. The air is
> looking at it constantly, on nobody's authority, and that is a mercy,
> not a technicality.

Then, underneath:

> They found a machine that quietened the mercy, called the silence a
> shortfall, and billed the street to fix what they had broken.

And at the bottom, in the smaller hand she used for the sentences she
did not entirely want to admit to writing:

> The moon does not need forty-one witnesses. It needs the sky to be
> allowed to keep noticing it. Most things do.

Rule: The moon stays definite because the whole sky keeps physically
recording it, not because forty-one signatures have been collected.

# **Chapter 11: An Uninvited Reflection**

The Phase Two papers arrived at two, in eleven boxes, and Beatrix Sloan
had read four of them by half past five with the expression of a woman
conducting a post-mortem on someone she had known.

Lolly Wren had been given box seven, which contained the consent
architecture, and it was in box seven that she found the thing that
created her go and stand outside for a while.

"It's the invitation," she said when she came back in.

Fainrose noted up. "Say that again."

"Phase Two doesn't take memories." She put the folder on the table. "It
can't. Not directly. A memory cannot be read out by external survey. It
only becomes definite when the person is asked."

Beatrix said, "So they ask."

"So they ask," said Lolly. "And the asking is the whole mechanism. If a
citizen fills in a form, describes the memory, signs the declaration,
then the memory becomes whatever the form says, because the form was the
measurement." She turned the folder round. "Look at the title of the
volume."

Fainrose read it aloud. "Voluntary Recollection Assurance: Establishing
Lawful Access Through Citizen-Initiated Enquiry."

"It cannot come in," mentioned Lolly, "unless you invite it."

Jago Flint, who had been asleep in the chair again and had apparently
been listening through it, observed without opening his eyes, "Oh,
that's an old shape."

"What is?" said Lolly.

"That." He sat up. "Cannot cross the threshold uninvited. Very old.
Doesn't usually turn up in policy documents."

At that point the lamps dimmed by one degree, in a manner Lolly had last
seen in Mrs Chain's hallway and had learned to distrust profoundly.

"Ah," said a voice from the doorway. "Forgive me. I did knock, but the
door was in two minds."

The man standing there was extremely old and radiantly untidy.

He had a great disorganised nimbus of white hair, a moustache of
magnificent indifference, a cardigan that had outlived several
arguments, and no shoes. His face was one everyone in the room had seen
on posters and school laboratory walls. The resemblance produced not
recognition but a sort of vertigo.

He beamed at them.

"Alfred Weinstein," he said. "Please don't."

"Don't what?" said Lolly.

"Say it. Everybody says it. I have had eleven decades of it and it was
tiresome by 1931." He came in without hurrying, and Lolly noticed that
the doorframe appeared to accommodate him rather than the reverse. "No
relation. Well. Not in the sense you mean by relation."

Fainrose had risen from her chair.

"Dr Fainrose," said Weinstein warmly. "I have read you. The correlation
paper. Beautiful. Wrong in one place, which we shall come to, but
beautiful."

"Where wrong?"

"Later. You will enjoy it more when you are frightened." He surveyed
round the room with enormous, benevolent interest. "Ms Sloan. Mr Flint.
Mrs Chain. Ms Wren."

He stopped. "Ms Wren, you have found the invitation clause."

"About ten minutes ago."

"Yes," remarked Weinstein. "That is when I could come."

Nobody moved.

Mrs Elspeth Chain, who had developed a settled policy of addressing the
impossible directly and letting it explain itself, said, "Who are you?"

Weinstein considered the question with visible relish, like a man
choosing from a good cellar.

"I am the oldest consequence," he said. "There is no better phrase. Some
have tried. The Babylonians had one, but it does not translate."

Beatrix said, "That is not an answer."

"No. Try this." He crossed to the long window. The late sun came through
it and lay on the floor in a rhombus. He stepped into the middle of the
rhombus and stood there.

He cast no shadow.

Lolly peered at the floor for a considerable time. The chairs had
shadows. The table had a shadow. The old man in the cardigan stood in
full August light, and the floor beneath him was as bright as the floor
beside him.

"There," reflected Weinstein comfortably. "That usually does it."

Jago said, "You'll want the mirror as well."

"You are a quick young man, and I shall try to keep you alive."
Weinstein turned to the glass-fronted cabinet on the far wall, in which
every one of them was reflected: the sunlight, the doorway, and the
eleven boxes.

He was not.

Mrs Chain took a firm hold of the back of a chair.

"You are a vampire," she said.

"That is the local word and I have stopped resisting it," said
Weinstein. "It is roughly as accurate as calling the sun a lamp. But
yes, approximately, functionally, for our purposes this afternoon."

"Then what do you drink?"

Weinstein's beam faded, not into menace, but into something worse, which
was seriousness.

"Ah," he said. "You go straight to it. Good. I do so dislike the ones
who ask about bats."

He came away from the window and sat, uninvited, in the chair Jago had
vacated.

"I do not drink blood, Mrs Chain. Blood is a courier." He laced his old
hands together. "When a question is put to the world, and the world is
obliged to produce one answer, the other answers are not filed. They
cease, and in ceasing they release something." He shrugged. "I take
that. I have taken it since the first measurement."

Lolly felt the room tilt. "You feed on collapse."

"On the discarded branch, yes. The road not taken, though your poet was
sentimental and I am a diner."

"Then---" Lolly's voice came out wrong, and she started again. "Then the
Simplification Initiative---"

"Is the finest thing that has happened to me since agriculture," said
Alfred Weinstein.

The silence that followed had a physical weight.

"Nine thousand dwellings," he said, "each forced from a rich state into
a thin one, tens of thousands of alternatives extinguished per address,
per week, with certificates. It is not a meal. A meal is small. This is
industry. A national programme with a budget line."

Lolly opened the green notebook and\-\--before Weinstein could
continue\-\--wrote quickly in the margin:

> *I hid in equations for ten years. Then in Transit Reconciliation for
> five.*
>
> *Now in notebooks, keeping score of things without having to act.*
>
> *When did noticing become another way not to decide?*

She closed the book. Looked up.

\"Yes,\" mentioned Weinstein, with real approval, as if he\'d been
waiting for exactly that question. \"Your Dr Fainrose is right that the
country can be recovered from its correlations. But if you ask leading
questions about what people remember, and each answer becomes true as it
is given, the agreements will be manufactured, the syndrome will be
clean, and the code will report itself intact: around nothing.\"

Fainrose said, very carefully, "And the anchor. 4C."

Weinstein's expression changed for the first time.

"No," he said. "That is not mine, and I want it understood. I did not

is not appetite, Dr Fainrose; that is waste, and it will end with the

He sat forward. "This is why I have come. Not to gloat."

"Then why?" asked Fainrose.

"Because Phase Two is a mistake even by my standards," considered

He picked up box seven's folder and turned it over with distaste.

"Houses I can dine on for a century. But memory is the record of which

trying to repair itself."

Lolly observed slowly, "The error correction."

"Yes," said Weinstein, with real approval. "Your Dr Fainrose is right
that the country can be recovered from its correlations. But if you ask
leading questions about what people remember, and each answer becomes
true as it is given, the agreements will be manufactured, the syndrome
will be clean, and the code will report itself intact: around nothing."

Beatrix said, "It would look like a successful recovery."

"It would be a successful recovery of the wrong state. Certified.
Permanently. With no residue for anyone to appeal to, and no residue for
anyone to eat." He sat back. "You have created me pedagogical. I resent
it."

Mrs Chain, who had not sat down, said, "You want us to stop them."

"I want the ledger to survive. Yes."

"For your own reasons."

"Entirely for my own reasons," observed Weinstein. "Mrs Chain, I have

Fainrose said, "You noted my paper was wrong in one place."

"I did."

"Where?"

Alfred Weinstein smiled. It was not a comfortable smile, and Lolly

"You write," said Weinstein, "as though a measurement were an event: a

He shook his head. "That is the working assumption of every laboratory

believes it is standing outside the room. It is not. There is no
outside. When it finally asks what everybody remembers, it will not be
reading the answer."

something that, in a warmer species, might have been affection.

"It will be writing it," said Alfred Weinstein. "And it will be inside
the sentence when it does. Good evening. Do not invite me anywhere
without meaning it."

He left.

The lamps came back up by one degree.

a moment's reflection, stood up again and chose another.

Beatrix said, to nobody, "We have an ally who eats the thing we are

"He eats the offcuts of the thing we are trying to save," considered

Mrs Chain finally sat.

"He cast no shadow," she said, "and he did not lie to us once. I have
met the opposite arrangement all week, and I know which I find more

Then:

Phase Two does not read memory. It writes it, and signs it, and the

# **Chapter 12: The Duc de Broccoli Explains Himself Slowly**

Phase Two went live at seven the next morning in four sub-districts,
because Vale had been suspended at midnight and the Board had
discovered, on reading his file, that they preferred the version of him
that had signed things.

Fainrose had a view, delivered to the syndrome table. "They have taken
the one signature off the directive," she said. "Now it has no author,
and whoever stands up in that inquiry and says what it did will be
thanked for the disruption and not the truth. The cheapest move there
is, and they will make it every time."

By eight, Beatrix Sloan had established that the suspension had been
proposed by a committee none of whose members would consent to be named.
By nine she had stopped shouting at telephones and started drafting,
which Lolly Wren had learned to recognise as the more dangerous phase.

Lolly, by ten in the morning, was no use to anybody.

This was not modesty. She had checked. Fainrose needed silence and
stabiliser algebra; Beatrix needed a lawyer; Gideon was in the
requisition archive with fifteen years of ledgers. Jago had gone to
Croydon on an errand unlikely to be relevant. Mrs Chain had gone to
Sub-District 6 to sit with another woman whose sister's name had gone
the same way, and had taken the umbrella.

The woman in Sub-District 6 had a classification: P-9, pending status,
for a citizen the survey had caught in the middle of something, a name
half-said, a journey not yet agreed to, and then required to sign
regardless. Beside the box, on file after file, a second field read no
action required. The log stood at ninety-one that morning, and was
climbing.

Lolly had a green notebook marked PRIVATE/UNHELPFUL containing forty
pages of observations, every one of which had turned out, on arrival, to
be something a professional had known since 1935.

She went out to sit on the wall by the annex car park, which was where
the Duc found her.

"You are the one who writes things down," said a voice.

Lolly looked up.

The man standing over her was slight, elderly, and dressed with an
exactness that had gone out of fashion so long ago it had returned as a
moral position: a dark suit of beautiful cut, a stiff collar, a narrow
grey tie, and gloves. He held a hat. He gave the impression of someone
who had been waiting to be noticed, and who considered impatience a
failure of breeding.

"I am sorry to intrude," he said. His English was slow, exact, and
unhurried in a way that rendered Lolly realise how quickly everybody
else had been speaking for three days. "May I present myself?
Louis-Raymond-Victor, Duc de Broccoli."

Lolly got up. She was not entirely sure why, except that one did.

"Lolly Wren."

"Yes. Transit Reconciliation." De Broccoli inclined his head very
slightly. "I have read your notebook."

"You have not."

"Not the object. The shape of it. Dr Fainrose quoted four of your lines
to me this morning to complain about them, which is the highest form of
French compliment." He looked at the wall. "May I sit? It is a long time
since I have sat on a wall and I should like to know whether it is still
possible."

They sat on the wall.

"You are not with the Ministry," offered Lolly.

"Certainly not."

"Are you with him? The old man. Weinstein."

De Broccoli's face did something complicated and courteous.

"I have known Alfred Weinstein," he said, "for ninety-one years, and I
have never once forgiven him. I would trust him with my life and nothing
smaller. He telephoned me last night. He said: Louis, they have a clerk
who has understood four things by herself, and she is about to stop."

Then De Broccoli turned his head. "Was he correct?"

Lolly eyed at the car park.

"I was a physicist," she said. "For about eleven minutes. Then
electronics for ten years, then Transit Reconciliation. Three days ago I
started noticing things in a notebook, and every one turns out to be a
standard result with somebody's name on it."

She did not much care how it sounded. "I write that the house is larger
than the form and feel clever until somebody names it."

De Broccoli listened with grave attention, as though it were a technical
report.

Then he said, "May I tell you what I was, in 1911?"

"All right."

"I was twenty-two. I had taken my degree in history." He said it without
emphasis. "My family had been soldiers and diplomats for six hundred
years and I was, by inclination, an archivist. My brother was the
physicist. I was the one who read."

Lolly turned to look at him.

"In 1911 my brother served as secretary to a congress of physicists,"
mentioned de Broccoli, "and brought home the papers. I read them because
I read everything." He turned the hat over. "I had no laboratory, no
standing. What I had was the habit of a historian: when two authorities
disagree, one does not choose. One looks for the document they have both
misread."

"And what were the two authorities?"

"Light," ventured de Broccoli, "which everybody knew was a wave, and
which Einstein had shown behaved in some circumstances as though it were
a great number of small hard things."

He shrugged. "The physicists were embarrassed. I was not, because I was
not a physicist, and so I took it seriously in both directions at once."

"Meaning?"

"Everyone was asking how a wave could be a particle." He looked at
Lolly. "Nobody was asking whether a particle might be a wave. It
required no apparatus and no genius: only somebody outside the room,
with the wrong training."

"And you were right."

"I was right," said de Broccoli, "and it took four years to say it
properly. When I noted it, my examiners did not believe it. That is
usually how it goes."

Lolly said nothing for a while. "You are telling me the outsider
sometimes gets it."

"No." De Broccoli's voice was suddenly quite firm. "That is a comforting
story and I dislike it. The outsider usually gets nothing and dies
unpublished. I am telling you something narrower." He set the hat down
on the wall. "Ms Wren, every observation in your notebook has a name
already. Which of them did you write down before you were told the
name?"

"All of them. That's rather my point."

"No," offered de Broccoli. "That is my point. You are treating the
pre-existence of the name as evidence that the observation was
worthless. It is evidence that the observation was correct."

He let that sit. "One may receive a piece of physics and possess a fact.
Or one may arrive at it and possess the reason, and go on to the next
thing, which has no name yet. The second is the only kind that is any
use in a crisis, and it is the only kind you have."

Lolly found that her throat had gone rather tight, which was
inconvenient.

Somewhere behind her a phone began ringing and did not stop for a long
time. When it cut off, Jago's voice carried across the car park in
fragments "live", and Lolly understood, without turning round, that
Phase Two was still spreading while she sat on a wall discussing 1911.

"Fainrose does both," she said.

"Dr Fainrose is formidable and will save perhaps nine thousand houses
this week," noted de Broccoli. "But she cannot hear the question she was
trained out of hearing. That is what training costs."

He picked up the hat again. "Weinstein told you there is no view from
nowhere, and that nothing is definite until agreement has spread. It
frightened you."

"Yes."

"Good. It should. It is also not the only available account, and he did
not tell you that because he is an old snob who enjoys the vertigo." De
Broccoli discerned out at the car park. "May I show you the other one?
It is mine, unfashionable, and I have been out of favour for eighty
years, which gives a man leisure to be sure."

"Please."

"Then tell me. When Mr Flint pulled the cable from beneath Mrs Chain's
bed, and the wallpaper became flowers again, where had the flowers
been?"

Lolly opened her mouth and stopped.

She remembered writing it down, standing in a bedroom with two duvets
and feeling the strange intellectual terror of recognition.

"They were still there," she said. "Underneath. Suppressed, not deleted.
It was being held in place by continuous intervention."

"Yes," said de Broccoli quietly. "On Weinstein's account, that is loose
talk. On his account there was no fact about the flowers until agreement
reached them, and what you felt in that room was a metaphor."

He turned. "But you did not write down a metaphor, Ms Wren. You wrote
down a mechanism. You noted the lie required electricity."

"I did say that."

"It is the best sentence in your notebook, and you have not understood
it yet."

Lolly said slowly, "Go on."

"Suppose the house always had one definite configuration. One actual
wallpaper. An actual state, at every moment."

De Broccoli raised one gloved finger. "And suppose there exists a wave:
a real physical wave, spread over every configuration the house might
have had. Suppose the actual configuration is guided by that wave:
steered, never anywhere but somewhere, yet always told where to go next
by a thing that fills the whole space of alternatives."

Lolly sat very still.

"Then the unrealised versions are real," she said. "Even though they
never happened."

"Real, and empty, and effective," mentioned de Broccoli. "That is the
whole of it. The branch nobody took is not a ghost and not an accounting
error. It is part of the object that decides where you go. Interference
is what it feels like when an empty possibility pushes."

"So when Venn scanned the mantelpiece---"

"He did not create a fact. There was already a fact." De Broccoli's
voice sharpened. "He deformed the guidance. He altered the wave steering
that woman's household. The actual configuration went somewhere thin.
When Mr Flint tore the cable out, the deformation ceased. The flowers
came back because the wave that had always contained them was permitted,
briefly, to matter again."

The air over the car park did something. Lolly chose to disbelieve it.

"That's a different crime," she said.

"It is an entirely different crime," noted de Broccoli, "and this is the
only thing I came to say." He turned to face her. "Ms Sloan is building
a case that the Ministry falsified records: true, and small. Dr Fainrose
is building a case that they interfered with lawful evolution: truer,
and larger. But if what I have described is so, then everything they
have done (the queue, the scanner, the certificates, and now the
memories) was not an intrusion into knowledge."

"It was an intrusion into what steers."

"Yes."

"They have been standing in the wave."

"They have been standing in the wave," reflected the Duc de Broccoli,
"with clipboards, for fifteen years, charging rent."

Lolly was already writing. She was aware that her hand was not entirely
steady and did not much care about that either.

"Weinstein considered you cannot record the phase," she said, still
writing. "That the vault was worthless because there is no phase
record."

"He is right."

"But if the wave is real, a physical thing in configuration space." She
looked up. "It is still there. Deformed, but there. You would not
restore the country from a copy. You would restore it by taking your
apparatus out of the wave and letting the guidance relax."

De Broccoli did not answer at once.

When he did, his voice had changed, and Lolly realised with a small
shock that the old man had not, until that moment, been certain of her.

"Ms Wren," he said. "Nobody told you that."

"No."

"It does not have a name."

"Doesn't it?"

"There are people who would recognise the shape of it," reflected de
Broccoli. "There is no one who has ventured it about a country." He
rose. "You will now go upstairs and say it to Dr Fainrose. She will tell
you it is unfalsifiable metaphysics and a hundred years out of fashion.
Then she will be unable to sleep, because it also specifies a procedure,
and hers does not."

Lolly stood. "And is it true?"

"I have believed it for eighty years without being able to prove it,"
considered the Duc de Broccoli, "which I am told is called faith, though
I maintain it is merely patience."

He put out a gloved hand. Lolly shook it. It was quite cold. "One last
thing."

"Yes."

"You said you were a physicist for eleven minutes, once." He was already
turning away. "I was a historian until twenty-two, and a physicist for
sixty years afterward, and never once both. That is why I could not see
what your bureaucracy was doing to those houses and you could. Do not
stand on a wall about it."

He walked off across the car park. The sunlight fell on him properly,
and he cast a completely ordinary shadow, for which Lolly was obscurely
grateful.

Upstairs, Fainrose did not look up.

"If that was the Duc," she said, "he is a mystic and I love him and he
is eighty years out of date."

"Probably," replied Lolly. She put the notebook on the console, open.
"Read it anyway."

She read it.

Then she stopped working, which she had not done in nineteen hours.

"Lolly," she said.

"Yes."

# **Chapter 13: The Panel of Experts**

# **Chapter 14: Eleven Dimensions and a Whisper**

They went into Sub-District 6 on the Thursday with two vans, a portable
timing rack, four hired photodetectors, and paperwork Beatrix had signed
in a hand that dared anyone to query it. Fainrose was in the primary
school hall running cable. Jago had gone for a fifth detector. Lolly,

pages from box seven.

There was one other person in the reading room: a slight man in his
seventies, entirely bald, papers colonising two additional tables. He
wrote in pencil, continuously, pausing every twenty minutes to look at
the shut-down carpet showroom. The librarian had given up asking him to
consolidate, and simply reshelved around him.

At about four o'clock he said, without turning round, in a voice so
quiet that Lolly was not initially certain it had been aimed at her:
"You are the person doing the Bell test on the housing lattice."

Lolly put down her pen. "How do you know that."

"Dr Bellboy mentioned it." The man turned. He had a pleasant, unemphatic
face and the mildest possible manner, and he spoke so softly that Lolly
found herself leaning forward. "I think it is an excellent idea. It is
the correct instinct. Forgive me. Eduard von Wittenberg."

"Lolly Wren."

"Yes." Von Wittenberg gathered three sheets of paper together,
apparently at random, and came and sat down opposite her. "May I ask you
something before you ask me anything?"

"Of course."

"When you say the country is held together by correlations" --- he
offered it gently, as though it might be fragile --- "what do you
imagine the correlations are between?"

Lolly thought about it. "Houses," she said. "Records. Routes. Districts.
People. The nodes on Fainrose's lattice."

"Yes. And what are those made of."

"Bricks. Paper. Copper. People are forced of cells." She heard where
this was going. "Cells are created of molecules, molecules of atoms,
atoms of nuclei and electrons, nuclei of quarks."

"And quarks?"

"Are wrought of nothing. They're fundamental. Points."

Von Wittenberg smiled very slightly. "That is the standard answer and it
is the best-tested answer in the history of thought," he said. "It is
also, I believe, wrong, and I have believed it for about fifty years and
been unable to prove it."

Lolly sat back. "You're going to tell me they're strings."

"I am going to tell you something a little worse than that," noted
Eduard von Wittenberg apologetically. "May I use your notebook? I shall
use a clean page."

He drew, very small, a dot. "A point particle," he said. "It has no size
and no internal structure. If you wish to explain why there are so many
different particles, you must simply list them."

Beneath the dot he drew a tiny closed loop. "Now suppose instead that
the fundamental object is not a point but a small loop, of a length so
short that every experiment ever performed sees it as a point. A loop
can do something a point cannot." He eyed up. "It can vibrate."

"Different vibrations, different particles," said Lolly.

"Different vibrations, different particles. Not seventeen unrelated
facts. One object, seventeen notes." He said it without any drama
whatever, which somehow drove it land harder. "A violin string is not
seventeen instruments. It is one string, played differently."

Lolly observed at the loop for a while. "And the electron and the quark
and the photon---"

"Notes."

"And gravity?"

"Ah," said von Wittenberg. "That is the part that persuaded me. For
fifty years the scandal was that our theory of the very small and our
theory of gravity would not sit in the same room. Put them together and
the answers came out infinite. Every attempt failed."

He tapped the little loop. "But if the objects are loops rather than
points, the infinities do not arise. And more than that: among the notes
the loop can play, one of them, unavoidably, is a massless spin-two
particle."

"Which is?"

"The graviton," said von Wittenberg. "You do not put gravity in. You
cannot keep it out. It is simply one of the things the string does." He
paused. "I have never got over that. I am seventy-four and I have not
got over it."

Lolly realised she had stopped taking notes and started again. "Where's
the catch."

"There are three, and I shall not hide them." Von Wittenberg counted
them off on his fingers. "First: the strings are so small that no
accelerator we could build could see one directly. There has never been
a direct experimental test. Dr Fainrose is entirely right to say so."

"Second?"

"The mathematics does not work in four dimensions. It requires ten." He
said this the way another man might mention that a recipe requires
buttermilk. "Nine of space, one of time. Since we experience three, the
other six must be curled up small --- wound so tightly that nothing you
or I can do will ever push anything along them."

Lolly peered out of the window at the carpet showroom. "That is a great
deal to ask of a car park," she said.

Somewhere outside, a car alarm went off and was silenced. Lolly glanced
at her watch, a new habit, three days old, and found that two hours had
gone, with 4C still losing mass somewhere behind all of this.

"It is, yes." Von Wittenberg surveyed pleased. "And the third catch is
why I am in a library in Sub-District 6 rather than at home. For a long
time there were five different string theories. An embarrassment: one
had set out to explain why there is only one world and had produced five
candidates."

"So what happened."

"In 1995," said Eduard von Wittenberg, "I gave a talk." He mentioned
this with the caution of a man aware that institutions have been damaged
by less. He said it so quietly and so flatly that Lolly nearly missed
the size of it.

"The five theories were not five theories," mentioned von Wittenberg.
"They were five descriptions of one structure, related by dualities.
Assembled properly, they did not fit in ten dimensions. They fitted in
eleven, and the objects were not only strings but membranes."

"And that's the theory."

"That is the theory, and it has no proper formulation yet, and so it has
no proper name. I called it M-theory."

"What does the M stand for."

"Membrane," said von Wittenberg. "Or mystery. Or magic. According to
taste. I have never settled it, and I have come to think the vagueness
was accidentally honest."

Lolly closed the notebook. "Professor von Wittenberg," she said. "I will
write down anything that tells me what they dumped into 4C. I will not
take dictation on eleven dimensions until then. In three days I have
been told four incompatible accounts of what is real. And you have now
told me everything is made of eleven-dimensional membranes nobody can
see."

She surveyed at the little loop. "I have a woman in this district who
was stopped in the middle of remembering her sister's name. What am I
supposed to do with strings?"

Von Wittenberg did not take offence. "That is the right question," he
said. "First: nothing. You are supposed to do nothing with strings. The
Bell measurement is what matters this week. Anyone who tells you eleven
dimensions will help you get an injunction is lying to you."

"All right."

"Second: I did not come here to tell you what everything is fashioned
of. I cannot demonstrate it." He turned one sheet round. "I came because
of your black hole."

Lolly discerned down at the sheet. It was covered in an extremely dense
and entirely orderly hand, and among the symbols she could make out
almost nothing, but at the top, underlined once, was written:

4C: HORIZON ENTROPY: WHERE IS THE LEDGER

Her mouth went dry. "Weinstein," she said.

"Alfred telephoned me on Tuesday and shouted at me for eleven minutes."
Von Wittenberg's mouth twitched. "His concern is that your Ministry has
used a small engineered black hole as a disposal channel for suppressed
alternatives, that the object is evaporating, and that when it has gone
there will be no record of what was destroyed. He calls this the
falsification of the ledger."

"And you can tell him it's all right?"

"No," said von Wittenberg. "But I can tell you why he may be wrong, and
it is the one thing my subject has actually achieved." He turned the
sheet over. "A black hole has an entropy," he said. "Bekenstein saw it
and Hawking made it precise: the entropy is proportional to the area of
the horizon. Not to the volume inside. To the area of the surface."

He let that sit for a moment. "That was extremely strange, and for
twenty years nobody could say what it was counting. Entropy counts
states. Which states? Of what? The black hole was supposed to have
almost no properties at all."

"And?"

"In 1996 two colleagues took a particular class of black hole in string
theory, counted the configurations of membranes and strings, and found
that the number of states gave exactly the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy.
Exactly. With the correct factor." He said it very quietly. "That is the
first time anyone counted the microscopic states of a black hole and got
the entropy formula back exactly."

Lolly replied slowly, "So there are states. It isn't featureless."

"It is not featureless. It has an enormous number of internal
configurations, and the horizon area measures how many."

"Then when it evaporates---"

"Then the question becomes whether the radiation carries them out,"
offered von Wittenberg. "The area law suggests something outrageous:
that everything inside a region can be encoded on its boundary. The
account is on the surface. There is a precise version in which gravity
in a certain space is equivalent to a theory without gravity on its
edge. Two descriptions. Same physics."

Lolly sat completely still. "Then the ledger isn't inside it," she said.

"That is what I came to say," said von Wittenberg. "The record of what
fell in, of what they dumped, isn't in the interior waiting to be
annihilated when the interior stops existing. It's on the horizon. It's
in the surface."

She was writing now, fast and badly. "And the horizon is exactly what
the radiation comes out of."

"Yes," considered Eduard von Wittenberg. "And so the current view, not
proven, but the view, is that evaporation does not destroy the
information. It is scrambled almost beyond recovery, but it is not
annihilated. Unitarity survives. The books balance."

He put the sheet down. "Tell Alfred that the ledger is not kept in the
vault. It is kept on the wall."

Lolly looked up. "Do you believe it?"

"I believe it in the way de Broccoli believes his wave," said von
Wittenberg. "Which is to say: I have thought about it for decades, the
mathematics is far too good to be an accident, and I cannot show it to
you. Dr Bellboy would give me no credit at all and he would be right
to."

He gathered his papers. "This is the difficulty with being in my line of
work. We have produced, I think, the most beautiful structure in the
history of physics, and we have not produced a single number a committee
could check. Your Bell test will produce one in two days. I am aware
which of us is more useful this week."

He stood, and paused. "May I say one more thing. It is not physics."

"Please."

"You told Dr Bellboy you were a physicist for eleven minutes, once." Von
Wittenberg peered faintly embarrassed. "I was a history major. I
intended to be a journalist. I came to physics late and by an unusual
road, as did de Broccoli, as did, in his fashion."

He picked up his pencil. "I mention it only because everybody who has
come to talk to you this week arrived from somewhere else, and I do not
think that is a coincidence. The people who ask what a thing is are
usually people who were not trained to take it for granted."

He went back to his table, and to his papers, and did not speak again
for the rest of the afternoon.

crate eating a sandwich beside a rack of timing electronics.

# **Chapter 15: Final State Pending**

The fault took a day to find and an afternoon to starve.

What the four detectors had been agreeing about, in the end, was the
time. Every timing rack the Ministry owned drew its clock from the same
civic distribution. The two ends of a Bell test are meant to be
strangers to one another. Hers had been keeping time by the same
heartbeat, drawing their random setting-choices off the same central
feed, and between them they had produced a beautiful, stable and
entirely worthless agreement: the apparatus, measuring the Ministry, and
subtracting it from itself.

"It is the cleanest result I have ever recorded," Fainrose said, "it
means nothing whatsoever, and had I taken it to a committee they would
have funded three more."

Lolly had the shape of it before the explanation finished, because by
then she had the shape of it everywhere. "It's Venn's scanner. The
instrument was inside the thing it was measuring."

"The instrument was inside the thing it was measuring," Fainrose agreed,
"and the instrument was ours, so nobody thought to look."

The repair was not clever. It was subtraction, which by now Lolly
understood to be the only move the whole business had ever actually
rewarded. They cut the rig loose from anything with a crest on it. Two
clocks instead of one: a pair of cheap oscillators left to run free and
deliberately out of step, one at each station. The setting-choices now
came off two smoke-detector sources Gideon had signed out of the annex
stores, on the principle that decay does not wait for a central feed.
Jago left the second detector rack in a van on the ridge with a car
battery and a young man from the annex whose entire brief was to change
nothing. What the rig did, once it was properly deaf, was put the same
two questions to a pair of houses at opposite ends of Fainrose's
lattice, houses the Ministry had surveyed as separate cases, on separate
afternoons, in separate folders, over and over, in an order neither end
could predict, and count how often their answers agreed.

They agreed far too often.

"That's the one Bell minded about most," said Bellboy, who had come out
to the hall to watch a trace he had no need to watch, being both dead
and certain. "Not the detectors. The choices. If something you cannot
see is deciding what gets measured and which measurement gets made, the
argument folds and you never learn that it folded. He worried about it
to the end. So should you."

They ran it clean on the Sunday. The number Bellboy had promised in the
conference room came up a little after four.

Fainrose read it off the timing computer with no expression at all,
which was how Lolly knew it was real. "Two point four one. Plus or minus
three in the last place."

"Ceiling's two," noted Bellboy.

"The ceiling is two. The threshold we put in front of Pilbeam in writing
was two point three, error bars clear of it."

"Then you're clear of it." He noted at the trace a moment longer. "A
change drove in one place altering what is true in another, and nothing
crossing between. Nine thousand houses, surveyed one at a time,
answering as one thing. Werner cannot call that metaphysics: it is an
instrument reading, and instrument readings are the only things he has
ever accepted. Max cannot call it harmless: it is his instrument reading
too. There is no interpretation anywhere in the figure. That is
precisely why it will hold."

Fainrose did not pack the rig away afterwards. She moved one station
indoors, retuned it from Bellboy's cross-lattice correlation to the
district's own internal one, and set it to log. "That one stays," she
said. "Different pair, different number. It will sit flat and stupid for
as long as the anchor is still listening to these houses. The morning it
starts to climb is the morning we have actually let go."

Lolly wrote the legal number in the green notebook, and then the
sentence beneath it that mattered more:

> You cannot test a thing with an instrument that shares its clock. The
> apparatus was part of the Ministry, so of course it agreed with the
> Ministry.

And under that:

> They surveyed nine thousand houses separately. The houses answered as
> one. That is now a measured fact rather than an argument, and it is
> the only sentence in this entire affair that a court cannot talk its
> way around.

She telephoned Beatrix at six and was told to say the number. She said
it. Beatrix hung up without another word, which from Beatrix was
effusive.

Beatrix Sloan won the injunction application on the Monday morning and
lost the argument about what to do with it by lunchtime, because the
Board, faced with an order it could not ignore, did what boards do when
cornered: it asked for a plan, in writing, by four o'clock, confident
that no plan could possibly exist and that confidence, once minuted,
would constitute a defence.

"They think we're bluffing," said Beatrix, setting down the telephone
with the particular gentleness of a woman who would have preferred to
throw it.

"We are not bluffing," remarked Fainrose. "We simply do not yet have a
plan."

"That is the same thing to a Board, for four hours and two biscuits."

Lolly Wren, who had spent the walk back from the courthouse turning
something over in the green notebook that she could not yet make into
sentences, said, "We have four plans. We have never had fewer than
four."

Everyone looked at her.

"Weinstein: spread better agreement. De Broccoli: take the apparatus out
of the wave. Fainrose: repair from surviving correlations, in the right
order. Von Wittenberg: listen to the horizon before it finishes
burning."

"Those are four different physics," said Fainrose, "held by four people
who have never once agreed on anything else."

"Yes," mentioned Lolly. "And Bellboy told me not to pick until something
forced me to, and to use whichever one told me where to put the probe."

She closed the notebook. "Nothing has forced me to pick. So I'm not
going to. I'm going to use all four, because none of them actually
contradicts what we do with a cable and a timing rack on an ordinary
working afternoon. They only contradict each other about what it means.
Nobody has ever asked us to certify what it means. The injunction asks
us to fix nine thousand houses in eleven weeks."

Fainrose was quiet for a moment, in the stillness that Lolly now knew to
wait out rather than fill.

"Say the plan," she said. "Slowly. I want to write it down exactly
once."

She offered the plan slowly.

Von Wittenberg's people would listen at the horizon for whatever of the
four hundred and eleven P-9 states could still be pulled from the
radiation before the anchor finished evaporating. That deadline would
not move.

Fainrose's team would treat the lattice as error-correcting code: repair
the pattern, in syndrome order, without measuring the citizens who
carried it.

And the apparatus would be switched off first. If the scanners had been
deforming the guidance, the most useful move before any clever repair
was to stop making it worse.

"That's not physics," said Fainrose. "That's just turning it off."

"It is the least fashionable intervention available," said Lolly. "De
Broccoli didn't give me a new machine. He gave me permission to stop
running the old one."

Gideon Wigglesworth, who had been sitting by the window with a coffee
gone cold, said, "You'll still need to know where the off switch
actually is."

Fainrose observed round. "I beg your pardon."

"The scanners. The certification passes. The entire industrial machinery
of asking, you said." Gideon set his cup down with care. "Nobody built
one switch. They built forty, over fifteen years, in whatever cupboard
was nearest. Nobody ever plans a shutdown; they only ever plan a launch.
If you want it off, you don't need a theory. You need someone who has
spent three years reading requisition forms and already knows which
cupboard the relay is in."

"And you know," replied Lolly.

"I know which nineteen cupboards it might be in," said Gideon. "I can
find out the other one by Thursday." He stood to go and refill his
coffee, misjudged the doorway by the same half-inch he had been
misjudging it for as long as Lolly had known him, and did not, on this
occasion, spill any of it, which she chose to take as a good omen.

Beatrix wrote this down in the flattened, unpoetic language a court
could not misread, and delivered it at four o'clock precisely. The
Board, which had wanted a plan mainly in order to reject one, discovered
it could not credibly call this absent, and was obliged, for the first
time in the Initiative's history, to fund something rather than merely
surviving a review of it.

The deadline was no longer an administrative fiction. At 4C, the anchor
had begun to lose mass. Every hour they spent arguing about
interpretation was an hour in which information could become physically
unrecoverable.

Gideon looked at the clock. "We should probably stop debating whether
we're ready."

Beatrix scanned at him. "Why?"

"Because the universe doesn't appear to have asked whether we are."

It was that same afternoon, with the plan on the Board's desk and not a
scanner yet switched off, that Mrs Elspeth Chain came to the annex and
asked to see Fainrose alone, which she had not done before.

Lolly was not present for the conversation. She learned what mattered
from Fainrose afterward, on the crate by the timing rack, with two mugs
of the annex tea, which nobody had improved.

"She asked me for a number," said Fainrose. "How much of her sister we
would get back. A percentage. She wanted it before the injunction ran
out, not after, because she said she had spent her whole life being told
good news arrived on time and had never once seen it happen and did not
intend to be surprised twice."

"And you gave her one."

"I told her the truth, which is that I did not know, and that anybody
who told her a number this week was lying to her for the same reason
Voller lied to himself about the vault: because a number is comforting
and a distribution is not, and she deserved the distribution."

Fainrose turned the mug in her hands. "She said that was the first
honest answer anybody in this building had given her, and that she would
come back when there was one worth hearing. Then she left without
shouting, which frightened me considerably more than if she had."

Lolly thought about the photograph on the mantelpiece, restored by a man
pulling a cable out of a skirting board without permission, and about
the two duvets, and about a woman standing in a doorway in her own house
asking a government to explain itself.

"What do we actually owe her," she said. "Not the district. Her."

Fainrose did not answer immediately, which she had learned to recognise
as the shape of an answer arriving.

"I used to think the job was restoration," she mentioned at last. "Put
it back. That's what I trained for. But de Broccoli asked whether a
fully restored district would be a country, or a forgery of the country
that would have existed if nobody had touched it. I did not have an
answer. I am unused to that, and I dislike it less than I expected."

"What's the alternative to restoration."

"Not a cartoon. Not a copy. Not a photograph of a conversation."
Fainrose perceived at her. "A district that remembers, imperfectly, that
a Ministry once stood in its wave with a clipboard. Mrs Chain wants her
sister recoverable, not undamaged (my words from week one, which she
kept). None of the rest of us did."

Lolly opened the green notebook and found the page from the night of the
cold vault, Fainrose's words, jotted beneath the note about the code,
almost as an afterthought: She will be recoverable. That is not the same
as unharmed.

"Then that's the plan," she said slowly. "Not full-state reconstruction.
Not the vault's dream of exactness. A negotiated settlement between what
was and what can honestly be recovered: published, itemised, with the
gaps named rather than papered over. Von Wittenberg's list, when it
comes. Whatever fraction it turns out to be."

"A country that admits its own scar tissue," said Fainrose, "rather than
one that pretends to have been born without a wound."

"Will the Board accept that."

"The Board," offered Fainrose, "will accept whatever a court orders it
to accept, and courts, it turns out, have a great deal of respect for an
itemised list of gaps and no respect whatever for a certificate of
perfection. Beatrix explained this to me on the second day and I did not
believe her and I believe her now."

They sat for a while without talking, which by then had become one of
the more comfortable things either of them did.

"I keep thinking about the moon," said Lolly eventually.

"Outer Fenwick."

"It didn't need forty-one people to stay a moon. It needed the air left
alone to keep noticing it. That is what we're trying to give Mrs Chain
back: not a certificate. Permission for an ordinary life to stay itself
without a form standing over it."

"That is either the plainest sentence you have considered since
breakfast," said Fainrose, "or the most sentimental, and I find I am too
tired to tell which, which may itself be the first reliable instrument
reading we have had all afternoon."

She wrote it down anyway.

Beatrix eyed in from the corridor. "It's on their desk," she said. "We
have a plan. They have a Board. I know which of those I would rather
take into a courtroom."

Rule: The honest plan repairs what survives and names what does not; it
does not require picking an interpretation first.

# **Chapter 16: The Decision**

The written order was sealed on the Tuesday, once the plan the Board had
demanded on the Monday, as the price of losing the hearing, was on the
judge's desk. He understood none of the physics and all of the
paperwork, which turned out to be sufficient. Beatrix Sloan had not
argued that reality had been damaged. She had learned, by then, that
courts do not adjudicate ontology. She had argued instead that the
Ministry had certified nine thousand separate local determinations while
holding, in its own filing system, a measurement establishing that they
were one act; and that a body which certifies nine thousand times what
it knows to be one thing has not created an error of physics but a false
instrument in a live system. Which is a thing the law has known how to
dislike since the fourteenth century, as the Duc de Broccoli observed
with some satisfaction from the public gallery.

They got eleven weeks. It was not enough, and nobody had prepared a form
in which enough was an available option. Von Wittenberg's people
listened to the horizon for nine of them, from a rented industrial unit
outside Sub-District 6, with equipment that cost more than the
district's entire housing stock and had, for that reason, a more
generous maintenance schedule, and that Fainrose described, in her only
published remark on the matter, as "an ear held against a burning wall."

By the second week of November the operation had stopped resembling
physics and had started resembling a hospital corridor: mostly waiting,
occasionally interrupted by everyone in the room forgetting, briefly and
in unison, how to breathe.

Von Wittenberg's team worked in shifts around a horizon that would not
hold still. Numbers came back out of the radiation in fragments, the way
a memory comes back to someone recovering from a fever: a name, a
colour, a smell of toast, and then nothing for six hours. Fainrose
stopped sleeping in any sense that would have satisfied an inquiry.
Beatrix filed three separate emergency applications and lost none of
them, which she treated, privately, as evidence that the universe had
finally noticed her paperwork.

It was Jago Flint who noticed the other thing.

He had been carrying courier bags in and out of the annex for eleven
weeks without once being asked to sit down, which meant he had spent
eleven weeks looking at the coupling lattice the way nobody paid to
understand it ever did: not as a diagram of what it was for, but as a
knot. He wondered, the way anyone does with a knot, what would happen if
you simply let it go.

"You could cut it," he said, on the night of the thirteenth, to a room
that had not asked him.

Fainrose did not look up from the trace. "It's live."

"So's the bit you're listening to. You're not proposing to stop that."
Jago shrugged, the particular shrug of a man who delivers difficult
envelopes for a living and has engineered his peace with difficult
envelopes. "I'm saying: you've got what you're going to get. The
coupling's not bringing anything more back. It's just keeping the door
open for whatever's still in there to come out the wrong way."

Nobody objected, which in a Ministry annex at one in the morning was the
closest thing to unanimous consent that existed.

Gideon Wigglesworth, who had spent the last two hours failing to locate
a plug socket that was, by his own later admission, directly behind him,
straightened up with the abrupt clarity of a man who has just understood
something he wasn't trying to understand. "It's not a valve," he said.
"Everyone's been treating it like a valve. It's a --- "

He stopped, visibly hunting for the word, found it, looked faintly
betrayed by how obvious it now seemed. "It's a bridge. You don't turn a
bridge off. You take it down."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. "That is either the most useful
thing anyone has mentioned in this room in eleven weeks," she said, "or
you have been standing near a filing cabinet radiating something we
should be measuring."

"Possibly both," observed Gideon, and walked, with some dignity,
directly into the filing cabinet.

They cut the coupling at 06:04 on the fourteenth of November. It was not
dramatic to watch. That was the problem. A technician in a borrowed coat
opened a junction box that had not been opened since the annex was
built. She located the correct relay by the light of a torch held in her
teeth. She removed it the way one removes a fuse. The junction box
itself had been found three days earlier by Gideon Wigglesworth, who had
gone through nineteen requisition ledgers by hand and got it right on
the first try, a fact he mentioned only once and only when nobody
appeared to be listening.

Lolly's tea had gone cold an hour ago. She did not put it down. She
watched the woman's hand and the small dark mouth of the empty fuse slot
and felt, with humiliating clarity, that if she breathed too loudly she
might tip four hundred and eleven pending states: including Margaret
Chain, including Dorothy Kell's brother, including every name that had
been reduced to a certificate.

Nobody forced a joke. For eleven seconds nothing happened, which was the
longest eleven seconds of her professional life: one entire district
balanced on a torch battery and a steady hand.

Even Beatrix Sloan, who had spent eleven weeks being audibly certain
about everything, said nothing at all for the whole eleven seconds, and
did not reach for her pen until it was over, which Lolly understood to
be the nearest thing to fear that Beatrix Sloan permitted herself in
public.

Then the coherence monitor Fainrose had left running since Sub-District
6, flat at 1.2 for eleven weeks, with the patience of something that had
given up being interesting, climbed to 2.79 in slightly under forty
minutes, and stopped.

Fainrose read it twice. "That's disconnection," she said, in the voice
of someone reporting a result she had stopped allowing herself to
expect. "Clean disconnection. The number climbs because the houses can
finally hold together with one another again --- they had been spending
all of it on the anchor, and the anchor has let go. It's not listening
to us any more. It's just --- a black hole. An ordinary, boring,
evaporating black hole, doing the only thing black holes are supposed to
do, which is leave."

Nobody cheered. Lolly noted, and later wrote in the green notebook, that
this seemed correct: there is no ceremony appropriate to successfully
stopping a government department from continuing to be haunted.

Of the four hundred and eleven P-9 states, they recovered three hundred
and eight. It was not a triumph and Fainrose refused, in every interview
afterwards, to let it be described as one.

They were pending human states, unscrambled from the radiation and
returned to the districts they had come out of: a child's route to
school, a marriage in the middle of being decided, an apprenticeship,
two reconciliations, a great many small unfinished intentions of no
civic significance whatever.

One hundred and three were not recovered, and their names were
published, because Beatrix insisted, and because Miss Dorothy Kell,
whose brother's apprenticeship was one of the hundred and three, and who
had written to the inquiry every week since the freeze, said that a list
of the unrecovered was the only memorial such things could have.

Lolly read the list twice, standing in the annex corridor with cold tea
in her hand, and found that her cleverness had nothing useful to say.
She wrote Margaret Chain's name in the green notebook under RECOVERED,
and then, underneath, in smaller letters that were not for any inquiry:
one hundred and three people the Ministry will never get to apologise to
properly.

Rule: You can close the coupling; you cannot unspend the heat already
paid.

# Chapter 17: A Very Good Obituary

The districts resolved over the following month, badly and thinly and in
the wrong order. On every screen it looked like collapse, which is why
Crispin Vale, who had testified for eleven days against the directive he
had signed, was blamed for the disorder the recovery made rather than
credited for making the recovery lawful. Fainrose had considered that
would happen. She took no visible pleasure in being right.

The Fenwick mast was found in the weeks after the coupling was cut: a
decorative weathercock on the Memorial Hall with more electronics under
the tail than any weathercock requires, and a plate reading

> PROPERTY OF THE SIMPLIFICATION INITIATIVE

Jago opened it with a screwdriver he was not supposed to be carrying.
Mrs Prosser arrived with tea, confirmed what fourteen months of moon
rota had already taught her, and watched the Ministry switch it off
eleven days later, apologise without using the word sorry, and send her
a revised quorum of thirty-nine. "It is encouraging," she said, "to see
government respond to evidence."

Lolly delivered Mrs Elspeth Chain's certificate herself, on a Tuesday in
March, because she had learned by then that some documents should not
simply be posted.

Twenty-Two Coldharrow Rise, where she had been moved when the old house
was condemned, smelled of instant coffee and furniture polish, applied
in that order and recently. Mrs Chain read the certificate at her own
kitchen table, twice, without comment, while Lolly sat opposite and did
the thing she had got, over the winter, genuinely good at: nothing. Not
filling the silence. Not offering the Ministry's preferred sentences.
Just sitting there like a person who had finally understood that grief
does not want a briefing.

Not explaining that the seventy-eight per cent was for Margaret, not the
district; that a person is a poor thing to render as a fraction; that
"settled record" was not the same as "returned"; that Lolly had
rehearsed, on the bus, the sentence I am sorry, and had found it too
small for the room.

"Seventy-eight per cent," Mrs Chain said eventually, not as a question.

"Yes."

"Of what, exactly."

Lolly had rehearsed several answers to this on the bus, all of them
technically accurate and all of them, she now realised, useless. She
gave Mrs Chain the true one instead. "Of Margaret. As much of the
pattern as survived the listening. Enough that we know, properly know,
who she was: the milk, the wrong jokes, most of what happened to her.
Not enough to bring her back. I am not going to pretend the percentage
is a kindness."

Mrs Chain nodded slowly, the nod of a woman filing the information
somewhere it would not need retrieving again. "So it's not a
resurrection."

"No."

"It's a very good obituary."

"Yes," reflected Lolly. "I think that's exactly what it is."

Mrs Chain was quiet for a while after that, looking at the certificate
the way one looks at a photograph of a house one used to live in.

When she spoke, her voice was the same dry instrument it had always
been, only smaller, as if something had been spent. "I'd have taken the
obituary from the start," she said, "if anyone had had the decency to
offer it instead of a form. Forms are for people who expect to get
something back. Obituaries are for people who have already understood
the arithmetic."

She did not come back. Lolly did not pretend otherwise, then or later.
On the walk to the bus she discovered she was crying, which was
inconvenient and undignified and entirely correct. It was this, the
refusal to lie, more than the certificate, more than the apology the
Ministry eventually and grudgingly issued, that Mrs Chain would describe
at the inquiry as the first honest thing the Ministry had ever given
her.

The photograph turned up that spring, Margaret and Elspeth laughing at
some unseen mishap, taken from the condemned house by a man who
reflected it peered like something that shouldn't be left lying about.

It went back on the mantelpiece at Twenty-Two Coldharrow Rise as if it
had never left. Mrs Chain touched the glass once, lightly, the way one
checks a kettle is real.

"A photograph a burglar had the decency to return," she told the
inquiry, in the remark that was quoted rather more widely than the
Ministry would have liked, "is worth more than a certificate a
government had the nerve to sign."

Rule: Seventy-eight per cent of a life, named as such, is truer than a
hundred per cent of a form.

# Chapter 18: There Is No Lid

And on a Friday in March, in the reading room of the branch library on
Coldharrow Street, at the table by the window overlooking the shut-down
carpet showroom, she found a very old Austrian gentleman waiting for her
with a wooden box on the table and two cups of coffee already going
cold.

"Sit," reflected the old man. "I have ordered you a coffee and it is
already unpleasant, which is the most reliable thing in this building.
Erich Schrottfinger. You have been sent to me by a dead Irishman, a
French duke, and a vampire, which is a referral pattern I have not had
since 1953."

He was very old and very Viennese, in a bow tie that had been tied by
somebody who cared, with a great deal of white hair and an air of
enormous and slightly disreputable charm. The box between them was about
the size of a hatbox, with air holes.

Lolly noted at the box.

"Don't," replied Schrottfinger. "It is empty. It has always been empty.
It was always a joke, Ms Wren: that is the thing nobody will accept. I
invented it to be ridiculous, and within twenty years it was on coffee
mugs. You cannot win. You make one sarcastic remark about a cat and it
outlives your equation. The universe has a sense of humour. It is not a
kind one."

"Then why bring it."

"Because people sit down faster. Also the coffee is terrible, and shared
suffering is efficient."

Schrottfinger pushed it aside. "Now. You have finished a thing, and you
want somebody to tell you what it was all for, and Fainrose cannot tell
you because she is a working physicist and von Wittenberg cannot tell
you because he will start on the eleven dimensions. So it falls to me,
who am not respectable."

"Weinstein remarked you'd been waiting since 1935."

"For someone to ask what the cat was about, yes. Nobody asks. They ask
whether it is alive."

Schrottfinger drank some of the terrible coffee. "Very well. I shall
give you what you came for, and then you will go home. And I shall call
it a Prologue, and you will object, and I shall explain."

He put down the cup.

"Here is the outlook. Your Ministry believed it was managing a country.
It was interfering, in a small and provincial way, with something that
is not finished. I mean the universe is not finished, and everything you
have lived through this year took place in what future arrangements of
matter will regard as the opening seconds.

"The stars are the first thing to understand. There are stars now. This
is not the normal condition. Star formation peaked long ago; the gas
will run down; in a hundred trillion years or so the last star will form
and the sky will go out. Then a very long time of embers. Almost all of
history is afterwards. Then, if the protons are not eternal, and we do
not know, even the embers dissolve.

"And then it is the age of the black holes, and they do exactly what
your 4C did in a car park in Surrey: they evaporate. The largest will
take on the order of 10 to the power of 100 years. But they go. After
that: a thin, cold, dark space, expanding, and nothing in it that could
be called an event.

"That is the outlook. You asked for it. That is what is next in the
galaxy and in the universe, on the current evidence, and I have not
exaggerated in either direction."

He let it sit for a moment, in the reading room, over the cold coffee.

"Now," said Erich Schrottfinger. "The interesting part. Every physicist
my age was taught that the universe runs down. Order becomes disorder;
the coffee goes cold. Perfectly true. And when I was younger I scanned
at a living thing and thought: how does that happen. Because a living
thing does the opposite: holds a pattern against the current for eighty
years.

"And the answer is not that life defies the law. The answer is that a
living thing survives by feeding on order: taking in structure,
excreting disorder, paying the bill locally and passing the cost
outward. A living thing is a place where order is temporarily
concentrated because it has arranged to be paid for elsewhere. You have
spent a year on it.

"Your Ministry, Ms Wren, was doing the same thing, badly, and without
knowing it. It wished a district to be simple. And it discovered what
every living system discovers: you cannot simply have that. You must pay
for it, and the disorder goes somewhere else. So they built a hole and
put it in there. That was thermodynamics by people who had not been told
they were doing thermodynamics, and it ended in a fire because they
never asked where the bill was going. Every institution that promises to
make things simple is proposing to move complexity somewhere you cannot
see. Always, and by law. That is the sentence I should like you to take
away."

Lolly was writing.

"And so, the outlook," said Schrottfinger. "Yes. In the long run: the
embers, the holes, the dark. I shall not soften it. But consider the
enormous middle: a very long period with order still left to be spent,
in which anything that can arrange to be paid for elsewhere may persist,
and think, and ask questions.

"And here is what I think, and I am one hundred and fourteen and
entitled to think it. The universe does not contain many places where it
is described. Almost everywhere, things simply proceed. But wherever
something has assembled itself into an arrangement that can hold a model
of the rest, a green notebook: the universe acquires, locally and
temporarily, an account of itself. That is not nothing. That may be the
rarest thing in it. It is certainly rarer than gravity."

"Your Ministry was engaged in reducing the number of such accounts, and
calling it efficiency, and the reason that was a crime and not merely a
mistake is that the accounts are the scarce commodity and the simplicity
was not. Anyone can have simplicity. You can have all the simplicity you
want at the end of time, and it will be perfect, and there will be
nobody in it. So: what is next in the universe. More of this, for a very
long while, and then less, and then none. And in the meantime, an
unreasonably interesting middle, in which the correct posture is neither
optimism nor despair but attention, which is your job title, I
understand, and which somebody has finally thought to fund."

He picked up the empty box and put it on his lap.

"Now. Why a Prologue. Because you want an ending, Ms Wren, and I will
not give you one, and my reason is technical rather than sentimental."

He tapped the lid.

"The whole trouble with my cat was that everybody read it as a story
about a box. It is not. It is a story about the lid. The absurdity I was
pointing at is the belief that there exists a moment at which the matter
is settled, and then the fact is a fact and may be filed. There is no
such moment. Your friend Weinstein was right about that much and it is
the only thing he and I agree on. There is only the widening business of
things becoming correlated with other things, indefinitely, forever, at
the speed of gossip, and a state that has not yet finished resolving is
not a mystery awaiting a verdict. It is the ordinary condition of
everything."

"Three hundred and eight of your recovered items were pending. Not
memories. Things that had not finished happening. You gave them back to
people, and they are still not finished, and they will go on not being
finished, which is what it means to be alive rather than certified. So
this is not the end of your book. It is the front matter of what those
three hundred and eight are going to do. It is a Prologue, and it is at
the back, and if that offends the printers they may take it up with the
second law."

He stood, and tucked the box under his arm, and drained the appalling
coffee with the enthusiasm of a man who had survived worse.

"One last thing. You will be tempted, in your new post, to find out
which of them was right: de Broccoli and his wave, Weinstein and his
agreement, Tengelman and his branches, von Wittenberg and his strings.
You will want the answer, because you spent ten years in electronics and
you want to know what the circuit is."

Schrottfinger put on his hat. "You will not get it. Not from me, not
this century, possibly not ever. And you must not let that stop you from
noticing things, because the noticing is the part that turned out to
matter and the interpretation was never what saved the district. What
saved the district was a clerk who wrote down that a lie required
electricity.

"Keep the notebook, Ms Wren. It is the only apparatus that has never
once been wrong about what it saw."

He went out through the reading room, past the local history shelves,
and cast a completely ordinary shadow, and did not look back.

Lolly sat by the window for a while with the cold coffee and the noise
of Coldharrow Street coming through the glass: a district in the middle
of resolving, badly and slowly and in the wrong order, and not finished.

She thought of Margaret Chain's name under RECOVERED, and of the one
hundred and three who were not, and of Mrs Chain's hand on a photograph
glass, and understood, without drama, that attention was not a metaphor.
It was the only honest form of love a Ministry clerk was allowed.

Then she took out the notebook, and turned to a clean page, and wrote at
the top:

PROLOGUE

And underneath:

> Order is not free. Simplicity is always paid for somewhere else, and
> any institution that will not say where is lying or ignorant, and it
> does not matter which. The universe is mostly not yet. Almost
> everything is still pending. That is not a fault in the filing.

Then, last, in the ordinary handwriting of someone who had somewhere to
be:

There is no lid.

Which is to say: the beginning of the part that has not been written
down.

Rule: Keep the ledger honest. Keep noticing. There is no lid.

**Part One: What the universe is actually doing (Chapters 1--5)**

- A thing's **state** is the complete list of what can be said about it.
  It is not a location, an opinion, or an entry in a register.

- A state may be a **superposition**: it has no definite value for some
  property until that property is measured. This is not merely the thing
  hiding an answer it has already picked.

- **Measurement** is a physical interaction, not an act of attention.
  Nothing in the universe requires a witness, a clipboard, or a member
  of staff.

- The result you can get depends on the question you ask. Asking a
  different question is not asking the same question more carefully.
  There is no basis-independent true answer waiting to be transcribed.

- Measurement is an intervention. It changes the thing measured.
  Officers who measure repeatedly in order to be thorough should be
  aware that they are not observing a system; they are pinning one.

- Between measurements, states evolve smoothly, predictably and
  reversibly, according to the Schrödinger equation. This is the least
  mysterious part of the entire subject and is routinely reported as the
  most.

**Part Two: What happens when you file it (Chapters 6--10)**

- **Black holes** are not permanent. They radiate, cool the universe's
  patience, and evaporate. A small one does this fast, and faster as it
  shrinks. This is a law, not a fault, and cannot be appealed.

- Information that falls into a black hole poses a serious unresolved
  problem in physics. It should not, therefore, be used as departmental
  storage.

- Two things that interact can become **entangled**: correlated so
  completely that neither has a definite individual value, only a shared
  one. This transmits nothing faster than light and cannot be used to
  send a memo.

- Correlation leaks. When a system's alternatives get recorded in its
  surroundings, interference between them becomes practically
  unrecoverable. This is **decoherence**.

- Decoherence is why large ordinary objects look definite: the air, the
  light and the warmth of the world are interacting with them
  constantly, on nobody's authority and at no charge.

- Consequently, the moon does not require forty-one residents with
  clipboards. Any officer who has built a billing schedule on the
  contrary assumption should expect difficulty.

**Part Three: What cannot be done (Chapters 8--9, restated for
emphasis)**

- An unknown quantum state cannot be copied. This is the **no-cloning
  theorem**, and it is not a procurement problem.

- A state may, however, be moved, and a known state may be prepared
  again.

> The distinction is the difference between a backup and a crime.

- Certain pairs of properties, position and momentum among them, cannot
  both be sharply defined at once. This is the uncertainty principle. It
  is a fact about the world, not about the quality of the instruments,
  and requesting both columns to four decimal places does not overcome
  it.

- Information can nonetheless be protected, by spreading it redundantly
  across many parts without ever copying it. This is **quantum error
  correction**, and it works up to a threshold, beyond which it does
  not.

**Part Four: What the experts do not agree about (Chapters 11--14)**

- If evolution is smooth and universal, nothing in the mathematics says
  when a single definite outcome occurs. This is the **measurement
  problem**. It is unsolved.

- It is unsolved in a specific and embarrassing way: an observer can be
  inside another observer's unresolved description. (See **Wigner's
  friend**, and Mr Weinstein's dietary arrangements.)

- Matter has wave properties, with a wavelength set by momentum.

> **Pilot-wave theory** takes this seriously: particles have definite
> positions at all times, guided by a real wave. It is deterministic. It
> is also explicitly nonlocal.

- **Bell's theorem** proves that no theory in which distant things have
  their own separate pre-existing properties can reproduce the
  correlations we measure. Experiment has confirmed the violation
  repeatedly and, since 2015, without loopholes.

- This rules out local hidden variables. It does not rule out all hidden
  variables.

> Pilot-wave theory survives precisely because it never claimed to be
> local.

- String theory and M-theory propose that everything is compelled of
  extended objects in more dimensions than are currently invoiced. The
  mathematics is extraordinary. The experimental confirmation is nil,
  and should be described as such in all correspondence.

- A black hole's entropy is proportional to the area of its horizon, not
  to its volume. What a region contains can apparently be accounted for
  on its boundary. This is the **holographic principle**, and it is the
  reason evaporation may constitute disclosure rather than destruction.

- Officers are reminded that Copenhagen, many-worlds and pilot-wave are
  interpretations, not competing products, and that no experiment has
  yet distinguished between them. The Ministry has no position. The
  Ministry is not entitled to one.

**\**

**Part Five: What can be recovered (Chapters 15--18)**

- Recovery is partial. Below a threshold, loss can be corrected; above
  it, it cannot. Some things are genuinely gone, and saying so in
  writing is the whole of professional honesty.

- One may act correctly on quantum mechanics without knowing which
  interpretation is true. This is inconvenient for philosophy and
  sufficient for work.

- The second law stands: total entropy does not decrease. Local order is
  always paid for by exporting disorder somewhere else.

- Living things do not defeat this. They maintain themselves by feeding
  on order and excreting disorder, Schrödinger's negentropy, which is a
  scheme within the accounts, not an exemption from them.

- Therefore: any institution promising to make things simpler is
  proposing to move complexity somewhere you cannot see. Always. The
  only honest question is where, and the only crime is refusing to say.

- On the longest timescales the stars go out, the remnants cool, the
  black holes evaporate, and a dark and extremely uneventful era
  follows. The timetable depends on unsettled physics and should not be
  entered in the diary.

- In the meantime there is an enormous middle, in which order remains to
  be spent and some arrangements of matter can ask what they are drove
  of.

- The universe contains very few places where it is described.

> These are the scarce resource. Simplicity is not.

- There is no moment at which a state is finally settled and may be
  filed. Correlation continues outward indefinitely.

- There is no lid.

You need first: nothing. This is where we begin.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State what a quantum state is,
what a measurement is, and why a superposition is not merely an unknown
pre-written answer.

**Start here.** Think of a recipe card still being written, with two
genuinely possible dinners pencilled in: soup if the weather stays cold,
salad if it clears. Until someone makes the deciding trip to the shops,
neither dinner is the one being cooked. The image helps with one point:
sometimes it is wrong to imagine that one settled answer has merely been
concealed from us.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. Start from the card: until dinner is made, more than one meal can
still be written there.

2\. Cooking is the measurement --- a physical interaction that leaves a
record (a plate, a click, a mark on a form).

3\. A superposition is the card with both meals still open: not a secret
single choice waiting in a drawer.

4\. When you cook one property, you get one allowed result; the state
sets the odds, not a pre-written fate.

5\. Before that, the open alternatives can interfere --- reinforce or
cancel --- which ordinary ignorance never does. That is why
superposition is not just "we don't know yet."

**In one breath.** Before anyone looks, the recipe card can still have
two dinners on it. A measurement is someone actually cooking one of them
--- a physical act that leaves a plate. The state is whatever best
predicts which plates you might get; a superposition means both dinners
are still genuinely on the card, not that the cook has already chosen
and hidden the answer.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Lolly's complaint file
does not merely hide one correct status behind a faulty screen:
APPROVED, MISROUTED, and CLOSED keep presenting incompatible
descriptions. Mrs Chain is likewise caught between not having committed
to the journey and already having arrived. Her bus, the terminal, and
Whitbury-on-Sea are comic enlargements, not literal quantum systems, but
they give a useful first picture of a state that has not yet yielded one
answer to a particular question. The Simplification Initiative wants the
file to settle promptly because it finds unresolved alternatives
administratively inconvenient. The story's problem is therefore not that
the Ministry has misunderstood a hidden answer. It has begun acting as
though one must exist before the measurement that would make it
definite.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** You will often hear that
superposition means a particle is simply "in two places at once". That
slogan can be useful for getting someone to put down their tea, but it
is not the full idea. Superposition is a state with no definite value
for the property being measured in that basis, and its alternatives can
interfere. Nor does a conscious person make it collapse by looking. What
matters is a physical interaction that records or can spread information
about the relevant alternative.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. In the standard
notation, a state with two alternatives can be written as a weighted
combination; the weights are called probability amplitudes, and their
squared magnitudes give the probabilities when that property is
measured:

**\|**ψ**⟩** = *a***\|**A⟩ + *b***\|**B**⟩**,  *P*(A) =
**\|***a***\|**^2^, *P*(B) = **\|***b***\|**^2^

The amplitudes can be complex numbers, which is why they can interfere
before probabilities are calculated. That probability rule --- the Born
rule --- has been the working standard since the mid-1920s. Interference
of single photons, electrons, atoms, and larger molecules is observed in
carefully isolated experiments; it is a testable feature of quantum
states, not a metaphor about indecision.

**\**

**What sticks**

• A state is physics' description of what results a system can give in
specified measurements.

• A measurement is a physical interaction with a recording apparatus;
the eye watching it has no special licence.

• A superposition is not merely an unknown pre-existing answer.

• Interference is the observable clue that the alternatives form one
coherent quantum state.

**One-line summary.** Before a measurement forces one answer, a quantum
state may be more than a list of answers waiting in a drawer.

## **Chapter 2: Lecture: Measurement Basis and Forced Collapse**

![](media/image2.jpeg){width="5.3in" height="3.6532141294838145in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 2: The Wrong
Queue]{.underline}](#chapter-2-the-wrong-queue)

You need first: Lecture 1 (state, measurement, superposition).

When you walk out, you should be able to: Explain what a measurement
basis is, and why asking one quantum question can spoil the answer to
another.

**Start here.** A kitchen sieve can sort a mixed tin into things smaller
than its holes and things larger than its holes. A magnet can sort some
objects from the same tin by a different feature. Neither tool reveals
every useful fact at once; each makes one distinction and ignores
another. The important habit of thought is the same: the question built
into the sorting tool matters.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. The sieve and the magnet ask different questions of the same tin.
Each apparatus's allowed answers are its measurement basis.

2\. Use one sorter and you get one kind of answer --- and the tin is
left prepared for that answer, not for every other question.

3\. Some questions are incompatible: sorting by size does not leave a
definite "magnetic" fact waiting politely underneath.

4\. Asking the second question is therefore an intervention, not a
neutral relabelling of something already sharp in both ways.

5\. Change the order of two incompatible questions and the later answers
can change --- because the first interaction prepared a new state.

**In one breath.** A measurement can answer only the particular kind of
question its apparatus is built to ask; that choice is its **measurement
basis**. For incompatible quantum questions, there is no
basis-independent set of sharp "true values" waiting to be read off, and
the first measurement can change what a later one is able to find.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Queue Management forces
Mrs Chain's one experience into an intake frame: intent, route, or civic
stability. When the clerk asks whether her destination was "emotionally
authorised", that question does not simply uncover a pre-existing
official category; it starts reducing her account to the distinctions
the queue permits. Lolly spots the diagnostic line "PRIMARY MEASUREMENT
FRAME: INTENT" and understands the danger. The queue is measuring the
public by insisting that its first question determines what sort of
problem the citizen may become.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** It is tempting to say that a
measurement basis is just a different way of looking at one fully
specified object. That is false for incompatible quantum properties. The
theory does not say that a particle carries a little card listing sharp
answers to every possible basis and that our apparatus merely reads one
line. A measurement is a physical interaction, never a matter of
conscious attention, and the apparatus determines which distinction can
leave a record. This does not mean that every everyday question creates
its own reality; it is a precise claim about quantum observables and
their experimental arrangements.

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. For a
two-outcome measurement, the apparatus has states such as **\|**u⟩ and
**\|**d⟩ as its basis. A state can be expanded in that basis, and an
ideal measurement gives one result with the corresponding squared
amplitude before preparing that outcome state:

**\|**ψ**⟩** = *c*~u~**\|**u**⟩** + *c*~d~**\|**d**⟩**,  *P*(u) =
**\|***c*~u~**\|**^2^

Two observables are incompatible when their operators do not commute,
written AB ≠ BA. The Stern--Gerlach experiment (1922) showed discrete
outcomes for a property called spin --- an intrinsic angular momentum
with no everyday counterpart; sequences of differently oriented
Stern--Gerlach magnets make the dependence on the chosen axis visible.
The word "collapse" is useful textbook shorthand for the ideal
state-update rule, while fuller accounts model the measuring apparatus
and its physical entanglement with the system.

**\**

**What sticks**

• A measurement basis specifies which alternatives an apparatus can
distinguish.

• The question built into a quantum measurement helps determine the kind
of answer obtainable.

• Incompatible properties cannot generally all have simultaneous sharp,
basis-independent values.

• Measurement is a physical state-changing interaction, not a passive
glance.

**One-line summary.** Ask reality through the wrong sorting frame, and
the answer may be the only part of it your apparatus has left standing.

## **Chapter 3: Lecture: Premature Collapse**

![](media/image3.png){width="5.3in" height="6.715857392825897in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 3: A Small Error in
Reality]{.underline}](#chapter-3-a-small-error-in-reality)

You need first: Lectures 1--2.

When you walk out, you should be able to: Say why measurement is an
intervention, and how asking too soon can destroy coherence that cannot
be recovered by declaring the result settled.

**Start here.** Imagine trying to watch the colours blend in wet paint
by pressing a clean sheet of paper onto it every few seconds. Each press
leaves a mark, but it also interrupts and spoils the pattern you wished
to see. The record and the disturbance come together. The comparison is
imperfect, but it shares the crucial feature that obtaining a record
involves contact, not passive surveillance.

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A quantum state can evolve so that its alternatives acquire
relationships that later produce interference.

2\. A **measurement** couples the system to an apparatus that records
one of the alternatives.

3\. In the ideal textbook description, this produces **state reduction**
to the recorded outcome.

4\. Uncontrolled interactions with the surroundings can also spread
information outward; this is **decoherence**.

5\. When usable information about an alternative is left in a detector
or environment, the interference that required keeping alternatives
coherent is generally lost.

6\. "Premature collapse" is not a standard technical term: here it means
intervening before the evolution or interference one cares about has had
a chance to develop.

**In one breath.** Measurement does not merely uncover a completed
quantum story: it physically couples a system to something that can
record an outcome, changing the state in the process. If that
interaction happens before the alternatives have had time to evolve or
interfere in the way an experiment requires, the lost coherence cannot
simply be recovered by calling the result settled.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Mrs Chain's Equivalent
Residence Unit 7B has been forced too soon into a narrow approved
version of her home. The teal bowl, artificial daffodils, incorrect
photographs, and competing clocks show a bureaucratic attempt to make
one description stick before the details agree. When Lolly removes the
Civic Stability Substrate from behind the cracked mirror, suppressed
versions of the house reappear: geraniums, the photograph of Mrs Chain's
husband, and conflicting furnishings. The novel's house is not
quantum-scale physics, but it makes the right point about the Ministry's
intervention: labelling and monitoring are doing the damage, not
neutrally reading a finished reality.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** The familiar claim that
"observation makes it real" is misleading. Quantum experiments do not
require a mind, an eye, or a person with a clipboard. They require
physical interactions capable of producing records. In a two-slit
experiment, for example, a detector that obtains reliable which-path
information changes the experimental situation and removes the
interference pattern. Decoherence explains why interference becomes
inaccessible in practice, but by itself it does not settle the deeper
question of why one particular outcome is experienced.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. For two paths,
an unmeasured state may combine two amplitudes; the probability at a
screen includes an interference term:

**\|**ψ**⟩** = *a***\|**L**⟩** +
*b***\|**R**⟩**,  **\|***a*+*b***\|**^2^ = **\|***a***\|**^2^ +
**\|***b***\|**^2^ + 2 Re (*a*\**b*)

A reliable path record correlates the paths with different detector
states. When those record states are distinguishable, the cross-term
responsible for the interference disappears from the observed pattern.
How much which-path information you have, and how much interference you
keep, trade off against each other in a precise, measurable way. This is
an experimentally routine feature of quantum setups, not a rule about a
scientist's thoughts.

**\**

**What sticks**

• Measurement creates a physical record by interacting with the system.

• That interaction can remove the coherence required for interference.

• Decoherence is environment-spread information, not a complete solution
to the measurement problem.

• A forced outcome can discard correlations and alternatives rather than
reveal a fully formed truth.

**One-line summary.** A measurement gets its answer by touching the
system, which is why asking too soon can spoil what the system might
otherwise have shown.

finished answer and forcing one into existence?

## **Chapter 4: Lecture: Repeated Measurement and the Quantum Zeno Effect**

![](media/image4.jpeg){width="4.974025590551181in"
height="3.4285247156605423in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 4: Continuity
Review]{.underline}](#chapter-4-continuity-review)

You need first: Lectures 1--3.

When you walk out, you should be able to: Describe how a measurement
prepares as well as reports a state, and what the quantum Zeno effect is
under frequent ideal checks.

**Start here.** Picture a spinning office chair that is allowed to turn
freely only between inspections. If someone stops it, lines it up with a
strip of tape, and repeats that intervention again and again, the chair
is kept near its starting direction. The chair is only an everyday
comparison, but it gives the honest intuition: the repeated checks are
not harmless looks; they are what keeps it there.

**The idea, step by step**

1\. An ideal measurement of a chosen property produces one outcome and
prepares the associated state.

2\. If the same property is measured again immediately, with no relevant
evolution in between, the prepared state gives the same outcome.

3\. Left alone, a quantum system can normally evolve away from that
prepared state.

4\. Very frequent physical measurements that repeatedly test whether it
is still in the initial state can interrupt this departure.

5\. In the appropriate short-time regime, increasing the rate of those
checks can suppress the change; this is the **quantum Zeno effect**.

6\. The effect depends on the system, timing, and measurement protocol,
so it is not a magical rule that any amount of watching freezes
anything.

**In one breath.** A measurement prepares as well as reports a state, so
an immediate repeat of the same ideal measurement gives the same result.
Under carefully specified conditions, very frequent physical checks can
inhibit a quantum system's normal change; this real and experimentally
observed effect is called the **quantum Zeno effect**.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Alistair Venn scans Mrs
Chain's house object by object, asking whether a photograph, a book, or
her sister belongs to the Ministry's "authorised continuity path". Lolly
sees the sequence clearly: question, narrowing, selection. Continuity
Review treats each inspection as a harmless check, yet every scan
presses the household towards a smaller approved version and keeps other
details suppressed. The cable Jago pulls and the module Beatrix takes

the single duvet, and the photograph of Mrs Chain with her sister to
return. It is an allegory, not household quantum mechanics, but it is a
good one for the physical lesson that repeated measurement can pin
behaviour.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** "A watched pot never boils" is
not quantum theory, and no human attention freezes a system. The quantum
Zeno effect concerns repeated physical interactions or measurements,
designed to test a particular state on the right time scale. Nor does it
mean repeated measurements always freeze change. Other protocols can
speed change, an effect known as the anti-Zeno effect, and real
environments complicate both. Venn's scanner is therefore a moral
analogy for invasive monitoring, not a literal Zeno machine.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Immediately
after preparation, quantum theory predicts that the probability of
remaining in the initial state falls quadratically for a sufficiently
short interval, rather than at a fixed constant rate:

*P*~survival~(*t*) ≈ 1 − (Δ*H*)^2^*t*^2^ / ℏ^2^

Repeated ideal checks spaced within that regime can repeatedly
re-prepare the state and inhibit its transition. That inhibition is the
quantum Zeno effect: watching in the right physical way can slow a
change that would otherwise happen. Laboratory demonstrations with
trapped ions and many later experiments have seen related
measurement-induced inhibition. Modern accounts stress that a
"measurement" is a physical coupling and that the outcome depends on the
complete protocol and environment.

**\**

**What sticks**

• An ideal measurement prepares the state corresponding to its recorded
result.

• An immediate repeat using the same apparatus distinction, or basis,
can return that result with certainty.

• Repeated physical measurement can alter, and under suitable conditions
inhibit, subsequent evolution.

• The quantum Zeno effect is real experimental physics, not a claim that
conscious watching controls reality.

**One-line summary.** Inspect a quantum state often enough in the right
physical way, and the inspection itself can help keep it where it was.

## **Chapter 5: Lecture: Lawful Evolution Between Measurements**

![](media/image5.jpeg){width="4.948051181102362in"
height="3.583969816272966in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 5: Dr Fainrose Is Not
Reassured]{.underline}](#chapter-5-dr-fainrose-is-not-reassured)

You need first: Lectures 1--4.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State what happens between
measurements: smooth, reversible evolution by a precise rule --- until a
record is made.

**Start here.** Think of a perfectly compelled clockwork toy under a
glass cover. If nobody touches it, each wheel makes the next one turn in
a particular way; run the motion backwards and, in principle, every
wheel could retrace its route. The difficult moment is not the clockwork
itself. It is opening the cover, prodding a wheel with a ruler, and then
asking why the arrangement has changed.

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A **quantum state** is the best complete description available of a
small system, including the possible results of a later measurement.

2\. Left alone, that state does not wait around for a decision; it
changes continuously according to a fixed rule.

3\. That rule is the **Schrödinger equation**, which uses the system's
energy and interactions to determine its next state from its present
one.

4\. For a genuinely isolated system, this evolution is **deterministic**
and **reversible**: the starting state fixes the later state, and the
earlier state could in principle be recovered.

5\. A **measurement** is not a conscious person looking; it is a
physical interaction with an apparatus or environment, which must itself
be included in the story.

6\. The measurement problem concerns how definite recorded outcomes
arise from that wider interaction, not a failure of the law governing
the evolution beforehand.

**In one breath**. Between measurements, an isolated quantum system
changes by a precise rule: smoothly, predictably and reversibly. The
strangeness enters when it physically interacts with a measuring device
or its surroundings, not because the ordinary evolution has stopped
obeying a law.

What this chapter was actually showing you. Dr Fainrose sees that Mrs
Chain's domestic stabiliser has selected a cheap-looking result rather
than allowing the rich pattern of her home to develop by its proper
history. Her phrase, "they're vandalising the evolution", is the
chapter's physics lesson in miniature. Lolly's note --- "Need law of
change" --- is the corrective: knowing the possible home-states is not
enough if the Ministry has imposed an end point while discarding the
lawful relations that lead there. Houses are not isolated laboratory
systems, mercifully, but the contrast is exact as an analogy. At the end
of the visit she first reads the descending gravitational trace as a
failure of the domestic module --- ornamental thermal shielding, a local
fault --- and then has to say she was wrong: that is not domestic. The
line is 4C.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** Quantum mechanics is often
presented as though the system is mysterious and random all the time,
until a person looks at it and makes it real. That reverses the
emphasis. Between measurements, the standard quantum rule is
exceptionally orderly; nor does human attention have a special physical
job. "Observation" means an interaction that can leave a record, such as
a detector absorbing light or an environment becoming correlated with a
system. The unresolved part is how to understand a single definite
recorded result, not whether the pre-measurement state was lawless.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. In 1926
Schrödinger wrote down the time-evolution law for a non-relativistic
quantum state. In words, the Hamiltonian is the operator representing
the system's energy, and it tells the state how to change.

iℏ ∂/∂*t* **\|**ψ(*t*)⟩ = Ĥ**\|**ψ(*t*)**⟩**

When Ĥ does not explicitly change with time, this produces unitary
evolution,

**\|**ψ(*t*)**⟩** = e^−iĤt/ℏ^**\|**ψ(0)**⟩**

which preserves total probability and can be reversed mathematically.

A measurement is instead a physical coupling to apparatus and
environment; treating that whole enlarged system quantum mechanically is
precisely what makes the later question of outcomes so demanding.

**\**

**What sticks**

• A quantum state needs a law of change as well as a list of possible
measurement results.

• The Schrödinger equation gives smooth, deterministic and reversible
evolution for an isolated system.

• Measurement is physical interaction, not audience participation.

• The measurement problem concerns definite outcomes, not a gap in
ordinary quantum evolution.

**One-line summary.** Quantum theory is not strange because nothing
obeys a law; it is strange because the law is so orderly until a record
is made.

## **Chapter 6: Lecture: Hawking Radiation**

![](media/image6.png){width="5.116882108486439in"
height="3.3106867891513563in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 6: The Object Is Not
Mislaid]{.underline}](#chapter-6-the-object-is-not-mislaid)

You need first: Lectures 1--5 (especially states and records).

When you walk out, you should be able to: Link falling mass to rising
temperature for an evaporating black hole, and name why information loss
is a problem.

**Start here.** Picture a very large block of ice in a quiet, cold room.
It melts so slowly that, for a long while, it looks permanent. A smaller
block has less staying power: as it dwindles, the conditions that
rendered it seem solid no longer protect it in the same way, and the
ending becomes noticeably brisker. A black hole is not ice, and one
should resist improving the analogy past its useful life, but the change
of pace matters.

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A **black hole** is a region whose gravity is so strong that, once
past its event horizon, light cannot return to a distant observer.

2\. In quantum field theory on curved spacetime, a distant observer
predicts that a black hole emits faint **thermal radiation**, called
**Hawking radiation**.

3\. Radiation carries energy away, and energy has mass-equivalent, so an
isolated black hole loses mass.

4\. A smaller black hole has a higher **Hawking temperature**, so it
emits more strongly than a larger one.

5\. The loss therefore accelerates as the hole shrinks; very large
astrophysical holes change absurdly slowly, while a hypothetical small
engineered one would be less patient.

6\. If the final radiation looks only thermal, we must ask how the
detailed **quantum information** about what formed and entered the hole
can still be preserved.

**In one breath.** A black hole is not perfectly black: quantum physics
predicts that it emits thermal radiation and therefore loses mass. The
smaller it becomes, the hotter it gets and the faster the loss proceeds,
while what happens to the information in everything it swallowed remains
a deep problem.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Fainrose's two curves
--- Asset 4C's mass descending and its temperature rising --- are the
distinctive linked signs of Hawking evaporation. The Ministry has not
mislaid a civic object; its black hole is shrinking by law, which is why
"within projected envelope" is such a poor comfort. When Lolly notices
that thermal radiation does not plainly reveal what fell in, she reaches
the black-hole information problem. Mrs Chain's home, the queue records
and the overflowed alternatives give that question human stakes: if a
system has stored irreplaceable structure in 4C, it matters fiercely
whether that structure is recoverable. In the novel, that makes the
black hole a deadline rather than merely a scientific curiosity: when
the anchor evaporates, information that has not been recovered may no
longer be recoverable by any ordinary intervention.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** The usual tale says that a
pair of "virtual particles" appears at the horizon, one falls in and one
escapes. It can gesture towards the answer, but it is only a heuristic
and is widely criticised as a literal account; it suggests little
particles popping out at one special place and can get the energy
bookkeeping badly wrong. The better framing is about quantum fields in
curved spacetime. What counts as an empty vacuum, and even what counts
as a particle, is not observer-independent there; comparing the field's
distant past and distant future around a black hole yields radiation for
a far-away observer. No little horizon factory is required. A separate,
more calculable derivation treats the process as quantum tunnelling ---
the same barrier-crossing mechanism behind alpha decay and the scanning
tunnelling microscope, where a particle has some probability of crossing
a region classical physics says it cannot reach --- applied to the
horizon itself; it reproduces Hawking's temperature exactly, with no
factory and no story about where the pair was born.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Hawking's 1974
calculation applied quantum field theory to a black-hole spacetime and
found a thermal spectrum at infinity. In words, the temperature is
inversely proportional to the mass: double the mass and halve the
temperature.

*T*~H~ = ℏ*c*^3^ / (8π*GMk*~B~).

In the semiclassical treatment, the evaporation time of an isolated,
non-rotating, uncharged black hole scales as *t*~evap~ ∝ *M*^3^. That
calculation creates the information problem: quantum evolution normally
preserves distinctions between initial states, but exactly thermal final
radiation seems not to display them. Most modern work expects
information to be preserved, yet the complete mechanism requires quantum
gravity and is not settled in a simple textbook picture.

**\**

**What sticks**

• Hawking radiation is a predicted thermal emission from black holes.

• Smaller isolated black holes are hotter and evaporate faster as they
lose mass.

• The "particle pair at the horizon" story is a limited heuristic, not
the mechanism to take literally.

• Black-hole evaporation raises the question of how quantum information
is preserved.

**One-line summary.** Asset 4C is not going missing; it is becoming
hotter, smaller, and harder to forgive.

## **Chapter 7: Lecture: Entanglement and the Start of Decoherence**

![](media/image7.jpeg){width="5.030614610673666in"
height="3.4675328083989503in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 7: What Remains
Correlated]{.underline}](#chapter-7-what-remains-correlated)

You need first: Lectures 1--6.

When you walk out, you should be able to: Define entanglement without
faster-than-light messaging, and say how decoherence makes interference
practically unavailable.

**Start here.** Imagine two gloves packed into two sealed boxes by a
mischievous warehouse. Opening one box and finding a left glove tells
you the other contains a right glove, but only because each glove had a
hand before the boxes were sent. Now imagine a much odder arrangement:
before either box is opened, it is not correct to say which box contains
which glove, yet opening both always reveals a relation between the
results. The relation is real; the usual separate labels are the part
that has failed.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A **quantum state**, as earlier chapters established, can specify
possible measurement results without assigning a definite value in every
measurement basis.

2\. Two systems are **entangled** when the full pair has a quantum state
that cannot be written as one independent state for each part.

3\. The pair can then have definite shared correlations even though
neither member has a definite individual value of the relevant property
before measurement.

4\. Measuring one side produces a result correlated with the other, but
each local result is still unpredictable, so no usable message travels
faster than light.

5\. Real systems interact with air, light, apparatus and other
surroundings; these interactions spread the system's phase relations
into an **environment**.

6\. That spreading is **decoherence**: interference becomes practically
inaccessible, though it does not by itself explain why one particular
outcome is experienced.

**In one breath.** Entangled objects have one shared quantum
description, so their correlations can be stronger than any set of
separate local instructions allows, though they cannot send messages
faster than light. When those delicate correlations leak into
uncontrolled surroundings, **decoherence** makes interference
effectively unavailable and the system begins to look ordinary and
separate.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** At the Regional
Compression Annex, Lolly discovers that Mrs Chain's house, transport
routes, identity links and the queue have been treated as though their
connections could be cut off as optional "metadata". Fainrose's
correlation budget is an analogy for the fact that the meaningful
description may belong to the whole pattern, not to each component
separately. By sending unresolved correlations into 4C, Mr Voller's
system has coupled the district to a noisy, evaporating anchor.
Fainrose's warning --- that the environment starts carrying away the
phase relations --- is the chapter's first look at decoherence: the
returning fragments will not simply recombine into their earlier
coherent pattern.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** Entanglement is regularly
advertised as instant communication, or as a secret signal sent from one
particle to another. It is neither. Entanglement can produce
correlations that are revealed only when records from both sides are
later compared by ordinary, slower-than-light means. Nobody at one end
can choose the random local result needed to spell a message at the
other. A second confusion is to say decoherence "solves" measurement. It
explains why interference becomes unobservable in practice, but it does
not on its own select the one outcome a person finds in a record.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. For a schematic
entangled pair,

**\|**Ψ**⟩** = ( **\|**0⟩~A~**\|**0**⟩**~B~ + **\|**1⟩~A~**\|**1**⟩**~B~
) / √2

has a definite joint pattern but does not give either side its own
definite 0 or 1 before a measurement in that basis. Decoherence is
described by entanglement with many environmental degrees of freedom;
after ignoring the environment, the interference terms of the system's
reduced density matrix are strongly suppressed.

**What sticks**

• Entangled systems are described by a shared state, not by complete
independent states for their parts.

• Entanglement produces strong correlations without faster-than-light
communication.

• Environmental interactions can disperse phase relations and cause
decoherence.

• Decoherence explains lost observable interference, not by itself a
single chosen outcome.

**One-line summary.** When the Ministry treated connections as
expendable, it learned that a shared quantum state shares the damage as
well.

## **Chapter 8: Lecture: No-Cloning and Quantum Error Correction**

![](media/image8.jpeg){width="6.135960192475941in"
height="2.4675328083989503in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 8: The Backup That Could Not
Exist]{.underline}](#chapter-8-the-backup-that-could-not-exist)

You need first: Lectures 1--7.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State the no-cloning theorem,
and contrast an impossible perfect copy with protection by distributed
correlations and syndrome checks.

**Start here.** A wax seal on an unopened letter can be carried
elsewhere without changing its pattern. You can also make many seals
from a design you already know. What you cannot do is press a blank lump
of wax against an unknown sealed letter, make a perfect second seal, and
leave the first one untouched while learning every fine ridge. Some
kinds of detail can be moved or reconstructed from a known recipe; they
cannot all be duplicated from a single unknown original.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. An **unknown quantum state** can contain relations that no one has
already written down as a complete classical recipe.

2\. The **no-cloning theorem** says that no single physical procedure
can make a perfect independent copy of every possible unknown state.

3\. It does not forbid moving a state from one system to another; a
transfer can leave the original changed or destroyed.

4\. It also does not forbid preparing many copies of a state already
known from its recipe, such as a repeatedly prepared laboratory state.

5\. A measurement produces classical outcomes, but collecting those
outcomes is not the same as retaining the full unknown state that
produced them.

6\. **Quantum error correction** encodes one logical state across
several physical parts, so limited damage can be found and repaired
without making a forbidden duplicate.

**In one breath.** Physics forbids making a perfect duplicate of an
unknown arbitrary quantum state, so Mr Voller's "reference copy" cannot
exist in the way he claims. Quantum error correction protects
information by spreading it across a carefully arranged pattern, then
checking for damage without reading out the protected state itself.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Mr Voller's cold vault
contains ticked boxes, classical representations and the fatal line
"PHASE RECORD: NOT APPLICABLE". It has preserved selected answers about
Mrs Chain's home, not an independent copy of its full quantum relations;
Fainrose's "photograph of a conversation" is exactly right. Her
alternative is not a magical backup. When Lolly notices Mrs Chain's
sister represented across records, tokens, photographs and routes, she
has found the story analogue of quantum error correction: the useful
information lives in the pattern across the lattice. Fainrose's
left-hand/right-hand test is a **syndrome measurement**, designed to
reveal an error without asking the encoded state to disclose itself.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** "You cannot copy a quantum
state" does not mean quantum information is uniquely doomed, or that
every quantum state may exist only once. The theorem forbids a universal
copier for an unknown arbitrary state. You may prepare another instance
of a known state, and you may transfer a state rather than duplicate it.
Nor does error correction evade the theorem by quietly copying the state
into several boxes. The encoding distributes the one logical state in
nonlocal correlations; no one component is a spare full original waiting
in a cupboard.

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. The no-cloning
theorem (1982) is short and final:

if one linear machine copied both **\|**ψ⟩ and **\|**φ**⟩**,

it would have to turn **\|**ψ⟩**\|**0**⟩** into **\|**ψ⟩**\|**ψ**⟩**

and likewise for **\|**φ**⟩**, which requires **⟨**ψ**\|**φ**⟩** =
**⟨**ψ**\|**φ**⟩**^2^.

That fails for general distinct, non-orthogonal states. Quantum
error-correcting codes use extra physical systems and structured
entanglement. In a stabiliser code, carefully ch**yy**osen parity-like
checks return a syndrome identifying an error pattern while commuting
with, and therefore not revealing, the encoded logical information.

**What sticks**

• No physical process can perfectly copy an unknown arbitrary quantum
state.

• Moving a state and preparing a known state again are allowed; neither
is forbidden cloning.

• Measurement records are not automatically a full copy of the state
that generated them.

• Quantum error correction protects one state through distributed
correlations and indirect error checks.

**One-line summary.** The Ministry built an impossible copy; Fainrose
needs a recoverable pattern instead.

## **Chapter 9: Lecture: Uncertainty: Position and Momentum**

![](media/image9.jpeg){width="4.383483158355205in"
height="3.4675328083989503in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 9: The Two
Columns]{.underline}](#chapter-9-the-two-columns)

You need first: Lectures 1--8 (especially incompatible questions).

When you walk out, you should be able to: State the uncertainty
principle as a limit on preparing two sharp answers at once --- not as a
complaint about clumsy instruments.

**Start here.** Think of a sound heard through a wall. A very brief
click can be placed very accurately in time, but it contains a muddle of
musical notes; a long, pure note has a clear pitch, but no equally clear
beginning or end. Making one description tight makes the other spread
out. No amount of better listening turns one sound into both a perfect
click and a perfectly pure note.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. **Position** answers where a thing is, while **momentum** describes
its motion, including its direction and how hard it is to stop.

2\. A quantum state --- the description introduced earlier in the book
--- behaves in key respects like a wave rather than like a tiny bead
carrying a complete hidden docket.

3\. A wave packed into a small region needs many wavelengths mixed
together, and that mixture corresponds to a wide spread of possible
momenta.

4\. A wave with one very well-defined wavelength has a well-defined
momentum, but it must extend over a wide region rather than having one
sharply fixed position.

5\. This unavoidable trade-off is the **uncertainty principle**: a state
cannot supply arbitrarily sharp values of both properties at the same
time.

6\. A measurement can certainly disturb a system, but the principle
remains even for an ideal measurement that adds no avoidable clumsiness.

**In one breath.** A quantum thing cannot be prepared with arbitrarily
exact answers to both "where is it?" and "how is it moving?" at once.
This is a built-in feature of nature, not a complaint about inadequate
equipment.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Directive 11-C demands
that each dwelling and person receive two certificates: Column A fixes
its present configuration and Column B fixes its rate and direction of
change. Fainrose sees why the demand is impossible before Miss Pike
does. The scans and their correction factor dramatise one real
difficulty --- physical interventions can alter what they probe --- but
the deeper point is stronger: even an imagined perfectly gentle
apparatus, one that disturbed nothing at all, could not prepare a state
with arbitrarily sharp values in both columns at once. Column A and
Column B are not two facts hidden behind one clumsy instrument; they are
two questions that do not both have sharp answers to give, however
careful the asking. When the Ministry insists otherwise, Miss Pike's
spreadsheet has to manufacture a Column B and then mistakes the
manufacture for evidence against the people living in its rows. In the
story, the temptation is to demand both columns at once: exact present
state and exact future trajectory. Quantum mechanics refuses the
request, and the Ministry's paperwork does not change that refusal.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** The usual story says that a
particle secretly has an exact place and an exact motion, but an
observer inevitably knocks it about while looking. That can happen in a
real experiment, yet it is not what Heisenberg's principle says.

Quantum theory does not merely hide two exact pre-existing answers
behind bad instruments; it sets a limit on how narrowly the two
corresponding distributions can coexist in one state.

Here and throughout the book, an observation means a physical
interaction, never a person's attention.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Heisenberg
stated the uncertainty idea in 1927; the precise statistical form below
is a property of the quantum state itself, not merely of clumsy
instruments.

For a collection of identically prepared systems, position and momentum
have spreads measured by their standard deviations; quantum mechanics
requires

Δ*x* Δ*p* ≥ ℏ / 2

The symbol ℏ is Planck's constant divided by 2π, a very small quantity,
which is why the restriction is usually invisible for houses and people.

The mathematical reason is a Fourier relation: narrowing a position wave
pattern requires combining a broad range of wavelengths, while a narrow
range of wavelengths makes the pattern spatially broad.

The relation is about the state itself, not a procurement opportunity
for a better scanner.

**\**

**What sticks**

• Position and momentum are linked quantum properties, not independent
boxes that can both be filled to unlimited precision.

• A sharply localised wave pattern necessarily has a broad spread of
momenta.

• The uncertainty principle is intrinsic to quantum states, not merely
damage caused by crude instruments.

• Directive 11-C turns an impossible demand into fabricated records and
then punishes people by those records.

**One-line summary.** Quantum mechanics permits a precise location or a
precise motion, but not two limitless certificates at once.

filled to arbitrary precision, even with a perfectly gentle apparatus?

## **Chapter 10: Lecture: Decoherence and Einselection**

![](media/image10.jpeg){width="5.203836395450569in"
height="3.2077930883639545in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 10: The Moon That Was
Pending]{.underline}](#chapter-10-the-moon-that-was-pending)

You need first: Lectures 1--9 (especially entanglement and environment).

When you walk out, you should be able to: Explain why ordinary things
look definite because the environment continually records them, not
because a person notices.

**Start here.** Imagine a muddy path after a busy morning. Shoes,
bicycle tyres, rain, and a dog have each left traces of what passed
through; by lunchtime there is no special official whose glance makes
the path a path. The surroundings have been taking part all along, and
some marks survive the traffic better than others.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A **superposition** is a quantum state that does not yet have one
definite value for the property being considered; it can produce
interference between alternatives.

2\. No large object is truly alone: light scatters from it, air
molecules strike it, and it exchanges heat with its surroundings.

3\. Those physical interactions entangle the object with its
environment, leaving many outward traces correlated with its possible
states.

4\. **Decoherence** is the resulting loss of practically accessible
interference between those alternatives; it does not require a mind to
look at anything.

5\. The environment does not preserve every possible state equally:
**einselection** --- environment-induced superselection --- favours
robust **pointer states**, such as stable, reasonably definite
positions.

6\. Decoherence explains why a moon behaves as though it has one settled
location for all practical purposes, but it does not by itself answer
why one outcome is experienced rather than another.

**In one breath.** Ordinary things look definite because they are
continually jostled and marked by the world around them, not because a
person has noticed them. That constant contact makes some
familiar-looking states durable and makes quantum interference
practically unavailable.

**What this chapter was actually showing you**. Mrs Prosser's
forty-one-person moon rota mistakes a civic quorum for a physical
process. Outer Fenwick's moon needs no clipboard holders: photons from
the Sun and the moon, air, thermal radiation, and the rest of its
surroundings are interacting with it continuously. Within the chapter's
extraordinary machinery, Fainrose infers a filter --- probably a modest

definite moon. The Ministry bills the street for the damage. Finding the
mast and switching it off comes later. When it does, the moon comes up
at its ordinary time because the sky is again allowed to keep physically
noticing it, whether or not anyone attends the whist drive.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** "Observation makes reality" is
a catchy phrase and almost wholly misleading. A conscious observer is
not the missing ingredient; an observation in physics is a physical
interaction, and the relevant interactions are already supplied in
overwhelming numbers by photons, molecules, and radiation. The opposite
overstatement is also wrong: decoherence does not simply prove that one
unique outcome has been selected. It tells us why interference is absent
for practical observers and why stable records arise, while the question
of one experienced outcome remains for the next chapter.

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Decoherence
theory explains how environmental coupling continually spreads
information about especially robust states into many separate fragments
of the surroundings, making them independently available to different
physical systems --- einselection of "pointer" states. Experiments have
watched this loss of interference in increasingly controlled systems.
None of these results supplies a final, universally accepted solution to
the measurement problem: they explain the effective classical appearance
extremely well, not why exactly one result is experienced.

**What sticks**

• Large objects become classically stable through continual
environmental interaction, not conscious observation.

• Decoherence makes interference between macroscopic alternatives
practically inaccessible.

• Einselection favours robust pointer states rather than every possible
quantum description.

• Decoherence does not, by itself, settle why one definite outcome is
experienced.

**One-line summary.** The moon stays definite because the whole sky
keeps physically recording it, not because Mrs Prosser secures forty-one
signatures.

## **Chapter 11: Lecture: The Measurement Problem and Wigner's Friend**

![](media/image11.jpeg){width="4.6349518810148735in"
height="3.194805336832896in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 11: An Uninvited
Reflection]{.underline}](#chapter-11-an-uninvited-reflection)

You need first: Lectures 1--10.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State the measurement problem
clearly, and why an observer can sit inside another observer's
unresolved description.

**Start here.** Picture a colleague in a sealed office opening a folded
card marked either YES or NO and writing the answer in a notebook. For
the colleague, the matter is settled at once; for someone outside who
has not opened the door, the office is still a closed story whose
contents must be inferred. In ordinary life there is no puzzle about
this. Quantum theory asks whether the two descriptions can both be taken
seriously when the whole office is part of the physical system.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A **quantum state** evolves smoothly according to the same basic
laws whether it describes a particle, a detector, or, in principle, the
materials of a laboratory.

2\. Yet a textbook **measurement** is usually said to yield one definite
recorded result, with probabilities supplied by the quantum rules.

3\. The **measurement problem** asks what physical feature licenses that
change from a smooth superposition to one outcome, and where any
boundary between system and apparatus could be.

4\. In **Wigner's friend**, a friend inside a sealed laboratory measures
a system and obtains a definite result in their own record.

5\. An observer outside can, before interacting with the laboratory,
describe the friend, record, apparatus, and system together as one
larger quantum system; that is the uncomfortable tension, not a parable
about bad eyesight.

6\. Decoherence --- the environment-blabbing effect of Chapter 10 ---
makes records robust, but interpretations still disagree about whether
collapse, branching, hidden variables, or some relational account
supplies the final story.

**In one breath.** Quantum rules describe change smoothly, yet an
experiment seems to end with one actual answer. The measurement problem
is the unresolved question of what, if anything, turns the smooth
description into that single experienced result.

**A closer look: the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation**. One way to
resolve the measurement problem is to deny that anything special happens
at measurement at all. On this view --- Everett's 1957 proposal, later
called many-worlds --- the smooth quantum law never breaks down and
never needs to: when Wigner's friend measures YES or NO, both outcomes
occur, in branches of one universal wavefunction that have stopped
interacting with each other because they now differ in enormous,
physically irreversible detail --- a decohered friend, a decohered
notebook, a decohered you. There is no collapse to explain and no
boundary to locate, only a universe that was always described in full by
the smooth law, branching every time a measurement-like interaction
happens. Its cost is generosity taken to an extreme: every possible
outcome is realised somewhere. That is not obviously a comfort. A theory
in which nothing is ever truly lost does not, on its own, tell you which
losses are still worth grieving --- and readers who continue on to
Chapter 13 will meet that exact tension argued out in person, by people
with a considerable stake in the answer.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Alfred Weinstein turns
discarded outcomes into vampire nourishment, which is fiction with
excellent table manners, but his arrival exposes the real difficulty.
Phase Two asks a citizen to state a memory, then treats the completed
form as if it merely retrieved a pre-existing official fact. Fainrose
and Lolly see that the question, the person, and the paper form one
physical intervention: the programme may be writing the record it claims
to read. Weinstein's "widening region of agreement" gives the chapter's
version of the Wigner's-friend pressure: Mr Venn, Mrs Chain, the grid,
and the Ministry need not all stand at one mythical instant outside the
same measurement.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** Wigner's friend is often
retold as proof that human consciousness magically causes collapse. It
proves no such thing. Wigner did at one stage discuss consciousness in
connection with measurement, but the thought experiment is valuable
precisely because quantum theory has no agreed need for a conscious
spectator: physical interactions create records. Nor does the scenario
establish that one interpretation has won. It shows sharply why a
universally smooth quantum evolution and one unambiguously definite
outcome are hard to fit together without further interpretive work.

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. By the early
1930s it was already clear you could push the quantum description from a
measured system through an apparatus and, in principle, to an observer
--- which is exactly where the measurement problem refuses to go away.
The smooth law is conventionally written

iℏ ∂/∂*t* **\|**ψ**⟩** = *Ĥ***\|**ψ**⟩**

which says that the state **\|**ψ**⟩** changes continuously under the
system's energy operator H. Wigner's friend (1961) imagines someone who
has measured inside a closed laboratory while you remain outside.
Collapse theories add a real stochastic collapse; Everett's many-worlds
keeps all branches; de Broglie--Bohm theory adds definite particle
positions; relational accounts characterise facts differently. These are
live interpretive programmes, not experimentally settled winners.

**\**

**What sticks**

• Smooth quantum evolution and the appearance of one definite
measurement result create the measurement problem.

• Wigner's friend makes the problem unavoidable by placing an observer
and record inside the system described from outside.

• Observation is something the world does, not something a mind confers.

• Decoherence stabilises records but does not by itself choose one
experienced outcome.

**One-line summary.** If quantum evolution applies to everything, the
unanswered question is what makes one answer the one we live with.

## **Chapter 12: Lecture: Matter Waves and Pilot-Wave Theory**

![](media/image12.png){width="5.558172572178478in"
height="3.831169072615923in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 12: The Duc de Broccoli Explains Himself
Slowly]{.underline}](#chapter-12-the-duc-de-broccoli-explains-himself-slowly)

You need first: Lectures 1--11.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State de Broglie's matter-wave
idea and the pilot-wave picture: definite positions guided by a real,
nonlocal wave.

**Start here.** Imagine a small toy boat in a canal. The boat is at one
particular place, but ripples arriving from different parts of the water
can still steer its next turn; the route depends on a wider pattern than
the boat alone. The image is not a literal model of an electron, but it
earns the central thought: something can have a definite location while
being guided by an extended pattern.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that material particles have an
associated **matter wave**, extending the already puzzling
wave-and-particle behaviour of light.

2\. The **de Broglie wavelength** is shorter for greater momentum, so
the wave aspect of a fast macroscopic object is far too fine to notice
in daily life.

3\. Electrons and other particles produce diffraction and interference
patterns, showing that "a particle is only a tiny hard ball" is not an
adequate account.

4\. **Pilot-wave theory**, also called **de Broglie-Bohm theory**, adds
a definite particle position at every moment and a real wavefunction
that guides its path.

5\. The particle occupies one path, but unoccupied or "empty" parts of
the guiding wave can still affect its motion and produce interference;
the wave does not collapse in this account.

6\. The theory is explicitly **nonlocal**: the guidance of one particle
can depend on the wider configuration, including distant entangled
particles. That is not a defect concealed by the theory but the reason
Bell's theorem, next chapter, does not rule it out.

**In one breath.** Matter can show a wave-like pattern, and one
interpretation says a particle is always somewhere definite while a real
guiding wave directs its motion. That interpretation is deterministic,
but its guidance reaches across distance in a way that is explicitly
nonlocal.

**\**

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** The Duc de Broccoli
gives Lolly an alternative to Weinstein's account. When he speaks of one
actual wallpaper, a wave spread over other possible configurations, and
"empty" alternatives that still steer, he is describing pilot-wave
theory in the language of Mrs Chain's house. On that interpretation Mr
Venn's scanner did not conjure a fact out of conscious observation; it
physically changed the guiding conditions, while the actual
configuration remained definite.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** Matter waves do not mean that
an electron is a miniature ripple of water, nor that standard quantum
mechanics has simply been replaced by a classical hidden mechanism.
Pilot-wave theory is a serious interpretation that reproduces the
ordinary quantum statistics under its usual assumptions, but it pays for
definite particle positions with genuine nonlocality. Bell's theorem
rules out **local** hidden-variable accounts, not every possible
hidden-variable account; pilot-wave theory survives precisely because it
does not pretend to be local. The guiding wave is a physical part of the
theory, not a record waiting for a conscious observer to read.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. De Broglie's
1924 doctoral thesis proposed the relation

λ = *h* / *p*

where λ is the matter wavelength, h is Planck's constant, and p is
momentum. Electron diffraction in the late 1920s made the wave aspect
impossible to dismiss; de Broglie's idea earned the recognition that
follows being right about something that strange. Bohm later gave
pilot-wave theory its modern general form: a deterministic guiding law
once the initial positions and wavefunction are fixed, with the usual
probabilities arising from an unknown distribution of those positions.
In the normal quantum-equilibrium form, it makes the same tested
predictions as standard nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, so it remains
an interpretation rather than an experimentally established replacement.

**\**

**What sticks**

• Matter has a de Broglie wavelength and can produce wave-like
diffraction and interference.

• Pilot-wave theory gives particles definite positions guided by a
non-collapsing real wave.

• Empty parts of the guiding wave can affect the particle that is
actually present.

• Pilot-wave theory is nonlocal, which is why Bell's theorem does not
exclude it.

**One-line summary.** A pilot-wave particle is always somewhere, but the
whole wave, including its empty possibilities, helps decide where it
goes next.

## **Chapter 13: Lecture:** **Bell's Theorem and the CHSH Inequality**

![](media/image13.png){width="5.4854943132108485in"
height="3.5844160104986877in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 13: The Panel of
Experts]{.underline}](#chapter-13-the-panel-of-experts)

You need first: Lectures 1--12.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State Bell's theorem: local
hidden variables cannot reproduce the observed correlations; name what a
CHSH violation means for administration.

**Start here.** Imagine two restaurants at opposite ends of a city, each
choosing at random whether to serve tea or coffee. Two friends have
agreed in advance how to answer either question, but cannot phone one
another after they leave home. Their answers can match rather cleverly,
yet there is a maximum cleverness possible when all the agreement was
packed into the plan at breakfast. If the matches are better than that
maximum, the friends did not merely bring a local plan.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A pair of particles can be prepared in an **entangled** state,
meaning that separate measurements show a pattern of joint results that
cannot be reduced to two independent descriptions.

2\. A **hidden-variable** account says each particle carries additional,
pre-existing instructions that determine its result for every
measurement that might be chosen.

3\. **Locality** adds that the result here cannot depend on which
measurement is freely chosen far away, except through influences no
faster than light.

4\. Bell showed that local pre-written instructions put a numerical
ceiling on the correlations between the two sets of results.

5\. The **CHSH test** uses four alternative settings, chosen after the
pair has separated, and experiments find correlations above that
ceiling.

6\. Each result seen at either end remains unpredictable on its own, so
the shared pattern cannot be turned into a faster-than-light telephone.

**\**

**In one breath.** Bell's theorem says that if distant things carry
pre-written local answers, their joint results can only agree up to a
fixed limit. Experiments exceed that limit, so nature cannot be
explained by **local hidden variables**, even though it still cannot be
used to send a message faster than light.

What this chapter was actually showing you. Jack Bellboy gives Mr
Pilbeam a number rather than a favourite account of reality. The
Ministry calls its surveys of nine thousand dwellings separate local
acts; Lolly sees that a Bell-style test across Fainrose's correlation
lattice can test that claim. Heisenburger, Tengelman and Bellboy
disagree about what quantum mechanics means, but all three can be bound
in advance by the reading from the timing rack in Sub-District 6. Mrs
Chain's correction matters too: even if Tengelman's branches are part of
an interpretation, harm in her branch is not fashioned cost-free by an
account of other branches.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** The slogan is not that Bell
proved that "nothing is real", nor that a conscious observer reaches
across space and changes a distant particle. A measurement is settled by
the apparatus, not by anyone's gaze. Bell's result rules out the
combination of local influence and pre-existing hidden instructions
under the usual assumption that the setting choices are not secretly
coordinated with the source. It does not rule out every hidden-variable
theory: the pilot-wave theory met in Chapter 12 has definite particle
positions but is explicitly **nonlocal**, and so it survives Bell's
theorem. Nor does a Bell violation settle the competition among
Copenhagen-style accounts, many-worlds, and pilot-wave theory; those
interpretations are not currently experimentally distinguished.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Bell's theorem
was a direct answer to a 1935 challenge from Einstein, Podolsky and
Rosen --- the "EPR paradox" --- who argued that if quantum mechanics
were complete, a measurement here should not be able to fix the value of
a property over there, and that the theory must therefore be incomplete.
In Bell's 1964 argument, and in the later CHSH form used in most modern
tests, each side chooses one of two settings, a, a′ and b, b′. If the
outcomes are recorded as +1 or −1, a local-hidden-variable model must
obey

**\|***S***\|** = **\|***E*(*a*,*b*) + *E*(*a*,*b*′) + *E*(*a*′,*b*) −
*E*(*a*′,*b*′)**\|** ≤ 2

Quantum mechanics predicts up to 2√2 for suitable entangled states,
while its individual outcomes remain random. Experiments from the 1970s
onward --- including loophole-free tests in 2015 --- have violated the
local ceiling again and again. The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics marked
that long experimental programme. These tests support the precise
conclusion, not a mystical extra one: local hidden-variable models
cannot reproduce the observed correlations.

**\**

**What sticks**

• A Bell test compares observed joint correlations with a ceiling
required by local hidden-variable theories.

• Quantum experiments violate the CHSH ceiling, although they do not
transmit usable information faster than light.

• Bell's theorem excludes local hidden variables, not nonlocal
pilot-wave theory or every possible interpretation.

• A pre-agreed measurement can constrain a committee's story without
choosing a metaphysics.

**One-line summary.** Bell gave Lolly the rarest administrative
instrument: a number that shows local innocence cannot explain the
facts.

You need first: Lectures 1--13. Optional depth: unconfirmed framework,
honest limits.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State what string/M-theory
claims (extended objects; dualities; eleven dimensions) and what it has
not experimentally confirmed.

**\**

**Start here**. A violin is one object that can make many notes. Change
how the string moves and the sound changes; you have not invented a new
violin each time. String theory asks whether the zoo of particles might
be like that: one kind of tiny vibrating thread, many notes. It is a
beautiful idea. Beauty is not evidence. Hold both facts in your head at
once.

**In one breath.** String theory proposes that the basic ingredients of
nature may be tiny vibrating threads rather than points, and that
several seemingly different string theories are, mathematically, the
same underlying object seen from different angles --- a broader picture
called M-theory. It is an unconfirmed framework, not an established
fact.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1.  **String theory** replaces pointlike fundamental particles with
    extraordinarily small

> one-dimensional strings whose different vibrations would look like
> different particles.

2.  The mathematics naturally includes a vibration with the properties
    expected of a **graviton**,

> a proposed quantum carrier of gravity, but no experiment has confirmed
> strings or gravitons.

3.  Consistent versions require extra **spatial dimensions**, usually
    imagined as

> compact directions too small to notice, and their several formulations
> are related by **dualities**.

4.  **M-theory** is the still-incomplete proposed framework linking
    those formulations in

> eleven dimensions and allowing extended objects called branes as well
> as strings.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** "Everything is made of
strings" is not an established fact, and neither string theory nor
M-theory has experimental confirmation. The mathematics is elegant and
internally disciplined, which is not the same thing as being true of our
universe.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. In the
mid-1990s, work associated especially with Witten connected five
superstring theories as limits of a broader eleven-dimensional proposal
called M-theory --- five descriptions of one thing, related by dualities
that are provable in the simplest cases and, so far, consistent
everywhere else: turn a quantity up very high in one description and it
becomes a different description with that quantity turned very low.

**\**

**What sticks**

• String theory and M-theory are ambitious, mathematically rich but
unconfirmed frameworks.

• Replacing points with loops removes the short-distance infinities that
had wrecked earlier attempts to combine gravity with quantum theory ---
at least in every calculation anyone has managed to finish --- and a
graviton-like vibration falls out of the mathematics, unasked for.

• The five original string theories were unified by Witten in 1995 as
different limits of one underlying eleven-dimensional structure.

• Mathematical elegance and experimental confirmation are two different
things, and this framework currently has only the first.

## **Chapter 14: Lecture (Part 2 of 2): Black-Hole Entropy and Holography**

![](media/image15.png){width="5.3in" height="5.973857174103237in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 14: Eleven Dimensions and a
Whisper]{.underline}](#chapter-14-eleven-dimensions-and-a-whisper)

You need first: Lecture 14, Part 1, and Lecture 6.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State the area law for
black-hole entropy and the holographic idea that a region's description
may live on its boundary.

**\**

**Start here.** A circle drawn on paper can be fully described by a
single number written on its edge: the radius. You do not need to
describe the whole disc it encloses to know everything geometrical about
it --- the boundary already carries that information. Holography
proposes something far stranger but structurally similar: a full
description of a volume of space, gravity included, might be completely
captured by a description living one dimension down, on its boundary
alone.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A black hole has **entropy**, a measure of how many microscopic
arrangements can look the same from outside, and its entropy grows with
the **area** of its horizon rather than the volume it encloses.

2\. In the mid-1990s, string theorists tested this directly for a
special, mathematically convenient class of black hole: they counted the
microscopic string-and-brane arrangements and got exactly the predicted
entropy back out --- a rare case where string theory produced a
checkable number rather than another elegant hope.

3\. The **holographic principle** is the proposal, realised exactly in
some special mathematical models, that information about a region with
gravity can be represented by a lower-dimensional boundary description.

**In one breath.** A black hole's information capacity follows the area
of its horizon rather than its volume --- and this area law motivates
the holographic principle, the idea that a region's full description
might live on its boundary. Together they turn evaporation from a simple
ending into a slow, scrambled disclosure.

**\**

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** This is von Wittenberg's
useful contribution to 4C: Hawking's area-law entropy makes the horizon
a vast ledger of possible states, and holography makes Fainrose's phrase
"an ear held against a burning wall" intelligible. When the engineered
black hole evaporates, the story treats its radiation as a time-limited,
badly scrambled disclosure channel. The dialogue is right to call that a
working hope rather than a certificate of recovery. In the story, the
horizon is therefore not a storage cabinet but a boundary whose
information capacity matters. The deadline in 4C is not administrative
theatre.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** Holography does not mean that
the universe is literally a flat cinema screen, nor that a horizon is a
filing cabinet with readable labels on it. The claim is about an exact
equivalence between descriptions in certain carefully defined theories.
The old picture of a black hole as a pair of particles torn apart at the
horizon is only a heuristic; Hawking radiation is better understood as
radiation predicted by quantum fields in curved spacetime. Whether the
full information in realistic evaporation can be recovered in practice
remains an extraordinarily difficult question.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Black-hole
entropy was proposed in the early 1970s; Hawking's 1974 calculation
fixed its value:

*S*~BH~ = *k*~B~*c*^3^*A* / (4*G*ℏ)

The equation says that the entropy SBH is proportional to the horizon
area A, with the striking factor of one quarter in suitable natural
units. A mid-1990s microscopic count for a special supersymmetric class
of black holes reproduced that formula exactly --- one of string
theory's few genuine numerical victories. The holographic idea, made
precise in certain models soon after, says a gravitational region can be
fully described by a lower-dimensional theory on its boundary.

**What sticks**

• Black-hole entropy scales with horizon area, suggesting that a horizon
counts genuine microscopic possibilities.

• That mid-1990s microscopic count is a major string-theory success: for
a special class of black holes, the entropy formula came out exactly.

• Holography supports the idea that evaporation may preserve information
in correlations, even when recovery is impracticable.

**One-line summary.** In von Wittenberg's cautious picture, the black
hole's vanishing is not proof of loss but a brief, terrible chance to
read a scrambled account.

## **Chapter 15: Lecture: Quantum Error Correction, Partial Recovery and Planning Without an Interpretation**

![](media/image16.png){width="4.930734908136483in"
height="2.740260279965004in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 15: Final State
Pending]{.underline}](#chapter-15-final-state-pending)

You need first: Lectures 1--14 (especially 8, 12--14).

When you walk out, you should be able to: Explain why a sound recovery
plan can use tools from rival interpretations without deciding which
ontology is true.

**Start here.** Think of a mural copied across a row of tiled walls. If
one tile cracks, the repeated pattern around it can show a restorer what
probably belonged there. If a fire takes half the walls, the surviving
pieces may still support a repair, but no honest restorer can promise to
recover every brushstroke.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. The no-cloning result from Chapter 8 says that an unknown quantum
state cannot be kept as an ordinary perfect backup copy.

2\. **Quantum error correction** instead encodes one logical state
across several physical systems, so that the information lives in their
joint pattern rather than in one vulnerable place.

3\. A **syndrome measurement** asks which sort of damage occurred
without reading out the protected logical state itself; it is a physical
interaction, not a conscious observation.

4\. Every code has a **threshold**: below its tolerable error rate,
repeated correction can make the logical information more reliable as
the code is enlarged; above it, damage accumulates faster than
correction can contain it.

5\. When uncontrolled surroundings have carried away too much of the
joint pattern, the missing information cannot be reconstructed from the
survivors alone, however much paperwork asks for it.

6\. Different interpretations can disagree about what the state means
while agreeing on the same practical sequence: stop adding damage,
collect surviving evidence, correct what the syndromes justify, and
publish the uncertainty.

**\**

**What this chapter was actually showing you**. Fainrose's first Bell
run is beautifully clean and worthless: the apparatus shared the
Ministry's clock, so it measured the Ministry and subtracted it from
itself. Lolly sees the shape before the explanation finishes. The
repaired Sunday test gives Bellboy's number; Beatrix takes it to court
on the Monday; the Board, cornered, demands a plan by four o'clock.
Lolly refuses to pick an interpretation. She puts all four into one
plan: switch the scanners off first (de Broccoli), repair the lattice
from surviving correlations (Fainrose), listen to the horizon before 4C
finishes evaporating (von Wittenberg), and spread agreement rather than
forge a copy (Weinstein). Gideon knows where the actual relays are. That
same afternoon Mrs Chain asks Fainrose for a percentage and is refused a
comforting number. The chapter's standard of success is named here and
not yet delivered: recoverable, not unharmed; an itemised list of gaps,
not a certificate of perfection. Beatrix confirms it is on the Board's
desk. They have a plan. The Board has a Board. The scanners are still
on.

**\**

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** Error correction is not a time
machine and it is not covert cloning. It protects a state only because
the information was encoded before the relevant damage and because
enough controlled correlations remain accessible. **Decoherence** -- the
environment-blabbing effect from Chapter 10 -- is especially serious
here: once an uncontrolled environment has become entangled with
information and the needed correlations are unavailable, a local repair
cannot simply command it back. A threshold is not a universal percentage
at which all recovery suddenly becomes hopeless; it depends on the code
and the noise. Nor does successful engineering choose an interpretation.
Copenhagen-style accounts, many-worlds and pilot-wave theory remain
experimentally indistinguishable, even though they can all guide the
same laboratory precautions.

**\**

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. The first
quantum error-correcting codes (mid-1990s) showed explicitly how a
logical qubit can be protected by entangling it with several physical
qubits and measuring error syndromes rather than the encoded amplitudes.
A code's distance determines how many errors it can certainly correct in
an idealised model; for an error-correction threshold pth, physical
error rates p \< pth can in principle be suppressed by increasing the
code size and running fault-tolerant correction. For p \> pth, enlarging
the procedure does not rescue the computation because new faults outrun
the redundancy. A useful recovery score is fidelity,

*F*(ρ,σ) = ( Tr √( √ρ σ √ρ ) )^2^

which compares a recovered state ρ with its target σ. It permits a
graded, honest result, but no score transforms unavailable environmental
information into an exact original history.

**What sticks**

• Quantum error correction uses distributed correlations and syndrome
measurements, not a forbidden copy of an unknown state.

• Thresholds are code- and noise-dependent limits beyond which errors
accumulate faster than a code can control them.

• Information that has escaped into inaccessible surroundings can make
exact recovery genuinely impossible.

• Practical quantum work can be correct without settling which
interpretation of quantum mechanics is right.

**One-line summary.** The honest plan is not the one that promises a
perfect past, but the one that repairs what survives and names what does
not.

When you walk out, you should be able to: State the second law: local
order is paid for by exporting disorder; cutting a coupling does not
cancel the bill.

**Start here**. A tidy kitchen does not become tidy because the
washing-up has ceased to exist. Someone uses energy to wash, dry and
stack it, while warm air, dirty water and a fuller bin carry the
untidiness elsewhere. The mess has been paid for, not abolished.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. Entropy measures, roughly, how many detailed arrangements can
produce the same broad appearance; spread-out energy has many more such
arrangements than a hot cup and a cold room kept separate.

2\. The second law of thermodynamics says that the total entropy of an
isolated whole does not go down, which is why heat flows unaided from
hot coffee to a cooler room rather than the reverse.

3\. Hiding a cost, or moving it into a hole with a budget code, changes
neither the physical account nor the human one.

4\. The Ministry tried to make districts administratively simple by
displacing complexity into 4C. Cutting the coupling does not repeal that
account. It stops the pretence that the hole was a cupboard.

In one breath. The second law says that total entropy does not decrease:
local order can be produced only by releasing greater disorder
elsewhere. That is why Asset 4C could never be storage, and why cutting
the coupling does not cancel the bill --- it stops you pretending the
hole was a filing cabinet.

**\**

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** They cut the coupling at
06:04. For eleven seconds nothing happens; then the houses hold together
with one another again, and 4C becomes what Fainrose names without
ceremony: an ordinary, boring, evaporating black hole, doing the only
thing black holes are supposed to do, which is leave. The three hundred
and eight recovered P-9 states, and the published one hundred and three
unrecovered names, are the visible ledger of a bill that was never going
to be filed as "storage." Nobody cheers, correctly. Moving disorder out
of sight is not the same as removing it.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** The book's recurring claim ---
that any institution making things simpler must be shifting the
difference somewhere unseen --- is a moral analogy, not a theorem.
Administrative complexity, meaning and thermodynamic entropy are three
different quantities, and only the last is governed by the second law.
Where erasing information does carry an unavoidable physical price, the
principle is Landauer's, and it holds only under the conditions it
names.

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Entropy counts
how many microscopic arrangements look the same from far away;
Boltzmann's famous form is S = kB ln Ω. For an isolated system the
second law is expressed as

*ΔStotal ≥ 0.*

**What sticks**

• Total entropy does not decrease. Local order is paid for by exporting
disorder somewhere else.

• A black hole used as a cupboard does not remove the cost. It relocates
it, and then radiates.

• Cutting a coupling can stop an institution from adding to the damage.
It cannot make the second law issue a refund.

**One-line summary.** Close the door if you must; the bill was never in
the cupboard, and never will be.

## **Chapter 17: Lecture: Negentropy**

![](media/image18.png){width="5.97584208223972in"
height="2.4415583989501313in"}

[[← Back to Chapter 17: A Very Good
Obituary]{.underline}](#chapter-17-a-very-good-obituary)

You need first: Lecture 16.

When you walk out, you should be able to: Define Schrödinger's
negentropy as a scheme within the second law's accounts, not an
exemption from them.

**In one breath.** A living thing maintains local order by importing
usable structure and exporting disorder. Schrödinger called that intake
negative entropy, or negentropy. It is a scheme within the accounts, not
an exemption from them --- which is why a seventy-eight per cent
certificate can be honest and a perfect copy cannot.

**Start here**. A living body works on the same principle as the tidy
kitchen: it keeps an intricate pattern going by taking in useful food
and sunlight and sending heat and waste out into the wider world. The
pattern is real. It is also never a sealed, complete duplicate of what
it was yesterday**.**

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. A living thing is an open system: it maintains local order by
importing low-entropy resources and exporting heat, waste and entropy to
its surroundings.

2\. Schrödinger's phrase negative entropy, or negentropy, describes that
intake of usable order; it is not a loophole in the second law.

3\. What can be honestly said about a recovered life is the pattern that
still holds, not a forged completeness.

4\. A certificate of perfection would conceal the exported cost. An
itemised gap keeps the ledger readable.

**What this chapter was actually showing you**. Lolly delivers Mrs Chain
a figure --- seventy-eight per cent of her sister, as much of the
pattern as survived the listening --- and Mrs Chain names it correctly:
a very good obituary, not a resurrection. The photograph a burglar has
the decency to return is worth more than a government signature because
it is a remaining correlation, not a claimed whole. Lolly's new work,
"Noticing, professionally, with a budget," is the humane version of this
bookkeeping: trace what still holds, name what does not, and refuse the
vault's dream of exactness. The Fenwick mast, found in the weeks after
the coupling was cut and billed as an amenity, is the same accounting in
civic miniature: order on the street, disorder on the invoice, a revised
quorum of thirty-nine pretending to be a response to evidence.

**\**

**Where the popular version goes wrong**. "Negentropy" does not mean
life beats entropy. Life exports entropy while maintaining local
structure. Schrödinger's cat, which belongs to the next note, was not a
claim that living things occupy contradictory states until observed; it
was a 1935 complaint about measurement. A person is a poor thing to
render as a fraction, and the fraction is still more honest than a green
tick on a vault manifest.

**Going deeper (skip freely**). This block is optional. In What Is Life?
(1944), Schrödinger said that organisms feed on "negative entropy";
modern thermodynamics states the point more precisely as entropy export
by open systems. The Ministry's vault promised a perfect classical copy
of a prior state. That was already forbidden by the no-cloning result in
Chapter 8. This chapter adds the living-system reason the same promise
is dishonest even as an ideal: a life is a pattern held against the
current, paid for elsewhere, and never a sealed file.

**\**

**What sticks**

• Schrödinger's negentropy is an account of life maintaining order, not
an exception to the second law.

• Honest recovery reports the pattern that survives. It does not mint a
person who was never damaged.

• A tidy certificate can conceal a mess; it cannot repeal the ledger.

**One-line summary**. Seventy-eight per cent of a life, named as such,
is truer than a hundred per cent of a form.

## **Chapter 18: Lecture: The Long Future**

When you walk out, you should be able to: Place the book's ending on
cosmological timescales, and state why there is no final filing moment
--- no lid.

**Start here.** A cup of coffee going cold is a small, local version of
the same rundown: order spent, heat shared, the drink finished though
the evening is not. Scale that up far enough and you get stars, then
embers, then evaporating holes, then a dark that is not an appointment
in anyone's diary.

**\**

**The idea, step by step**

1\. On familiar, still-provisional assumptions, star formation declines,
remnants cool, and black holes evaporate. Almost all of history, on
those assumptions, is afterwards.

2\. The timetable depends on unsettled physics: proton decay, if it
occurs, changes the numbers; so does the behaviour of dark energy.

3\. Between now and any such dark there is an enormous middle in which
order remains to be spent and some arrangements of matter can hold a
model of the rest.

4\. There is no closing act after which a fact is a fact and may be
filed. Correlation continues outward. A state that has not finished
resolving is the ordinary condition of everything.

**In one breath**. The universe is not close to a final filing state.
Stars, remnants and black holes point to an immense future whose
timetable depends on physics not yet settled. On those timescales,
places that can keep an honest account of what happened --- noticing,
recording, refusing to tidy away the difficulty --- are rarer than
gravity. There is no moment at which a state is finished and may be put
away. There is no lid.

**What this chapter was actually showing you.** Schrottfinger's Prologue
--- placed at the back, to the printers' annoyance --- turns the
injunction's outcome into a timescale. The wooden box is empty and was
always a joke; the trouble with his cat was that everyone read it as a
story about a box rather than about the lid. The three hundred and eight
recovered items were pending, not memories, and they are still not
finished. Lolly writes "There is no lid." The noticing funded in the
previous chapter is what remains worth doing in the middle.

**Where the popular version goes wrong.** Schrödinger's cat was not a
claim that real cats are literally half alive and half dead until
somebody looks. In his 1935 thought experiment he used the cat as a
reductio ad absurdum: he was criticising the unresolved measurement
problem, not announcing a zoological fact. Opening a box is a physical
interaction, not consciousness making reality happen. A cold dark future
is a provisional physical scenario, not a dated appointment.

**Going deeper (skip freely).** This block is optional. Schrödinger's
1935 paper, "The Present Situation in Quantum Mechanics", introduced the
cat as a deliberately absurd challenge about measurement, not a
zoological claim. A commonly used, highly provisional far-future
sequence is the stelliferous era of stars, then a degenerate era of
stellar remnants, then a black-hole era, and finally a thin dark era
after black holes evaporate. If ordinary protons eventually decay,
matter changes the timetable; if they do not, it changes again. On
familiar assumptions, the evaporation time for the largest black holes
can be of order 10\^100 years, but no calendar can make those
assumptions final.

**\**

**What sticks**

• The long future may pass through stellar, remnant, black-hole and dark
eras, but its timing remains conditional.

• Schrödinger's cat was a 1935 critique of the measurement problem, not
a literal half-alive animal.

• Honest accounts --- places that keep noticing --- are rarer than
gravity; "simplifying" by hiding the bill is not tidiness, it is a crime
against the ledger. And there is no final filing: an ending is only the
beginning of what has not yet been written.

**One-line summary. Keep the ledger honest. Keep noticing. There is no
lid.**

**Glossary**

A quick reference for terms used in the novel and explained at length in
the lectures. The chapter in brackets is the place to start.

**Holographic Duality (sometimes labelled AdS/CFT) ---** A fully worked
example of holography: gravity in a curved box of space is exactly
equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory, with no gravity, living only
on that box\'s boundary. Same physics, two descriptions; the boundary
carries the whole story. (Chapter 14)

**Black-hole (area-law) Entropy** --- A black hole's entropy,
proportional to the area of its event horizon rather than the volume
inside. It counts how many microscopic configurations look identical
from outside. Often written with Hawking's 1974 prefactor. (Chapter 14)

**Bell's theorem** --- The proof that no theory in which separated
things carry their own pre-existing properties and cannot influence one
another faster than light can reproduce the correlations quantum
mechanics predicts. Experiment sides with quantum mechanics. (Chapter
13)

**Black Hole** --- A region whose gravity is strong enough that light
cannot escape past its event horizon, once formed. Despite the name, not
perfectly black: it emits Hawking radiation and slowly evaporates.
(Chapter 6)

**Bra--Ket Notation** --- The standard shorthand for quantum states,
written **\|**like this**⟩**. Lolly writes in it when frightened, which
turns out to be often. (Chapter 1)

**CHSH Inequality** --- A testable four-setting form of Bell's theorem.
Local hidden-variable theories cannot push the combined correlation S
above 2; quantum mechanics reaches 2√2 ≈ 2.83. (Chapter 13)

**Collapse** --- Textbook shorthand for the abrupt update of a state to
one definite outcome when a measurement rec ords a result. Whether it is
a real physical event is the measurement problem. (Chapter 2)

**De Broglie Wavelength** --- The wavelength associated with any moving
object, shorter for greater momentum: λ = h/p. Far too small to notice
for anything larger than a molecule. (Chapter 12)

**Decoherence** --- The leakage of a system's quantum relationships into
its uncontrolled surroundings, which makes interference between
alternatives practically unrecoverable and is why large objects look
definite. Not a solution to the measurement problem. (Chapter 10)

**Determinism** --- The property of a theory in which the present state
fixes the future exactly. Quantum evolution is deterministic between
measurements; pilot-wave theory is deterministic throughout. (Chapter 5)

**Duality** --- A relation showing that two apparently different
physical descriptions are one theory seen from different sides --- the
reason the five string theories turned out to be one. (Chapter 14)

**Einselection** --- Environment-induced superselection: the way
constant environmental interaction singles out a small set of robust,
stable pointer states from all the states a system could in principle
occupy. (Chapter 10)

**Entanglement** --- A single quantum description shared by two or more
objects, so that neither has a definite individual value for the
relevant property --- only a shared, correlated one. It sends no usable
signal faster than light. (Chapter 7)

**Entropy** --- A measure of how many microscopic arrangements are
consistent with a system's observable, large-scale state; more such
arrangements means higher entropy. The quantity the second law of
thermodynamics says cannot, overall, decrease. (Chapter 16)

**Event Horizon** --- The surface of a black hole: the boundary past
which nothing, light included, can return to a distant observer.
(Chapter 6)

**Hawking Radiation** --- The faint thermal radiation a black hole emits
by quantum processes, causing it to lose mass. Smaller black holes are
hotter and evaporate faster. (Chapter 6)

**Hidden Variables** --- Proposed extra properties a system carries in
advance that would fix measurement outcomes. Bell's theorem rules out
the local kind; pilot-wave theory is a surviving nonlocal kind. (Chapter
13)

**Holographic Principle** --- The proposal that everything happening
inside a region of space, gravity included, can be fully described by
information on its lower-dimensional boundary. (Chapter 14)

**Information Problem (black-hole)** --- The unresolved question of what
becomes of the detailed quantum information in everything a black hole
swallows, given that its radiation looks merely thermal. Most physicists
expect the information survives. (Chapter 6)

**Interference** --- The reinforcing or cancelling of quantum
alternatives that have not been recorded, and the observable sign that a
superposition is more than ordinary ignorance. (Chapter 1)

**Landauer's Principle ---** The result that erasing one bit of
information has a minimum unavoidable energy cost, released as heat. The
genuine, narrow link between information and thermodynamics. (Chapter
16)

**Locality** --- The assumption that what is true here cannot depend on
a distant, freely chosen measurement except through influences no faster
than light. Bell's theorem shows nature does not respect it in the way
local hidden variables would require. (Chapter 13)

**M-theory** --- The still-incomplete eleven-dimensional framework in
which the five string theories are one structure seen five ways, and the
fundamental objects include membranes as well as strings. (Chapter 14)

**Many-Worlds (Everett) Interpretation** --- The view that the smooth
quantum law never breaks down and every measurement-like interaction
branches the universe, all outcomes occurring in branches that no longer
interact. No collapse; unlimited branches. (Chapter 11)

**Matter Wave** --- The wave aspect of material particles --- the reason
electrons produce diffraction and interference patterns. (Chapter 12)

**Measurement** --- In physics, any physical interaction that records or
spreads information about an outcome. Not an act of attention: no mind,
eye or clipboard required. (Chapter 1)

**Measurement Basis** --- The particular set of alternatives a given
apparatus can tell apart. For incompatible quantum properties there is
no basis-independent set of true values waiting to be read off. (Chapter
2)

**Measurement Problem** --- The open question of what, if anything,
turns the smooth quantum description into the single definite outcome an
experiment records. (Chapter 11)

**Negentropy ---** Schrödinger's phrase for the usable order a living
thing takes in to maintain itself while exporting disorder to its
surroundings. A scheme within the second law's accounts, not an
exemption from them. (Chapter 17)

**No-cloning Theorem** --- The proof that no physical process can make a
perfect copy of an unknown arbitrary quantum state. Moving a state, or
re-preparing a known one, is still allowed. (Chapter 8)

n**onlocality** --- A dependence of what is true here on a distant
configuration, with no signal passing between. Pilot-wave theory is
openly nonlocal; Bell's theorem shows nature is not locally real.
(Chapter 13)

**Pilot-Wave Theory (de Broglie--Bohm)** --- An interpretation in which
particles always have definite positions and are guided by a real,
non-collapsing wave spread over every possible configuration.
Deterministic and explicitly nonlocal. (Chapter 12)

**Pointer States** --- The robust, stable states --- typically
well-defined positions --- that survive constant environmental
monitoring and make up the everyday classical world. (Chapter 10)

**Quantum Error Correction** --- Protecting information by spreading it
across many systems as a pattern, then checking for damage with syndrome
measurements that locate errors without reading the protected data.
Works below a threshold error rate, not above it. (Chapter 15)

**Quantum Zeno Effect** --- The real, observed effect by which very
frequent measurements of a quantum system can inhibit its normal change.
Not a licence for anyone to freeze a thing by watching it. (Chapter 4)

**Schrödinger Equation** --- The rule governing how an isolated quantum
state changes between measurements: smoothly, predictably and
reversibly. The least mysterious part of the subject. (Chapter 5)

**Schrödinger's Cat ---** Schrödinger's 1935 thought experiment, meant
as a reductio ad absurdum of an unexplained measurement process --- not
a claim that a cat is literally half alive. (Chapter 18)

**Second Law of Thermodynamics ---** Total entropy does not decrease.
Local order can be built only by exporting greater disorder somewhere
else. (Chapter 16)

**State (Quantum state)** --- The complete account physics can give of a
system: what results its possible measurements can produce, and with
what probabilities. Not a location, and not a hidden ledger of answers.
(Chapter 1)

**State Reduction** --- The idealised update of a state to the outcome
just recorded; an immediate repeat of the same measurement then returns
the same result with certainty. (Chapter 4)

**String Theory** --- The proposal that the basic objects of nature are
tiny vibrating extended things rather than points, with different
vibrations appearing as different particles. Mathematically rich,
experimentally unconfirmed. (Chapter 14)

**Superposition** --- A quantum state that combines alternatives without
secretly holding one definite answer to the question being asked; its
alternatives can interfere. Not "two places at once" in the naive sense.
(Chapter 1)

**Syndrome Measurement** --- A measurement, in quantum error correction,
that reveals which kind of damage has occurred without reading out, and
so without disturbing, the protected logical state itself. (Chapter 15)

**Tsirelson Bound** --- The ceiling quantum mechanics puts on its own
Bell (CHSH) correlations: S ≤ 2√2 ≈ 2.83 --- above the local-realistic
limit of 2, but not unlimited. (Chapter 13)

**Uncertainty Principle** --- The intrinsic limit that a quantum system
cannot be prepared with arbitrarily sharp values of both position and
momentum at once: Δx·Δp ≥ ℏ/2. A fact about states, not about
instruments. (Chapter 9)

**Unitary Evolution** --- The technical name for the smooth, reversible,
probability-preserving change a state undergoes under the Schrödinger
equation. (Chapter 5)

**Wigner's Friend** --- A thought experiment in which an observer inside
a sealed laboratory records a definite result while an observer outside
must still describe the whole laboratory as one unresolved quantum
system. It sharpens the measurement problem. (Chapter 11)

The name behind the pseudonym is

**Lothar J. Musiol**

Mr Musiol spent more than forty years in the electronics industry: the
kind of career that leaves a person fluent in semiconductors, RF, and
the precise moment at which an engineering deadline becomes a
philosophical position.

Before that he studied physics seriously enough to know exactly how much
of it he is simplifying here for comic effect, and exactly how much he
is not.

Readers who found the bra-ket notation in Lolly Wren's green notebook
oddly natural rather than decorative may draw their own conclusions.

He wrote *Schrödinger's Paperwork* the way Lolly Wren does most things
--- carefully, in the margins of a life spent elsewhere, and with the
slowly hardening suspicion that the paperwork was more interesting than
anyone had given it credit for.

He is German by birth and writes in certified bad English by choice,
which may explain the book's affection for the almost precise word in
the wrong register.

# **About the Series**

**Schrödinger's Paperwork** is Book 1 of *Lolly Wren's Curious Science
Adventures*, a series in which large institutions attempt to administer
physics, and physics declines to cooperate.

Each book stands alone. Each teaches its own set of ideas honestly, in
the same two-layer form: a comic novel that never stops to lecture, and
clearly separated lectures for readers who want the real science and are
prepared to be told when it is unsettled.

Book 2 is already waiting: a different story with a rather different
arrangement of collapsing certainties --- for readers who did not, in
the event, get enough the first time.

Look for the next Lolly Wren adventure wherever you found this one.

**If this wrought you curious**

None of the physics in this book was invented for the joke. If you would
like to go further than a Ministry can take you, three places are worth
the trip:

Sean Carroll's *Something Deeply Hidden* takes many-worlds seriously and
argues for it properly.

Carlo Rovelli's *Helgoland* makes the case for relational quantum
mechanics in prose good enough to make you forgive him for being
persuasive. And if you want the same territory covered by the same
author's non-fiction companion, at considerably greater length and with
rather less bureaucracy,

*The Quantum World* goes much further into most of the questions this
book only had time to dramatise.

**\**

**If you enjoyed this book**

Reviews are how small books find their readers. If *Schrödinger's
Paperwork* made you laugh, or helped you understand something you had
always been told was impossible to grasp, a few honest sentences on the
store page will do more good than the author ever could alone.

And if you want more paperwork failing at physics, Book 2 of *Lolly
Wren's Curious Science Adventures* is already sharpening its pencils.

If the book also made you look differently at an official letter, at the
sentence that quietly moves a difficulty somewhere you cannot quite see,
then it has done its real work. In that case, you may consider the
notebook successfully passed on.
