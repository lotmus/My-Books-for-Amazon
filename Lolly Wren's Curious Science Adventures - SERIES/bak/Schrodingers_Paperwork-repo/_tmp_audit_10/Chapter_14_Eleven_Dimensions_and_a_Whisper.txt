1797|Heading 1|Chapter 14: Eleven Dimensions and a Whisper
1798|Normal|They went into Sub-District 6 on the Thursday with two vans, a portable timing rack, four hired photodetectors, and paperwork Beatrix had signed in a hand that dared anyone to query it. Fainrose was in the primary school hall running cable. Jago had gone for a fifth detector on terms nobody had asked about. Lolly, sent out for exceeding her quota of useful questions, ended up in the Coldharrow Street library with the green notebook and eleven photocopied pages from box seven.
1799|Normal|There was one other person in the reading room: a slight man in his seventies, entirely bald, papers colonising two additional tables as though the furniture had signed a memorandum. He wrote in pencil, continuously, pausing every twenty minutes to look at the shut-down carpet showroom. The librarian had given up asking him to consolidate, and simply reshelved around him.
1800|Normal|At about four o’clock he said, without turning round, in a voice so quiet that Lolly was not initially certain it had been aimed at her: “You are the person doing the Bell test on the housing lattice.”
1801|Normal|Lolly put down her pen. “How do you know that.”
1802|Normal|“Dr Bellew mentioned it.” The man turned. He had a pleasant, unemphatic, slightly abstracted face and the mildest possible manner, and he spoke so softly that Lolly found herself leaning forward before the sentence was finished. “I think it is an excellent idea. It is the correct instinct — to find the one claim that does not depend on taste. Forgive me. Leonhard von Wittenberg.”
1803|Normal|“Lolly Wren.”
1804|Normal|“Yes.” Von Wittenberg gathered three sheets of paper together, apparently at random, and came and sat down opposite her. “May I ask you something before you ask me anything?”
1805|Normal|“Of course.”
1806|Normal|“When you say the country is held together by correlations” — he said it gently, as though it might be fragile — “what do you imagine the correlations are between?”
1807|Normal|Lolly thought about it. “Houses,” she said. “Records. Routes. Districts. People. The nodes on Fainrose’s lattice.”
1808|Normal|“Yes. And what are those made of.”
1809|Normal|“Bricks. Paper. Copper. People are made of cells.” She heard where this was going. “Cells are made of molecules, molecules of atoms, atoms of nuclei and electrons, nuclei of quarks.”
1810|Normal|“And quarks?”
1811|Normal|“Are made of nothing. They’re fundamental. Points.”
1812|Normal|Von Wittenberg smiled very slightly and looked out at the carpet showroom. “That is the standard answer and it is the best-tested answer in the history of thought,” he said. “It is also, I believe, wrong, and I have believed it for about fifty years and been unable to prove it, which is a condition your Duc de Brévanne has described to you rather well, I understand.”
1813|Normal|Lolly sat back. “You’re going to tell me they’re strings.”
1814|Normal|“I am going to tell you something a little worse than that,” said Leonhard von Wittenberg apologetically. “May I use your notebook? I shall use a clean page.”
1815|Normal|He drew, very small, a dot. “A point particle,” he said. “It has no size and no internal structure and no way of being anything other than what it is. If you wish to explain why there are so many different particles, you must simply list them, and we have listed them, and the list is seventeen items long and nobody knows why it is that list rather than another.”
1816|Normal|Beneath the dot he drew a tiny closed loop. “Now suppose instead that the fundamental object is not a point but a small loop, of a length so short that every experiment ever performed sees it as a point. A loop can do something a point cannot.” He looked up. “It can vibrate.”
1817|Normal|“Different vibrations, different particles,” said Lolly.
1818|Normal|“Different vibrations, different particles. Not seventeen unrelated facts. One object, seventeen notes.” He said it without any drama whatever, which somehow made it land harder. “A violin string is not seventeen instruments. It is one string, played differently.”
1819|Normal|Lolly looked at the loop for a while. “And the electron and the quark and the photon—”
1820|Normal|“Notes.”
1821|Normal|“And gravity?”
1822|Normal|“Ah,” said von Wittenberg, and for the first time something in his face brightened. “That is the part that persuaded me. You must understand — for fifty years the great scandal of physics was that our theory of the very small and our theory of gravity would not sit in the same room. Put them together and the answers came out infinite. Not large. Infinite, which is nature’s way of saying that the question was malformed. Every attempt failed.”
1823|Normal|He tapped the little loop. “But if the objects are loops rather than points, the infinities do not arise. And more than that — among the notes the loop can play, one of them, unavoidably, is a massless spin-two particle.”
1824|Normal|“Which is?”
1825|Normal|“The graviton,” said von Wittenberg. “You do not put gravity in. You cannot keep it out. It is simply one of the things the string does.” He paused. “I have never got over that. I am seventy-four and I have not got over it.”
1826|Normal|Lolly realised she had stopped taking notes and started again. “Where’s the catch.”
1827|Normal|“There are three, and I shall not hide them.” Von Wittenberg counted them off on his fingers, mildly. “First: the strings are so small that no accelerator we could build on this planet or in this solar system could see one directly, and so there has never been a direct experimental test of it. Not one. Dr Fainrose is entirely right to say so and she says it to me every time we meet.”
1828|Normal|“Second?”
1829|Normal|“The mathematics does not work in four dimensions. It requires ten.” He said this the way another man might mention that a recipe requires buttermilk. “Nine of space, one of time. And since we plainly experience three of space, the other six must be curled up small — at every point in what you think of as empty room there are six additional directions, wound so tightly that nothing you or I can do will ever push anything along them.”
1830|Normal|Lolly looked out of the window at the carpet showroom. “That is a great deal to ask of a car park,” she said.
1831|Normal|Somewhere outside, a car alarm went off, was silenced, and was not explained. Lolly glanced at her watch without meaning to — a new habit, three days old, that she had already stopped being able to switch off — and found that two hours had gone since she had sat down, with 4C still losing mass somewhere behind all of this.
1832|Normal|“It is, yes.” Von Wittenberg looked pleased. “And the third catch is the one that mattered most, and it is the reason I am in a library in Sub-District 6 rather than at home. For a long time there were five different string theories. All consistent. All beautiful. All slightly different. Which was, you understand, an embarrassment — one had set out to explain why there is only one world and had produced five candidates and a great deal of silence.”
1833|Normal|“So what happened.”
1834|Normal|“In 1995,” said Leonhard von Wittenberg, “I gave a talk.” He said this with the caution of a man aware that institutions have been damaged by less. He said it so quietly and so flatly that Lolly nearly missed the size of it.
1835|Normal|“The five theories were not five theories,” said von Wittenberg. “They were five descriptions of one structure, related by dualities — the same thing seen from different sides. Assembled properly, they did not fit in ten dimensions. They fitted in eleven, and the objects were not only strings but membranes.”
1836|Normal|“And that’s the theory.”
1837|Normal|“That is the theory, and it has no proper formulation yet, and so it has no proper name. I called it M-theory.”
1838|Normal|“What does the M stand for.”
1839|Normal|“Membrane,” said von Wittenberg. “Or mystery. Or magic. According to taste. I have never settled it, and I have come to think the vagueness was accidentally honest.”
1840|Normal|Lolly closed the notebook. “Professor von Wittenberg,” she said. “I will write down anything that tells me what they dumped into 4C. I will not take dictation on eleven dimensions until then. I have to say this. In three days I have been told by an extremely old vampire that nothing is definite until agreement spreads. I have been told by a French duke that everything is definite and steered by a wave over all the alternatives. I have been told by a Swede that every alternative actually happens somewhere. I have been told by a dead man from Belfast to stop picking and go and measure something. And you have now told me that everything is made of eleven-dimensional membranes that nobody can see and no experiment can reach.” She looked at the little loop on the page. “I have a woman in this district who was stopped in the middle of remembering her sister’s name. What am I supposed to do with strings?”
1841|Normal|Von Wittenberg did not take offence. He seemed, if anything, to have been waiting for it. “That is the right question and I shall answer it in the correct order,” he said. “First: nothing. You are supposed to do nothing with strings. The Bell measurement is what matters this week and I would not have you divert a single cable from it. Anyone who tells you that eleven dimensions will help you get an injunction is lying to you.”
1842|Normal|“All right.”
1843|Normal|“Second: I did not come here to tell you what everything is made of. Dr Bellew would rightly say that I cannot demonstrate it, and I cannot.” He shifted the three sheets of paper he had brought and turned one round. “I came because of your black hole.”
1844|Normal|Lolly looked down at the sheet. It was covered in an extremely dense and entirely orderly hand, and among the symbols she could make out almost nothing, but at the top, underlined once, was written: 4C — HORIZON ENTROPY — WHERE IS THE LEDGER
1845|Normal|Her mouth went dry. “Keinschein,” she said.
1846|Normal|“Alfred telephoned me on Tuesday and shouted at me for eleven minutes, which at his age is a considerable investment and, I think, a form of affection.” Von Wittenberg’s mouth twitched. “His concern, as I understand it, is that your Ministry has used a small engineered black hole as a disposal channel for suppressed alternatives, and that the object is now evaporating, and that when it has gone there will be no record of what was destroyed. He calls this the falsification of the ledger. He is frightened. I have known him ninety years and I have not heard him frightened before.”
1847|Normal|“And you can tell him it’s all right?”
1848|Normal|“No,” said von Wittenberg. “But I can tell you why he may be wrong, and it is the one thing my subject has actually achieved.” He turned the sheet over. “A black hole has an entropy,” he said. “Bekenstein saw it and Hawking made it precise: the entropy is proportional to the area of the horizon. Not to the volume inside. To the area of the surface.”
1849|Normal|He let that sit for a moment. “That was extremely strange, and for twenty years nobody could say what it was counting. Entropy counts states. Which states? Of what? The black hole was supposed to have almost no properties at all.”
1850|Normal|“And?”
1851|Normal|“In 1996 two colleagues took a particular class of black hole in string theory, built it out of membranes and strings, counted the possible configurations of those objects — an ordinary counting problem, tedious but honest — and found that the number of states gave exactly the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy. Exactly. With the correct factor. No adjustment.” He said it very quietly indeed. “That is the first time anyone counted the microscopic states of a black hole and got the entropy formula back exactly, and it came out of strings, and I do not think it was luck.”
1852|Normal|Lolly said slowly, “So there are states. It isn’t featureless.”
1853|Normal|“It is not featureless. It has an enormous number of internal configurations, and the horizon area measures how many.”
1854|Normal|“Then when it evaporates—”
1855|Normal|“Then the question becomes whether the radiation carries them out,” said von Wittenberg. “The area law suggests something outrageous: that everything inside a region can be encoded on its boundary. The interior is not the fundamental description — the account is on the surface. There is a precise version in which gravity in a certain space is equivalent to a theory without gravity on its edge. Two descriptions. Same physics.”
1856|Normal|Lolly sat completely still. “Then the ledger isn’t inside it,” she said.
1857|Normal|“That is what I came to say,” said von Wittenberg. “The record of what fell in — of what they dumped — isn’t in the interior waiting to be annihilated when the interior stops existing. It’s on the horizon. It’s in the surface.”
1858|Normal|She was writing now, fast and badly. “And the horizon is exactly what the radiation comes out of.”
1859|Normal|“Yes,” said Leonhard von Wittenberg. “And so the current view — not proven, not settled, but the view — is that evaporation does not destroy the information. It is scrambled almost beyond recovery, it emerges in correlations so subtle that no practical measurement could unpick them, but it is not annihilated. Unitarity survives. The books balance.”
1860|Normal|He put the sheet down. “Tell Alfred that the ledger is not kept in the vault. It is kept on the wall.”
1861|Normal|Lolly looked up. “Do you believe it?”
1862|Normal|“I believe it in the way de Brévanne believes his wave,” said von Wittenberg. “Which is to say: I have thought about it for decades, the mathematics is far too good to be an accident, and I cannot show it to you. Dr Bellew would give me no credit at all and he would be right to.”
1863|Normal|He gathered his papers. “This is the difficulty with being in my line of work. We have produced, I think, the most beautiful structure in the history of physics, and we have not produced a single number a committee could check. Your Bell test will produce one in two days. I am aware which of us is more useful this week.”
1864|Normal|He stood, and paused. “May I say one more thing. It is not physics.”
1865|Normal|“Please.”
1866|Normal|“You told Dr Bellew you were a physicist for eleven minutes, once.” Von Wittenberg looked faintly embarrassed. “I was a history major. I intended to be a journalist. I worked on a political campaign. I came to physics late and by an unusual road, as did de Brévanne, as did — in his fashion — Alfred, who came by no road at all.”
1867|Normal|He picked up his pencil. “I mention it only because I notice that everybody who has come to talk to you this week arrived from somewhere else, and I do not think that is a coincidence, and I do not think it is a compliment either. It is simply that the people who ask what a thing is are usually people who were not trained to take it for granted.”
1868|Normal|He went back to his table, and to his papers, and did not speak again for the rest of the afternoon.
1869|Normal|At half past six Lolly walked back to the school hall through streets that were still visibly deciding things, and found Fainrose sitting on a crate eating a sandwich beside a rack of timing electronics.
1870|Normal|“You’ve been with von Wittenberg,” she said. “You’ve got the expression. Everyone gets the expression.”
1871|Normal|“He says everything is eleven-dimensional membranes.”
1872|Normal|“Yes.”
1873|Normal|“And that the record of everything the Ministry dumped into 4C is written on the horizon rather than inside it, so it can’t be destroyed by evaporation.”
1874|Normal|Fainrose stopped chewing. “Say that again,” she said.
1875|Normal|Lolly opened the notebook and showed her the page. She read it, twice, and then said something Lolly had not expected at all. “That’s not a defence,” she said. “That’s a deadline, which is the one thing this building can be relied upon to understand.”
1876|Normal|“How?”
1877|Normal|“Because if the information is on the horizon, then it comes out in the radiation, in the correlations, over the entire remaining lifetime of the object.” She was already up, sandwich abandoned. “Which means there is a window. And the window closes when 4C does. And the smaller it gets, the hotter and faster it goes.” She looked at her. “Lolly. How long have we been treating the evaporation as the catastrophe?”
1878|Normal|“Three days.”
1879|Normal|“It isn’t the catastrophe,” said Stephanie Fainrose. “It’s the disclosure. The thing is telling us everything they put into it, in the most scrambled possible handwriting, and it will stop when it has finished burning.”
1880|Normal|Lolly sat down slowly on the crate.
1881|Normal|Then she opened the notebook and wrote:
1882|Normal|One object. Many notes. Five theories that were one theory seen from five sides.
1883|Normal|Then:
1884|Normal|The entropy goes as the area, not the volume. The account is kept on the surface.
1885|Normal|And beneath it, underlined twice:
1886|Normal|The evaporation is not the destruction of the record. It is the reading of it, aloud, once, at increasing speed, and nobody has been listening.
1887|Normal|The first detector clicked.
1888|Normal|Then the second.
1889|Normal|Then all four together.
1890|Normal|Fainrose stopped writing. “That’s not a signal.”
1891|Normal|“What is it?” Lolly asked.
1892|Normal|She looked at the timing trace. “It’s the apparatus measuring itself.”
1893|Normal|Rule: One object, many notes — and still no certificate that restores a sister.
