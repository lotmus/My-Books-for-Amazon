1445|Heading 1|Chapter 10: The Moon That Was Pending
1446|Normal|The complaint came in from Outer Fenwick at half past seven in the evening, which was itself unusual, because Outer Fenwick did not officially exist until the moon came up, and the moon did not officially come up until enough people had filled in the form saying they had seen it.
1447|Normal|Lolly Wren read the complaint three times before she accepted that she had read it correctly.
1448|Normal|“Ratepayer states,” she said aloud, “that on the night of the fourteenth, insufficient observation returns were logged by nine p.m., and the moon”—she checked the wording again—“‘did not come up properly, and hung about at the edge looking unconvincing until gone eleven, at which point enough of the residents had done their forms and it committed.’”
1449|Normal|Beatrix Sloan did not look up from her boxes. “Committed to what?”
1450|Normal|“Being a moon, I think.”
1451|Normal|“That is not how moons work, Ms Wren.”
1452|Normal|“I am aware of that, Ms Sloan. I am reading you the complaint.”
1453|Normal|Stephanie Fainrose, who had not slept in a describable way for two days and had reached the particular plateau of tiredness at which she became lucid about everything except her own coat, which she had put on inside out, took the sheet from her.
1454|Normal|She read it once. She did not read it a second time, which Lolly had learned to find ominous.
1455|Normal|“Outer Fenwick,” she said. “Get the car.”
1456|Normal|The road out to Outer Fenwick ran along the ridge above Sub-District 6. At a quarter to nine, with the sky doing something tentative and unfinished in the east, Lolly first understood the shape of what they were driving towards. People sat on their front steps. Not many—eleven, perhaps fourteen, scattered along half a mile of terraced housing—each with a folding chair, a flask, and a clipboard, all facing the same direction with the settled patience of racegoers told that the principal attraction was running late.
1457|Normal|The far side of the ridge fell away towards Sub-District 6, which lay under a continuity freeze — Vale’s order, to hold the local grid steady while 4C was argued about elsewhere. It showed from up here as a grid of windows all lit the same flat yellow, not one of them changing. The people on the steps had their backs to it.
1458|Normal|“What are they doing?” she said.
1459|Normal|“Observing,” said Fainrose. “Or preparing to. Look at the forms.”
1460|Normal|She looked. Each clipboard held a single sheet headed OUTER FENWICK NOCTURNAL AMENITY — CONFIRMATION OF SIGHTING, with boxes for name, address, and time, and a tick beside the words I confirm that I have observed the lunar body described in Schedule 4 and consider it to be present.
1461|Normal|“Schedule 4.”
1462|Normal|“Don’t,” said Fainrose, in the tone of a woman reserving strength for worse news later.
1463|Normal|Jago Flint, driving, took the last bend onto the ridge somewhat faster than the road recommended and said, without turning round, “I’ve heard of this. Didn’t credit it. There’s a district over Coldharrow way that does the same with its high-street lamp—the one by the butcher’s. Goes out on cloudy nights if nobody’s looked at it by six. Everyone assumed the wiring was at fault.”
1464|Normal|“It is not a fault in the wiring,” said Fainrose.
1465|Normal|They parked outside a house with a hand-lettered sign in the window—FENWICK RESIDENTS’ MOON WATCH, EST. FOURTEEN MONTHS AGO—and a woman of about sixty came out to meet them before they had properly got out of the car. She wiped her hands on an apron which suggested that she had left something on a stove from sheer bureaucratic duty.
1466|Normal|“You’ll be from the Ministry,” she said, without much enthusiasm. “I’m Mrs Prosser. I do the rota.”
1467|Normal|“The rota for what, precisely?” said Fainrose.
1468|Normal|“The moon.” Mrs Prosser said it as one might say the bins: a fact of civic life requiring management rather than wonder. “It came in with the Continuity Review, love. Fourteen months back. Somebody at the Ministry decided Outer Fenwick’s night sky was an ‘unresolved amenity pending classical adequacy determination’—those are the exact words; I kept the letter—and until it was resolved, resolving it required sufficient corroborated observation from the resident population. So. Rota.”
1469|Normal|Lolly had the green notebook marked PRIVATE/UNHELPFUL out already. “How many observers constitute sufficient?”
1470|Normal|“Forty-one,” said Mrs Prosser, with the flat authority of a woman who had fought this battle with a spreadsheet and won. “It was thirty-eight for the first six months. Then they moved the district boundary, picked up three more houses, and never revised the number back down. I have written to them nine times about it, and I would like it on record that I have written to them nine times.”
1471|Normal|“And on the night it dips below forty-one?”
1472|Normal|“It gets vague,” said Mrs Prosser. “Not gone. Vague. You get a sort of grey smudge where it ought to be, like a photograph somebody’s thumb was over half of. Cats won’t sit by the window, and my Reginald says his knee plays up, though Reginald’s knee plays up regardless and I don’t put that one on the Ministry. I have standards.”
1473|Normal|Fainrose had gone very still, in the specific way Lolly now recognised as her thinking so hard that stillness was the only way to contain it.
1474|Normal|“Mrs Prosser,” she said. “On the night the complaint describes, talk me through it minute by minute.”
1475|Normal|Mrs Prosser was, it turned out, exactly the witness a desperate physicist could have ordered from a catalogue. She had logged everything. At 8:52 there had been thirty-four confirmed observation returns: people on steps, forms ticked, the usual quorum falling short by the usual margin, which normally resolved itself by nine as the last few latecomers emerged with tea. But on the fourteenth, seven regular observers had been at the church hall for the whist drive, and the moon—Mrs Prosser used the word with the weary precision of someone who had stopped finding it strange—had “sort of dithered.” It was visible from some houses and not from others. One household reported a crescent; the house two doors along, looking at the identical patch of sky, reported nothing at all, “which caused words, because the Hendersons have form for exaggerating and everyone said so to their faces.”
1476|Normal|“And then,” said Mrs Prosser, “gone eleven, the whist drive let out, and everybody came home and had cocoa and looked up out of habit more than hope, and there it was. Properly there. Committed, like the letter said.”
1477|Normal|“Like the complaint said,” Lolly corrected gently.
1478|Normal|“Same thing round here,” said Mrs Prosser.
1479|Normal|Fainrose walked to the pavement’s edge and looked up at the sky. It was presently offering a perfectly ordinary, extremely convincing gibbous moon, hanging in high cloud with the smug indifference of an object that had never once been asked to complete a declaration about itself.
1480|Normal|“It is not that observation makes it real,” she said, half to herself and half to Lolly. She had learned that this half-address was the closest thing to a lecture she was going to get, and had trained herself to write quickly. “That is the vulgar version, and it is wrong, and it is exactly the version this Ministry has legislated into a rota.”
1481|Normal|“Then what is happening?”
1482|Normal|Fainrose looked at the moon for another moment. “A moon does not require forty-one people with clipboards. The world is already in contact with it. That is enough to keep it ordinary.”
1483|Normal|“Then why did it dither?”
1484|Normal|“Because someone built a filter,” said Fainrose. Now she turned to look at her, and there was something close to fury in it, banked low. “Somewhere in that Continuity Review paperwork is a device—probably a receiver mast, something modest and municipal, put up ‘to improve local signal’—coupled to the observation quorum. It is interfering with the local conditions that keep the moon settled, then letting them return only when the paperwork says the humans have earned a definite sky.”
1485|Normal|Lolly looked from her to Mrs Prosser to the moon and felt the particular vertigo she had come, over the past few days, to recognise as understanding arriving slightly ahead of comfort.
1486|Normal|“They’re not making it real by looking,” she said slowly. “They have broken the thing that lets it be real without looking, and then charged the residents to fix it. With a rota.”
1487|Normal|“With a rota,” Fainrose agreed, “and quorum thresholds, and presumably a line item.”
1488|Normal|“Why?”
1489|Normal|“Because a moon requiring forty-one confirmed observations to commit is a moon the district can be billed for,” said Beatrix Sloan, who had got out of the car at some point during this and had already found, unerringly, the one sentence in the whole business that was actually about money. “Ongoing amenity-resolution services. I should like to see the invoice.”
1490|Normal|Mrs Prosser brightened for the first time since they had arrived. “There is an invoice,” she said. “I kept that too.”
1491|Normal|On the drive back that night, Lolly opened the green notebook.
1492|Normal|She wrote:
1493|Normal|Nobody has to look at the moon for it to stay a moon. The air is looking at it constantly, on nobody’s authority, and that is a mercy, not a technicality.
1494|Normal|Then, underneath:
1495|Normal|They found a machine that quietened the mercy, called the silence a shortfall, and billed the street to fix what they had broken.
1496|Normal|And at the bottom, in the smaller hand she used for the sentences she did not entirely want to admit to writing:
1497|Normal|The moon does not need forty-one witnesses. It needs the sky to be allowed to keep noticing it. Most things do.
1498|Normal|Rule: The moon stays definite because the whole sky keeps physically recording it, not because forty-one signatures have been collected.
