1967|Heading 1|Chapter 16: The Decision
1968|Normal|The written order was sealed on the Tuesday, once the plan the Board had demanded on the Monday — as the price of losing the hearing — was on the judge’s desk. He understood none of the physics and all of the paperwork, which turned out to be sufficient. Beatrix Sloan had not argued that reality had been damaged. She had learned, by then, that courts do not adjudicate ontology. She had argued instead that the Ministry had certified nine thousand separate local determinations while holding, in its own filing system, a measurement establishing that they were one act — and that a body which certifies nine thousand times what it knows to be one thing has not made an error of physics but a false instrument in a live system. Which is a thing the law has known how to dislike since the fourteenth century, as the Duc de Brévanne observed with some satisfaction from the public gallery.
1969|Normal|They got eleven weeks. It was not enough, and nobody had prepared a form in which enough was an available option. Von Wittenberg’s people listened to the horizon for nine of them, from a rented industrial unit outside Sub-District 6, with equipment that cost more than the district’s entire housing stock and had, for that reason, a more generous maintenance schedule, and that Fainrose described, in her only published remark on the matter, as “an ear held against a burning wall.”
1970|Normal|Of the four hundred and eleven P-9 states, they recovered three hundred and eight. It was not a triumph and Fainrose refused, in every interview afterwards, to let it be described as one. They were pending human states, unscrambled from the radiation and returned to the districts they had come out of: a child’s route to school, a marriage in the middle of being decided, an apprenticeship, two reconciliations, a great many small unfinished intentions of no civic significance whatever.
1971|Normal|One hundred and three were not recovered, and their names were published, because Beatrix insisted, and because Miss Dorothy Kell — whose brother’s apprenticeship was one of the hundred and three, and who had written to the inquiry every week since the freeze — said that a list of the unrecovered was the only memorial such things could have.
1972|Normal|By the second week of November the operation had stopped resembling physics and had started resembling a hospital corridor: mostly waiting, occasionally interrupted by everyone in the room forgetting, briefly and in unison, how to breathe.
1973|Normal|Von Wittenberg’s team worked in shifts around a horizon that would not hold still. Numbers came back out of the radiation in fragments, the way a memory comes back to someone recovering from a fever — a name, a colour, a smell of toast, and then nothing for six hours. Fainrose stopped sleeping in any sense that would have satisfied an inquiry. Beatrix filed three separate emergency applications and lost none of them, which she treated, privately, as evidence that the universe had finally noticed her paperwork.
1974|Normal|It was Jago Flint who noticed the other thing.
1975|Normal|He had been carrying courier bags in and out of the annex for eleven weeks without once being asked to sit down, which meant he had spent eleven weeks looking at the coupling lattice the way nobody paid to understand it ever did — not as a diagram of what it was for, but as a knot, the way he had once heard about a street lamp over Coldharrow way that everyone blamed on faulty wiring, and known, without ever going to look, that the wiring was never the actual problem. He wondered, the way anyone does with a knot, what would happen if you simply let it go.
1976|Normal|“You could cut it,” he said, on the night of the thirteenth, to a room that had not asked him.
1977|Normal|Fainrose did not look up from the trace. “It’s live.”
1978|Normal|“So’s the bit you’re listening to. You’re not proposing to stop that.” Jago shrugged, the particular shrug of a man who delivers difficult envelopes for a living and has made his peace with difficult envelopes. “I’m saying: you’ve got what you’re going to get. The coupling’s not bringing anything more back. It’s just keeping the door open for whatever’s still in there to come out the wrong way.”
1979|Normal|Nobody objected, which in a Ministry annex at one in the morning was the closest thing to unanimous consent that existed.
1980|Normal|Gideon Wigglesworth, who had spent the last two hours failing to locate a plug socket that was, by his own later admission, directly behind him, straightened up with the abrupt clarity of a man who has just understood something he wasn’t trying to understand. “It’s not a valve,” he said. “Everyone’s been treating it like a valve. It’s a — ” He stopped, visibly hunting for the word, found it, looked faintly betrayed by how obvious it now seemed. “It’s a bridge. You don’t turn a bridge off. You take it down.”
1981|Normal|Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. “That is either the most useful thing anyone has said in this room in eleven weeks,” she said, “or you have been standing near a filing cabinet radiating something we should be measuring.”
1982|Normal|“Possibly both,” said Gideon, and walked, with some dignity, directly into the filing cabinet.
1983|Normal|They cut the coupling at 06:04 on the fourteenth of November. It was not dramatic to watch — a technician in a borrowed coat opened a junction box that had not been opened since the annex was built, located the correct relay by the light of a torch held in her teeth, and removed it, the way one removes a fuse. The junction box itself had been found, three days earlier, by Gideon Wigglesworth, who had gone through nineteen requisition ledgers by hand and got it right on the first try, a fact he mentioned only once and only when nobody appeared to be listening. For eleven seconds nothing happened, which was the longest eleven seconds of Lolly’s professional life, four hundred and eleven pending states and one entire district balanced on a torch battery and a woman’s steady hand.
1984|Normal|Even Beatrix Sloan, who had spent eleven weeks being audibly certain about everything, said nothing at all for the whole eleven seconds, and did not reach for her pen until it was over, which Lolly understood to be the nearest thing to fear that Beatrix Sloan permitted herself in public.
1985|Normal|Then the coherence monitor Fainrose had left running since Sub-District 6 — flat at 1.2 for eleven weeks, with the patience of something that had given up being interesting — climbed to 2.79 in slightly under forty minutes, and stopped.
1986|Normal|Fainrose read it twice. “That’s disconnection,” she said, in the voice of someone reporting a result she had stopped allowing herself to expect. “Clean disconnection. The number climbs because the houses can finally hold together with one another again — they had been spending all of it on the anchor, and the anchor has let go. It’s not listening to us any more. It’s just — a black hole. An ordinary, boring, evaporating black hole, doing the only thing black holes are supposed to do, which is leave.”
1987|Normal|Nobody cheered. Lolly noted, and later wrote in the green notebook, that this seemed correct: there is no ceremony appropriate to successfully stopping a government department from continuing to be haunted.
1988|Normal|Rule: Close the door if you must; the bill was never in the cupboard, and never will be.
