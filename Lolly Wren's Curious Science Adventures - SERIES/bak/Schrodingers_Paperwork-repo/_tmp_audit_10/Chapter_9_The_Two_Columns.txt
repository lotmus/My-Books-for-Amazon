1312|Heading 1|Chapter 9: The Two Columns
1313|Normal|At eight she had been downstairs. The requisition archive occupied a basement room designed for files and since surrendered to them. Gideon sat at the central table with his coat still on, because the heating in the annex had an interpretive relationship with winter, and a stack of ledgers arranged in a kind of apologetic order.
1314|Normal|“You look as if you have been here all night,” Lolly said.
1315|Normal|“I have been here all night,” said Gideon. “The distinction is one I have been meaning to mention to someone.” He took the tea she had brought, missed the saucer by the usual margin, and recovered both without spilling, which she chose not to comment on. “Vale signed for the vault isolation. Voller countersigned. The second page of 11-C is in a different folder, in a different year, and nobody who signed the first page appears to have seen it.”
1316|Normal|“That is going to matter.”
1317|Normal|“Yes,” he said. “I thought I’d mention it before I walked into anything.”
1318|Normal|She looked at him. He looked at the ledger. The heating made a decision and reversed it.
1319|Normal|“You’re doing the useful part,” she said.
1320|Normal|“I’m reading other people’s handwriting. That’s not the useful part. That’s the part I can do without breaking the building.” He turned a page. “Go upstairs. Something on paper is coming. Paper is never a social call.”
1321|Normal|She went. At six minutes past nine, he was proved right.
1322|Normal|The order came down on paper, which was itself a bad sign.
1323|Normal|Ministries that intend to be reasonable send email. Email can be amended, forwarded, or quietly forgotten. Paper is sent when someone senior wishes there to be an object later.
1324|Normal|Beatrix Sloan read it twice at the annex reception desk, then carried it to the long table in the observation room and put it down in front of Stephanie Fainrose without comment.
1325|Normal|Fainrose read it. Then she laughed: one short, unlovely sound, like a chair leg on tile.
1326|Normal|“Oh, they’ve done it,” she said. “They’ve actually legislated it.”
1327|Normal|Lolly Wren leaned over. The document ran to a page and a half and had passed through at least four hands, each of which had made it worse.
1328|Normal|DIRECTIVE 11-C: CIVIC RECOVERY ASSURANCE
1329|Normal|In order that public confidence be maintained during the current period of adjustment, all recovery operations shall be conducted so as to satisfy both of the following conditions simultaneously:
1330|List Paragraph|The precise present configuration of each affected dwelling, record, and person shall be established and certified to district level, such that no citizen enquiry may receive an indeterminate response.
1331|List Paragraph|The precise rate and direction of ongoing change in each affected dwelling, record, and person shall be established and certified to district level, such that all future states may be projected and no citizen enquiry may receive an unforecastable response.
1332|Normal|Recovery may not proceed until both certificates are held.
1333|Normal|Signed, on behalf of the Board, C. Vale
1334|Normal|Lolly read it again.
1335|Normal|“Both,” she said.
1336|Normal|“Both,” said Fainrose. “To arbitrary precision. Simultaneously. With certificates.”
1337|Normal|“That’s—”
1338|Normal|“Yes.”
1339|Normal|“That’s not a difficult instruction.”
1340|Normal|“No,” said Fainrose. “A difficult instruction is one you fail. This is an instruction you cannot begin.” She set the paper down very precisely, squaring it with the edge of the table, which Lolly had learned was what she did instead of throwing things. “Somebody has looked at physics, disliked the terms, and issued a rebuttal.”
1341|Normal|Jago Flint, who had spent the night in a chair and looked entirely unaffected by it in a way Lolly found personally offensive, said, “Is it truly impossible, or merely very hard? I ask because I have made a living in the distinction.”
1342|Normal|“There is no distinction.”
1343|Normal|“There is always a distinction.”
1344|Normal|“Not here.” Fainrose pulled a blackboard on castors round from the wall. Something in the room’s temperature changed; even Voller, hovering by the door, stopped pretending to consult his clipboard. “Mr Flint. If I insist on knowing exactly where a thing is, what have I done?”
1345|Normal|Jago considered. “Pinned it.”
1346|Normal|“Yes. And if I ask, with equal insistence, exactly where it is going?”
1347|Normal|“You have asked for a different sort of answer.”
1348|Normal|“One which the first answer has made worse.” She put down the chalk. “They are not two independent facts that a sufficiently determined civil servant can requisition. The more tightly one is fixed, the less honestly the other can be certified. There is no apparatus, budget, or signature that alters the bargain.”
1349|Normal|Mrs Elspeth Chain, who had appointed herself to the tea and to the moral summary, said, “And they have ordered both.”
1350|Normal|“To district level.”
1351|Normal|“With certificates.”
1352|Normal|Mrs Chain set down the pot. “Then they have ordered the tide.”
1353|Normal|“Considerably worse,” said Fainrose. “The tide would refuse and continue being the tide. A civil service does not refuse. It complies.”
1354|Normal|Lolly felt the floor of the argument give slightly beneath her.
1355|Normal|“What does compliance look like,” she said, “when the thing is impossible?”
1356|Normal|Fainrose looked at her for a long moment.
1357|Normal|“That,” she said, “is the only question in the directive.”
1358|Normal|The answer arrived at ten forty in the form of a young woman from Certification named Miss Adaeze Pike, who had a laptop, a lanyard, and the specific brightness of a person doing well within a system she had not yet examined.
1359|Normal|She was, Lolly thought, entirely blameless, and she disliked what was about to happen to her.
1360|Normal|“We’ve had 11-C too,” she said cheerfully, setting up at the end of the table. “Field Certification. We’ve been running since seven.”
1361|Normal|Beatrix’s head came up. “Running what?”
1362|Normal|“Both columns.” Miss Pike turned the laptop so that they could see. “Column A is present configuration. Column B is rate of change. Every affected address gets a line.”
1363|Normal|The spreadsheet was beautiful. Lolly had spent long enough in electronics to recognise a well-built sheet: conditional formatting, locked headers, validation on every field. Somebody had cared about this, and she felt, for one traitorous moment, the old satisfaction of a well-soldered joint — a thing built so it would not fail you, which she had not let herself miss in years.
1364|Normal|Every single row was filled.
1365|Normal|“How,” said Fainrose.
1366|Normal|Miss Pike blinked. “How what, sorry?”
1367|Normal|“How did you fill Column B?”
1368|Normal|“Oh—from the survey,” said Miss Pike. “The Continuity Review data. We take the certified present configuration, compare it to the previous certified present configuration, divide by the interval. That gives rate of change.” She smiled, pleased with the tidiness of it. “It’s rather elegant, actually. The same measurement serves both columns.”
1369|Normal|There was a pause of perhaps three seconds.
1370|Normal|Then Fainrose said, very gently, “Miss Pike, would you sit down?”
1371|Normal|Miss Pike sat down, because that was the sort of voice it had been.
1372|Normal|“When you certified the present configuration,” said Fainrose, “what did the surveyor do to the dwelling?”
1373|Normal|“Scanned it. Object by object. It’s a full-resolution pass, so every element is fixed to certified state. That’s what makes Column A reliable.”
1374|Normal|“Fixed.”
1375|Normal|“Yes.”
1376|Normal|“And then you scanned it again a week later, compared the results, divided the difference by seven days, and called the result the rate of change of the house.”
1377|Normal|“Exactly.”
1378|Normal|“Miss Pike,” said Fainrose, “you have not measured the rate of change of the house. You have measured the rate at which your own instrument disturbed it.”
1379|Normal|Miss Pike’s expression did not change immediately. Lolly watched it fail to change, and then watched it change.
1380|Normal|“No,” she said. “No, because the disturbance is calibrated out. There’s a correction factor. Annex Four.”
1381|Normal|“Show me Annex Four.”
1382|Normal|She showed her. Fainrose read it in about eleven seconds, and Lolly, who had developed a reasonable ear for her silences, understood that the eleven seconds were courtesy.
1383|Normal|“This factor,” said Fainrose, “was derived by comparing scan results against a reference configuration.”
1384|Normal|“Yes.”
1385|Normal|“Held in the cold vault.”
1386|Normal|Miss Pike’s fingers stopped on the trackpad.
1387|Normal|“The vault’s been sealed since last night,” she said. “There’s a note. I assumed it was procedural.”
1388|Normal|“It was me,” said Beatrix.
1389|Normal|“Why?”
1390|Normal|“Because it is a photograph of a conversation,” said Beatrix, “and I did not intend to let anyone restore the country from it.”
1391|Normal|Miss Pike looked at the spreadsheet. She looked at it for rather a long time. Lolly, who had spent ten years watching people discover that the ground was not where the drawing said it was, recognised every stage of it and could not think of a single kindness to offer.
1392|Normal|“Then Column B is—” She stopped and started again, and got it out properly the second time, which she respected. “Then Column B is the instrument looking at itself.”
1393|Normal|“Yes,” said Fainrose.
1394|Normal|“Nine thousand rows.”
1395|Normal|“Yes.”
1396|Normal|“And Column A is real.”
1397|Normal|“Column A is an answer,” said Fainrose. “It is real in the way a verdict is real. It became true when you asked.”
1398|Normal|Miss Pike put both hands flat on the table.
1399|Normal|“So the more precisely we certified where things were,” she said slowly, “the more nonsense we generated about where they were going.”
1400|Normal|“Yes.”
1401|Normal|“And the directive requires both.”
1402|Normal|“Yes.”
1403|Normal|“So the harder we comply, the worse it gets.”
1404|Normal|“Now,” said Fainrose, “you have understood 11-C better than the man who signed it.”
1405|Normal|It was Mrs Chain who asked the question that mattered, because it was always Mrs Chain who asked the question that mattered. “What has it done to the people?”
1406|Normal|Nobody answered.
1407|Normal|“Dr Fainrose. Nine thousand rows. They are addresses. Somebody lives at each of them.” She folded her hands. “What has it done to them?”
1408|Normal|Fainrose crossed to the main display, pulled up the domestic event stream, and filtered it. Lolly saw her hesitate before she let it render: the first time she had seen her flinch from her own data.
1409|Normal|The pattern was unmistakable once you knew to look for it.
1410|Normal|Every dwelling certified to the highest precision in Column A showed, in the days following, the wildest instability in every other respect. Routes to the shops changed on alternate mornings. Correspondence arrived addressed to residents who were and were not the residents. Two households’ front doors had, for eleven hours, both opened onto the same hallway, an incident logged as shared vestibule, low priority. A man in Sub-District 9 had been certified so exactly that his own employment record could no longer predict where he would be at nine in the morning, and had accordingly been marked unreliable.
1411|Normal|“They pinned them,” said Lolly.
1412|Normal|“They pinned them,” said Fainrose, “and then wrote down that they were erratic. The more certain the pin, the more erratic the record; the more erratic the record, the stronger the case for a further pin.” She turned. “It is a machine for manufacturing exactly the instability it was built to remove, and it will run as long as it is funded, because every cycle produces evidence that it is needed.”
1413|Normal|Jago exhaled. “That is the most administratively perfect thing I have ever heard.”
1414|Normal|“It is not a flaw in the Initiative,” said Fainrose. “It is the Initiative. Vale does not need reality to be simple. He needs it to be reportably simple. Those are different projects. Only one is achievable, and it is achieved by insisting on two certificates that cannot both be honest.”
1415|Normal|Beatrix had gone very quiet, which in her case meant she had stopped being furious and started constructing.
1416|Normal|“Miss Pike,” she said.
1417|Normal|Miss Pike looked up.
1418|Normal|“Column B is fabricated.”
1419|Normal|“Not deliberately—”
1420|Normal|“I did not say fraudulently. I said fabricated. Do you dispute it?”
1421|Normal|A long pause. Then, to her lasting credit: “No. I don’t.”
1422|Normal|“Will you say so in writing?”
1423|Normal|Miss Pike looked at the spreadsheet—nine thousand rows, locked headers, conditional formatting, four weeks of good work in service of an impossibility—and Lolly watched her decide. Whatever else came of this appalling week, she thought, that decision had been worth witnessing.
1424|Normal|“Yes,” she said.
1425|Normal|“Then we have him,” said Beatrix. “Not for the black hole. Not for the houses. Nobody is ever hanged for a black hole.” She picked up the directive and held it out. “For requiring, in writing, over signature, a certification that cannot exist, and then acting on it. That is not a scientific dispute. That is a false instrument in a live system.”
1426|Normal|Fainrose looked at the paper with something like respect.
1427|Normal|“He wrote it down,” she said.
1428|Normal|“He wrote it down,” said Beatrix.
1429|Normal|Mrs Chain refilled the pot.
1430|Normal|“Men like that always do,” she said. “They cannot bear a thing to be true unless it has been copied twice and misfiled once.”
1431|Normal|Lolly opened the green notebook marked PRIVATE/UNHELPFUL.
1432|Normal|She wrote:
1433|Normal|You may know where it is. You may know where it is going. You may not have both, and the shortfall is not a shortage of effort.
1434|Normal|Then, below:
1435|Normal|They demanded both. So the second column had to be invented. And then they punished people for failing to match the invention.
1436|Normal|And at the bottom, smaller:
1437|Normal|The cruelty is not the uncertainty. The cruelty is the certificate.
1438|Normal|The two columns were meant to be reassuring. One recorded the state the Ministry had certified. The other recorded the state it intended to certify next. Lolly stared at them until she understood the trap: the system had no column for we don’t know. It had only two kinds of certainty, and both were now being fed back into the thing they were supposed to describe.
1439|Normal|“How long?” she asked.
1440|Normal|Fainrose looked at the clock. “Until it becomes self-consistent.”
1441|Normal|“And if it does?”
1442|Normal|She did not answer.
1443|Normal|Rule: Quantum mechanics permits a precise location or a precise motion, but not two limitless certificates at once.
