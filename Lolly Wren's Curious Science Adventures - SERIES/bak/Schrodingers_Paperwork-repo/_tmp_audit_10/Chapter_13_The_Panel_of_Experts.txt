1693|Heading 1|Chapter 13: The Panel of Experts
1694|Normal|The Board of Inquiry sat at eleven in the annex conference room, and its first act was to establish that it did not intend to decide anything.
1695|Normal|“We are not here to adjudicate physics,” said the chair, a Mr Pilbeam, who had been brought in from Transport on the grounds that he had once managed a bridge, although the bridge had not supplied a reference.
1696|Normal|“We are here to determine whether a recommendation exists. If the expert panel is unanimous, we may act. If it is divided, the matter is unresolved and current arrangements continue.”
1697|Normal|Beatrix Sloan, at the side table with eleven boxes and a fountain pen, wrote a single word on her pad. Lolly leaned over and read it. Divided.
1698|Normal|“They’ve designed it so that disagreement means Phase Two proceeds,” she said.
1699|Normal|“They have designed it,” said Beatrix, “so that nothing is ever anybody’s fault. It is the same instrument every time. Convene the ones who cannot agree and call the noise a lack of evidence.”
1700|Normal|Fainrose said, “Who did they convene.”
1701|Normal|Beatrix turned her pad round. Three names. Fainrose read them and put her face in her hands, briefly, which Lolly had not seen before.
1702|Normal|“Oh, that’s marvellous,” she said. “That’s genuinely inspired. They’ve picked three men who have spent their entire lives disagreeing about exactly this, and one of them is dead.”
1703|Normal|“Which one is dead,” said Lolly.
1704|Normal|“It has not noticeably impeded his work,” said Fainrose.
1705|Normal|They came in at ten past. The first was tall, hollow-cheeked, immaculately grey, with the posture of a man who had spent decades being the cleverest person in rooms that were themselves dangerous. He carried nothing at all — no papers, no case — and sat down at the far end with his hands folded, and Lolly noticed that he chose the seat furthest from the window.
1706|Normal|“Professor Werner Heitmann,” said Pilbeam. “Foundations.”
1707|Normal|“Formalism,” said Heitmann. “Not foundations. There is a difference and it is the entire difference.”
1708|Normal|The second arrived talking. He was younger by decades, sandy-haired, in a jacket over a T-shirt, and he came in mid-sentence with a tablet in one hand and a coffee in the other and an air of enormous delight at being alive in a universe that had turned out to be so interesting. “-which is the thing nobody wants to say out loud, that if the equations are the ontology then this whole crisis is a bookkeeping dispute — sorry, hello, Max Tegmark, cosmology, I’ve read the Fainrose paper four times, Dr Fainrose it’s an honour, your syndrome table is gorgeous, I think it’s solving a problem that doesn’t exist but it’s gorgeous.”
1709|Normal|Fainrose said, “Sit down, Professor Tegmark.”
1710|Normal|“Absolutely.”
1711|Normal|The third came in last and quietly, and the room’s temperature dropped about it. He was a smallish man with a red beard going white, in a jumper, with the specific bright unblinking gaze of somebody who has spent thirty years being polite to people he considers to have been sloppy. He had a Belfast voice, unhurried and precise, and he sat down without shaking hands with anyone.
1712|Normal|“Dr Jack Bellew,” said Pilbeam.
1713|Normal|“Aye,” said Bellew.
1714|Normal|“Dr Bellew, forgive me, our records indicate—” said Pilbeam.
1715|Normal|“They do,” said Bellew. “Shall we get on.”
1716|Normal|Nobody pursued it. Pilbeam distributed the question. It had been drafted by a committee and ran to a paragraph, but stripped of its clothing it asked this: does the Ministry’s programme of civic simplification cause damage to physical reality that is not captured by the loss of records?
1717|Normal|Heitmann answered first, and answered fast. “No,” he said. “And the question is malformed.”
1718|Normal|Pilbeam looked up hopefully. “You find no damage?”
1719|Normal|“I find no meaning.” Heitmann’s hands stayed folded. “The formalism relates preparations to observations. It tells you what your instruments will report. It says nothing about what the electron is doing when nobody asks, because that question generates no numbers.” He turned his head slightly. “Your survey teams asked; they received answers; the answers were correct. Unkindness is politics. A hidden state of the house is a fiction.”
1720|Normal|Lolly said, before she could stop herself, “The woman couldn’t finish her sister’s name.”
1721|Normal|Heitmann looked at her with no unkindness at all, which was worse. “Then she was harmed,” he said. “I do not dispute the harm. I dispute the ontology of the harm. She was harmed as a citizen, by a procedure, and you should prosecute the procedure. Do not ask me to certify that a wave function was injured. I have spent my life declining to say what the wave function is and I decline again this morning.”
1722|Normal|Tegmark had his hand half up like a schoolboy. “See, this is where I get off the bus,” he said. “Werner — may I — this is the thing. You say the question generates no numbers. But the equation generates numbers, and the equation has a mathematical structure, and my position, and I’ll be totally clear that it’s a minority position, is that the mathematical structure isn’t a description of reality, it is the reality. There’s nothing else. The universe is a mathematical object and we’re self-aware substructures inside it.”
1723|Normal|Pilbeam wrote this down, visibly hoping relevance might emerge if given enough ruled paper.
1724|Normal|“And so?” said Fainrose.
1725|Normal|“And so nothing was destroyed,” said Tegmark.
1726|Normal|The room went very quiet.
1727|Normal|“Say that again,” said Beatrix.
1728|Normal|“Nothing was destroyed.” He said it gently, and Lolly understood he was neither stupid nor cruel, which was worse. “If the equation never stops, and there’s no collapse, the universe didn’t pick one answer and delete the rest. It branched. Photograph and clock — both real, both with a Mrs Chain.” He spread his hands, trying to offer comfort. “The alternatives weren’t extinguished. They were decohered from. Everything this Ministry allegedly destroyed still exists. It’s just not here.”
1729|Normal|Mrs Elspeth Chain had come in at some point during this, with the umbrella, and had been standing at the back by the door. She said, “Then where is my sister.”
1730|Normal|Tegmark turned, and to his credit he did not flinch. “She’s in the branches where the record wasn’t overwritten,” he said. “Which is most of them, statistically—”
1731|Normal|“Where is she here.”
1732|Normal|“…Here, she’s gone. Yes.”
1733|Normal|“Then do not tell me nothing was destroyed,” said Mrs Chain, “because I am here, and I am the one who noticed.”
1734|Normal|There was a silence of some length.
1735|Normal|“That’s fair,” said Max Tegmark quietly. “That’s actually a fair objection and it’s the one I’ve never had a good answer to.”
1736|Normal|Beatrix Sloan was already writing, fast. “Chair,” she said. “I want it minuted that a member of the expert panel has advanced a position under which no act of the Ministry can constitute destruction of anything.”
1737|Normal|Pilbeam brightened. “Well, that would be—”
1738|Normal|“That would be the defence,” said Beatrix. “That is what they convened him for. Not to disagree with Professor Heitmann. To supply Vale’s successors with a physics under which the harm is relocated rather than caused. You will see it in the Board’s response within a fortnight and it will say: no alternative is ever lost, and therefore no citizen has suffered a loss, and therefore Phase Two may proceed as a matter of record-keeping.”
1739|Normal|Tegmark put his coffee down. “I’d hate that,” he said. “For the record — I’d hate that. It’s a real misuse. The multiverse doesn’t make cruelty free. My branch still has the crying person in it—”
1740|Normal|“It does not matter what you would hate,” said Beatrix. “It matters what you have said in a minuted proceeding.”
1741|Normal|“Yeah,” said Tegmark. “Yeah, I know. That’s — yeah.”
1742|Normal|And it was into this that Jack Bellew spoke for the second time. “Now that,” he said, “is the whole disease, and the two of you have just performed it for me, so I’m obliged.”
1743|Normal|Everyone looked at him. Bellew had not moved. He sat with his forearms on the table and his bright pale eyes going from Heitmann to Tegmark and back. “Werner says the question has no meaning. Max says the question has too many answers, all of them real. And between the pair of you there’s not one single thing a Board of Inquiry could go and check.” He tapped the table once, softly. “You’ve both got theories in which nobody can ever be wrong. I’ve spent my career objecting to that, and I’ll object to it in a car park in a suspended county if the room-booking requires it.”
1744|Normal|Heitmann said, “The formalism is confirmed to twelve decimal places.”
1745|Normal|“The formalism is grand,” said Bellew. “I’m talking about the word.” He looked round the table. “Measurement. The Ministry’s directives say it four hundred times. It is the most disastrous word in the vocabulary of physics, because it sounds like a special class of event the rest of nature is exempt from. There isn’t. A surveyor is a physical system. A form is a physical system. There is no magic in a clipboard.”
1746|Normal|Lolly said, “Then what did happen in Mrs Chain’s house.”
1747|Normal|And Bellew turned to her, and Lolly had the disconcerting experience of being properly looked at by somebody who had died before he had finished caring about this. “You’re the woman with the notebook.”
1748|Normal|“Yes.”
1749|Normal|“The Duc’s been at you.”
1750|Normal|“Yesterday.”
1751|Normal|“Aye, he does that.” Something almost like affection crossed Bellew’s face and left again. “Right. Here’s your answer, and it’s the only part of this morning worth putting in the minutes.”
1752|Normal|He held up two fingers. “There are two things a reasonable person wants to believe. One: things have properties whether or not anybody’s asking — the wallpaper is some colour when the room’s empty. Call that realism. Two: what you do here cannot instantly affect what’s true over there — no influence outrunning light. Call that locality.”
1753|Normal|He lowered the fingers. “Every sensible person in this room wants both. Werner avoids the problem by giving up the first. Max keeps both by multiplying the worlds until the question dissolves. And in 1964 I sat down and did a small piece of arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: you cannot have both. Not as a matter of taste. As a matter of measurable numbers.”
1754|Normal|Pilbeam said, “Measurable.”
1755|Normal|“That’s the point of it, Mr Chairman. That’s the whole point of it.” Bellew leaned forward. “If both things were true — properties in advance, no influence at a distance — then certain correlations between distant events have a ceiling. A definite number. You can work it out with school algebra in an afternoon. And if nature ever exceeds that ceiling, then one of your two comfortable beliefs is finished, and no amount of interpretation gets it back.”
1756|Normal|“And has it been done,” said Beatrix.
1757|Normal|“It’s been done for sixty years,” said Bellew, “in Berkeley and Orsay and Innsbruck and Delft and Vienna, tighter and tighter, and every hole anybody found in it has been plugged — the detectors, the timing, the choice of what to measure, all of it — and they gave a Nobel Prize for it in 2022, and the answer is the same every time and it isn’t close.”
1758|Normal|He sat back. “The ceiling is violated. Nature is not locally real. That is not philosophy and it is not fashion and it does not depend on which interpretation you take home at night. It is an experimental fact and it is the only weapon in this building.”
1759|Normal|Fainrose had gone extremely still. “Say the last part again,” she said.
1760|Normal|“You’ve been told it’s metaphysics,” said Bellew, “and that her guiding wave is unfalsifiable, and both dismissals are true and both are beside the point.” He nodded toward Lolly. “You don’t need to win the interpretation. You need one thing in front of Mr Pilbeam — the one thing tested to death: that a change made in one place can alter what is true in another, with no signal between them.”
1761|Normal|Lolly felt the whole architecture of three days slide into position with an almost audible click. “The overflow channel,” she said.
1762|Normal|“Go on.”
1763|Normal|“They tied nine thousand dwellings to one anchor. Fainrose’s correlation lattice.” She was standing now, without having decided to. “The Board’s position is that each dwelling was surveyed separately, so each certificate is separately defensible. Local decisions. Local harms. Nine thousand small administrative matters, each too small to be worth an inquiry.”
1764|Normal|“And?”
1765|Normal|“And if the correlations exceed Bellew’s ceiling,” said Lolly, “then they weren’t separate. It’s not an interpretation that they weren’t separate. It’s a number. You can go and measure it in Sub-District 6 this afternoon and get a figure that no committee can call metaphysics, because the arithmetic doesn’t mention wave functions or branches or guidance at all. It just says: if these were independent systems being independently surveyed, this correlation is impossible.”
1766|Normal|Bellew smiled for the first time, and it was not a warm expression, it was the smile of a man watching a tool being picked up by the correct end. “There you are,” he said. “Now you’ve got a case.”
1767|Normal|Heitmann said, quietly, “The inequality does not tell you what is real.”
1768|Normal|“It does not,” Bellew agreed. “It tells you what is not permitted. That’s a smaller claim and it’s the reason it can’t be argued with. Werner, you spent your life saying we should confine ourselves to what can be checked. I took you at your word and went and checked something, and you’ve never forgiven me for it.”
1769|Normal|“I have forgiven you,” said Heitmann. “I have never agreed with you.” A pause. “There is a difference, and after everything, it is the only distinction I have left that I am still entitled to.”
1770|Normal|Something passed across the old man’s face — Lolly could not read it, and thought later that she perhaps could — and Heitmann folded his hands again and said nothing further for the remainder of the proceeding.
1771|Normal|Tegmark said, “Jack. If they measure it and it violates, my picture survives that.”
1772|Normal|“It does,” said Bellew. “Your picture survives everything, Max. In a less robust theory that would be an administrative concern.”
1773|Normal|“No,” said Tegmark, with real cheerfulness, “but I’ll take it. And for what it’s worth—” he turned to Mrs Chain, and did it properly, and looked at her while he said it — “you were right and I was glib. Nothing being destroyed globally doesn’t buy anybody anything locally. Somebody in this branch lost her sister. I’ll put that in the minutes.”
1774|Normal|“Thank you,” said Mrs Chain.
1775|Normal|Pilbeam, who had understood perhaps a third of the last twenty minutes but had grasped with the unerring instinct of his species that a decision was forming, said, “So is the panel unanimous?”
1776|Normal|“No,” said all three of them.
1777|Normal|Pilbeam sagged.
1778|Normal|“But we’re agreed on a measurement,” said Bellew. “Which is worth ten unanimous opinions and you may minute that too. Werner will accept it because it’s an instrument reading and that’s all he ever accepts. Max will accept it because his branch has instruments in it. I’ll accept it because I designed it. And whatever number comes back, none of the three of us can wriggle.”
1779|Normal|Beatrix laid down her pen. “Chair,” she said. “The panel proposes a test with a pre-agreed threshold and three incompatible experts bound in advance to accept the result. Refusing that is not caution. It is obstruction, and I will write it up as obstruction, and I will name the committee that would not give its members’ names.”
1780|Normal|Pilbeam looked round the table for somebody to save him and found nobody. “Very well,” he said. “Minute it.”
1781|Normal|Afterwards, in the corridor, Bellew stopped Lolly with two fingers on her sleeve. “A word.”
1782|Normal|“Yes.”
1783|Normal|“Your Duc’s account and Max’s account and Werner’s silence — you’ll be tempted to pick one, because you’ve a tidy mind and you’re ten years in electronics and you want to know what the circuit is.” The pale eyes were unblinking. “Don’t pick yet. Pick when something forces you. Until then hold all three, and use whichever one tells you where to put the probe. That’s not cowardice, that’s method.”
1784|Normal|“And if none of them tells me?”
1785|Normal|“Then you’ve found the good part,” said Jack Bellew, “and you’d better write it down.”
1786|Normal|He walked off down the corridor and Lolly did not watch him go, on the grounds that she was not entirely certain she would see it.
1787|Normal|She opened the notebook instead.
1788|Normal|She wrote:
1789|Normal|Properties in advance, or no influence at a distance. Choose one. Nature has already chosen, and it is measurable.
1790|Normal|Then:
1791|Normal|I do not have to win the interpretation. I have to produce a number the committee cannot call philosophy.
1792|Normal|And beneath that:
1793|Normal|They surveyed nine thousand houses separately. If the correlations exceed the ceiling, then there were never nine thousand cases. There was one.
1794|Normal|“Get the number,” said Bellew, from a doorway that was no longer occupied. “Then you may philosophise on your own time.”
1795|Normal|Rule: Local innocence cannot explain the correlations; a number can show that, and a committee cannot talk it away.
