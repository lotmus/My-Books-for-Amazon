SCHRÖDINGER'S PAPERWORK — BOOK CLUB DISCUSSION GUIDE
Lolly Wren's Curious Science Adventures, Book 1

================================================================================
HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE
================================================================================

Ten questions, organized loosely by theme rather than chapter order, so
groups that read at different paces can still discuss without spoiling
each other. A "Go Deeper" prompt follows each question for groups who
want to pair the discussion with one of the book's own physics lectures.

================================================================================
ON BUREAUCRACY AND MEASUREMENT
================================================================================

1. The Ministry's core mistake is described as "believing that reality
   could be made simpler by paperwork." Where else in the book does
   measuring or categorizing something make it worse rather than better?
   Can you think of a real-world example of the same failure?

   Go Deeper: Pair with Lecture 02 (Measurement Basis and Forced
   Collapse) — the idea that asking a question can force a particular
   answer into existence.

2. Directive 11-C (Chapter 9) demands two contradictory certifications
   at once — precise location AND precise trajectory. Why does the
   Ministry not simply accept that both cannot be known together? What
   does its insistence say about how institutions handle uncertainty?

   Go Deeper: Pair with Lecture 09 (Uncertainty: Position and Momentum).

3. Outer Fenwick's moon needs forty-one witnesses with clipboards to
   "commit" to being visible. Discuss the difference between something
   requiring observation to exist and something merely requiring the
   world to be *undisturbed* enough to stay definite. Why does this
   distinction matter to the book's argument?

   Go Deeper: Pair with Lecture 10 (Decoherence and Einselection).

================================================================================
ON CHARACTER
================================================================================

4. Lolly spends much of the book noticing things and writing them down
   rather than acting on them. Trace her arc: where does noticing
   start to become insufficient, and what finally tips her toward
   action?

5. Dr Fainrose is described as someone who is "rarely reassured."
   Is this a flaw or a virtue in a physicist? Would the book's events
   have gone differently with a more optimistic scientist at the
   center of the investigation?

6. Albrecht Eilstein is a vampire who feeds on collapsed possibilities —
   morally compromised, not remotely on the Ministry's side, and not
   really on anyone's side but his own — yet he is also the character
   most alarmed by Phase Two. What does it mean that the book's most
   honest character about his own appetite is also its moral compass
   on this one point?

7. Mrs Elspeth Chain is not a scientist, but she is repeatedly the
   person who names what is actually happening in plain language.
   What does her presence do for the book that a second physicist
   couldn't?

================================================================================
ON THE PHYSICS
================================================================================

8. The book insists, through its "How to Read This Book" framing, that
   you can enjoy the novel without reading a single lecture. Did you
   read the lectures? Did doing so (or not doing so) change how you
   experienced the story?

   Go Deeper: If your group is split — some read the lectures, some
   didn't — compare notes on Chapter 11 (the vampire's explanation of
   Phase Two) specifically. Readers who took Lecture 07 (Entanglement)
   first often read that scene differently.

9. "Measurement forces collapse" is both a literal physics claim in
   the lectures and the book's central metaphor for bureaucracy. Which
   direction do you think the author started from — the physics or the
   metaphor? Does it matter?

================================================================================
ON THE ENDING
================================================================================

10. The book's closing line is "There is no lid." What do you take
    this to mean, in light of everything that happens in Chapters
    15-18? Is it a hopeful ending, a warning, or both?

================================================================================
BONUS: ONE QUESTION PER PHYSICIST-CHARACTER
================================================================================

The book's "A Word on Who Is Who" reveals that its consultant characters
are real twentieth-century physicists in disguise (Einstein, de Broglie,
Heisenberg, Tegmark, Bell, Witten, Schrödinger). Pick one and discuss:
does knowing who they "really" are change how you read their scenes on
a re-read?
