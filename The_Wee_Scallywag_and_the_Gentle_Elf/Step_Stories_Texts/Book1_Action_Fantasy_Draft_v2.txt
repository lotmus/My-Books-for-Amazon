The Wee Scallywag and the Gentle Elf
Book 1 of The Step Stories
by Otis Umberlight

DRAFT v2: more chase, danger, fun, food and drink, weather, grasshoppers, a toad, a fish and a ghost. Same cast, same bargain, same deadpan voice. Not yet in the docx.

Chapter 1. The Boots

The mill stood by the stream. Wilf sat on the step with an empty sack, a bowl of porridge going cold, and the old boots. The sack was empty because the wheel had stopped, and the wheel had stopped because something was stuck in it.

The something dropped out of the wheel and landed on its feet. It was a boy of about eight with pointed ears, black spiky hair and a grin like a lit match.

“Those boots,” said Sparefunk, “look made for someone my size.”

He pulled them on. They fitted. He also ate the porridge, which was not in the bargain, but he did it so fast that nobody could object.

Then he made his bargain, which was an old one. Three days of looking like a gentleman. Or guess his true name. Or the boots stay his, and one small thing besides.

Wilf, having nothing left to lose, agreed.

Sparefunk stamped once, and the ground answered. A crack of green light ran across the yard, up the wall of the mill and over the roof. Sparefunk went with it. He ran up the wall like a squirrel up a tree and was gone.

“That,” said Wilf, “was not in the bargain.”

“It was in the small print,” said a voice from the step.

Hopsi had been sitting there the whole time. Her real name was Elfie Didlhop, but nobody had used it since she was four. She was nine, with pointed ears and long wavy hair, and she had a needle in one hand and a bowl of hops in the other. She ate one hop. She spoke only when what she said was true, which saved a great deal of time.

“He is not wicked,” she said. “He is quick. Those are different things.”

Chapter 2. The Lord of the Riverbank

Next morning two messengers in blue and purple coats came down the road with feathers in their hats. The king’s men. They had heard of a lord who owned a mill by the stream.

Sparefunk dropped from a tree.

“The Lord of the Riverbank,” he announced, “approaches.”

He stood Wilf on a fallen log across the stream. It was the sort of log that looks as if it has opinions. Sparefunk jumped on the high end. The log tipped, Wilf shot into the air, and the messengers saw a lord rise slowly above the water and come down on the bank, quite undamaged.

They bowed. Sparefunk bowed deeper.

That was when the sky turned the colour of a bruise.

The wind came first. It took the messengers’ feathers and the king’s men’s hats, and then the king’s men’s dignity. Then the rain arrived all at once, sideways, with hail in it the size of peas. The messengers ran for their carriage. Wilf ran for the mill. Sparefunk ran for both, because he was thoroughly enjoying himself.

The carriage wheel stuck in the mud. The messengers shouted. The horses sulked. Wilf and Sparefunk put the log under the wheel, Hopsi stood on the end of it, and the carriage came free with a noise like a very large cork. The messengers waved a wet and grateful wave and went away.

Ten minutes later the sun was out and the whole valley steamed. A rainbow stood across the mill like an arch, and nobody remarked on it.

“A lever,” said Wilf, dripping, “and a straight face.”

“A lever,” said Sparefunk, “and a thunderstorm.”

Chapter 3. The Ghost in the Wheel

That night the wheel creaked, though the stream was still.

Wilf followed the sound out into the yard. The moon was up. The wheel was turning, slowly, with nobody pushing, and on top of it, pale and thin as spilled milk, sat a ghost.

It was a miller’s ghost, with a flour sack for a cloak and a dusty hat. It looked at Wilf for a long moment.

“Boo,” said the ghost, rather flatly.

Wilf sat down on the step. Sparefunk came running out of the dark, saw the ghost, shrieked with delight and chased it twice round the mill. The ghost, which had not been chased in two hundred years, found that it rather liked it. It flew through the wall and out the other side and came back with its hat on backwards.

Hopsi arrived with a pot of hop tea and three cups. She poured a fourth for the ghost. The ghost looked at it sadly. It could not drink. But it held the cup, because it had been a long time since anyone had put one in its hand.

“Why the wheel?” said Hopsi.

“It stopped,” said the ghost. “I do not like it stopped. Something is in the stream above the mill.”

Something was. Sparefunk found it first. He had gone down to the water, stamped on a wet stone, and gone straight through the bank into a bog up to his waist.

The ghost shrieked. This was the first useful thing it had done in two centuries. Wilf grabbed Sparefunk’s collar. Hopsi tied her sash to a root. Between them they hauled the scallywag out, black to the armpits and smelling like old cabbages, and the ghost drifted overhead holding a lantern it had found.

“Thank you,” said Sparefunk, looking suddenly a good deal smaller.

“Fast is not the same as careful,” said Hopsi.

The ghost went back to the wheel and sat on the top with its hat on straight. In the morning there was flour in the sack. Nobody asked where it came from.

Chapter 4. The Toad of the Ford

On the second day they went down the path to find bread, and the path went through a ford, and the ford had a toad in it.

It was the largest toad in the county. It sat on a flat stone in the middle of the water with its eyes half shut and its throat going in and out. It had a moustache of pondweed.

“Toll,” said the toad.

“What toll?” said Wilf.

“Something good to eat. Or something clever to say. Or a boot.”

Sparefunk, who was wearing a very good pair of boots, put one foot behind the other.

The toad’s tongue came out like a fishing line, took the left boot clean off his foot and swallowed it. Sparefunk fell over. The toad looked pleased. It burped, softly, and a small grasshopper flew out.

The grasshopper landed on Hopsi’s hand. A great many more arrived behind it, a green cloud of grasshoppers, rising off the long grass all along the ford. They were not angry. They were simply many, and all of them wanted the hops in Hopsi’s bowl.

“They’re eating the toll!” shouted Wilf.

“Run!” shouted Sparefunk, hopping on one boot.

They ran through the water with a cloud of grasshoppers at their heels and the toad in pursuit, bounding across the stones like a small bad-tempered boulder. Wilf slipped and went under. Titch, who had arrived on the far bank with two crusts in her hands, put out a hem to catch him, and was dragged in too. The grasshoppers landed on everything. The toad landed on Sparefunk.

“The boot,” said Hopsi, once everyone was out, and breathing, and covered in green. “It is a fair trade, I think. The toad kept the bargain too.”

She put a hop in the toad’s mouth. The toad closed its eyes, swallowed, and gave the boot back, rather damp. It also gave back a small silver fish that had been in the toe.

“That’s mine,” said Titch, and took it. She had a hem to mend and a stomach to fill, and a crust in each hand. She had come for the crusts, not for the toad.

Chapter 5. The Pike

By afternoon they were hungry enough to eat the grasshoppers. Hopsi would not allow it.

“Fish,” said Wilf.

The mill pond lay still and brown beneath the willows. A shadow slid beneath the surface, long as a canoe. It was a pike, old and enormous, with a head like a hatchet, and it had been waiting for something to bite.

A small grey shape came padding down the bank. It was a wolf baby, thin and sleepy, and it had been living in the spare boots by the door for a week, though nobody had noticed. It pressed its nose to the water.

“Boot!” shouted Titch.

The pike rose. The water burst open. A mouth like a barrel came out of the pond and snapped where the wolf baby had been, one second earlier. Boot, who was a very quick wolf, was already running along the bank with Sparefunk on one side and Wilf on the other.

They chased the pike round the pond. The pike chased them. Sparefunk slid on the mud and went straight into the water. The pike dragged him round the pond in a circle, still holding the end of his boot, while Hopsi ran after them with a net she had made from Titch’s hem.

“Drop it!” shouted Wilf.

“I haven’t picked it up!” shouted Sparefunk.

Titch swung. The hem went over the pike’s head. Hopsi and Wilf hauled on one side, Boot on the other, and the biggest fish in the pond came out on the bank in a spray of silver and landed on Sparefunk.

They ate it that night in the yard, roasted on a fire the ghost lit by accident, with hop tea and warm bread and a few apples that Hopsi found. The sparks went up into the dark. Somewhere above, the wheel creaked, and the ghost watched from the roof with its hat on straight and its cup in its hand.

Chapter 6. The Soft Voice

On the third morning a girl came down the path from the next village. She had curly hair in a bun and a bag of apples. Her name was Tabitha Smoke. She was eight. She sat on the step, ate an apple, and from that moment was one of them.

That afternoon the weather changed.

First the sun went. Then a thin white fog came up from the stream, so thick that nobody could see the end of the garden. Then a soft voice came out of the fog.

“Sweeties,” it said. “A quicker way. A shorter way. Come with me and I’ll show you a very quick way.”

Hopsi shut her mouth. She never answered a voice like that, and that was usually enough.

A tall figure stood at the end of the road. Thin, in a coat the colour of dusk, with a smile too wide and a bag that clinked. Its shadow was longer than it should have been, and it lay across the grass toward them like a spill.

“Run,” said Wilf.

They ran. All five and Boot, along the path with the stream flashing beside them. The fog closed in. Hail began to fall. The ghost, who had been sleeping on the wheel, woke up with a start and flew after them with its lantern, lighting the path ahead like a small, pale star.

The shadow slid after them along the ground. The voice kept pace.

“Just a little further. Just a little closer.”

Sparefunk’s boots took long strides and Titch’s hem flew out behind her. Tabitha dropped her apples, one by one, and a trail of them rolled down the path. Boot ran ahead, barking. The grasshoppers rose from the grass in a green cloud and went straight at the shadow, and for one bright moment the stranger flapped its arms and spat.

They reached the bend. Hopsi had set three white stones in a line across the path the evening before. Sparefunk, at the very back, kicked the middle stone aside as he passed, and then, on a thought, ran back and kicked it into place again.

“In case,” he said.

The stranger came to the line. It put out one long soft foot. The stones glowed, a thin white light like the first of a moonbeam, and the foot stopped. It stood there with its mouth open, and the shadow stretched out behind, and the fog thinned around it. Then it turned and walked back along the road with its bag clinking, and the sun came out as if nothing had happened.

The five of them sat in a heap with Boot on top, and the ghost hovered above and said, “Boo,” very quietly, to nobody in particular.

“It couldn’t cross,” said Titch.

“It didn’t want the true way,” said Hopsi. “It only knows the quick one.”

Chapter 7. The True Name

That night, before the third day closed, Hopsi gave Wilf the last leaf.

Wilf read it. He looked up. In the dark yard, the wee scallywag was leaning on the wheel with his arms folded, and the ghost beside him, wearing his hat.

“Sparefunk,” said Wilf.

The scallywag laughed so hard he fell off the wheel. He stamped once. The green light went out of the ground, the hill settled, and the bargain closed as promised. Boots, yes. Nothing else.

“Nobody ever guesses it,” he said, wiping his eyes. “You know why?”

“Because you tell the truth when it’s funny,” said Hopsi.

“Rotten luck,” said Sparefunk, and sat down on the step.

They had a feast. There was porridge with honey, and bread, and apples, and the last of the pike, and a jug of cold elderflower cordial that Hopsi had been saving. There was hop tea for the ghost, who held it in both hands and smiled. Boot got the heads. The toad came up from the ford and sat at the edge of the lamplight with its eyes half shut and its throat going in and out, and was given a hop.

Afterwards the days settled.

The grain rose uneven in the field. The rain tried the edge of the plot and the log sent it round. In the field behind the mill, the rectangular eggs sat in the grass with their corners all in order, and nobody asked what would come out of them. The three white stones kept their line. The way back to the step stayed open.

The sun went down. Light that had left the sun eight minutes before lay on the path. Five children and a wolf sat in it and ate the last of the bread, and a small ghost turned the wheel just once more before bedtime.

The stream went on.

The End
