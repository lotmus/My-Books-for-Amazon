The Wee Scallywag and the Gentle Elf

The mill stood quiet by the stream. The miller’s son sat on the step with an empty sack and an old pair of boots. A small figure hopped down from the wheel.

“Those boots,” said the wee scallywag, “look made for someone my size.”

He pulled them on. They fitted. He offered the old bargain: three days of looking like a gentleman, guess the true name, or the boots and one small thing remain his. The boy, having nothing left to lose, agreed. The scallywag stamped once and ran.

That night the gentle elf came in the way quiet helpers always have. She mended the torn coat and left a small loaf on the table.

Next morning the scallywag met the king’s messengers. “The Lord of the Riverbank approaches.” He stood the boy on a fallen log. The log tipped; the boy rose; the carriage people waved. It was the sort of improvement that required only a lever and a straight face.

That night the elf walked among woodland flowers. The young plants were never exact copies of their parents — taller, shorter, different in petal, some happier in shade. She left a leaf:

Little ones take after their own,
yet none are ever quite the same.

On the second day the scallywag ran so fast the afternoon stretched for him alone. The elf’s next rhyme observed it without fuss. At dusk he tried to catch a moonbeam; it slipped through his fingers and reappeared elsewhere, as if made of messengers that prefer not to be held. In the wood a pine cone gathered speed down a slope, a leaf danced because the air pushed back, and a spider felt a single shake travel the whole thread. The wood had always known these things. No one gave them longer names.

Before the third day closed, the elf showed two seeds from one plant. Same story, different writing. She handed the boy the final leaf. He read the true name. The scallywag stamped, laughed, and kept the bargain exactly — boots yes, nothing else. A promise kept needs no extra magic.

They planted the last grains. He shifted a stone with the log so water could reach them. Later the elf set three white stones in a line on the path from the wood. The scallywag kicked the middle one aside, then put it back. “In case.” They did not lose their way.

The grain rose unevenly, as predicted. A crow investigated; loose netting discouraged it. Rain tried the edge of the patch; log and stones sent the water round. The scallywag’s boots grew wet. “Smaller problem,” the boy said. “Acceptable.”

A traveller talked at length about weather, roads, and a dog. The elf told him where the stream was. He drank and, eventually, left. People talk too much. Quiet was permitted to remain.

A cart stuck in soft ground. Its driver explained the history of the misfortune. They applied the log. The wheel came free while the explanation was still unfinished. Useful work does not always wait for the end of a sentence.

A woman arrived with a torn apron. Her husband loved her, she said, yet left the gate open so the goats reached the washing. The elf mended the cloth. “Means well is a start. Doesn’t shut the gate.” The woman thought she might shut it herself on the way home. Love is easy to announce and harder to aim at the actual latch.

The scallywag had once improved stories in the old tailor’s style — three mills, trout in a stony stream. After the boy named them lies and the elf noted that small untruths empty the rest of belief, he trimmed the claims. Messengers received the plain account: one mill, uneven grain, wet stream. They looked faintly disappointed and continued. When he later asked whether the king had been truly impressed, the elf regarded the ordinary water and said nothing further. Shutting up avoided every untruth.

One hasty afternoon he tried to hurry the stream and returned mud to the knees. The elf and the boy, having taken the longer firm path, were dry. Steady care left fewer dark boots. He emptied his and offered no grand remark.

They shared the last of the bread without speeches. In the mornings they were simply still there — the sort of loyalty that requires no announcement.

The days after the bargain settled into a shape.

Mornings began with the boots by the door and the smell of whatever the elf had left warming. The scallywag ate first and commented only if the bread was notably dry or notably not. The boy checked the grain. Some stalks had decided to be taller; the elf had already said they would. He pulled a weed or two and left the rest to their own arrangements.

One afternoon a boy from the village arrived with a split bucket and a long account of how it had split. The scallywag inspected the crack.

“Wants a bit of tin and two nails,” he said.

“I was going to explain—”

“Bucket first.”

They mended it on the step. The village boy watched, then carried the bucket home half full of water to test it. It held. He looked faintly surprised that the explanation had not been required.

“People bring the story,” the scallywag observed when he had gone. “The hole is usually smaller.”

“Yes,” said the elf.

A wind came up and tried the loose board on the mill roof. The scallywag climbed, boots and all, and weighed it down with a stone the boy passed up. From the roof the stream looked the same as it did from the ground, only farther. He came down muddier and reported the fact without decoration.

“Still a stream.”

“It is.”

They ate the last heel of bread in the last of the light. A moth tried the lamp and thought better of it. Somewhere a gate may have stood open; somewhere a crow may have tested the netting again. Neither required immediate speech.

The scallywag stretched his legs so the boots could dry.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“Tomorrow,” said the boy.

The elf folded her hands and let the evening finish itself. Outside, the three white stones kept their line on the path. The grain continued its uneven rise. And the small problems of the world, which had not finished arriving, would find the log, the needle, the stone, and the habit of shutting up still waiting on the step when they did.

Problems continued to arrive: crows, rain, stuck wheels, open gates, too many words, the occasional small devilish improvement that needed undoing. Each was met with whatever lay nearest — netting, log, needle, cup, stone, or silence. The scallywag still ran when it suited him and still loved a story, provided it stayed inside the facts. The elf still mended what was torn and spoke only what held. The boy still woke to find the boots by the door and both companions present.

People know love and still forget, often, what it asks of their hands in the moment. They talk when a lever or a thread would do. The three at the mill forgot too, sometimes. But they noticed, and the next difficulty was usually smaller than the one before.

The stream went on. The uneven stalks stood in the sun. The three white stones kept their line. Light moved through the leaves in its old, hard-to-catch way. And the wee scallywag remained, boots and all, in the place a kept bargain and a certain amount of ordinary attention had left him — which was, on balance, not a bad place to be.
