The Wee Scallywag and the Gentle Elf
Book 1 of The Step Stories
by Otis Umberlight

The mill stood by the stream. Wilf sat on the step with an empty sack and the old boots. Sparefunk hopped down from the wheel.

“Those boots look made for someone my size.”

He pulled them on. They fitted. He offered the bargain: three days of looking like a gentleman, guess the true name, or the boots and one small thing remain his. Wilf, having nothing left to lose, agreed. Sparefunk stamped once and ran.

That night Hopsi came the way quiet helpers do. She mended the torn coat and left a small loaf.

Next morning Sparefunk met the messengers. He stood Wilf on a fallen log. The log tipped; Wilf rose; the carriage people waved. It required only a lever and a straight face.

That night Hopsi walked among the flowers. The young plants were never exact copies — taller, shorter, different in petal. She left a leaf:

Little ones take after their own,
yet none are ever quite the same.

On the second day Sparefunk ran so fast the afternoon stretched for him alone. At dusk he tried to catch a moonbeam; it slipped through his fingers and reappeared elsewhere. In the wood a pine cone gathered speed, a leaf danced because the air pushed back. No one gave these things longer names.

Before the third day closed Hopsi showed two seeds from one plant. Same story, different writing. She handed Wilf the final leaf. He read the true name. Sparefunk stamped, laughed, and kept the bargain exactly — boots yes, nothing else.

They planted the last grains. Wilf shifted a stone so water could reach them. Later Hopsi set three white stones in a line. Sparefunk kicked the middle one aside, then put it back. “In case.”

The grain rose unevenly. A crow came; loose netting discouraged it. Rain tried the edge; log and stones sent the water round. The boots grew wet. “Smaller problem,” Wilf said. “Acceptable.”

A traveller talked at length. Hopsi told him where the stream was. He drank and left. People talk too much.

A cart stuck. Its driver explained the history. They applied the log. The wheel came free while the explanation was still unfinished.

A woman arrived with a torn apron. Her husband loved her, yet left the gate open. Hopsi mended the cloth. “Means well is a start. Doesn’t shut the gate.”

Sparefunk trimmed his improved stories after Wilf named them lies. Messengers received the plain account. When he asked if the king had been impressed, Hopsi regarded the ordinary water and said nothing. Shutting up avoided every untruth.

They shared the last of the bread. Mornings found them still there.

Titch began to come with crusts. Boot was found thin by the stream and stayed. The days settled. Sparefunk collected a spare pair of boots and, later, tyres. Hopsi ate hops. Wilf carried the slate to school and brought it home marked. The channel stayed clear. The rectangular eggs kept their corners.

One afternoon a soft voice offered sweets and a quicker way. Hopsi shut her mouth. Wilf said, “Run.” They ran only to the bend. The stranger did not follow.

Later they walked the path. The mill grew smaller. The eight-minute light lay across the flat place. The way back stayed open.

Problems continued — crows, rain, stuck wheels, open gates, too many words. Each was met with whatever lay nearest. People know love and still forget what it asks of their hands. On the step they noticed, and the next difficulty was usually smaller.

The stream went on. The uneven stalks stood in the sun. The three white stones kept their line. And Sparefunk remained, boots and all, in the place a kept bargain and ordinary attention had left him.

The End
