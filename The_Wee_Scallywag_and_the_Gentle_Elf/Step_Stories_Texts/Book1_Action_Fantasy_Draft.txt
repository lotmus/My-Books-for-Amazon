The Wee Scallywag and the Gentle Elf
Book 1 of The Step Stories
by Otis Umberlight

DRAFT: action and fantasy version. Same cast, same bargain, same quiet voice. Not yet in the docx.

Chapter 1. The Boots

The mill stood by the stream. Wilf sat on the step with an empty sack and the old boots. The sack was empty because the wheel had stopped, and the wheel had stopped because something was stuck in it.

The something dropped out of the wheel and landed on its feet. It was a boy of about eight with pointed ears, black spiky hair and a grin like a lit match.

“Those boots,” said Sparefunk, “look made for someone my size.”

He pulled them on. They fitted. Then he made his bargain, which was an old one. Three days of looking like a gentleman. Or guess his true name. Or the boots stay his, and one small thing besides.

Wilf, having nothing left to lose, agreed.

Sparefunk stamped once, and the ground answered. A crack of green light ran across the yard, up the wall of the mill and over the roof. Sparefunk went with it. He ran up the mill wall like a squirrel up a tree and was gone over the roof.

“That,” said Wilf, “was not in the bargain.”

“It was in the small print,” said a voice from the step.

Hopsi had been sitting there the whole time. Her real name was Elfie Didlhop, but nobody had used it since she was four. She was nine, with pointed ears and long wavy hair, and she had a needle in one hand and a bowl of hops in the other. She ate one hop. She spoke only when what she said was true, which saved a great deal of time.

“He is not wicked,” she said. “He is quick. Those are different things.”

Chapter 2. The Messengers

Next morning two messengers in blue and purple coats came down the road with feathers in their hats. The king’s men. They had heard of a lord who owned a mill by the stream.

Sparefunk dropped from a tree.

“The Lord of the Riverbank,” he announced, “approaches.”

He sat Wilf on a fallen log across the stream. It was the sort of log that looks as if it has opinions. Sparefunk jumped on the high end. The log tipped, Wilf shot into the air, and the messengers saw a lord rise slowly above the water and come down on the bank, quite undamaged.

They bowed deeply. Sparefunk bowed deeper. Behind the mill, Hopsi shut her eyes. It was the first of the three days, and the scallywag had been exactly as clever as he had promised.

“A lever,” said Wilf quietly, “and a straight face.”

“A lever,” said Sparefunk, “and nobody checking.”

Chapter 3. The Night Wood

That night Wilf followed the green light into the wood, because he wanted his boots back and because the wood was the kind that wants to be followed.

Moonlight lay across the ferns. Little lights drifted between the trunks. They were not fireflies, or not only. Hopsi walked ahead with her hops and her needle, and wherever she stepped, the flowers lifted their heads.

“Look,” she said. “The young ones.”

Under the oak grew a hundred small plants, all from the same parent. No two were alike. One was taller. One leaned toward the shade. One had a petal the colour of the moon. Hopsi tucked a leaf into Wilf’s hand with a rhyme on it:

Little ones take after their own,
yet none are ever quite the same.

Behind them something crashed through the bracken. A pine cone the size of a cabbage came bouncing down the hill. Then another. Sparefunk came after them at a dead run, laughing, with his hair on end and the whole hillside rolling at his heels.

“Moonbeam!” he shouted. “It went this way!”

It had. A thin silver beam was sliding between the trees, making a small, bright, deadpan nuisance of itself. Wilf reached for it. It slipped through his fingers and came out somewhere else, as if made of messengers who preferred not to be held. Sparefunk lunged and landed in a puddle. The beam went on, perfectly polite.

“It will not be caught,” said Hopsi. “It only takes the quickest way.”

Chapter 4. The Run

On the second day the afternoon stretched for Sparefunk alone. He ran so fast that the sun stood still above him, and when he stopped, the clock on the mill tower was an hour behind. He sat down on the step with grass in his hair and said nothing, which for him was a great many words.

“Fast,” said Hopsi, “is not the same as early.”

Titch arrived that evening with a crust in each hand. She was eight, and quick with a hem. She said she had been told there was a wolf in the wood. There was. It was a baby, thin and grey, and it had been sleeping in the spare boots, which Sparefunk had been keeping by the door. Nobody asked how it got there. It woke, looked at them all, and chose Wilf’s foot.

“Boot,” said Titch.

“That’s the boots,” said Wilf.

“No,” said Titch. “That’s his name.”

Chapter 5. The Soft Voice

On the third morning a new girl came down the path from the next village, curly hair up in a bun, with a bag of apples. Her name was Tabitha Smoke. She was eight. She sat on the step and ate an apple, and was, from that moment, one of them.

That afternoon a soft voice came from the road.

“Sweeties,” it said. “A quicker way. A shorter way. Come with me and I’ll show you a very quick way.”

Hopsi shut her mouth. Hopsi never answered a voice like that, and that was usually enough.

Something stood at the end of the road. Tall, thin, in a coat the colour of dusk, with a smile too wide and a bag that clinked. Its shadow was longer than it should have been. It took one soft step toward the mill.

“Run,” said Wilf.

They ran. All five and Boot, down the path with the stream flashing beside them. Sparefunk’s boots took long strides and Titch’s hem flew. Tabitha dropped her last apple. Behind them the shadow slid along the ground like spilled ink and the voice kept pace.

“Just a little further,” it said. “Just a little closer.”

They ran to the bend. Hopsi had set three white stones in a line across the path the evening before. Sparefunk, at the very back, kicked the middle stone aside as he went by, and then, on a thought, ran back and kicked it into place again.

“In case,” he said.

The stranger came to the line. It put out one long soft foot. The stones glowed, a thin white light like the first of a moonbeam, and the foot stopped. It stood there with its mouth open, the shadow stretched out behind, and then it turned and walked back along the road into the dusk with its bag clinking.

The five of them sat in a heap with Boot on top.

“It couldn’t cross,” said Titch.

“It didn’t want the true way,” said Hopsi. “It only knows the quick one.”

Chapter 6. The True Name

That night, before the third day closed, Hopsi gave Wilf the last leaf.

Wilf read it. He looked up. In the dark yard, the wee scallywag was leaning on the wheel with his arms folded.

“Sparefunk,” said Wilf.

The scallywag laughed so hard he fell off the wheel. He stamped once. The green light went out of the ground, the hill settled, and the bargain closed as promised. Boots, yes. Nothing else.

“Exactly as agreed,” he said, wiping his eyes. “Nobody ever guesses it. You know why?”

“Because you tell the truth when it’s funny,” said Hopsi.

“Rotten luck,” said Sparefunk, and sat down on the step.

Chapter 7. The Step

Afterwards, the days settled.

The grain rose uneven in the field. The rain tried the edge of the plot and the log sent it round. A cart stuck in soft ground and the driver explained the whole history of his misfortune while Wilf and Sparefunk lifted the wheel clear. A woman came with a torn apron and a husband who left the gate open. Hopsi mended the cloth and said, “Means well is a start. Doesn’t shut the gate.”

Sparefunk trimmed his tall stories down to the true ones. The king’s men were disappointed and went home. Nobody minded.

In the field behind the mill, the rectangular eggs sat in the grass with their corners all in order, and nobody asked what would come out of them. The three white stones kept their line. The way back to the step stayed open.

The sun went down. Light that had left the sun eight minutes before lay on the path. Five children and a wolf sat in it and ate the last of the bread.

The stream went on.

The End
