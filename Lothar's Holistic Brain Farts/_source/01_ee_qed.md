# Foundations: Charge, Fields, and the Math Behind Circuits

Every circuit you will ever meet obeys a few rules about charge, fields, and arithmetic. Learn them here, once, and the rest of electronics becomes bookkeeping with occasional sparks.

*Status: Settled. Textbook physics and engineering throughout.*

## Chapter 1 — Electric Charge
Electric charge is one of nature's fundamental and stubbornly conserved properties. It comes in discrete lumps, always some whole-number multiple of the smallest possible charge, and nothing you do to a system ever creates or destroys charge overall. You can only shuffle it from one place to another.

Money moving between wallets is a serviceable everyday picture of both ideas at once. Hand someone a five-dollar bill and the total cash in the room stays fixed, and whatever changes hands arrives in whole coins and bills, never some impossible fraction of a cent. The picture breaks at one point: a government can print new money whenever it likes, and nothing in economics forbids it. Physics permits no such institution for charge. Every measurement ever made says the charge in the universe today was already here in some form from the start, and nothing has ever been caught minting a fresh electron's worth of it out of nothing.

Rub a balloon on a sweater or touch two objects together, and electrons migrate from one material to another. Bring a charged rod near a neutral one without touching it, and something related but distinct happens. The rod's field shoves the neutral object's own free electrons around inside it, piling them up on the near side and leaving the far side correspondingly depleted, without a single electron ever crossing between rod and object. Migration between materials and reshuffling within one material are two different mechanisms. By either one, nothing is manufactured, only relocated.

What separates one kind of material from another here is simply how tightly it holds onto its electrons. Conductors let them roam freely; insulators clamp them down. A conductor is a crowded subway platform where anyone can shuffle to any open spot, and an insulator is a lecture hall with assigned, bolted-down seating: the electrons are present in both rooms but free to wander in only one. That single distinction, mobile charge versus immobile charge, is the seed from which practically all of electrical engineering grows. Nearly everything that follows studies either what free charge gets up to when it can move, or what happens when a field leans on charge that stubbornly can't.

## Chapter 2 — Coulomb's Law and the Electric Force
Coulomb's law is the electrical counterpart of Newton's law of gravity. The force between two charges depends on how large each charge is and falls away sharply, with the square of the distance between them, as they're moved apart, with a fixed constant of proportionality tying the whole thing to real-world units. Gravity is the natural yardstick, since most people already carry some intuition for how quickly its pull fades with distance, and both forces thin out at exactly the same inverse-square rate.

The parallel survives right up until you ask about sign. Gravity only ever pulls. Mass has no opposite, and two rocks floating in otherwise empty space can only drift toward each other. Charge comes in two flavors, and Coulomb's law pushes just as readily as it pulls. Like charges push each other apart, opposite charges pull together, and the force always acts straight along the line connecting them.

The inverse-square falloff is no isolated quirk of electricity. It is the geometry of something spreading out over the surface of an ever-expanding sphere, and the same pattern shows up for gravity and for the intensity of radiation spreading from a source.

When more than two charges are involved, their forces add as vectors. This is superposition, and it is straightforward right up until you're several charges deep and discover that the real difficulty is bookkeeping. Three people tugging on a rope from three directions present the same problem: every individual tug is trivial to understand, and the argument starts only once someone has to add them all up correctly. Coulomb's law strictly applies only to charges that are stationary and point-like, or neatly spherical. Once charges start moving or configurations get complicated, it is no longer sufficient on its own, and that gap is precisely what the field concept of the next chapter is built to fill.

## Chapter 3 — The Electric Field
The force between two charges is best retold as a two-step story instead of one spooky instantaneous action. A source charge first fills the surrounding space with a field, and only afterward does a second charge respond to whatever field it finds at its own location. This sidesteps the old "action at a distance" puzzle by inserting a local intermediary, the field, between cause and effect.

A pond is a decent everyday model. Toss a stone in at one end, and a leaf floating anywhere on the surface bobs according to whatever ripple happens to reach it, with no direct link back to the stone required. The water pattern is the intermediary, precisely the role the field plays. The picture breaks in one instructive way. Ripples need water to ripple in, and they take time to travel outward before dying away. The electric field needs no medium at all, filling empty vacuum as readily as air, and a static field set up by a stationary charge doesn't travel, spread, or fade with time. It simply sits there for as long as the charge producing it sits there too.

Nineteenth-century physicists, not unreasonably, assumed a field must be a ripple in some invisible medium after all, and spent decades hunting for the "aether" that supposedly did the rippling. Chapter 12 revisits that hunt, which came up empty.

The field is defined operationally, as the force a tiny test charge would feel divided by the size of that test charge, and it carries units of voltage per distance, a handy fact that resurfaces later. As with forces, fields from several sources simply add as vectors. Field lines show both direction (they run tangent to the field at every point) and strength (the more tightly packed the lines, the stronger the field). They borrow the logic of contour lines on a hiking map, where the spacing tells you where the terrain gets steep.

The electric dipole, two equal and opposite charges held a small, fixed distance apart, is a tiny tug-of-war in a permanent draw: neither side winning, both still very much pulling. Its field is a template pattern that will return repeatedly once polarization and dielectric materials enter the story.

## Chapter 4 — Electric Flux and Gauss's Law
Gauss's law states that however much electric field pokes out through any closed imaginary surface is fixed entirely by how much charge that surface encloses. The charge outside makes no difference, and neither does the exact shape of the surface.

Lower a closed net bag into a swarming school of fish. However oddly the bag is shaped, counting the fish that cross its surface tells you only about the fish inside, and nothing about the ones still swimming around outside it. Gauss's law makes the same promise for field lines crossing an imaginary closed surface. The comparison strains in one place: fish are discrete, countable objects with a location, while field lines are a drawing convenience, and what's actually being tallied is a continuous flow, the flux. Like the fish count, that flow depends on the charge inside alone.

Mathematically, Gauss's law is Coulomb's law wearing different clothes, but the wardrobe is far more convenient. For charge distributions with the right symmetry, a Gaussian surface chosen to match that symmetry turns what would otherwise be a nasty integral into simple algebra, yielding tidy results for the field near an infinite charged sheet or an infinite charged line.

Applied to a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the law delivers two clean facts for free: any excess charge crowds onto the outer surface, and the field inside the conductor's body is exactly zero, no matter how oddly the conductor is shaped. The crowding is easy to picture. Excess charge behaves like a crowd of people who all despise being touched; mutual repulsion pushes everyone as far apart as the room allows, which for a solid conductor means all the way out to its skin. This form of Gauss's law is, not coincidentally, the electrostatic member of the four equations Maxwell eventually assembled into a complete theory of electromagnetism.

## Chapter 5 — Electric Potential Energy and Voltage
Voltage, properly called electric potential, is potential energy per unit of charge. Because it is a plain scalar number with no direction attached, it is generally far more pleasant to work with than the field itself: no direction needs tracking, and quantities add algebraically. For a single point charge, potential falls off as one over the distance, one power gentler than the force, and the potential energy of a pair of charges follows the same pattern.

Field and potential are two descriptions of the same underlying physics. The field always points in the direction in which potential falls fastest, and in a uniform field, potential difference is simply field strength multiplied by distance.

Only differences in potential carry physical meaning. A point has no absolute, "true" voltage, only a voltage relative to some chosen reference, which behaves exactly the way elevation does on a hiking map. Nobody asks what a mountain's height above nothing is, only how high it sits above some agreed reference, usually sea level. Measure from your kitchen floor instead and every height on the map shifts by the same fixed amount, but the drop between any two points, and therefore how much a ball would gain or lose rolling from one to the other, stays exactly the same. Voltage works identically. A circuit's "ground" is a convenient sea level chosen for bookkeeping, and no point in the circuit is secretly the true zero of anything.

Equipotential surfaces, regions all sitting at the same potential, always cross field lines at right angles. The energy stored in a field gets a first look here and is deliberately left half-finished, so that Chapter 6 can pick it back up with capacitors in hand.

## Chapter 6 — Conductors, Capacitance, and Dielectrics
A capacitor, stripped to its essentials, is any pair of conductors holding equal and opposite charge with some voltage between them. Its capacitance is the ratio of stored charge to that voltage, and usefully, that ratio depends only on the capacitor's geometry, whatever charge happens to be on it at the moment.

A water tank or a balloon is the comparison most people reach for first. Pressure stands in for voltage and stored volume for charge, and a bigger or stretchier container holding more at the same pressure stands in for a bigger capacitance. That captures the idea that charge and voltage rise together, but it misleads on one specific point. Water sits inside a tank as one continuous body, while a capacitor's charge always comes in two separate, equal and opposite piles on two separate plates, with nothing but empty space or a dielectric between them. There is a plus pile on one plate and an identical minus pile on the other, and the whole device works by keeping that separation intact.

For the common parallel-plate case, capacitance comes directly out of combining the field of a charged sheet with the field-to-voltage relationship of the previous chapter, and slipping a dielectric material between the plates multiplies the result by that material's dielectric constant. The energy stored in a charged capacitor can be written several equivalent ways, depending on which of charge, voltage, or capacitance is most convenient to know. Capacitors combine in the mirror image of resistors: wired in parallel their values add directly, and wired in series their reciprocals add. File that reversal away before Chapter 8 introduces resistors in full.

Underlying all of this is the conductor-in-equilibrium fact from Chapter 4: zero field inside a conductor. A dielectric's whole trick is to polarize partially and cancel some of the field between the plates. Think of a room full of tiny compasses, each swiveling to partly oppose a field passing through. None aligns completely, but with enough of them cooperating the net field is meaningfully weakened, and that is precisely how a dielectric manages to boost capacitance.

## Chapter 7 — Electric Current and Resistance
Current is simply the rate at which charge flows past a point. By long-standing convention it is measured in the direction positive charge would move, even in an ordinary metal wire, where negatively charged electrons do the moving in the opposite direction. Everyone long ago decided the convention isn't worth the trouble of fixing.

Current density relates total current to a wire's cross-sectional area and to how fast individual charge carriers drift along. That drift speed turns out to be almost comically slow, a small fraction of a millimeter per second and far slower than a strolling pedestrian, which raises the obvious question of why a light switch seems to work instantly. The answer is that the electric field propagates through the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light. The carriers behave like a very long, very slow queue that starts shuffling forward all together the instant the front is given a shove.

Even the queue is too orderly. Real electrons in a wire don't stand patiently in line at all; they bounce around in every direction at enormous speed, colliding constantly with the metal's atoms, even with no current flowing whatsoever. Current adds only a slight, shared drift riding on top of all that chaos, the way a concert crowd keeps milling around individually even as the whole mass slowly shuffles toward the exit.

Resistance depends on a wire's length, its cross-sectional area, and an intrinsic material property called resistivity, which itself depends on temperature. For ordinary metals, resistivity climbs as things heat up. For semiconductors it falls, a clear tell that rather different physics drives the two behaviors underneath. At the extreme low-temperature end sits superconductivity, where resistivity drops all the way to exactly zero below some critical temperature, a phenomenon that gets only this passing mention here.

## Chapter 8 — Ohm's Law and a First Look at Circuits
Ohm's law says that the voltage across a component equals the current through it times its resistance. It has the comforting ring of a fundamental law of nature, and it is actually an empirical rule. It happens to describe metals and manufactured resistors extremely well, and later chapters will break it deliberately and gleefully once diodes and transistors show up.

Provisionally, think of resistance as a narrow pipe restricting water flow. Push a fixed pressure (voltage) through a narrower pipe (higher resistance) and less water (current) gets through. Ohm's law is the claim that, for a metal wire or a manufactured resistor, the pipe's narrowness stays fixed however hard you push: double the pressure and you get double the flow, no more and no less. Diodes and transistors refuse to behave like fixed-width pipes. Push harder on some of them and the effective narrowness itself changes, a nonlinearity Ohm's law cannot describe and never claimed to.

Power dissipated in a resistor can be written in terms of current and voltage together, or either one alone paired with resistance. In every case that power is lost for good, converted irreversibly into heat. Resistors combine the opposite way from capacitors: series resistances add directly, and parallel resistances add by reciprocal, the pattern noted back in Chapter 6.

Two resistors in series form a voltage divider, splitting an input voltage between them in proportion to their values. This unassuming little sub-circuit is one of the most common building blocks in all of electronics, turning up everywhere from sensor interfaces to biasing networks.

## Chapter 9 — Magnetism and the Magnetic Field
Magnetism plays by different rules from electricity right from the start. An electric field pushes on any charge, sitting still or moving. A magnetic field pushes only on charge that is already in motion, and it does so in a direction always perpendicular to both the charge's velocity and the field itself.

A rudder on a moving boat shows what that perpendicular push can and cannot do. A rudder can turn the boat's heading all day long, but pushing broadside to the boat's motion it never speeds the boat up or slows it down. Steering and propulsion are simply different jobs, handled by different forces. A magnetic field only ever does the rudder's job: it bends a moving charge's path without touching its speed, because a force perpendicular to motion does no work. A charged particle moving at right angles to a uniform magnetic field therefore never changes its speed at all. It travels in a circle, with a radius set by its mass, speed, charge, and the field strength.

Scale this up from one moving charge to a whole current-carrying wire, many charges moving together, and the same sideways force becomes the entire operating principle behind every electric motor ever built. Magnetic field strength is measured in tesla. The real mathematical skill to master is the cross-product geometry behind it all, which in practice means the right-hand rule (the same twisting sense as driving in an ordinary screw, for anyone who finds screws easier to picture than fingers). Every student privately resents it right up until it clicks.

## Chapter 10 — Sources of the Magnetic Field
Moving charge doesn't just feel magnetic fields; it creates them, too, and that two-way relationship is what unifies electricity and magnetism into a single subject. A long, straight current-carrying wire produces a field that weakens with distance from the wire. When two such wires run parallel, they attract each other if their currents flow the same way and repel if the currents run opposite, precisely the reverse of how two static charges behave.

That pleasingly perverse detail inverts the electrical intuition of the last several chapters. Two positive charges, both "the same sign," always repel, while two wires carrying current "the same way" always attract. Magnetism flips one of electricity's rules here, so any electrical intuition carried into a magnetic problem should travel with its sign reversed.

Ampère's law is magnetism's answer to Gauss's law. It relates the field circulating around a loop to the current threading through it, and its most useful application is deriving the pleasantly uniform field that builds up inside a solenoid, a simple coil of wire.

Slide a ferromagnetic core inside that solenoid and its field is amplified enormously. A megaphone held up to a voice is the tempting first comparison, and it breaks on inspection. A megaphone adds no acoustic energy and only funnels sound that already exists, while the core's own atomic dipoles align with the applied field and contribute real additional magnetization of their own. The amplified field genuinely has more put into it. That amplification is dramatic enough to be the whole reason transformer and inductor cores are made of iron or ferrite instead of being left as air.

## Chapter 11 — Electromagnetic Induction
Faraday's law is arguably the single most economically important sentence in electrical engineering: a magnetic flux that changes over time, threading through a loop of wire, induces an EMF around that loop. That one fact underlies every generator and every transformer in existence.

Induction responds only to change. A loop of wire is indifferent to how much magnetic flux happens to thread it at any given instant; a large, steady, unchanging flux produces no induced EMF whatsoever. The loop cares only about how fast that flux is climbing or falling. A shadow on a wall makes the point: a shadow merely sitting there, however large, does nothing, while a shadow's edge sweeping past at speed is what would make something happen, if walls responded to shadows at all. Sit a loop inside the strongest steady magnet in the world and, so long as nothing moves and nothing changes, not a single electron stirs on its account.

The induced current's direction is the minus sign hiding inside Faraday's law, made explicit. It always flows in whichever direction opposes the very change that produced it, a direct and unavoidable consequence of energy conservation that needs no separate rule. Induction that helped the change along would let you build a perpetual motion machine, and nature declines the offer. A conductor physically dragged through a magnetic field generates a motional EMF, a specific case of the same law.

Self-inductance describes a coil's built-in reluctance to let its current change quickly. It is the first real hint that the inductor deserves to sit beside the resistor and capacitor as a third fundamental circuit element. Energy stored in a magnetic field gets a first look as well, with the full treatment of inductors left for *Core Circuits and Components*, Chapter 9.

## Chapter 12 — Toward Maxwell's Equations
Maxwell's four equations gather everything built so far into one tidy, self-consistent system: two versions of Gauss's law, one electric and one magnetic, plus Faraday's law and a corrected form of Ampère's law. Maxwell's own contribution was spotting that Ampère's law as originally stated was slightly incomplete, and patching it with an extra term called the displacement current.

That small fix restores a symmetry between electricity and magnetism. Once it is in place, the equations predict that electric and magnetic fields can sustain each other in a self-propagating wave, with no charges or currents required to keep it going. Compute the speed of that wave from nothing but two fundamental constants of electricity and magnetism, and the number matches the measured speed of light to within experimental precision. That is how physicists first realized that light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

This is the payoff Chapter 3's aether hunt was quietly waiting for, though not the one those nineteenth-century physicists expected. They went looking for a medium because every other wave they knew needed one to wave in, and came up empty because there was none to find. An electromagnetic wave is the electric and magnetic fields themselves waving, and it propagates perfectly well through nothing at all. That finally made the aether not just unfound but unnecessary. Every circuit in the rest of this book and the next is, one way or another, a special case of these same four equations.

## Chapter 13 — Quantities, Units, and the SI System
A physical quantity is a number multiplied by a unit. Because that is a genuine multiplication, and no label stapled on afterward, units obey ordinary algebra: they cancel, combine, and multiply out like any other algebraic term, the same way common factors cancel in an ordinary fraction. A unit conversion, seen this way, is nothing more exotic than multiplying by a cleverly disguised form of the number one. Three feet over one yard equals one, however official-looking the units attached to it.

The SI system rests on seven base units. The electrical units used from here on (the coulomb, volt, ohm, farad, henry, weber, hertz, watt, joule, and siemens) are all convenient shorthand built by combining up to four of those seven.

Dimensional analysis is a cheap way to catch structural mistakes, since a formula that doesn't balance in units can't possibly be right. It is completely blind, though, to any dimensionless constant in an equation, including, notoriously, the factor of two pi that separates a frequency in hertz from the same frequency expressed in radians per second. Then there are the units that aren't really units. Decibels, percentages, and radians are dimensionless ratios that dress themselves up in unit-like clothing.

## Chapter 14 — Prefixes, Scientific Notation, and Engineering Notation
SI prefixes scale a unit up or down by powers of ten, from the vanishingly small femto- up to the enormous tera-. Case matters more than it looks like it should: a lower-case m and a capital M differ by a factor of a billion, which has ruined more than one component order.

Engineering notation is scientific notation with a house rule. Exponents are restricted to multiples of three, specifically so they line up directly with the standard prefixes and need no conversion.

Prefixes also pair up reciprocally in tidy ways: micro with mega, milli with kilo, nano with giga, pico with tera. Each pair multiplies back to one, a handy shortcut for quick mental arithmetic. A currency exchange rate and its reverse rate behave the same way, multiplying to exactly one, so converting there and immediately back leaves you with the number you started with. The one persistent trap in scientific-notation arithmetic is that exponents must be brought into line with each other before the mantissas in front of them can be added or subtracted.

## Chapter 15 — Precision, Significant Figures, and Uncertainty
Every real measurement carries some uncertainty. Significant figures are a convention for reporting a final result faithfully, and they say nothing about how intermediate calculations should be handled; rounding too early in a multi-step calculation is a reliable way to poison the final answer. When quantities are multiplied or divided, their relative uncertainties add. When quantities are added or subtracted, their absolute uncertainties add.

Subtracting two nearly equal numbers is a particular hazard, since it can wipe out most of the meaningful precision in an instant, a phenomenon with the suitably dramatic name of catastrophic cancellation. An exaggerated example catches it red-handed. Weigh an enormous truck, remove a single feather, weigh the truck again, and try to recover the feather's weight by subtracting the two readings. Both readings carry the same small uncertainty from the scale itself, but the answer you want is minuscule compared with that uncertainty, so the subtraction can as easily hand back a wildly wrong feather weight as a right one. Subtracting two big, nearly equal numbers to get a small one puts a magnifying glass on whatever error was already hiding in each of them.

Accuracy, precision, and resolution sound almost interchangeable in casual speech but describe three distinct ideas: how close a reading is to the true value, how closely repeated readings agree with one another, and the smallest change an instrument can register. Conflating them is one of the more common ways people fool themselves about how good a measurement really is. A resistor's tolerance rating, such as plus or minus five percent, grounds all of this in hardware, and it sets up the E-series of standard component values covered later.

## Chapter 16 — Algebra for Circuit Analysis
The reciprocal of a sum differs from the sum of the reciprocals. The fact sounds too obvious to need stating, and it reliably catches almost everyone exactly once, usually while combining parallel resistors under deadline pressure. For exactly two resistors in parallel, a shortcut exists, their product divided by their sum, which is quicker than the full reciprocal formula and valid only for that two-element case.

Voltage divides between series elements in proportion to each element's own resistance, while current divides between parallel branches in inverse proportion to each branch's resistance. Rederive that inversion from scratch a few times instead of simply memorizing it, since misremembering which way it goes is a classic error.

The discriminant of a quadratic equation, positive, zero, or negative, tells you in advance whether a second-order circuit will settle smoothly (overdamped), settle as fast as possible without overshooting (critically damped), or ring back and forth before settling (oscillatory). An ordinary screen door with an adjustable closer covers all three cases. Set the closer too weak and the door swings back and forth several times before it finally settles: oscillatory. Set it correctly and the door shuts in one smooth motion, as fast as possible without banging: critically damped. Set it too strong and the door closes without ever overshooting, but takes its own sweet time getting there: overdamped. The resonance material of Chapter 37 picks this up again.

Small-signal approximations get the last word, particularly the binomial approximation, which replaces one plus a small quantity x, raised to a power n, with simply one plus n times x. They recur constantly through the circuit chapters whenever an exact expression is more trouble than it's worth.

## Chapter 17 — Systems of Linear Equations
Kirchhoff's laws produce equations that are linear in a circuit's unknown voltages and currents, so the whole machinery of linear algebra becomes available for solving them. Elimination generally scales to larger circuits far better than substitution does.

A system whose determinant comes out to zero is trying to tell you something. Either the equations you wrote down are redundant restatements of each other, or, more rarely, the circuit itself describes something physically inconsistent. It is usually the former, and treating it purely as an arithmetic slip is the wrong instinct. A zero determinant is the algebra announcing that you handed it the same piece of information twice in different clothes. It is like being told a room's temperature twice, once in Celsius and once in Fahrenheit, and being surprised when together they still fail to pin down some third, independent fact about the room.

The matrix version of nodal analysis, a conductance matrix multiplying a vector of unknown node voltages to produce a vector of known currents, is, unglamorously, precisely what circuit simulation software such as SPICE does under the hood every time it "solves" a circuit.

## Chapter 18 — Functions, Graphs, and Limiting Behavior
A transfer function can often be understood at a glance without plotting the whole thing. Read off its behavior at low frequency, at high frequency, and around whatever corner sits between the two. Plotted on logarithmic axes, a curving power-law relationship becomes a dead straight line, and the slope of that line directly reveals the order of the underlying system, a single number that tells you how aggressively the response rises or falls.

One persistent gotcha of log axes is that they have no zero at all. Zero cannot be represented on a logarithmic scale, which trips up anyone trying to plot a response that starts from nothing. A log scale effectively counts how many times you'd have to double a number to reach some target, and no count of doublings, however large or however negative, ever lands exactly on zero. You can always halve again and still have something left over. This way of reading frequency-response plots gets heavy use in the filters of *Core Circuits and Components*, Chapter 6.

## Chapter 19 — Exponentials, Logarithms, and the Decibel
The exponential function has a peculiarly self-referential property: its rate of change equals itself. That single fact is the entire reason it governs every first-order RC or RL circuit encountered later on.

Logarithms earn their keep by turning multiplication into addition. A slide rule exploits exactly this trick, physically lining up two logarithmic scales so that adding their lengths multiplies the numbers those lengths represent, and the same trick is the whole justification for decibels. Power ratios convert to decibels with one multiplying factor (ten times the base-ten logarithm of the ratio), voltage ratios with another (twenty times). A short list of values is worth memorizing outright: 3 dB corresponds to doubling power, 6 dB to doubling voltage, 10 dB to a tenfold increase in power, and 20 dB to a tenfold increase in voltage.

Decibel values conveniently add up along a chain of cascaded stages, which is most of their appeal. Values in dBm, an absolute measure referenced to one milliwatt, do not add in that simple way, a distinction that trips people up more often than it should. For reference, 0 dBm corresponds to one milliwatt, +30 dBm to a full watt, and −30 dBm to a single microwatt.

## Chapter 20 — Trigonometry and the Sinusoid
A sinusoid, however it's dressed up, is completely pinned down by three numbers: how big it swings (amplitude), how fast it oscillates (angular frequency, related to ordinary frequency and to the period of one full cycle), and where in its cycle it starts (phase). A ticking metronome carries the same three numbers. How wide its arm swings is the amplitude, how fast it ticks back and forth is the frequency, and where in its swing it happened to be when you glanced up and started your stopwatch is the phase. Those three alone pin down its whole motion from then on.

Radians are the required currency for calculus to work properly, while degrees remain the everyday convention for quoting a phase difference between two signals. The Pythagorean identity is what later produces the magnitude of an impedance, and the sum-of-angles identities generate the mixing and modulation effects that appear when two sinusoids combine.

RMS, root mean square, is defined by matching heating effect, which is a different thing from simple averaging. The familiar factor of about 0.707 (one over the square root of two) applies only to sinusoids. A square wave's RMS value equals its peak, and a triangle wave's RMS value is its peak divided by the square root of three, so blindly reusing the sinusoid factor for another waveform shape is a reliable way to get the wrong answer.

## Chapter 21 — Complex Numbers
A complex number carries two pieces of information in one mathematical object at once. For an engineer, that almost always means magnitude and phase bundled together.

Think of a complex number as an arrow drawn from the origin out to some point on a flat page. Rectangular form gives the arrow's coordinates the ordinary way, so many steps east and so many steps north, the natural language for stacking arrows tip to tail, which is addition. Polar form gives the same arrow's length and compass bearing, the natural language for stretching and spinning it, which is multiplication and division. The arrow itself never changes between the two descriptions; only the facts easiest to read off it do.

Engineers write j for the square root of minus one, where mathematicians write i, purely so it doesn't get visually confused with current, which is also conventionally written as i. Rectangular form, a real part plus an imaginary part, is the natural choice for adding and subtracting complex numbers. Polar form, a magnitude and an angle, is the natural choice for multiplying and dividing them, since magnitudes multiply and angles simply add.

The complex conjugate, formed by flipping the sign of the imaginary part, is the standard trick for clearing an imaginary part out of a denominator, because a number multiplied by its own conjugate always comes out real. Multiplying by j rotates a number by ninety degrees on the complex plane, and squaring j to get minus one is that same ninety-degree rotation applied twice in a row, leaving you pointing the opposite way.

## Chapter 22 — Euler's Formula and the Complex Exponential
Euler's formula is the identity that ties a complex exponential to an ordinary rotating arrow. Raising the constant e to an imaginary-angle power produces exactly the point at that angle on a circle of radius one, with its real part equal to the cosine and its imaginary part equal to the sine of that same angle.

A real-valued sinusoid can then always be recovered as the real-part "shadow" of a complex exponential spinning steadily around that circle. Think of a seat on a Ferris wheel turning at a steady rate. Watched face-on, the seat traces a full circle: the complex exponential in its entirety. Watched from far off to the side, so that only its left-right position registers, the same seat appears merely to slide back and forth, slowing at the ends and rushing through the middle, which is precisely the shape of an ordinary sinusoid. The sinusoid was never a separate thing from the rotation. It is what the rotation looks like from an angle that throws away half the information.

The payoff comes when you differentiate that spinning exponential. Doing so just multiplies it by the angular frequency and a rotation factor, converting a calculus operation into plain multiplication. That single trick, more than anything else, is why the complex-exponential approach solves AC circuits so cleanly, and it leads directly to the phasor, the subject of the next chapter.

## Chapter 23 — Phasors and Impedance
In any linear circuit driven at a single fixed frequency, the only things that vary from point to point are amplitude and phase. The frequency is common to everything and can be factored out of the picture entirely, leaving a phasor: a single complex number that captures amplitude and phase together.

Imagine a row of clocks whose second hands all sweep at exactly the same rate, though each hand is a different length and each began its sweep from a different point on the dial. Because every hand shares that one sweep rate, you can stop worrying about the ticking altogether and describe each hand completely with two numbers: how long it is, and where on the dial it started. A phasor does the same with a voltage or current, freezing the shared business of waving in time so the signal can be handled as a static arrow instead of a moving target. The trick works only because every voltage and current in the circuit is locked to that one sweep rate. Mix in a second frequency and the hands no longer sweep together, comparing their positions stops meaning anything, and the method no longer applies. Phasors are strictly a single-frequency tool, however useful within that limit.

Impedance is the complex ratio of voltage to current that results, and it takes a different form for each of the three basic components. A resistor's impedance is its ordinary resistance, an inductor's grows with frequency, and a capacitor's shrinks with frequency, each carrying its own phase relationship between voltage and current. An inductor's growing impedance is the same stubbornness as inertia in anything heavy. A loaded shopping cart is easy to keep rolling at a steady speed and hard to yank back and forth rapidly, resisting harder the faster you try to reverse it. That is why an inductor looks like a dead short at DC, where nothing is reversing at all, and like an open switch at high frequency. A capacitor runs the opposite way, like a stiff gate that's hard to budge at rest but swings easily once something rattles it quickly. It blocks DC outright, since nothing is changing for it to respond to, and lets high-frequency signals through almost as if it weren't there.

With impedance defined this way, every technique already built for DC circuits carries straight over to AC circuits, with complex impedance replacing plain resistance throughout. Because component voltages now add as phasors, full vector sums instead of plain arithmetic sums, the voltage across an individual component can exceed the voltage of the supply feeding the whole circuit. This AC party trick looks like rule-breaking right up until you remember that phasors simply don't add the way ordinary numbers do. Admittance, the reciprocal of impedance, is the more natural quantity to reach for whenever a parallel combination is in front of you.

## Chapter 24 — The Smith Chart, Impedance Matching, and Bias Networks
Matching a complex load to the rest of a system requires a conjugate match: equal resistance, and reactance of the opposite sign. Matching the resistive parts alone, ignoring reactance, is not good enough.

Noise-canceling headphones run on a similar idea. Canceling an unwanted wave takes producing its mirror image, matched in size and flipped in sign; a same-sized copy won't do. In a conjugate match, though, only the reactance gets flipped. The resistance stays exactly as it was, same sign and same size. Flip its sign too and you would be describing a negative resistance, an active device pumping energy in, which is a different animal entirely from the passive matching network being built here. Matching only the resistive part of a load is like generating a cancellation wave of the right loudness and the wrong phase: same amplitude, same effort, and it does nothing to stop the reflection it was supposed to cancel.

The Smith chart plots the reflection coefficient, a measure of how much of a signal bounces back instead of being absorbed, on a grid of constant-resistance circles and constant-reactance arcs, so a matching network can be designed almost by eye instead of by grinding through algebra. Two more practical numbers follow directly from the reflection coefficient: the voltage standing wave ratio, and return loss in decibels. Both quantify how badly mismatched a system is, like an echo bouncing back down a hallway wherever the hallway suddenly changes width, a louder echo meaning a more jarring mismatch. On the chart, adding a series element moves the design's point along a circle of constant resistance, while adding a shunt element moves it along a circle of constant conductance. Once that visual rule clicks, matching-network design becomes almost mechanical.

Matching can be done with simple lumped components (an L-network) or with distributed microstrip lines and stubs, plus fine adjustment using small surface-mount components. A stage also has to be biased, fed its DC power, without disturbing that carefully designed match. An RF choke presents high impedance at the frequency that matters, while a suitably chosen high-value resistor simply draws too little current to disturb anything. The choke serves where real DC current has to flow, the resistor where almost none does, and either way DC gets in while the signal path stays undisturbed.

## Chapter 25 — Calculus for Electrical Engineering
A derivative is a rate of change; an integral is an accumulation of many small changes into one running total. A car's dashboard displays both. The speedometer reads a derivative, how fast position is changing moment to moment, and the odometer reads an integral, the running total of all that changing speed since the trip began.

One difference matters. A speedometer needle can jump around as a car brakes and accelerates, but the derivatives that show up in real circuits, a capacitor's current or an inductor's voltage, are tied to physical quantities that themselves can't jump. Chapter 33 leans on that directly once transient circuits are on the table.

Capacitors and inductors are defined entirely in terms of derivatives. A capacitor's current depends on how fast its voltage is changing, and an inductor's voltage depends on how fast its current is changing. That is the whole reason a capacitor blocks a steady DC signal (nothing is changing, so no current flows) while an inductor happily passes it (nothing is changing, so it offers no opposition).

Differentiating a sinusoid has a remarkably clean effect. It brings down a factor equal to the angular frequency and shifts the phase forward by ninety degrees, exactly what phasor analysis produces, which ties the calculus directly back to the phasor machinery of Chapters 22 and 23. Definite integrals produce average values and RMS values. A first look at differential equations, left deliberately unfinished, bridges into the transient circuits of Chapters 33 and 34.

The mathematical groundwork is now laid, and each piece has a job waiting. The transient and AC chapters draw heavily on algebra, systems of equations, exponentials and logarithms, and calculus, while the material from trigonometry through impedance matching is in continuous use throughout all the AC analysis that follows. Filter design leans particularly on graphs and decibels. RF work leans on complex arithmetic and decibels more or less constantly.

The Fourier and Laplace transforms, built step by step in *The Mathematics Tower*, Storeys 17 and 18, are natural generalizations of the same phasor idea. Fourier analysis breaks a signal into many sinusoids and lets the single-frequency phasor method handle each one, and the Laplace transform extends the steadily rotating exponential to rotations that grow or decay, which brings transients into the same framework.

The reassuring bottom line is that nothing later in this book or the next requires a new mathematical foundation beyond what these chapters have laid down. What is here does get pushed harder, and applied more demandingly, as the chapters go on.

## Chapter 27 — Circuit Elements and Kirchhoff's Laws
Kirchhoff's current law is charge conservation applied at a single point: whatever current flows into a node must equal whatever flows back out, with nothing left over. A highway interchange keeps the same books. Every car entering on one ramp has to leave by some ramp or other, since cars don't pool up and vanish at the junction.

Kirchhoff's voltage law follows from potential being a well-defined function of position. Starting and ending at the same point must return you to the same potential, so the total voltage change all the way around any closed loop must add up to zero. It is the electrical version of hiking a mountain trail and finding yourself back at your own tent: however winding the route, the total climb has to equal the total descent, or you didn't actually get back to where you started.

These two laws, together with each component's own relationship between its voltage and current, are jointly sufficient to solve any circuit whatsoever, however complicated. Alongside them comes the basic vocabulary for describing a circuit's structure (nodes, branches, and loops) plus the passive sign convention. That bookkeeping rule fixes voltage polarity relative to an assumed current direction, so power calculations come out with a sign you can trust and interpret instead of puzzling it out from scratch each time: positive means the component absorbs power, negative means it delivers power.

## Chapter 28 — Equivalent Resistance and Dividers
Whenever a circuit's structure allows straightforward series and parallel reduction, that is the fastest route to a solution, and it should be tried first before reaching for anything heavier.

A voltage divider splits an applied voltage between two resistors in direct proportion to each resistor's value. The larger resistor gets the larger share, the way splitting a restaurant bill "in proportion to what everyone ordered" gives the biggest share to whoever ordered the most expensive dish.

A current divider works the other way. It splits current between two parallel resistors in proportion to the *other* resistor's value. Current favors the path of least resistance, so the branch that is easier to flow through gets the bigger share, whatever number is stamped on its neighbor. The two rules are mirror images of each other, and they are easy to swap by mistake if the relationship isn't kept straight. The same series-and-parallel reduction then extends to progressively larger resistor networks, tackled by repeated simplification with no new technique required.

## Chapter 29 — Nodal Analysis
Nodal analysis treats every node's voltage as an unknown and applies Kirchhoff's current law at each node other than a chosen reference. The resulting system of equations can be solved for any resistive network at all. The circuit doesn't even need to be drawable flat on a page without crossings, which the mesh method of the next chapter does require.

Mapping the water pressure at every junction of a plumbing system works the same way. Once you know the pressure at each junction, the flow through every pipe connecting them follows automatically, and you never have to track individual drops of water on their way.

The method requires one node to serve as the reference, or ground, against which every other voltage is measured, and the most convenient choice is usually whichever node has the most connections. A voltage source wired directly between two non-reference nodes needs special handling: the two nodes are grouped into what's called a supernode. The technique scales systematically to much larger networks, matching the matrix machinery of Chapter 17.

## Chapter 30 — Mesh Analysis
Mesh analysis is the mirror-image dual of nodal analysis. It takes the current circulating around each loop, or mesh, as the unknown and applies Kirchhoff's voltage law around each one. It works only for planar circuits, those drawable flat without any wires crossing.

Where nodal analysis tracks pressure at junctions, mesh analysis tracks traffic circulating around a set of overlapping roundabouts. The flow on any shared stretch of road is the sum of the circulating flows of every roundabout it belongs to, added up where the roundabouts overlap. A current source shared between two adjacent meshes needs its own special handling, a grouping called a supermesh, exactly mirroring the supernode trick from nodal analysis.

Source transformations swap a voltage source in series with a resistor for an equivalent current source in parallel with the same resistor, or the reverse. They are a general simplification tool that works whichever overall method you are using. Choosing between nodal and mesh analysis for a given circuit is purely a question of practical efficiency. Done properly, the two methods always agree, so the choice comes down to whichever leaves you with fewer equations to solve.

## Chapter 31 — Thevenin and Norton Equivalents
Any linear network at all, viewed from just two terminals, can be boiled down to one of two equally valid equivalents: a single voltage source in series with a single resistance (the Thevenin equivalent), or a single current source in parallel with a single resistance (the Norton equivalent). The two are related in a simple, fixed way: the Norton current is the Thevenin voltage divided by the shared resistance.

It is the electrical version of judging a stranger purely by how they act in a doorway. However elaborate the machinery behind the door (gears, motors, a maze of wiring), anyone standing outside can probe it only through those two terminals, and from out there every possible complexity collapses to the same two numbers. A black box that hands you five dollars whenever you ask, up to some limit, behaves identically whether there's a vault inside or a person quietly emptying their own wallet.

The equivalent resistance is found by switching off every independent source in the circuit and looking into the terminals from outside. The equivalent voltage is the open-circuit voltage measured at those terminals, and the equivalent current is the short-circuit current that would flow if the terminals were joined directly together. In the laboratory, you can measure a real circuit's Thevenin parameters instead of only calculating them on paper: read the open-circuit voltage, then adjust a variable load until the terminal voltage falls to half that value, and the load resistance at that point equals the Thevenin resistance.

Maximum power reaches a load precisely when the load's resistance matches the source's Thevenin resistance. At that sweet spot, only half the power the source produces reaches the load, the other half being wasted inside the source itself. That is a memorably poor deal for anyone trying to run a power grid efficiently, which is why real utilities deliberately aim for the opposite arrangement, minimizing internal resistance instead of matching it.

## Chapter 32 — Superposition and Linearity
In a linear circuit with several independent sources, each source's individual contribution to any voltage or current can be worked out entirely on its own, with every other independent source switched off, and all those separate contributions added together at the end. This is superposition, and it follows directly from the linearity of Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws.

Two people in a room each ring their own bell. You can work out how much each bell alone would rattle a particular window, one bell at a time, and the window's total rattle with both bells ringing together is simply those two answers added up. That relies entirely on the window responding in a straight-line way to sound. Once anything nonlinear enters, the individual answers stop adding up to the truth.

Power is the obvious casualty. It depends on voltage and current nonlinearly, through squaring or multiplication, so superposition does not extend to it. The shortcut tempts nearly every student at some point and reliably betrays every single one who takes it. Any dependent sources must stay switched on throughout the process, since by definition their output depends on other circuit variables that keep changing as each source's contribution is worked out. In practice superposition serves more as a conceptual tool and a way of double-checking an answer than as the fastest route to solving most circuits outright.

## Chapter 33 — Capacitors and Inductors as Circuit Elements
The defining voltage-current relationships for capacitors and inductors, met earlier, return here for circuit-analysis purposes, and what matters is their implication. Both components are dynamic: they respond to how quickly their voltage or current is changing, while a resistor responds instantly to the values themselves.

That dynamic behavior produces two continuity conditions worth knowing cold. A capacitor's voltage and an inductor's current can never jump instantaneously, however abruptly a switch is thrown elsewhere in the circuit, and that single fact is the starting point for all the transient analysis to come.

A capacitor's voltage behaves like the water level in a bathtub. However hard you yank the tap open or slam it shut, the level can only rise or fall smoothly, never teleport from one height to another, since that would require moving a finite amount of water in no time at all. An inductor's current behaves the same way for the same underlying reason. Changing it instantly would demand an infinite voltage spike to push the change through in zero time, and infinite voltage spikes are not on the menu.

The energy stored in each component appears again, expressed through capacitance and voltage for the capacitor, and through inductance and current for the inductor. Inductors, usefully, combine the way resistors do (series values add directly, parallel values add by reciprocal), while capacitors combine the opposite way, mirroring the resistor-capacitor contrast of Chapters 6 and 8.

## Chapter 34 — First-Order Transients: RC and RL Circuits
After a sudden switching event, an RC or RL circuit doesn't jump to its new steady state. It relaxes there smoothly along an exponential curve, and the speed of that relaxation is governed by a single number called the time constant, set by resistance and capacitance together in an RC circuit, or by inductance divided by resistance in an RL circuit.

A cup of coffee cooling on a counter follows the same shape of curve. It doesn't cool at a constant rate and then stop at room temperature. It cools fastest at the start, while it's hottest, and more and more gradually as it nears the surrounding temperature, always approaching and, strictly, never quite arriving. The circuit is more disciplined than the coffee: its time constant is one precise number derived from the components themselves.

Remarkably, the same general exponential formula describes the transient of any quantity in any such circuit, whatever the specific topology, once its starting value, its final value, and the time constant are known. A handful of settling benchmarks are worth memorizing outright. After one time constant the response is 63 percent of the way to its final value, after two time constants 86 percent, after three 95 percent, and after five 99.3 percent. Technically it never finishes, since an exponential decay never quite reaches zero. It just gets embarrassingly close and, for all practical purposes, calls it done.

Capacitor charging and discharging both follow this pattern, as do the equivalent buildup and decay of current in an inductor. Add a second energy-storing element and the circuit becomes second-order, which is where the resonance of Chapter 37 begins.

## Chapter 35 — Sinusoidal Steady-State Analysis
With impedance defined back in Chapter 23, every DC analysis technique from Chapters 27 through 32 carries over to AC circuits unchanged: Kirchhoff's two laws, series and parallel combination, voltage and current dividers, nodal analysis, mesh analysis, Thevenin and Norton equivalents, and superposition. Simply replace ordinary real resistances with complex impedances throughout.

AC voltages and currents are normally quoted as RMS values instead of peak amplitudes, because RMS is what correlates with real heating and power. The "120 volts" printed on a household outlet is an RMS figure, while the wire actually peaks at about 170 volts, in the same way a trip is described by its average speed and not by the top speed briefly touched while passing another car. Looking back, all the DC machinery of the earlier chapters was a special case all along of a more general AC framework, waiting only for impedance to be introduced.

## Chapter 36 — AC Power and Power Factor
Average power delivered to an AC load depends on the RMS voltage, the RMS current, and the cosine of the phase angle between them. That cosine is called the power factor, and it captures how well voltage and current are lined up in time with each other.

Apparent power is RMS voltage multiplied by RMS current, with no regard for phase at all, and is measured in volt-amps instead of watts. Reactive power is the portion that sloshes back and forth without doing useful work, measured in a unit called the VAR. The three quantities, real, reactive, and apparent power, relate the way the three sides of a right triangle do: apparent power is the hypotenuse, real and reactive power are the two legs, and they combine by the Pythagorean rule instead of by simple addition.

A pint with a lot of foam on top covers one part of that story, the part about what you're charged for versus what's actually useful. Order a pint and you pay for the whole glass, foam included, yet only the liquid underneath quenches anyone's thirst. Apparent power is the whole glass, real power is the liquid you drink, and reactive power is the foam, taking up real space and paid for all the same, but delivering nothing useful once it's there. (A glass's volume is liquid plus foam added directly; for the arithmetic of power, trust the triangle.)

Power factor, in plain terms, is the fraction of the apparent power flowing through a circuit that is genuinely useful real power, as opposed to reactive power exchanged back and forth and never delivered anywhere. It is the fraction of the glass that's actually beer. A low power factor means more current has to flow to deliver the same real power, and that extra current causes correspondingly larger losses in the wires carrying it. That is the entirely practical, financial reason power-factor correction matters so much in industrial settings, where the electricity bill quietly depends on it.

## Chapter 37 — Resonance, Frequency Response, and Transformers
In a series circuit combining resistance, inductance, and capacitance, there is one particular frequency, the resonant frequency, at which the inductor's and capacitor's opposing effects cancel exactly. What remains is a purely resistive impedance, and current flows as strongly as the circuit is capable of.

It is the electrical cousin of pushing a playground swing. Push at any old rate and you'll get some motion, but push at the swing's own natural rhythm and even a gentle, well-timed push builds a large swinging arc, since each push arrives just when the swing is ready to receive it. Push wildly out of step, and your pushes start fighting the swing's motion instead of building on it, the same clash of timing that lets an inductor and a capacitor cancel each other only at one matched frequency.

The quality factor is a single number measuring how sharply peaked that resonance is. A high quality factor means a narrow, precise peak, and the circuit's bandwidth (how wide a range of frequencies it responds strongly to) is inversely related to it. A swing with very little friction responds dramatically to pushes right on its rhythm and barely at all to anything off-beat, while a rusty, sluggish swing responds a little to a much wider range of push rates but never builds much of an arc at any of them.

Mutual inductance between two coils gives rise to the ideal transformer, which trades voltage for current, or the reverse, in exact proportion to the ratio of turns on its two windings, while leaving the total apparent power flowing through it unchanged. A lever makes the same kind of trade, force for distance with the work unchanged: a longer arm lets you lift a heavier weight with less force, but only by pushing your own end that much farther. That trade is the entire basis of high-voltage, long-distance power transmission, since stepping voltage up and current down correspondingly slashes transmission losses. The simple low-pass and high-pass RC filters seen earlier return at the end, as a bridge into the dedicated filter chapter of *Core Circuits and Components*.

## Chapter 38 — Where the Circuits Go Next
Every technique developed so far applies only to linear circuits built from ideal components, a limitation that has mostly gone unstated until now. The restriction is meant to be temporary. Diodes and transistors are nonlinear, and the band physics that makes them work is in Physics, Chapter 40. Their small-signal behavior around a fixed operating point reduces right back to precisely the linear analysis built here, so none of it, reassuringly, was wasted effort.

The bridge is linearization: replacing a nonlinear device's curving behavior with a straight-line approximation around one operating point, the foundation of small-signal analysis, which *Core Circuits and Components* puts to work in its first chapter. It is the same trick as approximating a curving hillside by the flat, tilted plank you'd feel under your feet standing at just one spot on it. The plank is a fair description for a small step in any direction from where you stand, and a worse and worse one the farther you wander.

## Appendix E — A Practical Circuit-Analysis Checklist
When a circuit analysis has gone wrong somewhere and the mistake needs tracking down, this checklist works regardless of which method you used. It runs in three phases: before writing any equations, while solving them, and after reaching a solution. A mechanic uses the same discipline on a car that won't start, checking the obvious things before popping the hood, working through the system methodically once it's open, and test-driving the result afterward instead of declaring victory the moment the engine turns over.

**Before starting.** Identify every node correctly. Mark assumed current directions and voltage polarities consistently, according to the passive sign convention. Pick a sensible reference node. Confirm a circuit is planar before attempting mesh analysis on it, and stay alert for any supernodes or supermeshes lurking in the topology.

**While solving.** Count equations against unknowns to make sure the system is actually solvable. Get every impedance right, for each resistor, inductor, and capacitor, before plugging in any numbers. Add and subtract complex numbers in rectangular form, and multiply and divide them in polar form; mixing up the two forms is a reliable source of silly errors.

**Afterward.** A negative branch current is not automatically a mistake. It usually just means current flows opposite to the direction you originally assumed. Verify a solved answer by plugging the numbers back into Kirchhoff's laws, and cross-check it with an entirely independent method, for instance comparing a Thevenin-equivalent answer against a nodal-analysis answer. For any circuit with multiple sources, superpose the voltages and currents first and compute power only once, from the final totals, never by adding up powers computed separately for each individual source.

Every method covered here ultimately reduces to Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws from Chapter 27. So whenever a solution starts to feel shaky, skip the hunt for a fancier technique. Go back to those two laws and make the rest of the machinery earn back your trust, one carefully checked step at a time.

# Core Circuits and Components

Real components are ideal ones with a list of excuses. Here are the circuits worth building and the excuses worth knowing, from bias points and logic gates to resistors that are quietly part inductor.

*Status: Settled. The excuses are printed on the datasheets.*

## Chapter 1 — Analog Electronics
Real analog design adds two new ingredients to the idealized circuit theory of *Foundations*: signals that carry meaningful information, and circuits deliberately biased into a particular operating condition instead of being left to do whatever the applied voltage dictates.

A DC operating point, also called the bias point or quiescent point, is the fixed voltage or current a stage settles at before any smaller signal comes along to ride on top of it. A singer chooses a comfortable resting pitch to hum before a song starts, so the melody that follows has room to rise and fall around it. Headroom is the distance from that bias point to whichever supply rail is nearer, and it limits the largest signal a stage can pass undistorted. Push a signal past that limit and the peaks nearest the rail are clipped off first, like a singer who picked too high a resting note and finds the top of every high note flattening off instead of ringing true.

Small-signal behavior is the working assumption that a circuit's response to a sufficiently small signal can be treated as linear near one particular operating point, even though the underlying device is nothing like linear across its full range. It is a promise about how small the signal needs to stay; the device itself has not secretly become well-behaved. This is the tilted-plank trick from the very end of *Foundations*, finally put to real work. The plank stands in for the curving hillside only for a small step near the spot where you're standing, and small-signal analysis makes that same bargain explicit and gives it a name.

Gain and headroom trade off against each other for any fixed pair of supply rails. A higher-gain stage clips sooner as its input signal grows, so cranking up the gain simply means the stage runs out of usable range faster.

## Chapter 2 — Digital Electronics
A digital signal deliberately restricts itself to a small handful of voltage ranges, almost always exactly two, instead of a continuous spread of values. Contrary to popular belief, that doesn't make it immune to noise; no real signal ever is. What it does is recover cleanly at every logic gate along the way, through a process called regeneration. Each gate reads a somewhat fuzzy, noise-corrupted input and outputs a fresh, full-strength, clean level on the other side, resetting the noise budget at every stage.

Compare photocopying a photocopy with retyping a document from scratch at every step. A chain of copies degrades a little more with every generation, smudges compounding on smudges. A chain of people who each read a smudged page and retype a crisp new copy for the next person loses nothing at all, provided each reader can still make out the words well enough to retype them correctly.

A deliberate forbidden zone sits between the recognized high and low voltage ranges, a no-man's-land a valid signal is never supposed to occupy. Noise margin quantifies how much noise a signal can tolerate before it risks being misread, separately on the high side and the low side, and classic TTL logic allots a modest but workable margin on each side. The margins set exactly how smudged a page can get before a reader starts guessing at words instead of reading them.

Mixing logic families that run on different supply voltages, say a 3.3-volt chip driving inputs designed for 5-volt logic, can produce asymmetric noise margins, more forgiving in one direction than the other, and incompatible thresholds like that can cause intermittent faults. Underneath it all, a logic gate is built from the same kind of amplifying devices as an analog stage. What makes it "digital" is simply that it is driven hard between two extremes instead of being biased to sit in a linear region.

## Chapter 3 — Logic and Boolean Algebra
Every Boolean expression, however complicated it looks, reduces to three basic operations: AND, OR, and NOT. They are the same three words you'd use telling someone when to flip a light switch ("turn it on if the room is dark and someone is home, or if it's after nine"), stripped of every trace of ambiguity a spoken sentence still carries. A truth table, patiently listing every possible combination of inputs, is the final and unarguable authority on whether two different-looking expressions are in fact equal.

A handful of simplification laws do most of the practical work. The distributive law runs in both directions, where ordinary algebra allows only one: AND distributes over OR, and OR also distributes over AND. Absorption collapses a longer term harmlessly into a shorter one already present. A pair of rules, De Morgan's laws, describes how negation interacts with AND and OR, turning each into the other. The consensus theorem adds one more trick: if some variable appears uncomplemented in one term and complemented in another, a third term built from what's left over in both of those terms is redundant and can be dropped.

Those same negation rules are why NAND and NOR gates each qualify as "universal": an entire digital system of any complexity can in principle be built from nothing but one type of gate, repeated as often as needed. The Karnaugh map, a grid-based visual technique, offers a shortcut to the same simplified expressions for problems of up to about four or five variables. To prove two Boolean expressions equal, either match their truth tables across every row or derive one from the other with the algebraic laws. A quick spot-check of a few rows proves nothing at all.

## Chapter 4 — Combinational and Sequential Circuits
Combinational circuits are the easy case: their output depends only on whatever the inputs are right now, with no memory of the past. Sequential circuits are the harder and more interesting case: their output depends on present inputs plus the circuit's own history, which requires some kind of bistable element capable of remembering a past state.

A half adder produces a sum bit and a carry bit from two input bits but, true to its rather deflating name, can't accept a carry coming in from a previous stage. A full adder fixes that with a third, carry-in input, and full adders can be chained end to end to build an adder for numbers of any bit width you like.

The SR latch, built from either NOR gates (active-high) or NAND gates (active-low), is the simplest bistable memory element available, and both versions share the same flaw. One particular combination of inputs is strictly forbidden. It doesn't leave the output undefined; it deterministically forces both the output and its complement to the same value, violating the one thing a pair of "complementary" outputs is supposed to guarantee, that they never agree. What is actually undefined is what happens next. Release both inputs to their inactive level at exactly the same instant, and the state the latch settles into becomes a genuine race between its two gates, decided by microscopic differences no datasheet promises to specify.

Far more common in practice is the D flip-flop, with a single data input and edge-triggered behavior, which underlies the whole discipline of synchronous digital design. Real flip-flops need a brief window of setup and hold time around the clock edge, during which the input data must stay stable or the result becomes unpredictable. A photographer needs the subject to hold still just before and just after the shutter clicks; move too soon or too late and the picture comes out blurred, neither cleanly one thing nor the other.

Registers (a bank of D flip-flops sharing one clock), counters (registers that increment themselves automatically), and shift registers (flip-flops chained together so data marches along one stage per clock tick) are the three basic sequential building blocks everything else is constructed from. Propagation delay, clock skew, metastability, fan-out, and the practical differences between logic families such as TTL, CMOS, and LVDS are real engineering issues, left for a later, dedicated book.

## Chapter 5 — Operational Amplifiers
The op-amp is treated as an idealized element in the same spirit *Foundations* treated resistors, inductors, and capacitors: infinite open-loop gain, infinite input impedance, and zero output impedance. None of that is literally true of any real chip, but together the idealizations make analysis vastly simpler while staying close enough to reality to be useful.

From them follow the two so-called golden rules, valid only when the op-amp is wired with negative feedback. Its two inputs sit at the same voltage, a "virtual short" that is a voltage coincidence enforced by the feedback loop and no actual electrical connection. And no current flows into either input. Think of two obsessively attentive dancers, each constantly adjusting to mirror the other, so that from across the room they always appear to stand in the same spot, without ever touching or being tied together by so much as a string. The mirroring holds only because each dancer is actively watching and correcting. Break the feedback and the two positions drift apart immediately, which is exactly what happens to an op-amp's virtual short the moment its feedback loop is removed.

The inverting amplifier produces an inverted, scaled copy of the input, using a virtual ground that appears at the inverting input as a direct consequence of the golden rules. The non-inverting amplifier produces a scaled copy without inversion, and its gain can never drop below one: it can only amplify, never attenuate.

Real op-amps fall short of the ideal in specific, well-documented ways. A roughly constant gain-bandwidth product lets you trade gain for usable frequency range but never escape the trade entirely. Slew rate imposes a hard ceiling on how fast the output can change. That limit is distinct from headroom, and it shows up as visible distortion on large, fast signals instead of simple clipping. A car's top acceleration works the same way: mash the pedal as hard as you like, and demanding a faster launch than the engine can deliver doesn't clip the car's speed at some limit; the actual speed just lags visibly behind whatever the driver is asking for. Input offset voltage means the output isn't quite zero even when both inputs are perfectly matched, a small but sometimes troublesome imperfection.

Take away the feedback loop altogether, or route it to the wrong input, and an op-amp abandons all pretense of good behavior and saturates hard against one supply rail or the other. The golden rules describe a well-designed feedback circuit; the chip sitting there on its own has no such virtue.

## Chapter 6 — Passive and Active Filters
First-order RC low-pass and high-pass filters share the same formula for their corner frequency, differing only in which frequencies they let through, and past that corner the response rolls off at a fixed, well-known rate of 20 decibels per decade. Cascading several stages raises the overall filter order and steepens the roll-off by that same amount for every additional stage, at the obvious cost of a more complicated circuit.

Band-pass and band-stop (notch) filters target a specific range of frequencies instead of a single simple cutoff. The series RLC resonant circuit from *Foundations*, Chapter 37, provides both behaviors, simply by wiring the same three components differently.

An active filter adds an op-amp, both to isolate the filter's frequency response from whatever load gets connected afterward and to add genuine gain. No purely passive network of resistors, inductors, and capacitors can do that on its own, since passive components can only remove energy from a signal passing through them, never add any. A tollbooth can slow traffic down or turn some of it away, but it never speeds a car up past the rate it was already traveling. A genuine boost takes an engine doing real work, which is the role the op-amp's power supply plays here.

"Active" does not automatically mean "better," whatever the name suggests. Active filters need their own power supply and are bounded by the op-amp's voltage range, while passive filters need neither and can cheerfully handle much larger signals. Real-world component selection means solving for the mathematically exact value needed, rounding to whatever standard E-series value is actually available to buy, and checking afterward that the resulting small error is one you can live with.

## Chapter 7 — Resistors
Real resistors come in several distinct flavors, each with its own trade-offs. Carbon film and metal film types are the ordinary workhorses of the industry, with metal film offering tighter tolerance and lower electrical noise. Wirewound types handle the highest power levels but carry real, unwanted parasitic inductance baked into their construction. Surface-mount chip resistors dominate modern designs because they are so small.

Tolerance ratings of plus or minus one, five, or ten percent are common, and the E-series of standard values is spaced to roughly match the tolerance band, so tighter-tolerance product lines stock proportionally more distinct values to fill in the gaps. Power rating, commonly an eighth, a quarter, or half a watt for small parts, sets a hard thermal limit. Good design always leaves real margin below it, since the rating assumes a specific ambient temperature and derates further as things get hotter around it. Temperature coefficient, measured in parts per million per degree, quantifies how much a resistor's value drifts as temperature changes, and metal film types comfortably outperform carbon film on this measure.

At high enough frequency, every real resistor turns out to carry some parasitic inductance (wirewound types especially) and some parasitic capacitance too, departing from pure textbook resistance. Push any real component hard enough in frequency and it is always secretly a little of the other two as well, much as a supposedly rigid ruler flexes if you push on it hard enough, or a "waterproof" watch is waterproof only down to some quietly assumed depth. The textbook resistor, inductor, and capacitor are ideals nothing actually achieves. Real parts are good-enough approximations of them within whatever range of frequency and power the datasheet had the decency to specify.

## Chapter 8 — Capacitors
Ceramic capacitors split cleanly into two classes. Class 1 types (commonly labeled C0G or NP0) hold their rated capacitance stable regardless of applied voltage or temperature. Class 2 types (X7R, Y5V, and similar) pack far more capacitance into the same size at the cost of real DC-bias derating. A Class 2 part can legitimately lose half its rated capacitance once it sits at its rated voltage, a documented, specified characteristic rather than a manufacturing defect.

Electrolytic capacitors, aluminum or tantalum, reach much higher capacitance for a given size than ceramics can manage, but they are polarized. Wire one in backwards and it doesn't simply sulk; it can fail dramatically enough to leave an actual mark on the ceiling. Polarity is not a suggestion. Film capacitors are unpolarized and stable, a comfortable middle ground whenever a ceramic's voltage sensitivity or an electrolytic's polarity requirement is more trouble than it's worth. Every capacitor, of whatever type, carries a maximum voltage rating, and exceeding it risks permanent dielectric breakdown, a failure with no way back.

Real capacitors also carry an equivalent series resistance and an equivalent series inductance. Above a frequency called the self-resonant frequency, that parasitic inductance takes over completely and the part stops behaving like a capacitor at all. A rubber band stretches obediently the harder you pull, right up until it's stretched so far that it stiffens and fights back like a rigid rod: pull gently and it's a spring, pull hard enough and the spring behavior runs out. A capacitor's usefulness as a capacitor runs out the same way once frequency climbs past its self-resonant point. That matters enormously when choosing a bypass or decoupling capacitor for a real circuit, where getting the self-resonant frequency wrong defeats the entire purpose of putting the capacitor there.

## Chapter 9 — Inductors
Real inductors come in a few distinct flavors as well. Air-core types are perfectly linear and immune to saturation, but deliver comparatively low inductance for their size. Ferrite- or iron-core types deliver much higher inductance in the same space, at the cost of being vulnerable to saturation. Toroidal types close the magnetic path back on itself, which both boosts the inductance obtained per turn of wire and sharply cuts the stray field and electromagnetic interference leaking into the surrounding circuit.

Core saturation happens once a ferromagnetic core's internal magnetic domains have all fully aligned. Beyond that point, pushing more current through no longer raises the magnetic flux proportionally, and the inductance collapses with impeccably poor timing, most often during a current surge, precisely when the circuit depends on that inductance most. The falloff sharpens progressively instead of arriving as one clean threshold. A sponge behaves the same way. Dry, it soaks up more with every bit of water poured on, but once every fiber is soaked through, pouring on more does nothing further. The sponge, like the saturated core, has simply run out of unused capacity to respond with.

Hysteresis losses and eddy-current losses both dissipate energy as heat inside any real magnetic core, on every cycle. DC resistance is the winding's own inherent resistance. The quality factor measures how efficiently an inductor stores energy compared with how much it dissipates, and that figure eventually falls off at very high frequency because of skin effect and the onset of self-resonance. Like capacitors, real inductors have their own self-resonant frequency, where the winding's parasitic capacitance resonates with the intended inductance. Above it, they too stop behaving like the component they're supposed to be.

That completes the practical circuits: components that mostly behave, except when they don't.
