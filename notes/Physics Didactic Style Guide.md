#  Didactic Style Guide

To carry a reader from a falling stone to a Feynman diagram.

---

## 1. The principle, in one sentence

**Never define a thing before the reader has felt the need for it, and never
use a symbol before a measurement, a table, or a thought experiment has
already made the point.**

Everything below is a consequence of that sentence.

The failure mode this guards against is the textbook opening that names the
object first and earns it later: an epigraph, then straight into *"the
electric field at a point is the force per unit charge that would be
experienced by a positive test charge placed there."* That sentence is
correct. It is useless to someone meeting fields for the first time,
because it answers a question the reader has not yet been made to ask.

The same failure, one floor up: *"A particle's state is a vector in a
Hilbert space."* Correct. Dead on arrival.

---

## 2. Who the reader is

**Formally an equal. In practice, somebody who does not yet know anything
about the subject.**

Both halves matter and they are not in tension. The first governs tone:
never talk down, never write *as you surely know*, never apologise for
explaining. The second governs pace: assume nothing has been met before,
and build it.

The asymmetry is what settles every judgement call. Being told something
you already knew costs a reader four seconds. Being hurried past something
you did not know costs them the rest of the chapter, and sometimes the
book.

### The writer cannot feel the difficulty

Whoever writes a passage already understands it. That understanding is
invisible from the inside, and it quietly rewrites every estimate of what
needs saying. The feeling that *this is surely enough introduction* is not
a neutral observation — it is the opinion of the one person in the exchange
who cannot be confused by the material.

A special physics version of the same trap: the writer has lived so long
inside *F = ma*, *E = −∇V*, and *iħ ∂ψ/∂t* that those strings feel like
plain language. They are not. Compensate by rule instead:

- **If a passage feels slightly too slow to you, it is about right.** If it
  feels exactly right to you, it is already too fast.
- **Assume more introduction than seems necessary.** The on-ramps that work
  run between 380 and 660 words before the formal statement arrives. That
  is the calibration, and it is longer than instinct suggests.
- **One new thing per paragraph** wherever the material is hard. Two is a
  paragraph that will have to be read twice.
- **One new object per room** at first encounter. Force and field on the
  same page is two rooms pretending to be one.

### Nothing may be assumed in silence

Every term used has been built earlier in the book, or is built here, or is
signposted explicitly as coming later. There is no fourth option.

Two habits catch most breaches:

- When you write *recall that*, stop and check it was ever actually said.
  Often it was not, and *recall* is doing the work of a definition that
  never happened. *Recall that mass and energy are equivalent* is the
  classic offender.
- When you use a word the reader would have to look up — *Lagrangian*,
  *four-vector*, *eigenstate*, *gauge*, *renormalisation* — either it was
  introduced already, or it is introduced in the same sentence.

### Units are part of the sentence

A number without a unit is an unfinished thought. Write 9.8 m/s², not 9.8,
unless the unit has been fixed in the line above and repeating it would
clog the table. Never switch silently from SI to natural units. When ħ = c
= 1 arrives, say so in a sentence, say what it costs, and say where the
ordinary units live if the reader wants them back.

### What "equal" rules out

Not the explaining. The dismissiveness:

- *clearly, obviously, simply, just, of course, it is easy to see* — banned
  at a hinge, as the register section below repeats.
- *as you know*, *you will remember*, *needless to say* — each tells a
  reader who does not know that they ought to have.
- Apologising for pace: *labouring the point*, *at the risk of stating the
  obvious*. Slowness is not a fault to be flagged. Go slowly and say
  nothing about it.
- *just a mathematical trick*, *merely a convention*, *nothing but
  bookkeeping* — each is an attempt to shrink an idea the reader is still
  meeting. If it were nothing, it would not need a room.

A reader who already knows the material can skim four paragraphs in ten
seconds. A reader who does not cannot reconstruct four paragraphs that were
never written. Write for the second one; the first is never harmed.

## 3. The seven moves

Use these in order. Each move has a job, and skipping one is what makes an
explanation feel like it started in the middle.

Physics has two extra temptations the mathematics rooms did not. The first
is to open with the great name (*Newton said*, *Einstein realised*). The
name can wait until the idea exists; otherwise the reader is asked to
revere a sentence they cannot yet use. The second is to open with the
apparatus (*consider a test charge*, *imagine an inertial observer*).
Apparatus is Move 5 material. Move 1 is something the reader already owns
without a laboratory.

### Move 1 — Start from something the reader already owns

Open with a thing the reader could have done at primary school, or can see
out of a window, or has felt with their own hands. Not a recap of an
earlier floor: an actual possession.

> *Velocity:* you walk to the shop. Distance over time is how fast you
> went. You have used that fraction since you were small.
>
> *Force:* a shopping trolley takes a shove to get moving and another shove
> to keep it moving on a rough floor.
>
> *Energy / work:* lift a bag of shopping onto the counter. The heavier the
> bag, or the higher the counter, the more it costs you.
>
> *Electric force:* two socks from the dryer cling. Two like charges, if
> you have ever rubbed a balloon, push apart.
>
> *Time and simultaneity:* two claps, one near and one far. You heard the
> near one first. That is all.
>
> *Quantum chance:* a dropped coin lands heads or tails. You can say
> one-half. You cannot say which toss this one will be.

The test: could a reader who skipped the previous floors still follow the
first paragraph? If not, it is not Move 1 yet. "Recall the Lorentz force
from Floor 6" is not Move 1.

### Move 2 — Let the familiar thing break

Show the possession working, then show it failing. The failure must be
visible, not asserted.

> *Velocity:* a steady walk has one speed for the whole trip. Now sprint,
> then dawdle. "Ask how fast you were going and there is no longer a
> single answer."
>
> *Force:* the same shove on a tiled floor and on ice does two different
> things. The shove cannot be the whole story; the floor is in it too.
>
> *Work against a spring:* the first centimetre is cheap and the tenth is
> not. Force times distance, with one force, has nothing left to say.
>
> *Gravity as a force at a distance:* it works for the apple. It does not
> tell you what is happening in the empty space between the apple and the
> Earth, and it does not tell you why light, which has no mass, still
> bends.
>
> *Adding speeds:* walk at 5 km/h down a train that does 100 km/h and the
> ground says 105. Send a torch beam down the same train and the ground
> does not say *c + 100*. The addition rule has broken.
>
> *A particle with a path:* a marble through one slit makes a mark. Open a
> second slit and the marks on the far wall are not the sum of two marble
> stories. The path picture has broken.

This move creates the need. Machinery introduced before the need is felt
reads as arbitrary; the same machinery after it reads as a rescue.

### Move 3 — Change the question, out loud

Say explicitly what the new question is. Do not let the reader infer it.

> "Not 'how fast was the trip', but 'how fast at this one moment'?"
>
> "Not 'how hard did you push', but 'what did the push *do*, once the floor
> and the mass have had their say'?"
>
> "Not 'what force acts on this charge', but 'what is the condition of the
> space, so that any charge placed here would feel that force'?"
>
> "Not 'what time is it', but 'whose clock, and in which frame'?"
>
> "Not 'which slit did it go through', but 'what number do I square to get
> the chance of a mark here'?"

One sentence. It is the hinge of the whole passage, and it should be short
enough to be remembered.

### Move 4 — State the obstacle so it can be *felt*

Name the reason the new question cannot be answered directly, in concrete
terms, and do not hurry past it.

> "At a single instant you have not gone anywhere. The distance is zero and
> the time is zero, and zero divided by zero tells you precisely nothing."
>
> "A force at a distance names the two objects and is silent about the
> space between them. Ask what is *there*, in the gap, and the force law
> has no answer."
>
> "If every observer measures the same speed of light, then two claps that
> are simultaneous on the platform are not simultaneous on the train. You
> cannot keep both 'one public now' and 'one public *c*'."
>
> "To know which slit is to put a detector there. Put the detector there
> and the pattern on the far wall changes. The question you wanted to ask
> is not a question the experiment will answer and still remain the same
> experiment."

This is the move most often skipped, and skipping it is expensive. A
resolution is only satisfying in proportion to how real the problem felt.
If the reader does not feel the 0/0 wall, the limit looks like a ritual.
If the reader does not feel the two-slit wall, the wavefunction looks like
decoration.

Signal it. A phrase like *"and it is worth feeling this properly before we
get rid of it"* tells the reader the discomfort is intentional.

Do not replace the obstacle with a slogan. "Energy is conserved" is not an
obstacle. "You cannot say where the energy went without inventing a new
book, the field" is an obstacle.

### Move 5 — Offer the trick, and admit it looks like cheating

Give the idea in plain words before any notation.

> "The way round it looks like a cheat and turns out not to be one. Do not
> try to measure speed at the instant. Measure from the instant to a moment
> a little later, where there is a real distance and a real time to
> divide."
>
> "Stop asking the empty space to name a force. Ask it to name a *field* —
> a readiness to push, sitting there whether or not you have placed a
> charge yet."
>
> "Stop attacking the thing you cannot do — name one public time for both
> platforms — and replace it with a great many things you can: each
> observer keeps a clock, and a rule converts one clock into the other."
>
> "Stop asking which path. Assign a small complex number to each path you
> can write down, add them, and square the length of the sum. The square
> is the chance. The path itself is not recovered."

Naming the apparent cheat disarms the reader's suspicion instead of leaving
it to fester. Physics is full of moves that look like cheating: virtual
work, Gaussian surfaces, inertial frames that are only locally inertial,
renormalisation. The guide is the same each time — say that it looks
illegal, then show the measurement it correctly predicts.

### Move 6 — Numbers before symbols

A short table the reader can verify by hand. Five lines is plenty. The
arithmetic must do the persuading; the notation arrives later only to
record what was already believed.

Use quantities a reader can picture: metres, seconds, kilograms, newtons,
joules, volts. Natural units belong after the idea has landed, not in the
on-ramp.

```
Instantaneous speed, walking then sprinting
Δt = 1 s      Δs = 1.4 m     speed = 1.4 m/s
Δt = 0.5 s    Δs = 0.8 m     speed = 1.6 m/s
Δt = 0.1 s    Δs = 0.17 m    speed = 1.7 m/s
Δt = 0.01 s   Δs = 0.0172 m  speed = 1.72 m/s
Δt = 0.001 s  Δs = 0.001723 m speed = 1.723 m/s
```

```
Time dilation, a moving clock
v = 0            factor = 1         1.000 μs stays 1.000 μs
v = 0.6c         factor = 1.25      1.000 μs is seen as 1.250 μs
v = 0.8c         factor = 1.667     1.000 μs is seen as 1.667 μs
v = 0.98c        factor = 5.025     1.000 μs is seen as 5.025 μs
v = 0.999c       factor = 22.37     1.000 μs is seen as 22.37 μs
```

```
Photoelectric threshold, one metal
light of 1.8 eV per photon     no electrons leave
light of 2.0 eV per photon     no electrons leave
light of 2.2 eV per photon     electrons leave, leftover 0.1 eV
light of 2.5 eV per photon     electrons leave, leftover 0.4 eV
brighter 1.8 eV light          still none leave
```

Then — and this is not optional — **say what the numbers never do**:

> "Notice what they never do: they never arrive. Every one of those speeds
> was measured across a real stretch of time, so every one of them is the
> speed of an interval, not of an instant. Yet 1.73 m/s is plainly the
> number they are aiming at."
>
> "Notice what they never do: they never let the moving clock agree with
> the platform clock, except at v = 0. The factor grows without a ceiling.
> There is no speed at which 'almost at rest' returns."
>
> "Notice what they never do: they never let dim light above the threshold
> fail, and they never let bright light below the threshold succeed.
> Brightness was the wrong knob. Frequency was the knob."

Concealing non-attainment to keep things simple is a false economy. It is
the conceptual core, and a reader who is not told now will be confused
later by asymptotic freedom, by event horizons, by virtual particles, and
by every limiting argument in the book.

Verify the arithmetic exactly — as fractions and exact roots where they
exist, not as sloppy floating point — before it goes in. γ at 0.8c is
5/3, not "about 1.67". Every table  should be reproducible by
a reader with a pencil and, where needed, a stated value of *c* or *h*.

### Move 7 — Name it, then translate the notation

Name the object only once it exists, and keep the naming flat.

> "That is velocity at an instant, and nothing more alarming is going on.
> It is distance over time — the same fraction you have used since school —
> with one extra move at the end."
>
> "That readiness in the space is the electric field. It is not a new kind
> of stuff in the everyday sense. It is a rule that sits at each point and
> waits for a charge."
>
> "That conversion between clocks is time dilation. The moving clock is
> not broken. The idea that there was one public time for both clocks was
> broken."
>
> "That squared length is the probability. The complex number you added up
> is the amplitude. Nothing in the room has been observed except the mark
> on the far wall."

Then translate into whatever the reader arrived with.

> "The shrinking interval, which school calls Δt, is written dt from here
> on when we mean the limit — so Δs/Δt → ds/dt is two names for the same
> instruction."
>
> "School writes the push as F = ma. From here on the same fact is also
> written as the rate of change of momentum, F = dp/dt, because that form
> survives when mass does not stay constant."
>
> "The energy of a photon, which the table listed in eV, is written E = hf
> from here on. h is not a new kind of energy. It is the conversion
> factor that turns a frequency into that energy."
>
> "The object school called a wavefunction, ψ, is the amplitude as a
> function of x. |ψ|² is the thing the table was already computing: the
> chance of a mark in a small interval."

## 4. The structural rule: intuition and proof must share numbers

The informal table and the formal derivation that follows **must use the
same example and reach the same number.**

- An on-ramp that watches a falling stone from rest for 3 s and reads
  44.1 m must be the same stone that the next room integrates under
  a = 9.8 m/s² to get ½gt² = 44.1 m.
- An on-ramp that takes four strips under a v–t graph and gets 18 m must
  be the same graph the next room integrates to 18 m.
- An on-ramp that sends a muon at 0.994c across 10 km of atmosphere and
  watches it survive must be the same muon the next room treats with
  γ and proper time.
- An on-ramp that counts 2.2 eV photons on a metal with work function
  2.1 eV and leftover 0.1 eV must be the same metal the next room writes
  as K_max = hf − φ.

This costs nothing and buys a great deal: the reader meets the answer
twice, once as a belief and once as a derivation, and the two rooms lock
together instead of each starting cold. When writing a new on-ramp,
**look at what the next room derives and work backwards from its numbers.**

Thought experiments are allowed as the "table" when a real table would
lie. Relativity and quantum mechanics often have no kitchen-table
apparatus. The numbers must still be checkable: distances, times, γ
factors, electronvolts, counts.

Verify every figure exactly before it goes in. Do not write 3 × 10⁸ m/s
in one line and c in the next if the arithmetic needs a consistent choice.
State the choice once.

## 5. Forward references

When a passage hand-waves, say so and say where the rigour lives.

> "Room 14.2 does this properly, and gets 44.1 m again."
>
> "Room 22.3 drops the assumption that the field is static."
>
> "Room 31.2 does the Lorentz transformation in full, and the 5.025 μs
> comes back."
>
> "Room 41.4 replaces the slogan 'the particle takes every path' with the
> actual sum, and shows where the extra paths cancel."

This converts a gap into a promise. It also means the informal passage can
stay informal without apology, which keeps it readable.

Cross-references must point at rooms that exist and contain what is
claimed. A reference to a derivation the book never gives is worse than no
reference. Physics is especially prone to this with "it can be shown that"
and "Maxwell's equations imply." If the implication is not done yet,
do not cite it as if it were.

Historical names are forward-and-back references of a different kind. Use
them after the idea exists, as a label, not as an explanation. "Gauss's
law" is a name. It is not a reason.

## 6. Register

- **Short sentences at the point of difficulty.** Long ones are fine for
  scenery, never for the hinge.
- **One new idea per paragraph** wherever the material is hard.
- **Concrete nouns.** Stones, trolleys, springs, balloons, train
  platforms, slits, Geiger clicks, muon tracks, clock towers. Abstraction
  is what the reader is trying to reach, not what they should wade
  through to get there.
- **Dry humour is welcome; it must never be at the reader's expense.**
  "Arriving early and out of uniform" is fine. "Obviously" is not.
- **Banned words at a hinge:** *clearly, obviously, simply, just, of
  course, it is easy to see*. Every one of them tells a struggling reader
  the fault is theirs.
- **Banned physics hedges at a hinge:** *just a model*, *only a picture*,
  *strictly speaking* used as a shrug. If the model has a limit, state
  the limit in a sentence and say where the next model lives.
- **Address the reader as an equal.** Second person is good. Keep it
  steady from the first floor to the last. Upper-floor coldness is how
  quantum mechanics rooms lose people who were still with the book at
  energy and momentum.
- **Parallelism is an asset, not repetition to be edited out.** "An
  observer at rest on the platform measures… an observer on the train
  measures…" is doing work. Only remove a repeated word when it carries
  two *different* senses with no parallel structure.
- **Do not argue with the reader's eyes.** If a wave is drawn, say it is a
  drawing of amplitude, not a picture of a ripple in water, in the same
  paragraph as the drawing. If a Feynman line is drawn, say what is
  literal and what is bookkeeping, in the same paragraph.

### Equations are sentences

An equation is allowed only after the sentence it compresses has been
said. Then the equation is a translation, not an announcement.

Write the translation out loud at least once:

> "Force equals rate of change of momentum."
>
> Then: F = dp/dt.

Never open a hard paragraph with the symbol. The symbol is Move 7.

When two notations name the same act — Δt and dt, B and H, ψ and |ψ⟩ —
say they name the same act. Do not let the reader infer it from
typography.

## 7. When to use the full seven moves — and when not

Use all seven for the **first encounter with a genuinely new kind of
object**:

- velocity as a limit, acceleration, force, momentum, energy, work
- the field (electric, magnetic, gravitational)
- potential and potential energy
- waves on a string, then waves that carry no string
- entropy and the direction of heat
- inertial frames, the invariant speed, spacetime
- amplitude, superposition, measurement
- spin as an extra label that is not a tiny rotation in ordinary space
- the metric as the thing clocks and rulers report
- the vacuum as a state, not as empty space

Do **not** use them for:

- an idea that extends one already built (kinetic energy after work has
  been defined; the Biot–Savart law after the magnetic field exists; time
  dilation after the invariant speed has been faced);
- routine technique rooms, where the reader wants the method, not a story
  (resolving a vector, computing a centre of mass, using a Gaussian
  surface once Gauss's law is in hand, evaluating a Feynman diagram once
  the rules exist);
- "Looking Ahead" rooms, which are inventories by design.

A rough guide: if the room's title contains *"What … Is"*, it needs all
seven. If it names a technique, it needs Moves 6 and 7 only — a worked
number, then the notation.

Modern physics does not get a shorter on-ramp because the reader is now
"advanced." The objects are stranger. The on-ramp stays long. A Hilbert
space is not allowed to arrive in a sentence that also contains the word
*obviously*.

### A note on the upper floors

Relativity and quantum mechanics fail Move 1 if you start from the
nineteenth-century crisis. The reader does not own "the luminiferous
aether" or "the ultraviolet catastrophe." They own trains, torches,
clocks, coins, and two slits cut in card.

Start from those. Let the nineteenth century arrive later as history, once
the reader can see what the crisis was a crisis *of*.

The same rule applies to particle physics and to gravity as geometry. Do
not open with the Standard Model Lagrangian. Open with a track in a
chamber, or with two clocks at different heights in a tower.

## 8. Checklist before a room is finished

1. Could a reader who skipped the previous floors follow the first
   paragraph?
2. Does something familiar visibly **break** before new machinery appears?
3. Is the new question stated in one short sentence?
4. Is the obstacle stated concretely enough to be felt?
5. Do numbers, or a checkable thought experiment with numbers, appear
   **before** notation?
6. Is it said plainly what the numbers never do?
7. Is the object named only after it exists?
8. Is the reader's own notation (Δt, F = ma, E = ½mv², "a wave")
   translated explicitly into the book's notation?
9. Does the next room derive the same example and reach the same number?
10. Does every forward reference point at a room that exists and delivers?
11. Has every figure in the table been checked exactly, not numerically
    waved at? Are the units consistent through the table?
12. Are *clearly*, *obviously*, *simply* and *just* absent from the hinge?
13. Is every term used either built here, built earlier, or signposted as
    coming later?
14. Does any paragraph at the hard part carry more than one new idea?
15. Does the passage feel slightly too slow to you? If it feels exactly
    right, it is too fast.
16. Has a great name been used as a substitute for a reason?
17. If the room is modern physics, did Move 1 start from something a
    reader owns (clock, torch, coin, slit, tower), not from a crisis they
    have never had?

## 9. Worked sketches, basics to modern

These are not finished rooms. They are the shape, so a writer can copy the
moves rather than the sentences. Each sketch is shorter than a real
on-ramp; a finished passage should still land between 380 and 660 words
before the formal statement.

### 9.1 Instantaneous speed — mechanics, first encounter

1. Distance over time for a whole walk.
2. The walk is no longer steady; one number will not do.
3. Not the trip: this moment.
4. At a moment the distance is zero and the time is zero.
5. Measure a little later than the moment, then a little less later.
6. A five-line table heading toward 1.73 m/s and never landing on it.
7. That limit is the speed at the instant. Δs/Δt is the school name;
   ds/dt is the same instruction.

The next room must recover 1.73 m/s from the definition, for the same
walk.

### 9.2 Force and mass — mechanics

1. A trolley needs a shove.
2. The same shove on ice and on a carpet does two jobs. Two trolleys of
   different load, same shove, do two jobs.
3. Not "how hard the shove felt": what change in motion it produced, and
   what the object put up against being changed.
4. Feel is not a reading. A bathroom scale is a reading, and even that
   reading is not the whole story if the floor itself is accelerating.
5. Separate the shove from the object's reluctance to change its motion.
6. One shove, two loads, two accelerations; the product m a stays put.
7. That reluctance is inertial mass. F = ma is the sentence, not a
   definition of force from nowhere.

Do not smuggle in weight as if it were the same word as mass. Weight is a
later room.

### 9.3 Work and kinetic energy

1. Lift a bag onto a counter: heavier, or higher, costs more.
2. A spring fights back harder the further you go. One force times one
   distance is no longer a number you can write down in advance.
3. Not "how tired you are": what quantity you can credit to the bag so
   that the credit survives the details of the path.
4. Force is changing while the bag is still moving. The old product has
   nothing to grab.
5. Cut the lift into short stretches, each stretch almost one force, add
   them.
6. Four stretches, then eight, heading toward a number the next room will
   call ½kx² or mgh.
7. That running total is work. The credit stored in motion is kinetic
   energy. School’s ½mv² is what the running total becomes when the force
   in question was the net force and the bag started at rest.

### 9.4 The electric field — classical fields

1. Two rubbed balloons push apart. Coulomb’s number works for that pair.
2. A third balloon arrives. Then a fourth. The pair law becomes a crowd,
   and nothing in it describes the empty point in the middle of the room
   where you have not put a balloon yet.
3. Not "what force on this balloon": what is the condition of the space,
   ready for whichever balloon you next place.
4. A force law names two objects. The empty point is not an object. The
   pair law has no subject there.
5. Give the empty point a readiness: force-to-be-per-unit-charge, sitting
   there whether or not you bring the next balloon.
6. A small test charge q, a measured push F, the quotient F/q heading
   toward a number that stops depending on q once q is small enough not
   to shove the sources around.
7. That readiness is the electric field E. F = qE is the pair law rewritten
   so the space can be discussed when the second charge is still in the
   box.

Room n+1 must compute the same E from Coulomb and superposition, and get
the same number the table headed toward.

### 9.5 Entropy and heat’s direction — thermal physics

1. A kettle cools. A freezer, left open, does not freeze the kitchen.
2. Every molecule-by-molecule film of the cooling kettle can be run
   backwards and still obey the force laws. The film you never see is the
   one where the kitchen air gathers itself and boils the kettle.
3. Not "heat is a fluid": why the films you do see all go one way.
4. Counting the films is not something a thermometer does. The
   thermometer only tells you the kettle got colder.
5. Count the ways the energy can be arranged. The kettle-hot-and-room-cold
   stack has fewer ways than the even stack.
6. A toy with four units of energy and a few boxes: 5 arrangements one
   side, 35 the other. The ratio is already a verdict.
7. That count, turned into a logarithm so that two rooms add instead of
   multiply, is entropy. Heat goes the way that raises the total count.

Do not write "disorder." The next room should recover the same 5-to-35
verdict from the definition.

### 9.6 The invariant speed — special relativity

1. Walk down a train. Your speed and the train’s speed add.
2. A torch beam down the same train does not add. Every observer who
   measures that beam gets the same figure.
3. Not "how fast is the light": how can two observers share one *c* and
   still each see the other moving.
4. If *c* is shared, the claps that are "at the same time" on the
   platform are not "at the same time" on the train. One public now cannot
   survive.
5. Let each observer keep a clock and a ruler. Give a rule that turns one
   set of readings into the other and leaves *c* untouched.
6. A light-clock table: v = 0.6c, 0.8c, 0.98c — factors 5/4, 5/3, ~5 —
   a 1.000 μs tick seen as 1.250, 1.667, 5.025.
7. That rule is the Lorentz transformation. The stretch in the table is
   time dilation. Δt and Δt′ are not two names for one public interval.

The next room must return 5/3 for the same 0.8c clock.

Do not start with the aether. The reader does not own the aether.

### 9.7 Amplitude and the mark on the wall — quantum mechanics

1. A marble through one hole makes a mark. Two holes make two piles, added.
2. Electrons, same two holes, make stripes. The two-hole story is not the
   sum of two one-hole stories.
3. Not "which hole": what number, assigned to a mark on the wall, gives
   the chance of finding the mark there.
4. Ask which hole and the stripes go away. The question and the
   experiment cannot be had together.
5. Assign a small complex number to each hole, add, square the length of
   the sum. That square is the chance. The hole is not recovered.
6. A three-line table: hole A alone, hole B alone, both — probabilities
   0.50, 0.50, and then 0.00 / 1.00 in the bright and dark stripes, which
   no adding of 0.50 and 0.50 can make.
7. The complex number is the amplitude. The square is the probability.
   ψ(x) is the amplitude as a function of the wall. |ψ|² Δx is the chance
   of a mark in that strip.

The next room must recover the same bright-stripe 1.00 from the same two
amplitudes added.

Do not start with Planck’s committee, and do not start with Hilbert space.
The reader owns two holes and a wall.

### 9.8 Gravity as geometry — a first look

1. A stone dropped in a lift. In a lift at rest on Earth it falls. In a
   lift whose cable has been cut, it does not — not relative to the lift.
2. The same stone, the same local experiment, two verdicts. "A force
   called weight" cannot be the whole account, because the free-falling
   observer has every right to say there is no force at all.
3. Not "what force pulls the stone": what the stone and the light beam do
   when no local shove is felt.
4. A force you can transform away by falling is a suspicious force. Empty
   space is not suspicious in the same way — unless empty space itself is
   doing the steering.
5. Let the clocks and the rulers report a geometry. Let the stone follow
   the straightest line that geometry allows. In a weak field those
   lines look like Newton’s pulls.
6. Two clocks, one in a tower, one on the ground, a few microseconds per
   day. The next room must get those microseconds from the metric, not
   from a new force on the clockwork.
7. That geometry is spacetime with a metric. Weight, from here on, is
   what it feels like to be prevented from following the straightest line.
