TITLE: Why the Schrödinger Equation Is Not Enough
TAGLINE: A focused diagnosis of exactly what a relativistic wave equation for spin-1/2 must fix, and why the obvious fixes fail
NUM: 19
NEXT: Lesson 20 constructs the Dirac equation directly, following the logic developed here: a first-order equation, linear in both space and time derivatives, built from a new set of matrices whose algebra is fixed by requiring consistency with E² = |p|² + m².

# How to use this lesson

Lessons 12 and 18 each built a relativistic-in-spirit wave equation and found it wanting: the Schrödinger equation is not Lorentz covariant at all, and the Klein–Gordon equation, while covariant, has a probability current that is not positive definite, tied to its being second order in time. This short lesson pauses before the Dirac equation is introduced to state, with full precision, exactly what problem needs solving and why several tempting shortcuts do not solve it. It is a lesson of diagnosis rather than construction, and its payoff is that Lesson 20's construction of the Dirac equation will feel motivated at every step rather than pulled from nowhere.

The central insight, due to Dirac, is deceptively simple to state: build an equation that is first order in time, like the Schrödinger equation, but treats space and time symmetrically enough to be Lorentz covariant. Section 3 shows exactly what this requires and why it cannot be done with ordinary numbers. Expect four to six focused hours; this lesson is shorter than most because its job is conceptual clarity, not new machinery.

## Learning objectives

- State precisely, using the language of Lesson 4, why an equation cannot be both first order and manifestly covariant using an ordinary scalar or vector wave function.

- Explain why the Klein–Gordon current's sign problem is a direct consequence of the equation's order, not an independent difficulty.

- Show why simply taking a "square root" of the Klein–Gordon operator with ordinary numbers fails.

- Derive the algebraic condition that any candidate set of first-order coefficients must satisfy to reproduce E²=|p|²+m².

- Show that this condition cannot be satisfied by ordinary numbers and must be satisfied by matrices instead.

- State the minimum size of matrix needed and connect it to the appearance of spin.

- Summarize the list of requirements a successful equation must meet, to be checked one by one against the Dirac equation in Lesson 20.

- Recognize this lesson as a worked example of theoretical physics reasoning: turning a vague goal into a precise, solvable algebraic problem.

# 1 Restating the problem precisely

## 1.1 What went wrong with each equation

Lesson 12 identified the Schrödinger equation's flaw as an imbalance: one time derivative against two space derivatives, an asymmetry a Lorentz boost cannot respect (Lesson 4, Section 7.2, Worked example 8 of Lesson 12). Lesson 18 fixed the imbalance by taking two derivatives of each kind, producing a covariant equation, but paid for it with a current that is not positive definite, traced in Lesson 18, Section 3.3, directly to the equation's second-order-in-time character.

## 1.2 The goal

The task is to find an equation that is simultaneously first order in time (so that, as for the Schrödinger equation, specifying ψ(x,0) alone determines the state at every later time, and so that a genuinely positive-definite conserved probability density can be built by Lesson 10's original method) and consistent with the relativistic energy relation E²=|p|²+m² (so that it is compatible with special relativity). Section 1.1's two equations each achieve one of these properties at the expense of the other; the Dirac equation, previewed here and constructed in Lesson 20, achieves both simultaneously.

# 2 Why a naive square root fails

## 2.1 The tempting first attempt

Since the Klein–Gordon equation arose from squaring E = ±√(|p|²+m²) implicitly (by working with E² directly), one might try instead to take the square root before quantizing, writing a first-order equation directly from E = +√(|p|²+m²) (choosing, arbitrarily, the positive branch).

$$ Ĥψ = √(p̂²+m²) ψ

## 2.2 Why this does not work

The operator √(p̂²+m²) is perfectly well defined in momentum space, where p̂ is simply a number and its square root is unambiguous (Lesson 13, Section 2.1), but in position space p̂ = −i∇ is a differential operator, and the square root of a differential operator, defined for instance by its Taylor series √(1+x) = 1+x/2−x²/8+⋯ (Lesson 2, Section 4.1) applied to p̂²/m², produces an infinite series of ever higher spatial derivatives.

$$ √(p̂²+m²) = m√(1+p̂²/m²) = m + p̂²/(2m) − p̂⁴/(8m³) + ⋯

An equation with infinitely many derivatives is nonlocal: the value of ψ at one point would depend, through this expansion, on the value of ψ at increasingly distant points through higher and higher derivatives, an equation with no simple physical interpretation as a local field theory and, in most treatments, entirely unmanageable as a starting point.

### Worked example 1

Confirm that the second term of the expansion reproduces the nonrelativistic kinetic energy. m+p̂²/2m−p̂⁴/8m³+⋯ acting to first subleading order gives Ĥψ ≈ (m+p̂²/2m)ψ, exactly the nonrelativistic Schrödinger Hamiltonian of Lesson 12 shifted up by the rest mass m, matching the same expansion already carried out for the classical energy in Lesson 3, Worked example 5, and confirming that the naive square-root Hamiltonian at least has the right nonrelativistic limit, even though it fails to be local or practically useful beyond that limit.

## 2.3 What must be sacrificed instead

If a first-order equation is wanted without an infinite series of derivatives, the square root cannot be taken of the differential operator p̂² directly; instead, Dirac's insight was to look for a linear (not merely first-order-in-derivatives, but literally linear) combination of p̂ₓ, p̂ᵧ, p̂_z, and m whose square reproduces p̂²+m² exactly, with no higher powers of derivatives appearing at all. This shifts the difficulty from analysis (dealing with an infinite series) to algebra (finding coefficients with the right multiplication properties), and Section 3 shows precisely what those coefficients must satisfy.

# 3 The algebraic condition

## 3.1 The ansatz

Seek a Hamiltonian of the form

$$ Ĥ_D = α·p̂ + βm = αₓp̂ₓ + αᵧp̂ᵧ + α_zp̂_z + βm

linear in the momentum components and in the mass, with coefficients α = (αₓ,αᵧ,α_z) and β to be determined. This is manifestly first order in p̂, and, once identified with i∂_t via the usual correspondence rule, first order in time as well, exactly the structural feature the Klein–Gordon equation lacked.

## 3.2 Requiring consistency with E²=|p|²+m²

For Ĥ_D to be an acceptable relativistic Hamiltonian, its square must reproduce the correct dispersion relation: Ĥ_D²ψ should equal (p̂²+m²)ψ for every solution, as the Klein–Gordon operator does automatically. Squaring the ansatz,

$$ Ĥ_D² = Σᵢⱼ αᵢαⱼ p̂ᵢp̂ⱼ + m Σᵢ (αᵢβ+βαᵢ) p̂ᵢ + β²m²

Since p̂ᵢp̂ⱼ = p̂ⱼp̂ᵢ (partial derivatives commute, Lesson 2, Section 3.1), the first sum can be symmetrized, Σᵢⱼαᵢαⱼp̂ᵢp̂ⱼ = Σᵢⱼ (1/2)(αᵢαⱼ+αⱼαᵢ)p̂ᵢp̂ⱼ, using only the symmetric (in i,j) combination of the coefficients. Comparing term by term with the desired result p̂²+m² = Σᵢ p̂ᵢ² + m², which has no cross terms p̂ᵢp̂ⱼ (i≠j) and no terms linear in p̂ᵢ, forces three conditions on the coefficients.

$$ αᵢαⱼ+αⱼαᵢ = 2δᵢⱼ I,    αᵢβ+βαᵢ = 0,    β² = I

Writing these with the anticommutator notation {A,B}=AB+BA introduced in Lesson 1, Exercise 13, and used again for the Pauli matrices there,

$$ {αᵢ,αⱼ} = 2δᵢⱼ I,    {αᵢ,β} = 0,    β² = I

### Worked example 2

Verify the first condition explicitly reproduces the absence of cross terms. Suppose, for a moment, that αₓ and αᵧ were ordinary commuting numbers. Then {αₓ,αᵧ} = 2αₓαᵧ, and the condition αₓαᵧ=0 (from i≠j in the first relation with the numerical, commuting case) together with αₓ²=1 (from i=j) would require αₓ≠0 but αₓαᵧ=0, forcing αᵧ=0, and by the same argument α_z=0 and β=0 as well: an ordinary-number solution collapses trivially and cannot reproduce a nonzero mass term or nonzero momentum coupling simultaneously, foreshadowing the conclusion of Section 4.

# 4 Why ordinary numbers cannot satisfy the algebra

## 4.1 The obstruction

If αᵢ and β were ordinary (real or complex) numbers, they would commute with each other, so {αᵢ,αⱼ}=2αᵢαⱼ for i≠j, and the condition {αᵢ,αⱼ}=0 (from the δᵢⱼ=0 case) would force αᵢαⱼ=0. Combined with αᵢ²=1 (from the δᵢⱼ=1 case), this cannot be satisfied unless one of αᵢ,αⱼ vanishes, contradicting αᵢ²=1. Worked example 2 already exhibited this collapse concretely; Section 4.1 states it in general: no assignment of ordinary numbers to αₓ,αᵧ,α_z,β can satisfy all the required (anti)commutation relations simultaneously while keeping every one of them nonzero (as physically required, since a vanishing αᵢ would mean the equation does not actually depend on momentum in that direction, and a vanishing β would mean it does not depend on mass at all).

## 4.2 The resolution: matrices

The obstruction disappears if αᵢ and β are allowed to be matrices rather than numbers, since matrices need not commute (Lesson 1, Section 4.1), and {αᵢ,αⱼ}=0 for i≠j is then a nontrivial, satisfiable condition, exactly as {σᵢ,σⱼ}=0 for i≠j already holds for the Pauli matrices (Lesson 1, Section 8, combined with Lesson 15, Exercise 12, which is precisely a special case of this anticommutation structure). This is the central conceptual leap of the entire lesson: consistency with special relativity, together with the demand for a first-order equation, forces the wave function ψ to be a multi-component object, acted on by matrices, rather than a single complex number at each spacetime point.

### Worked example 3

Check whether the Pauli matrices alone could serve as αₓ,αᵧ,α_z, with β some fourth 2×2 matrix. The Pauli matrices do satisfy {σᵢ,σⱼ}=2δᵢⱼI (Lesson 1, Section 8), matching the first condition of Section 3.2 exactly. However, the second condition, {σᵢ,β}=0 for all three i, requires β to anticommute with all of σₓ,σᵧ,σ_z simultaneously; since any 2×2 Hermitian matrix can be written as a real linear combination of I,σₓ,σᵧ,σ_z (Lesson 1, Section 8, extended), and I commutes rather than anticommutes with everything, β would have to be built purely from σₓ,σᵧ,σ_z, but then β²=I forces further constraints (Exercise 8) that turn out to be incompatible with anticommuting with all three simultaneously in only two dimensions: 2×2 matrices are not large enough.

# 5 Finding the minimum size

## 5.1 A counting argument

The three matrices αₓ,αᵧ,α_z together with β must be four mutually anticommuting matrices (each pair, chosen from these four, must anticommute, by the second and, implicitly, an extended version of the first condition applied consistently), each squaring to the identity. A theorem of matrix algebra (stated here without proof, as the details belong to the representation theory of Clifford algebras, a topic beyond this course's scope) shows that the smallest matrices admitting four mutually anticommuting, square-to-identity Hermitian matrices are 4×4, not 2×2 or 3×3.

## 5.2 Why not 3×3

An odd-dimensional matrix has an odd number of eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity), each ±1 (since the matrix squares to I), and the trace, the sum of the eigenvalues, would then generically be odd, hence nonzero. But any two anticommuting matrices A, B with A²=B²=I can be shown to have zero trace: tr(A) = tr(AB²) = tr((AB)B) = tr(B(AB)) (cyclic property of trace, Lesson 1, Section 4.3) = tr(BAB) = tr(−ABB) (using AB=−BA) = −tr(AB²) = −tr(A) (using B²=I again), forcing tr(A)=0. An odd-dimensional matrix cannot have zero trace while also having every eigenvalue equal to ±1 in a way that sums to zero (equal numbers of +1 and −1 eigenvalues require an even total count), so no odd-dimensional solution exists, ruling out 3×3 and forcing the next even size, 4×4, once 2×2 has already failed by Worked example 3's more direct argument.

### Worked example 4

Verify tr(A)=0 for A=αₓ using B=αᵧ. From αₓ=αₓαᵧ², using αᵧ²=I, and cyclicity of trace, tr(αₓ) = tr(αₓαᵧαᵧ) = tr(αᵧαₓαᵧ) = tr(−αₓαᵧαᵧ) (using {αₓ,αᵧ}=0, so αᵧαₓ=−αₓαᵧ) = −tr(αₓαᵧ²) = −tr(αₓ), forcing tr(αₓ)=0. This confirms the general trace argument in this specific instance, and, applied to β in place of αₓ, shows tr(β)=0 as well.

## 5.3 The connection to spin

A 4×4 matrix acting on ψ means ψ itself must have four components, not one. This is not put in by hand to describe spin; it emerges from the pure algebra of Sections 3 through 5, and it is only after solving the resulting equation in Lesson 20 that the four components are found to organize themselves into two two-component spinors of the kind constructed in Lesson 15, with the extra factor of two (beyond the two needed for ordinary spin-1/2) tied to the particle and antiparticle content examined in Lesson 23. The appearance of spin was not assumed; it was forced by requiring a first-order, Lorentz-consistent equation at all.

# 6 The requirements list

## 6.1 What the successful equation must do

Collecting the conclusions of Sections 1 through 5, any acceptable relativistic wave equation for a fundamental spin-1/2 particle must:

- Be first order in both space and time derivatives, so that a genuinely positive-definite conserved current can be constructed by the method of Lesson 10.

- Be consistent with E²=|p|²+m² for every plane-wave solution, so that it correctly describes a particle of definite mass m.

- Require its coefficients (the αᵢ and β of Section 3.1, to be renamed and reorganized covariantly in Lesson 20) to be matrices satisfying a specific anticommutation algebra, forcing a multi-component wave function.

- Reduce, at minimum size, to a four-component object, whose components will need physical interpretation.

## 6.2 What is deliberately left open

This lesson has not yet written down the equation covariantly, has not yet solved it for plane waves, has not yet checked whether its own probability current is positive definite (the entire point of the exercise), and has not yet interpreted the four components physically. Every one of these is Lesson 20's task, approached with the confidence that the algebraic groundwork here guarantees a consistent starting point exists, precisely because the obstruction of Section 4.1 has been circumvented rather than merely avoided.

### Worked example 5

State, as a preview only, what Lesson 20 will show about the current built from a first-order equation satisfying Section 3.2's algebra. With Ĥ_D=α·p̂+βm first order in time, the equation i∂_tψ=Ĥ_Dψ has the same structural form as the ordinary Schrödinger equation of Lesson 12, so the same method used there (multiply by ψ† and its Hermitian-conjugate equation, subtract) will be shown in Lesson 24 to produce a current with ρ=ψ†ψ, manifestly positive definite (a sum of squared magnitudes, as in Lesson 10), resolving Lesson 18's central difficulty completely, provided the αᵢ and β can indeed be found as genuine matrices, which Section 5 has now confirmed is possible.

# 8 Summary

1. The Schrödinger equation fails Lorentz covariance because it has unequal numbers of space and time derivatives; the Klein–Gordon equation fixes this but loses a positive-definite current because it is second order in time.

2. The goal is a first-order equation consistent with E²=|p|²+m².

3. A naive square root of p̂²+m² produces an infinite series of derivatives and is nonlocal.

4. A linear ansatz Ĥ_D=α·p̂+βm, squared and matched to p̂²+m², forces {αᵢ,αⱼ}=2δᵢⱼI, {αᵢ,β}=0, and β²=I.

5. Ordinary numbers cannot satisfy this algebra while keeping every coefficient nonzero; matrices are required.

6. Four mutually anticommuting, square-to-identity matrices require at least 4×4 size, since odd dimensions are ruled out by a trace argument.

7. A 4×4 matrix algebra means the wave function must have four components, with spin structure emerging as a consequence rather than an assumption.

8. The next lesson uses exactly this groundwork to write, solve, and interpret the Dirac equation.

# Solutions

*Attempt each exercise before reading its solution. If you are stuck, read only the first sentence of the solution, then try again.*

**1.** m√(1+p̂²/m²) = m[1+p̂²/2m²−p̂⁴/8m⁴+⋯] = m+p̂²/2m−p̂⁴/8m³+⋯, so the next term beyond p̂²/2m is −p̂⁴/8m³, matching exactly the −p⁴/8m³c² relativistic correction term found in Lesson 3, Worked example 5 (with c=1), now understood as an operator acting on ψ rather than a classical number.

**2.** A local equation relates the value of a field and a bounded, finite number of its derivatives at a single point to its rate of change; an equation with infinitely many derivatives, by contrast, effectively samples the field's behavior over an extended neighborhood (since each derivative order probes progressively finer or more distant variation), blurring the sharp distinction between "what happens right here" and "what happens somewhere else," which is the operational meaning of locality that the Schrödinger and Klein–Gordon equations, each with a fixed, small number of derivatives, respect.

**3.** (α·p̂+βm)² = Σᵢⱼαᵢαⱼp̂ᵢp̂ⱼ + mΣᵢ(αᵢβ+βαᵢ)p̂ᵢ + β²m². The first term has degree 2 in p̂ (two momentum factors), the second has degree 1, and the third has degree 0; no term has an odd total combining momentum and mass factors inconsistently, and matching to p̂²+m² (degree 2 and degree 0 only, no degree 1) is exactly what forces the middle term's coefficient, {αᵢ,β}, to vanish.

**4.** tr(αᵢ) = tr(αᵢβ²) = tr((αᵢβ)β) = tr(β(αᵢβ)) = tr(βαᵢβ) = tr(−αᵢββ) (using {αᵢ,β}=0, so βαᵢ=−αᵢβ) = −tr(αᵢβ²) = −tr(αᵢ) (using β²=I again), forcing tr(αᵢ)=0.

**5.** {αₓ,β}=2αₓβ=0 (since ordinary numbers commute, the anticommutator is twice the product) directly gives αₓβ=0. Since neither is allowed to be zero (both αₓ²=1 and β²=1 require nonzero values), this is a contradiction: no such pair of nonzero ordinary numbers exists.

**6.** This is exactly the Pauli matrix identity σᵢσⱼ=δᵢⱼI+iεᵢⱼₖσₖ from Lesson 1; adding σᵢσⱼ+σⱼσᵢ = δᵢⱼI+iεᵢⱼₖσₖ + δⱼᵢI+iεⱼᵢₖσₖ = 2δᵢⱼI (since εᵢⱼₖ=−εⱼᵢₖ, the ε-terms cancel), confirming {σᵢ,σⱼ}=2δᵢⱼI.

**7.** {σₓ,β} = {σₓ,aI+bσₓ+cσᵧ+dσ_z} = 2aσₓ+2bI+c{σₓ,σᵧ}+d{σₓ,σ_z} = 2aσₓ+2bI+0+0 (using {σₓ,σᵧ}={σₓ,σ_z}=0 for distinct Pauli matrices), so requiring this to vanish forces a=0 and b=0. Repeating with σᵧ and σ_z similarly forces c=0 and d=0 (each condition kills the "self" coefficient and the identity coefficient a, which is already zero from the first condition), leaving β=0, which cannot satisfy β²=I=1≠0.

**8.** Because any 2×2 Hermitian matrix is some combination of I,σₓ,σᵧ,σ_z, and the only combination that anticommutes with all three Pauli matrices is the zero matrix (Exercise 7), there is no nonzero 2×2 matrix left over to serve as β.

**9.** tr(αᵧ) = tr(αᵧα_z²) = tr((αᵧα_z)α_z) = tr(α_z(αᵧα_z)) = tr(α_zαᵧα_z) = tr(−αᵧα_zα_z) (using {αᵧ,α_z}=0) = −tr(αᵧα_z²) = −tr(αᵧ), forcing tr(αᵧ)=0.

**10.** Since the matrix squares to I, every eigenvalue λ satisfies λ²=1, so λ=±1 only. The trace is the sum of all N eigenvalues (with multiplicity); if there are n₊ eigenvalues +1 and n₋ eigenvalues −1 with n₊+n₋=N, the trace is n₊−n₋. This vanishes only if n₊=n₋=N/2, which requires N to be even; for odd N, n₊≠n₋ is forced (they cannot be equal integers summing to an odd number), so the trace cannot be zero.

**11.** A 4×4 matrix acting on a vector must act on a 4-component vector, by the basic rules of matrix-vector multiplication (Lesson 1, Section 4.2); since αᵢ and β are 4×4, the object ψ that i∂_tψ=(α·p̂+βm)ψ acts on must have four components at each spacetime point for the matrix multiplication on the right-hand side to be well-defined at all.

**12.** Setting i=j in {αᵢ,αⱼ}=2δᵢⱼI gives 2αᵢ²=2I, so αᵢ²=I for each i. Combined with tr(αᵢ)=0 (Exercise 9, or Worked example 4 for the other index), and the fact that αᵢ²=I forces every eigenvalue to be ±1 (the argument of Exercise 10 applied to a single matrix), a vanishing trace forces equal numbers of +1 and −1 eigenvalues.

**13.** Equal (necessarily integer) numbers n of +1 and n of −1 eigenvalues sum to a total dimension N=2n, which is automatically even; this matches the minimum size 4×4 found in Section 5, since 2×2 has already been excluded by Worked example 3/Exercise 8, leaving 4 as the smallest even dimension actually available once 2 is ruled out.

**14.** In Lesson 15, nothing in the abstract angular momentum algebra of Lesson 14 forced half-integer spin to be realized in nature; it was the experimental fact of the Stern Gerlach splitting that established the electron actually has spin 1/2, a possibility the mathematics allowed but did not require. Here, by contrast, no experimental input has been used at all: purely algebraic consistency between "the equation is first order" and "the equation is compatible with E²=\|p\|²+m²" already forces a multi-component wave function, before any specific particle or any experiment is considered; the multi-component structure is a mathematical necessity of the framework itself, not a discovered empirical fact about one particular particle.

**15.** Wanting a first-order equation motivates a linear ansatz in momentum and mass; demanding this ansatz reproduce the correct relativistic dispersion relation upon squaring forces specific anticommutation relations among the coefficients; the step that specifically requires noncommuting objects is the demand that distinct coefficients (αᵢ for i≠j, or αᵢ and β) multiply to give zero when added in either order, since no pair of nonzero ordinary numbers can do this, but pairs of matrices, whose multiplication need not commute, readily can; from there, matrices of a minimum necessary size fix the number of components the wave function must have.

**16.** From {αᵢ,αⱼ}=0 (i≠j), αᵢαⱼ=−αⱼαᵢ. If αᵢv=λv, then αᵢ(αⱼv) = (αᵢαⱼ)v = (−αⱼαᵢ)v = −αⱼ(αᵢv) = −αⱼ(λv) = −λ(αⱼv), showing αⱼv is an eigenvector of αᵢ with eigenvalue −λ (provided αⱼv≠0, which holds since αⱼ is invertible, being its own inverse from αⱼ²=I). Since αⱼ is invertible, it maps the +1 eigenspace of αᵢ bijectively onto the −1 eigenspace (and vice versa), so the two eigenspaces must have the same dimension, reproducing the equal-eigenvalue-count conclusion of Exercise 12 without reference to the trace at all.
