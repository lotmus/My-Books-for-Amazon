# How to use this lesson

Lesson 19 did all the hard conceptual work: it showed that a first-order, Lorentz-consistent wave equation for a particle of mass m forces a linear ansatz Ĥ_D=α·p̂+βm, that the coefficients α,β must satisfy a specific anticommutation algebra, and that the smallest matrices satisfying it are 4×4. This lesson collects those results into the Dirac equation itself, exhibits an explicit representation of the matrices, rewrites the equation in the covariant notation that will be used for the rest of the course, and solves it completely for a free particle at rest and in motion.

The reward for the effort of Lesson 19 is considerable: the equation constructed here will be shown, over the next several lessons, to have a positive-definite conserved current (Lesson 24), to correctly describe a spin-1/2 particle without any additional postulate (Lesson 22), and to predict the existence of antiparticles (Lesson 23), one of the most striking successes in the history of theoretical physics. Expect eight to ten focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Write the Dirac equation in Hamiltonian form using the α, β matrices of Lesson 19.

- Construct the Dirac representation of α and β explicitly in terms of the Pauli matrices and the identity.

- Rewrite the equation covariantly using the gamma matrices γ^μ and verify the Clifford algebra {γ^μ,γ^ν}=2g^μν.

- Derive the free-particle plane-wave solutions and the algebraic equation satisfied by their spinor coefficients.

- Solve explicitly for a particle at rest and identify the four independent solutions.

- Solve for a moving particle and exhibit the general positive- and negative-energy spinors.

- Verify that every solution satisfies the Klein–Gordon equation component by component, confirming consistency with E²=|p|²+m².

- State clearly which questions (probability current, spin, antiparticles) remain open and are addressed in the immediately following lessons.

# 1 The Dirac equation in Hamiltonian form

## 1.1 Statement

Combining the ansatz of Lesson 19, Section 3.1, with the operator correspondence E→i∂_t of Lesson 12,

$$ i ∂_t ψ = (α·p̂ + βm) ψ = (−iα·∇ + βm) ψ

with ψ a four-component column (a Dirac spinor) and α=(αₓ,αᵧ,α_z), β the 4×4 matrices satisfying the algebra {αᵢ,αⱼ}=2δᵢⱼI, {αᵢ,β}=0, β²=I established in Lesson 19, Section 3.2. This is the Dirac equation in Hamiltonian, or Schrödinger-like, form: first order in time, exactly as required, with a Hamiltonian Ĥ_D=α·p̂+βm acting on a four-component object.

## 1.2 Hermiticity

For Ĥ_D to be an acceptable quantum Hamiltonian (Lesson 11, Section 1.2), αᵢ and β must be Hermitian matrices, so that Ĥ_D is Hermitian and generates unitary time evolution as in Lesson 12, Section 5.2. This is an additional physical requirement, not automatically implied by the purely algebraic conditions of Lesson 19, but it is straightforward to satisfy alongside them, as the explicit representation of Section 2 confirms directly.

# 2 An explicit representation

## 2.1 The Dirac representation

One explicit choice of 4×4 matrices satisfying every condition of Lesson 19, written in 2×2 block form using the identity I₂ and the Pauli matrices σ of Lesson 1 and Lesson 15, is

$$ αᵢ = [[0, σᵢ],[σᵢ, 0]],    β = [[I₂, 0],[0, −I₂]]

This is called the Dirac representation. It is not the unique choice; any representation related to this one by a unitary similarity transformation (Lesson 1, Section 4.4) satisfies the same algebra and describes the same physics, as a change of basis leaves a linear operator's essential content unchanged (Lesson 1, Section 5.2). Other common choices, such as the Weyl (chiral) representation, are more convenient for specific calculations and are introduced as needed in Lesson 23.

## 2.2 Verifying the algebra

Direct block-matrix multiplication, using {σᵢ,σⱼ}=2δᵢⱼI₂ from Lesson 1, confirms

$$ αᵢαⱼ+αⱼαᵢ = [[σᵢσⱼ+σⱼσᵢ, 0],[0, σᵢσⱼ+σⱼσᵢ]] = [[2δᵢⱼI₂,0],[0,2δᵢⱼI₂]] = 2δᵢⱼI₄

and similarly {αᵢ,β}=0 (the off-diagonal block structure of αᵢ combined with the diagonal, sign-flipping structure of β produces exact cancellation, verified explicitly in Exercise 2) and β²=I₄ (since (±I₂)²=I₂ in each diagonal block).

### Worked example 1

Verify αₓβ+βαₓ=0 explicitly using the block forms. αₓβ = [[0,σₓ],[σₓ,0]][[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]] = [[0,−σₓ],[σₓ,0]]. βαₓ = [[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]][[0,σₓ],[σₓ,0]] = [[0,σₓ],[−σₓ,0]]. Adding, αₓβ+βαₓ = [[0,0],[0,0]], confirming the anticommutation directly.

# 3 Covariant form: the gamma matrices

## 3.1 Definition

Multiply the Dirac equation i∂_tψ=(α·p̂+βm)ψ on the left by β and use β²=I.

$$ iβ∂_tψ = βα·p̂ψ + β²mψ = βα·p̂ψ + mψ

Rearranging and using p̂=−i∇,

$$ iβ∂_tψ + iβα·∇ψ − mψ = 0

Define γ⁰=β and γⁱ=βαⁱ (i=1,2,3). Then βα·∇ = γⁱ∂ᵢ, and the equation becomes, with γ^μ∂_μ = γ⁰∂_t+γⁱ∂ᵢ (Lesson 4's index convention, ∂_μ=(∂_t,∇)),

$$ (iγ^μ∂_μ − m) ψ = 0,    often written  (i∂̸ − m)ψ = 0

using the Feynman slash notation ∂̸=γ^μ∂_μ, a shorthand used constantly from this point in the course onward for any four-vector contracted with the gamma matrices.

## 3.2 The Clifford algebra

The defining algebra of the gamma matrices follows from that of α and β. Using γ⁰=β, γⁱ=βαⁱ, and the original relations,

$$ {γ⁰,γ⁰} = 2β² = 2I,    {γⁱ,γʲ} = βαⁱβαʲ+βαʲβαⁱ = −αⁱβ²αʲ−αʲβ²αⁱ = −(αⁱαʲ+αʲαⁱ) = −2δᵢⱼI,    {γ⁰,γⁱ} = βγⁱ+γⁱβ = β²αⁱ+βαⁱβ = αⁱ+βαⁱβ = αⁱ−αⁱβ² = αⁱ−αⁱ = 0

using {αᵢ,β}=0 to move β past αⁱ with a sign flip in the second calculation, and β²=I throughout. Collecting these into a single covariant statement, using the metric g^μν=diag(1,−1,−1,−1) of Lesson 4,

$$ {γ^μ, γ^ν} = 2 g^μν I

This is the Clifford algebra, the defining relation of the gamma matrices, and it is manifestly consistent with the metric signature fixed in Lesson 4: the algebra literally encodes the metric of spacetime into a set of matrices, a fact whose depth is not exhausted by this course but whose immediate practical use, computing traces and contractions of gamma matrices, is developed fully in Lesson 21.

### Worked example 2

Verify {γ¹,γ¹}=−2 directly from the definition γ¹=βαₓ. (γ¹)² = βαₓβαₓ = −αₓββαₓ (using βαₓ=−αₓβ) = −αₓβ²αₓ = −αₓ²=−I (using β²=I and αₓ²=I), so {γ¹,γ¹}=2(γ¹)²=−2I, matching 2g¹¹I=2(−1)I=−2I exactly.

# 4 Consistency with the Klein–Gordon equation

## 4.1 Squaring the Dirac operator

Multiply (iγ^μ∂_μ−m)ψ=0 on the left by (iγ^ν∂_ν+m).

$$ (iγ^ν∂_ν+m)(iγ^μ∂_μ−m)ψ = (−γ^νγ^μ∂_ν∂_μ − m²)ψ = 0

Since ∂_ν∂_μ is symmetric in ν,μ (partial derivatives commute), only the symmetric part of γ^νγ^μ contributes: γ^νγ^μ∂_ν∂_μ = (1/2){γ^ν,γ^μ}∂_ν∂_μ = g^νμ∂_ν∂_μ = □. So the equation becomes

$$ (−□ − m²)ψ = 0,    that is  (□+m²)ψ = 0

Every component of the Dirac spinor ψ therefore separately satisfies the Klein–Gordon equation of Lesson 18. This confirms, exactly as demanded in Lesson 19, that the Dirac equation is consistent with E²=|p|²+m²; it is, in a precise sense, a "square root" of the Klein–Gordon equation, achieved not through an infinite series of derivatives (Lesson 19, Section 2) but through the finite, purely algebraic device of the gamma matrices.

### Worked example 3

State explicitly why this calculation required the Clifford algebra and nothing more. The entire derivation used only {γ^ν,γ^μ}=2g^νμ (to replace γ^νγ^μ∂_ν∂_μ by its symmetric part) and the fact that ∂_ν∂_μ is symmetric; no explicit matrix representation was needed, illustrating that the physical consequences of the gamma matrices, here their compatibility with relativistic kinematics, follow from the abstract algebra alone, a pattern that recurs throughout Lesson 21's trace calculations.

# 5 Plane-wave solutions: particle at rest

## 5.1 Setup

Seek solutions of the form ψ(x) = u e^(−ip·x), with u a constant four-component spinor and p·x=Et−p·x as in Lesson 4. Substituting into the momentum-space (Hamiltonian) form of the equation, i∂_tψ=(α·p̂+βm)ψ becomes

$$ E u = (α·p + βm) u

an ordinary matrix eigenvalue equation for u, the kind solved in Lesson 1, Section 5, now for a specific 4×4 matrix depending on p.

## 5.2 The rest-frame solutions

For p=0, the equation reduces to Eu=βmu = m[[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]]u. Writing u=(u_A,u_B)ᵀ in terms of two two-component blocks, this becomes Eu_A=mu_A and Eu_B=−mu_B: u_A is an eigenvector of the identity (any two-component vector) with eigenvalue E=m, and u_B is likewise arbitrary but with eigenvalue E=−m.

$$ E=+m:  u = (χ, 0)ᵀ for any two-component χ,    E=−m:  u = (0, χ)ᵀ for any two-component χ

There are four independent solutions in total: two with E=+m (χ=(1,0)ᵀ or (0,1)ᵀ, the two spin states of Lesson 15) and two with E=−m, the negative-energy branch already anticipated in Lesson 18, Section 2.1, now carrying its own two-fold spin degeneracy as well.

### Worked example 4

Verify explicitly that u=(1,0,0,0)ᵀ solves Eu=βmu with E=+m. βmu = m[[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]](1,0,0,0)ᵀ = m(1,0,0,0)ᵀ = mu, confirming E=m for this choice, one of the four rest-frame solutions.

# 6 Plane-wave solutions: general momentum

## 6.1 The block equation

For general p, write the matrix equation Eu=(α·p+βm)u in 2×2 blocks using αᵢ=[[0,σᵢ],[σᵢ,0]] and β=[[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]], with u=(u_A,u_B)ᵀ.

$$ E u_A = (σ·p) u_B + m u_A,    E u_B = (σ·p) u_A − m u_B

Solving the second equation for u_B in terms of u_A (valid whenever E≠−m, the generic case for the positive-energy branch),

$$ u_B = [(σ·p)/(E+m)] u_A

## 6.2 Positive-energy spinors

Choosing u_A=χ, an arbitrary normalized two-component spinor as in Lesson 15, and E=+E_p=+√(|p|²+m²) (the positive branch), the full positive-energy plane-wave spinor is

$$ u(p) = N [ χ, (σ·p)/(E_p+m) χ ]ᵀ

with N a normalization constant fixed by convention (Exercise 6 works out one common choice). Substituting back into the first block equation and using (σ·p)² = |p|²I (an immediate consequence of the Pauli identity of Lesson 1, Section 8, since (σ·p)²=Σᵢⱼpᵢpⱼσᵢσⱼ=Σᵢpᵢ²I by the antisymmetric part of the identity vanishing under the symmetric sum, exactly as verified in Lesson 15, Exercise 12) confirms E_p(E_p+m)=|p|²+m(E_p+m), which rearranges to E_p²=|p|²+m², the correct dispersion relation, verifying self-consistency of the ansatz.

## 6.3 Negative-energy spinors

Similarly, choosing u_B=χ and E=−E_p (the negative branch), solving the first block equation for u_A gives

$$ v(p) = N [ (σ·p)/(E_p+m) χ, χ ]ᵀ

conventionally labeled v(p) rather than u(p) to keep the two branches notationally distinct, following the standard convention used throughout Lessons 22 and 23, where the physical interpretation of these negative-energy solutions is finally settled.

### Worked example 5

Verify the p=0 limit of u(p) reproduces the rest-frame solution of Section 5.2. As p→0, the lower block (σ·p)/(E_p+m)χ → 0 (since the numerator vanishes while the denominator approaches 2m, finite), leaving u(0)=N(χ,0)ᵀ, exactly the E=+m rest-frame solution of Section 5.2 with u_A=χ, confirming the moving-particle formula correctly limits to the previously found rest solution.

# 7 Normalization

## 7.1 A covariant choice

A convenient and, for later purposes (Lesson 46), standard normalization is ūu=2m, where ū=u†γ⁰ (the Dirac adjoint, whose full motivation and covariance properties are developed in Lesson 22). With this choice, direct computation using the explicit spinor of Section 6.2 (Exercise 7) gives N=√(E_p+m), and the resulting spinor satisfies

$$ u†(p)u(p) = 2E_p

matching the covariant normalization anticipated in Lesson 18, Exercise 16, for the scalar field case, and confirming that the same 2E_p factor, tied to the Lorentz-noninvariance of the naive three-momentum measure, appears again here for spin-1/2.

### Worked example 6

Compute u†(p)u(p) explicitly in terms of χ, assuming χ†χ=1. u†u = N²[χ†χ + χ†(σ·p)²/(E_p+m)² χ] = N²[1+|p|²/(E_p+m)²] (using (σ·p)²=|p|²I from Section 6.2 and χ†χ=1). Using |p|²=E_p²−m²=(E_p−m)(E_p+m), the bracket becomes 1+(E_p−m)/(E_p+m) = 2E_p/(E_p+m), so u†u=2N²E_p/(E_p+m); setting N²=E_p+m gives u†u=2E_p, confirming the normalization claimed in Section 7.1.

# 8 What remains to be shown

## 8.1 Open questions

This lesson has constructed the equation, verified its consistency with relativistic kinematics, and found its explicit plane-wave solutions. Three essential questions remain deliberately unanswered here: whether the equation admits a positive-definite conserved current (resolving Lesson 18's central difficulty, addressed fully in Lesson 24), how the four-component spinor's spin content is organized and measured (addressed in Lesson 22, which also introduces the covariant spin operator and confirms the electron's g-factor), and how the negative-energy solutions of Sections 5 and 6 are to be physically interpreted, given that the naive catastrophe of Lesson 18, Section 2.2, has not yet been avoided merely by writing a first-order equation (addressed in Lesson 23, via the hole theory and antiparticle reinterpretation).

## 8.2 Why proceed in this order

Each of these three questions is best addressed only after the preceding machinery, gamma matrix identities (Lesson 21) and the explicit spinor algebra just constructed (this lesson), is in hand; attempting them out of order would require re-deriving tools introduced more naturally in between. This is a deliberate pedagogical choice mirroring the historical development: Dirac himself wrote down and solved the equation essentially as done here before working out its full physical content over the following months.

### Worked example 7

Preview, without derivation, the resolution of the current question. Lesson 24 will show that j^μ=ψ̄γ^μψ (with ψ̄=ψ†γ⁰, the Dirac adjoint introduced in Section 7.1) satisfies ∂_μj^μ=0 whenever ψ solves the Dirac equation, and that j^0=ψ̄γ⁰ψ=ψ†ψ=Σᵢ|ψᵢ|², manifestly a sum of squared magnitudes and therefore positive definite for any nonzero ψ, resolving the difficulty that motivated the entire search beginning in Lesson 19.

# 10 Summary

1. The Dirac equation, i∂_tψ=(α·p̂+βm)ψ, is first order in time, uses the 4×4 matrices of Lesson 19, and acts on a four-component spinor ψ.

2. The Dirac representation writes α and β explicitly in 2×2 blocks built from the Pauli matrices and the identity.

3. Defining γ⁰=β and γⁱ=βαⁱ gives the covariant form (iγ^μ∂_μ−m)ψ=0, with the gamma matrices satisfying the Clifford algebra {γ^μ,γ^ν}=2g^μν.

4. Squaring the Dirac operator reproduces the Klein–Gordon equation for every component of ψ, confirming consistency with E²=|p|²+m².

5. At rest, the equation has four independent solutions: two with E=+m and two with E=−m, each carrying a two-fold spin label.

6. For general momentum, the equation splits into two coupled 2×2 block equations, solved by the explicit spinors u(p) (positive energy) and v(p) (negative energy).

7. A standard covariant normalization gives u†(p)u(p)=2E_p, matching the relativistic normalization convention introduced for the scalar field in Lesson 18.

8. Three questions remain open by design: the probability current (Lesson 24), the spin content (Lesson 22), and the physical meaning of the negative-energy solutions (Lesson 23).

# Solutions

**1.** αₓαᵧ = [[0,σₓ],[σₓ,0]][[0,σᵧ],[σᵧ,0]] = [[σₓσᵧ,0],[0,σₓσᵧ]]. αᵧαₓ = [[σᵧσₓ,0],[0,σᵧσₓ]]. Adding: [[σₓσᵧ+σᵧσₓ,0],[0,σₓσᵧ+σᵧσₓ]] = [[0,0],[0,0]] using {σₓ,σᵧ}=0 from Lesson 1.

**2.** αᵧβ = [[0,σᵧ],[σᵧ,0]][[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]] = [[0,−σᵧ],[σᵧ,0]]. βαᵧ = [[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]][[0,σᵧ],[σᵧ,0]] = [[0,σᵧ],[−σᵧ,0]]. Adding gives the zero matrix, confirming {αᵧ,β}=0.

**3.** γ² = βαᵧ = [[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]][[0,σᵧ],[σᵧ,0]] = [[0,σᵧ],[−σᵧ,0]]. γ³ = βα_z = [[0,σ_z],[−σ_z,0]], by the identical pattern.

**4.** γ⁰γ² = [[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]][[0,σᵧ],[−σᵧ,0]] = [[0,σᵧ],[σᵧ,0]]. γ²γ⁰ = [[0,σᵧ],[−σᵧ,0]][[I₂,0],[0,−I₂]] = [[0,−σᵧ],[−σᵧ,0]]. Adding: [[0,0],[0,0]], confirming {γ⁰,γ²}=0.

**5.** Setting μ=ν=0 in {γ^μ,γ^ν}=2g^μν gives 2(γ⁰)²=2g⁰⁰=2(1), so (γ⁰)²=I. Setting μ=ν=i (i=1,2,3) gives 2(γⁱ)²=2gⁱⁱ=2(−1), so (γⁱ)²=−I.

**6.** u₁=(1,0,0,0)ᵀ (E=+m, χ=(1,0)ᵀ); u₂=(0,1,0,0)ᵀ (E=+m, χ=(0,1)ᵀ); u₃=(0,0,1,0)ᵀ (E=−m, χ=(1,0)ᵀ); u₄=(0,0,0,1)ᵀ (E=−m, χ=(0,1)ᵀ).

**7.** From Worked example 6, u†u = N²[1+\|p\|²/(E_p+m)²]. Using \|p\|²=(E_p−m)(E_p+m), the bracket is [(E_p+m)²+(E_p−m)(E_p+m)]/(E_p+m)² = (E_p+m)[(E_p+m)+(E_p−m)]/(E_p+m)² = (E_p+m)(2E_p)/(E_p+m)² = 2E_p/(E_p+m). Setting N²(2E_p/(E_p+m)) = 2E_p gives N²=E_p+m, so N=√(E_p+m).

**8.** σ·p = p_zσ_z = p_z[[1,0],[0,−1]] = [[p_z,0],[0,−p_z]]. Acting on χ=(1,0)ᵀ gives (σ·p)χ = (p_z,0)ᵀ. So u(p) = √(E_p+m) (1, 0, p_z/(E_p+m), 0)ᵀ.

**9.** (σ·p)² = Σᵢⱼpᵢpⱼσᵢσⱼ = Σᵢⱼpᵢpⱼ(δᵢⱼI+iεᵢⱼₖσₖ) = (Σᵢpᵢ²)I + iΣᵢⱼₖεᵢⱼₖpᵢpⱼσₖ. The second sum vanishes because εᵢⱼₖ is antisymmetric in i,j while pᵢpⱼ is symmetric, leaving (σ·p)²=\|p\|²I.

**10.** E_p(E_p+m) = E_p²+E_pm. Setting this equal to \|p\|²+m(E_p+m) = \|p\|²+mE_p+m² and canceling the common mE_p term from both sides gives E_p²=\|p\|²+m², the correct dispersion relation.

**11.** As p→0, the upper block (σ·p)/(E_p+m)χ → 0 in v(p)=N((σ·p)/(E_p+m)χ,χ)ᵀ, leaving v(0)=N(0,χ)ᵀ, exactly the E=−m rest-frame solution of Section 5.2 with u_B=χ.

**12.** Since u(p) is a constant spinor (independent of x), ψ=u(p)e^(−ip·x) has □ψ = (∂_t²−∇²)ψ = (−E_p²+\|p\|²)ψ (each time derivative brings −iE_p, each space derivative brings ip, as in Lesson 4, Section 6.2), so (□+m²)ψ = (−E_p²+\|p\|²+m²)ψ = 0 exactly when E_p²=\|p\|²+m², which holds by construction, confirming the Klein–Gordon equation is satisfied component by component (since u(p) multiplies the same scalar exponential in every component).

**13.** Multiplying Eu=(α·p+βm)u by γ⁰=β on the left and using βα·p=γ·p (with γ=(γ¹,γ²,γ³)) and β²=I: βEu = γ·p u + mu, that is, Eγ⁰u−γ·pu = mu, or (Eγ⁰−γ·p)u=mu. Since p^μ=(E,p) and p_μ=(E,−p) (Lesson 4), p̸=γ^μp_μ=γ⁰E−γ·p (using γⁱp_i=−γⁱpᵢ from lowering the spatial index), so this is exactly p̸u=mu, that is (p̸−m)u=0.

**14.** (p̸+m)(p̸−m)u = (p̸²−m²)u. Using p̸²=γ^μγ^νp_μp_ν = (1/2){γ^μ,γ^ν}p_μp_ν = g^μνp_μp_ν I = p² I (the symmetric-part argument of Section 4.1, applied to p_μp_ν in place of ∂_μ∂_ν), this becomes (p²−m²)u=0, so p²=m² is required for a nonzero solution u, exactly the on-shell condition p·p=E_p²−\|p\|²=m² of Lesson 4.

**15.** In Lesson 18, the Klein–Gordon equation, second order in time, admitted both E=+E_p and E=−E_p plane-wave solutions for every p, and no additional structure distinguished them beyond their overall sign of energy. Here, the same two branches reappear, but each is now dressed with a specific spinor structure, u(p) for the positive branch and v(p) for the negative branch, reflecting that the underlying scalar equation each component satisfies (Section 4.1) still has the same two-branch dispersion relation; the Dirac equation has not removed the negative-energy branch, only clothed it in additional spin structure, exactly the question Lesson 19, Section 6.2, left open.

**16.** u(p;χ₁)†u(p;χ₂) = N²[χ₁†χ₂ + χ₁†(σ·p)†(σ·p)/(E_p+m)² χ₂] = N²[χ₁†χ₂ + χ₁†(σ·p)²/(E_p+m)²χ₂] (using (σ·p)†=σ·p, Hermiticity of the Pauli matrices) = N²[1+\|p\|²/(E_p+m)²]χ₁†χ₂ (using (σ·p)²=\|p\|²I from Exercise 9), which vanishes whenever χ₁†χ₂=0, confirming orthogonality of the two spin states carries over directly to the moving-particle spinors.
