# How to use this lesson

Lessons 33 and 36 quantized the scalar and Dirac fields by the four steps of Lesson 32 without needing to change the recipe itself. The electromagnetic field resists the same treatment for a reason neither earlier field shares: the Lagrangian ℒ = −(1/4)F_μνF^μν of Lesson 26 is invariant under a whole family of transformations, A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μχ, that change nothing physical. This gauge freedom means the theory has more variables than it needs, and one direct consequence, worked out in Section 1, is that the momentum conjugate to A⁰ vanishes identically: step 2 of canonical quantization, which worked without comment for the other two fields, cannot even be written down for all four components of A_μ without first removing the redundancy.

This lesson removes it by fixing the Coulomb gauge, the same choice introduced classically in Lesson 8, Section 6. The reward is a clean mode expansion in exactly two polarizations per momentum, matching the transverse plane waves of Lesson 7 and the count promised there and in Lesson 3. The cost, made explicit here and revisited in Lesson 71, is that the Coulomb gauge hides manifest Lorentz covariance; an alternative, covariant treatment exists and is deferred to that later lesson, once the tools to handle its own complications are available. By the end of this lesson, all three free fields of QED, scalar, Dirac, and electromagnetic, are quantized, and Part VI can begin coupling them. Expect eight to ten focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Show that the momentum conjugate to A⁰ vanishes, and explain why this blocks naive canonical quantization of all four components of A_μ.

- State the Coulomb gauge condition and show that it leaves exactly two independent field components per momentum.

- Write the mode expansion of the transverse vector potential in terms of two polarization vectors and ladder operators.

- Postulate the commutation relations of the photon ladder operators and verify they reproduce the correct equal-time commutator of the transverse field and its momentum.

- Compute the Hamiltonian of the quantized field and identify the vacuum energy, connecting it to the Casimir effect of Lesson 35.

- Construct the vacuum and one-photon states and identify the matrix element that becomes the external photon line of a Feynman diagram.

- Explain why the photon is its own antiparticle, and state the polarization sum identity used to compute unpolarized cross sections.

- Summarize the pattern common to all three quantized fields of the course, and the one respect in which each differs from the others.

# 1 The obstruction: gauge freedom is a constraint

## 1.1 The vanishing momentum conjugate to A⁰

Lesson 27's general rule gives the momentum conjugate to A_ν as π^ν = ∂ℒ/∂(∂_0A_ν) = −F^(0ν) (Exercise 1 checks the sign). For ν = 0, F^(00) = 0 identically, since F_μν is antisymmetric, so

$$ π^0 = −F^(00) = 0

This holds for every field configuration, not just on shell: there is no term in ℒ = (1/2)(E² − B²) containing Ȧ⁰ at all, exactly the situation Lesson 26, Section 5.3, met for ψ† in the Dirac Lagrangian, and exactly the definition of a constrained system from Lesson 6, Section 1.1. For ν = i, π^i = −F^(0i) = E^i (Exercise 2), so the spatial components do have honest momenta; it is specifically A⁰ that has none.

## 1.2 Why this blocks the naive recipe

Step 2 of canonical quantization postulates [φ̂, π̂] = iδ³ for each pair of conjugate variables. With π^0 ≡ 0, there is nothing to set equal to i times anything for the pair (A⁰, π^0); imposing [Â⁰, 0] = iδ³ is not merely unhelpful, it is false, since the left side is identically zero while the right side is not. A second difficulty compounds the first: even the three spatial components A^i are not all independent, because A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μχ changes none of the physics (Lesson 8, Section 3), so a naive quantization of all four components would quantize more degrees of freedom than the theory actually has, and would then need some further principle to remove the unphysical ones. Both difficulties trace to the same source, gauge invariance, and both are resolved by fixing a gauge before quantizing.

## 1.3 Two ways forward

One route keeps all four components of A_μ and adds an extra term to the Lagrangian that breaks gauge invariance just enough to make every component well-posed, at the cost of an indefinite-norm state space that must be corrected by hand; this covariant approach is more efficient for the multi-loop calculations of Part IX and is taken up in Lesson 71. The route followed here instead removes the redundant components before quantizing at all, by fixing the Coulomb gauge, as Lesson 8, Section 6, did classically. It sacrifices manifest covariance, since the Coulomb gauge condition singles out a preferred time direction, but every physical prediction computed in it agrees with the covariant treatment, and it makes the physical content, two transverse photon polarizations, visible from the start.

# 2 The Coulomb gauge

## 2.1 Fixing the gauge

In the absence of sources, impose A⁰ = 0 and ∇·A = 0 (Lesson 8, Section 6.1, specialized to the source-free case, where the instantaneous Coulomb potential φ sourced by ρ = 0 can also be set to zero). The free-field equation of motion, □A^μ = 0 in the Lorenz-type form of Lesson 8, Section 4.1, reduces in this gauge to

$$ (∂_t² − ∇²) A^i = 0,    ∇·A = 0,    A⁰ = 0

Only the three components A^i remain, subject to the one constraint ∇·A = 0, leaving two independent field components at each spatial point, matching the count anticipated in Lesson 8, Section 6.2, and Lesson 7, Section 5.3.

## 2.2 The transverse projection

For a plane-wave component with wave vector k, ∇·A = 0 becomes k·A(k) = 0: the Fourier coefficient is transverse to k, exactly the transversality condition k·E₀ = 0 found directly from the Maxwell equations in Lesson 7, Section 5.2. Introduce two real unit vectors ε_1(k), ε_2(k), orthogonal to each other and to k̂, so that {ε_1(k), ε_2(k), k̂} form a right-handed orthonormal triad at every k. Any transverse vector at that k is a linear combination of ε_1(k) and ε_2(k) alone.

$$ ε_λ(k) · k = 0,    ε_λ(k) · ε_λ′(k) = δ_(λλ′),    λ, λ′ = 1, 2

### Worked example 1

For k along the z axis, exhibit an explicit choice of polarization vectors and verify orthogonality and transversality. Take ε_1(k) = x̂ and ε_2(k) = ŷ. Then ε_1·k = 0 and ε_2·k = 0 since k = kẑ, and ε_1·ε_2 = x̂·ŷ = 0, with ε_1·ε_1 = ε_2·ε_2 = 1. This is the same pair of directions used to decompose a plane wave's polarization in Lesson 7, Section 5.3, now attached to a specific momentum direction rather than a fixed axis.

# 3 The mode expansion

## 3.1 Transverse plane waves

Following the pattern of Lesson 33, Section 2, and Lesson 36, Section 3.3, expand the field in plane waves with operator coefficients, one pair of ladder operators for each momentum and each of the two polarizations.

$$ A^i(x) = Σ_(λ=1,2) ∫ d³k/(2π)³ (2ω_k)^(−1/2) [ ε_λ^i(k) a_(k,λ) e^(−ik·x) + ε_λ^i(k) a_(k,λ)† e^(+ik·x) ],    ω_k = |k|

with k·x = ω_kt − k·x as in Lesson 4, and the reality of A^i (A is a Hermitian operator, since it represents a real classical field) built in by using the same real ε_λ^i(k) in both terms, as the real scalar field of Lesson 33, Section 2.1, used the same structure. By construction, ∂_iA^i = 0 term by term, since each ε_λ(k)·k = 0, so the gauge condition holds automatically once the expansion is written this way; nothing further needs to be imposed.

## 3.2 The transverse electric field

Since A⁰ = 0, E = −∂A/∂t (Lesson 8, Section 1.2, with no φ term), and differentiating the expansion gives

$$ E^i(x) = Σ_λ ∫ d³k/(2π)³ (ω_k/2)^(1/2) i [ ε_λ^i(k) a_(k,λ) e^(−ik·x) − ε_λ^i(k) a_(k,λ)† e^(+ik·x) ]

This is E^T, the transverse electric field, and it is the momentum conjugate to A^i found in Section 1.1. The magnetic field B = ∇×A follows in the same way from spatial derivatives of the expansion and is not needed explicitly in what follows.

### Worked example 2

Verify that A^i(x) as given is a solution of (∂_t² − ∇²)A^i = 0. Each plane wave e^(∓ik·x) satisfies this equation exactly when ω_k² = |k|², the massless dispersion relation of Lesson 3, Section 5.4 (m = 0), which is built into the definition ω_k = |k|. The check is identical in form to Lesson 18, Worked example 1, specialized to zero mass.

# 4 Commutation relations and the Hamiltonian

## 4.1 The ladder algebra

Postulate, as the quantization step, that photons are bosons with the same algebra as the scalar field's ladder operators, one independent pair for each momentum and each polarization.

$$ [a_(k,λ), a_(k′,λ′)†] = (2π)³ δ³(k − k′) δ_(λλ′),    [a_(k,λ), a_(k′,λ′)] = [a_(k,λ)†, a_(k′,λ′)†] = 0

Bosonic commutators, not anticommutators, are the correct choice for a spin-1 field on the same grounds Lesson 37, Section 3, established for spin 0 and spin 1/2: a stable, causal theory forces the assignment, and the photon's integer spin (1, though the argument for its exact value is not made in this course) places it on the same side of the spin-statistics theorem as the scalar field. The corresponding equal-time commutator of the fields follows by the calculation of Lesson 33, Section 7.1, adapted to a two-index sum over polarizations.

$$ [A^i(t, x), E^j(t, y)] = i ∫ d³k/(2π)³ Σ_λ ε_λ^i(k)ε_λ^j(k) e^(ik·(x−y)) = i [ δ^(ij) δ³(x − y) ] ^T

## 4.2 The transverse delta function

The polarization sum Σ_λ ε_λ^i(k)ε_λ^j(k) is not δ^(ij): it is the projector onto the plane transverse to k, since ε_1, ε_2, and k̂ form a complete orthonormal set (Lesson 1, Section 3.3, applied to three dimensions).

$$ Σ_(λ=1,2) ε_λ^i(k) ε_λ^j(k) = δ^(ij) − k̂^i k̂^j

Substituting into Section 4.1's result gives [A^i(t,x), E^j(t,y)] = i[δ^(ij)δ³(x−y) − ∫(d³k/(2π)³)k̂^ik̂^j e^(ik·(x−y))], a modified delta function with its longitudinal part subtracted, called the transverse delta function δ^T_(ij)(x−y). This is not an error; it is the correct canonical commutator for a constrained system quantized after the constraint has been solved, since A^i and E^j, both already transverse by construction (Section 2.2), cannot be canonically conjugate in the naive, unconstrained sense used for an unconstrained field like the real scalar of Lesson 33. The appearance of exactly the transverse projector, rather than the full delta function, is the direct fingerprint, at the level of the canonical commutator, of the gauge-fixing performed in Section 2.

### Worked example 3

Confirm the polarization sum identity of Section 4.2 using the explicit vectors of Worked example 1. With ε_1 = x̂, ε_2 = ŷ, and k̂ = ẑ, Σ_λ ε_λ^iε_λ^j has nonzero entries only for i, j ∈ {1, 2} (the x and y directions), each equal to 1 on the diagonal, matching δ^(ij) − k̂^ik̂^j, which equals 1 for i = j ∈ {1,2}, 0 for i = j = 3 (since k̂³k̂³ = 1 there), and 0 for i ≠ j, as the direct sum gives.

## 4.3 The Hamiltonian

The Hamiltonian density is ℋ = (1/2)(E² + B²) (the field-theoretic energy density of the electromagnetic field, matching the classical expression familiar from Lesson 26, Section 6.2, once (1/2)(E²−B²) is Legendre-transformed as the real scalar field was in Lesson 33, Section 3.1). Substituting the mode expansion and using the orthonormality of the polarization vectors, the cross terms between different λ vanish and, after normal ordering as in Lesson 33, Section 3.1 and Lesson 35, Section 3.2,

$$ H = Σ_(λ=1,2) ∫ d³k/(2π)³ ω_k a_(k,λ)† a_(k,λ)

Two independent oscillators contribute at each momentum, one for each polarization, doubling the mode count relative to the single real scalar field of Lesson 33 at the same |k|. The discarded normal-ordering constant is therefore exactly twice the scalar field's, and this factor of two is precisely the one used without derivation in Lesson 35, Section 5.2: the electromagnetic Casimir force per unit area, −π²/(240a⁴) in natural units, is twice the coefficient for a single scalar field with Dirichlet conditions, because the photon carries two polarizations to the scalar's one.

### Worked example 4

State the vacuum energy density of the free electromagnetic field with a momentum cutoff Λ, using the result of Lesson 35, Worked example 4. A single massless real scalar field has vacuum energy density Λ⁴/(16π²) (that example). The photon field, with two independent polarizations at each k, has exactly twice this, Λ⁴/(8π²), consistent with the factor of two carried through the Hamiltonian of Section 4.3.

# 5 The photon states

## 5.1 Vacuum and one-photon states

The vacuum |0⟩ is annihilated by every a_(k,λ). One-photon states are |k, λ⟩ = √(2ω_k) a_(k,λ)†|0⟩, with the relativistic normalization ⟨k′, λ′|k, λ⟩ = 2ω_k(2π)³δ³(k − k′)δ_(λλ′), the same convention used for the scalar and Dirac one-particle states (Lessons 33 and 36). The matrix element of the field between the vacuum and a one-photon state is

$$ ⟨0| A^i(x) |k, λ⟩ = ε_λ^i(k) e^(−ik·x)

analogous to ⟨0|ψ(x)|p,s⟩ = u_s(p)e^(−ip·x) of Lesson 36, Section 6.1: the polarization vector ε_λ(k) is the amplitude that plays, for the photon, the role the spinor u_s(p) plays for the electron. From Lesson 52 onward, ε_λ^μ(k) is the factor attached to every external photon line in a Feynman diagram, exactly as u, ū, v, v̄ are attached to external electron and positron lines.

## 5.2 The photon is its own antiparticle

Because A^μ is Hermitian, a single species of ladder operator, a and a†, suffices; there is no analogue of the independent b and d operators of the Dirac field. This is the structure already anticipated in Lesson 23, Exercise 10, and Lesson 31, Section 5.1: a real (Hermitian) field describes a particle that is its own antiparticle. The photon carries no conserved charge to distinguish it from an "anti-photon," consistent with its electric neutrality.

### Worked example 5

Explain why this reasoning does not apply to the electron field of Lesson 36. The Dirac field ψ is not Hermitian, ψ† ≠ ψ, because ψ carries the electron's charge and ψ† the opposite charge; the two are related by the charge conjugation operation of Lesson 23, Section 5, not by simple equality. A field can describe a particle equal to its own antiparticle only if it is possible for the field itself to be Hermitian, which requires the particle to be electrically neutral, among other conditions; the photon satisfies this, the electron does not.

# 6 Gauge invariance and the polarization sum

## 6.1 What the choice of gauge does not affect

Physical predictions, computed as squared amplitudes summed or averaged over polarizations, must not depend on the choice of ε_1(k), ε_2(k) at fixed k (any two orthonormal transverse pairs are related by a rotation about k̂, and no observable can prefer one such rotation over another), nor, more deeply, on whether the Coulomb gauge of this lesson or the covariant gauge of Lesson 71 was used to derive them. The two-polarization counting of this lesson and the four-component covariant field of Lesson 71, which carries unphysical polarizations removed only at the level of the full amplitude, must agree once every physical cross section is computed; this agreement is not automatic in the bookkeeping and is guaranteed by the Ward identities of Lesson 72.

## 6.2 The polarization sum used in cross sections

A calculation that sums or averages over photon polarizations needs Σ_λ ε_λ^με_λ^ν, the four-vector extension of Section 4.2's projector, contracted into the rest of the amplitude. Because every physical amplitude M^μ built from a conserved current satisfies the Ward identity k_μM^μ = 0 (stated here as a preview; derived in Lesson 72), any piece of Σ_λ ε_λ^με_λ^ν proportional to k^μ or k^ν drops out of |M|² once contracted with such an amplitude, and it becomes possible, and is standard practice from Lesson 54 onward, to make the replacement

$$ Σ_λ ε_λ^μ(k) ε_λ^ν(k) → −g^μν    (inside an amplitude contracted with conserved currents)

This is a substantial simplification: the left side depends on the explicit transverse vectors ε_1(k), ε_2(k), awkward to carry through a long calculation, while the right side is a single covariant tensor. Lesson 52 states the precise conditions under which the replacement is valid and uses it immediately; this lesson establishes only what the exact left-hand side is and why the extra, gauge-dependent terms are permitted to be dropped.

### Worked example 6

Explain in one sentence why the replacement of Section 6.2 could not be justified from Section 4.2's three-dimensional identity alone. Section 4.2's identity, Σ_λ ε_λ^iε_λ^j = δ^(ij) − k̂^ik̂^j, is exact and gauge-independent as written, but it is a purely spatial, frame-dependent statement tied to the Coulomb gauge's preferred time direction; promoting it to the covariant −g^μν of Section 6.2 requires the extra input of current conservation, which is a statement about the rest of the diagram, not about the polarization sum by itself.

# 8 Summary

1. The electromagnetic Lagrangian's gauge invariance makes π⁰ vanish identically, a constraint that blocks naive quantization of all four components of A_μ.

2. Fixing the Coulomb gauge, A⁰ = 0 and ∇·A = 0, leaves exactly two independent, transverse field components at each momentum.

3. The field is expanded in two real polarization vectors ε_1(k), ε_2(k) per momentum, with operator coefficients a_(k,λ) and a_(k,λ)†.

4. Bosonic commutators [a,a†] = (2π)³δ³δ_λλ′ reproduce the equal-time commutator of A and E with the transverse delta function, the fingerprint of the gauge-fixed, constrained quantization.

5. The Hamiltonian is a sum of two independent oscillators per momentum; the vacuum energy is exactly twice that of a single real scalar field, explaining the Casimir coefficient of Lesson 35.

6. The matrix element ⟨0|A^μ(x)|k,λ⟩ = ε_λ^μ(k)e^(−ikx) is the photon's one-particle amplitude, used as the external-line factor in Feynman diagrams.

7. The photon, described by a Hermitian field, is its own antiparticle.

8. The polarization sum Σ_λε_λ^με_λ^ν may be replaced by −g^μν inside an amplitude contracted with conserved currents, a simplification whose justification, the Ward identity, is deferred to Lesson 72 but is used constantly from Lesson 54 onward.

# Solutions

*Attempt each exercise before reading its solution. If you are stuck, read only the first sentence of the solution, then try again.*

**1.** F_αβF^αβ = g^(αμ)g^(βν)F_αβF_μν, so ∂(F_αβF^αβ)/∂(∂_0A_ν) = 2F^(αβ)(∂F_αβ/∂(∂_0A_ν)). Since F_αβ = ∂_αA_β − ∂_βA_α, ∂F_αβ/∂(∂_0A_ν) = δ_α^0δ_β^ν − δ_β^0δ_α^ν, so 2F^(αβ)(δ_α^0δ_β^ν−δ_β^0δ_α^ν) = 2F^(0ν) − 2F^(ν0) = 4F^(0ν) (using antisymmetry). So ∂ℒ/∂(∂_0A_ν) = −(1/4)(4F^(0ν)) = −F^(0ν).

**2.** F^(0i) = g^(00)g^(ii)F_(0i) = (1)(−1)F_(0i) = −F_(0i) = −E^i (using F_(0i)=E^i, Lesson 26, Section 6.2). So π^i = −F^(0i) = −(−E^i) = E^i.

**3.** For A(x) = A(k)e^(ik·x) (a single Fourier mode), ∇·A = ik·A(k)e^(ik·x). Setting this to zero for all x requires k·A(k) = 0.

**4.** ε_1·k = ŷ·(kx̂) = 0, ε_2·k = ẑ·(kx̂) = 0, ε_1·ε_2 = ŷ·ẑ = 0, ε_1·ε_1=ε_2·ε_2=1, confirming both orthonormality and transversality.

**5.** ∇·A^i means ∂_iA^i summed; differentiating the mode expansion brings down a factor ik_i (or −ik_i) from each exponential, giving terms proportional to k·ε_λ(k), which vanish by ε_λ(k)·k=0 for each λ, for every k in the integral, so ∇·A=0 identically.

**6.** ∂_tA^i brings down ∓iω_k from each exponential: ∂_tA^i = Σ_λ∫d³k/(2π)³(2ω_k)^(−1/2)[−iω_kε_λ^i a e^(−ikx) + iω_kε_λ^i a† e^(ikx)] = Σ_λ∫d³k/(2π)³ i(ω_k/2)^(1/2)[−ε_λ^i a e^(−ikx) + ε_λ^i a† e^(ikx)]. With E = −∂A/∂t, E^i = Σ_λ∫d³k/(2π)³ i(ω_k/2)^(1/2)[ε_λ^i a e^(−ikx) − ε_λ^i a† e^(ikx)], matching Section 3.2.

**7.** Symmetry: δ^(ij)−k̂^ik̂^j is manifestly symmetric under i↔j, since both δ^(ij) and k̂^ik̂^j are. Trace: Σ_iδ^(ii) − Σ_ik̂^ik̂^i = 3 − 1 = 2, using Σ_ik̂^ik̂^i = \|k̂\|² = 1. The trace 2 counts the number of transverse directions, the two physical polarizations.

**8.** For any unit vector n̂, choose any orthonormal ε_1, ε_2 completing {ε_1,ε_2,n̂} to a basis. The completeness relation for an orthonormal basis of ℝ³ (Lesson 1, Section 3.3, generalized) states Σ over all three basis vectors of their outer products equals the identity: ε_1^iε_1^j + ε_2^iε_2^j + n̂^in̂^j = δ^(ij). Rearranging gives Σ_λε_λ^iε_λ^j = δ^(ij) − n̂^in̂^j, the general form of Section 4.2's identity with k̂ replaced by the arbitrary n̂.

**9.** [A^i(t,x),E^j(t,y)] = Σ_(λ,λ′)∫∫d³kd³k′/(2π)⁶(4ω_kω_k′)^(−1/2) i [ε_λ^i(k)ε_λ′^j(k′)e^(−ikx+ik′y)[a_(k,λ),a_(k′,λ′)†] − ε_λ^i(k)ε_λ′^j(k′)e^(ikx−ik′y)[a_(k,λ)†,a_(k′,λ′)]]. Using the postulated commutator, [a_(k,λ),a_(k′,λ′)†]=(2π)³δ³(k−k′)δ_λλ′ collapses the k′ integral and the λ′ sum, and the second term similarly, giving [A^i,E^j] = i∫d³k/(2π)³Σ_λε_λ^i(k)ε_λ^j(k)e^(ik·(x−y)) (both terms combine with the same sign since one has an extra minus from E's definition, matching Section 4.1's stated result).

**10.** [H,N] involves [a_(k,λ)†a_(k,λ), a_(k′,λ′)†a_(k′,λ′)] for each pair of modes; for the same mode this vanishes trivially (an operator commutes with itself and with the identity), and for different modes both a_(k,λ)†a_(k,λ) and a_(k′,λ′)†a_(k′,λ′) commute with each other since they involve independent, mutually commuting ladder operators (Lesson 34, Section 2.1, applied mode by mode). Since [H,N]=0, N is conserved for the free field: a state with a definite number of photons keeps that number under free time evolution, though Lesson 40's interaction term, built to change particle number, will not commute with N once electrons are coupled in.

**11.** ⟨k,λ\|H\|k,λ⟩ = 2ω_k⟨0\|a_(k,λ)Ha_(k,λ)†\|0⟩/1 (using the normalization \|k,λ⟩=√(2ω_k)a_(k,λ)†\|0⟩, so ⟨k,λ\|H\|k,λ⟩ = 2ω_k⟨0\|a_(k,λ)Ha_(k,λ)†\|0⟩). Using [H,a_(k,λ)†]=ω_ka_(k,λ)† (the same commutator structure as Lesson 34's single oscillator, extended mode by mode) and H\|0⟩=0, a_(k,λ)Ha_(k,λ)†\|0⟩ = a_(k,λ)[a_(k,λ)†H+ω_ka_(k,λ)†]\|0⟩ = ω_ka_(k,λ)a_(k,λ)†\|0⟩ = ω_k(2π)³δ³(0)\|0⟩ formally, and normalizing correctly (dividing out the same δ³(0) convention used throughout for continuum normalization, as in Lesson 33's one-particle state checks) gives the finite, physical result ⟨k,λ\|H\|k,λ⟩/⟨k,λ\|k,λ⟩ = ω_k, the single-photon energy.

**12.** From Lesson 35, Worked example 4, the scalar field's vacuum energy density is Λ⁴/(16π²). The photon field's is twice this, Λ⁴/(8π²). With Λ = 1 GeV = 10⁹ eV, Λ⁴ = 10³⁶ eV⁴, so the density is 10³⁶/(8π²) eV⁴ ≈ 1.27×10³⁴ eV⁴ (a formal, cutoff-dependent number, not a physical prediction, exactly as flagged in Lesson 35).

**13.** ⟨0\|A^i(x)\|k,λ⟩ = √(2ω_k)⟨0\|A^i(x)a_(k,λ)†\|0⟩. Only the a_(k′,λ′) term of the mode expansion of A^i contributes, since the a† term would need ⟨0\|a_(k′,λ′)†a_(k,λ)†\|0⟩=0. So ⟨0\|A^i(x)\|k,λ⟩ = √(2ω_k)∫d³k′/(2π)³(2ω_k′)^(−1/2)ε_λ′^i(k′)e^(−ik′x)⟨0\|a_(k′,λ′)a_(k,λ)†\|0⟩. Using ⟨0\|a_(k′,λ′)a_(k,λ)†\|0⟩=(2π)³δ³(k−k′)δ_λλ′, the k′ integral collapses to k′=k, λ′=λ, giving √(2ω_k)(2ω_k)^(−1/2)ε_λ^i(k)e^(−ikx) = ε_λ^i(k)e^(−ikx).

**14.** A Hermitian field satisfies φ=φ†, but the U(1) transformation associated with a conserved charge acts as φ→e^(iqα)φ for a field of charge q (Lesson 31); demanding φ transform into itself under this (since Hermiticity must be preserved, (e^(iqα)φ)† = e^(−iqα)φ† = e^(−iqα)φ should also equal φ, or more directly, a Hermitian field's own charge current j^μ built from φ alone is forced to vanish unless q=0, by the same real-field argument used in Lesson 18, Exercise 15, and Lesson 23, Exercise 10, for the Klein–Gordon field) forces q=0. The photon field A^μ is Hermitian and therefore carries no electric charge, consistent with observation.

**15.** In words: Section 4.2's identity generalizes to four dimensions as Σ_λε_λ^με_λ^ν = −g^μν + (terms built from k^μ, k^ν, and the fixed time direction singled out by the Coulomb gauge). When this full expression is contracted with M_μ and M_ν* from a matrix element satisfying k_μM^μ=0 (and its conjugate k_νM^ν*=0), every term carrying a factor of k^μ or k^ν vanishes on contraction, leaving only the −g^μν piece to contribute to \|Σ_λε_λ^μM_μ\|² = Σ_λ\|ε_λ·M\|², which therefore equals −g^μνM_μM_ν* = M_μM^μ*, justifying the replacement of Section 6.2 for any amplitude built from a conserved current.

**16.** The extra term to be shown to drop out is k̂^ik̂^j (or its four-dimensional, frame-dependent generalization built from k^μ and the preferred time direction of the Coulomb gauge). For it to vanish upon contraction with the rest of a physical amplitude, the amplitude must satisfy the Ward identity, k_μM^μ(k) = 0: the current to which the photon couples must be conserved, so that any piece of the polarization sum proportional to the photon's own momentum contracts to zero against the amplitude, the property (established in general only in Lesson 72, but already used implicitly for the classical current of Lesson 24) that removes the gauge-dependent, unphysical part of the polarization sum from every physical prediction.
