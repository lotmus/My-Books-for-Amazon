# How to use this prologue

Feynman's third lecture adds the electron. Photons were Prologue 1–2. Electrons have arrows too: an amplitude to go from one place and time to another. The new event is a junction, a vertex, where an electron emits or absorbs a photon. The amplitude for that junction is a small number, about 0.1 in the units of those lectures, whose square is the same order as this course's α≈1/137. Three actions. That is the theory. Expect five to seven hours.

## Learning objectives

- Write the three basic actions of QED in words.

- Assign an arrow to an electron going from A to B, distinct from a photon going from A to B.

- Identify the vertex as the only interaction: an electron emits or absorbs a photon, with an amplitude of order 0.1.

- Draw the picture of an electron scattering off another by exchanging a photon.

- Connect the 0.1 to α=e²/4π of Lesson 44 without claiming the conventions already match.

Everything this course will compute is an assembly of those three. Compton scattering (Lesson 57) is two vertices and one internal electron. Møller scattering (Lesson 55) is two vertices and one internal photon. Pair production (Lesson 58) is the electron line running backward in time in the sense of Lesson 23.

# 2 Electrons have arrows too

An electron's amplitude to go from one place to another is not the same function as a photon's. The electron has mass, so there is an amplitude to go slower than light; a photon's amplitude for that is zero. The electron has a direction of spin, which Prologue 1 did not need. Lesson 20 will write the Dirac equation that encodes both. For this opening it is enough that E(A→B) is an arrow (later a spinor sandwich) and that it is added over paths just as the photon's was.

# 3 The vertex is small

The amplitude to emit or absorb a photon at a point is a small number. In Feynman's easy lectures it is about −0.1. In this course's Heaviside–Lorentz units the charge e satisfies e²/4π=α≈1/137, so e≈0.303, and the vertex of Lesson 45 is +ieγ^μ. The two numbers are the same coupling in different conventions and at different stages of packaging. The physics is: an extra photon on an electron line is a 10% effect on the amplitude, a 1% effect on a probability, which is why perturbation theory in Parts VIII–IX can start with tree diagrams and add loops.

### Worked example 1

Why does exchanging one photon between two electrons produce a factor of order α, not of order 0.1? Two vertices, each of order e, and a photon connecting them: e×e, which is 4πα. Rutherford scattering's 1/t pole in Lesson 54 is that factor sitting on a photon that is off shell.

# 4 Scattering as a picture

Two electrons in, two electrons out: each electron line runs through, and a photon is exchanged. That is the first diagram of Lesson 55. If the electrons are identical, a second diagram exchanges which outgoing line is which, with a minus sign (Lesson 37). The easy picture already knows there will be two ways; the later algebra computes their interference.

# Summary

1. A photon goes from place to place.

2. An electron goes from place to place.

3. An electron emits or absorbs a photon, with a small amplitude.

4. Scattering is exchange of a photon, drawn as a diagram.

5. α≈1/137 is that small amplitude, squared and conventionally normalized.

# Solutions

**1.** Photon goes; electron goes; electron emits or absorbs a photon.

**2.** The photon is massless and on-shell travels at c; the electron is massive.

**3.** e²/4π=α in this course's units.

**4.** Electron in, electron out; photon in, photon out; two vertices and an internal electron connecting them, plus the crossed diagram.

**5.** Each extra vertex brings another factor of e, so a loop, which has two extra vertices relative to a tree of the same process, is down by α.

**6.** Lesson 45.

**7.** The same electron-goes action, with the line oriented from the positron's momentum, Lesson 23.

**8.** Four vertices ~ e⁴ ~ α², a one-loop or two-photon-exchange order.
