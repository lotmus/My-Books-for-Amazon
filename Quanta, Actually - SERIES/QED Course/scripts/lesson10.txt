# How to use this lesson

Part I built the mathematics and the classical field theory that quantum electrodynamics rests on. Part II builds ordinary quantum mechanics, the theory whose relativistic and field-theoretic generalization is the actual subject of the course. This lesson takes the first step: it identifies the state of a quantum system with a vector in a complex vector space, the kind of space studied abstractly in Lesson 1, and it identifies that vector space concretely as a space of wave functions.

Two things are being asked of the reader at once. The first is a physical postulate, the Born rule, which cannot be derived and must be adopted. Lesson 9 has already met it for light: the classical field amplitude, squared, became the probability of detecting one photon. The Born rule says the same of an electron's wave function. The second is the mathematical structure, Hilbert space, that makes the postulate consistent. Keep the two separate: the physics says what |ψ|² means, and the mathematics of Lesson 1 says why that quantity behaves the way probabilities must. Expect six to eight focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- State the Born rule and explain what it says about a single measurement and about an ensemble of identical measurements.

- Define the wave function ψ(x, t) and its normalization condition.

- Define the Hilbert space L²(ℝ³) and its inner product, and connect it to the abstract inner product spaces of Lesson 1.

- Compute probability densities and probability currents and verify the continuity equation they satisfy.

- Expand a wave function in an orthonormal basis and interpret the expansion coefficients.

- Construct a normalized Gaussian wave packet and compute its position uncertainty.

- State the superposition principle and explain the physical meaning of interference between amplitudes.

- Recognize the structural parallel between ψ(x) and the abstract kets of Lesson 1, in preparation for the operator formalism of Lesson 11.

# 1 The physical postulate

## 1.1 Why a probabilistic description is needed

Classical mechanics describes a particle by a definite trajectory x(t). Experiments such as electron diffraction show that this description fails: identical particles prepared identically produce a spread of outcomes, with a definite statistical pattern, when their position is measured. Quantum mechanics abandons the trajectory and instead assigns a probability distribution to the outcome of a position measurement, updated in time by a wave equation to be introduced in Lesson 12.

## 1.2 The Born rule

The state of a particle at time t is described by a complex-valued function ψ(x, t), the wave function. The Born rule states that the probability of finding the particle in a small volume d³x around the point x, upon measurement at time t, is

$$ dP(x, t) = |ψ(x, t)|² d³x

Since the particle is somewhere with certainty, the total probability must be one.

$$ ∫ |ψ(x, t)|² d³x = 1

This is the normalization condition. A function satisfying it is called normalized, in exactly the sense of Lesson 1's normalized vectors, with the norm built from an integral rather than a finite sum. The complex phase of ψ carries no direct probabilistic meaning by itself, exactly as an overall phase was invisible to |z|² in Lesson 1; but relative phases between different parts of ψ produce interference, as shown in Section 6.

### Worked example 1

Verify that ψ(x) = A e^(−x²/2a²) (one dimension, for simplicity) can be normalized. Using the Gaussian integral of Lesson 2, ∫|ψ|²dx = A²∫e^(−x²/a²)dx = A²a√π. Setting this to 1 gives A = (πa²)^(−1/4). The probability density |ψ(x)|² = (πa²)^(−1/2)e^(−x²/a²) is itself a Gaussian, with the particle most likely to be found near x = 0 and the probability falling off over a distance of order a.

# 2 The Hilbert space of wave functions

## 2.1 Square-integrable functions

Not every function can be normalized; the integral ∫|ψ|²d³x must converge. The set of functions for which it does, with functions differing on a set of zero volume identified, is a vector space: any complex linear combination αψ₁ + βψ₂ of square-integrable functions is again square-integrable, by an inequality proved in Exercise 15. This space is denoted L²(ℝ³), and it is the concrete example, promised in Lesson 1, of an infinite-dimensional complex vector space.

## 2.2 The inner product

For φ, ψ ∈ L²(ℝ³), define

$$ ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = ∫ φ*(x) ψ(x) d³x

This satisfies every axiom of the inner product introduced abstractly in Lesson 1: it is conjugate symmetric, ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = ⟨ψ|φ⟩*; it is linear in the second argument and antilinear in the first, matching the physics convention of Lesson 1; and ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = ∫|ψ|²d³x ≥ 0, vanishing only for ψ = 0. The normalization condition of Section 1.2 is exactly ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1, unit norm in this inner product, the same condition met abstractly in Worked example 5 of Lesson 1.

## 2.3 Completeness and the meaning of "Hilbert space"

A Hilbert space is an inner product space that is complete: every sequence of vectors that ought to converge, in the sense that its terms get arbitrarily close to each other, does converge to a vector in the space. L²(ℝ³) has this property, which is a nontrivial fact of analysis not proved here, but the concept matters physically: it guarantees that limits of physically reasonable approximations, such as a sequence of increasingly sharply peaked wave packets, stay within the space of legitimate quantum states, and it guarantees that the orthonormal expansions of Section 4 actually converge to the function being expanded, not merely approximate it.

### Worked example 2

Check the inner-product axioms for two Gaussians centered at different points, φ(x) = (πa²)^(−1/4)e^(−(x−x₁)²/2a²) and ψ(x) = (πa²)^(−1/4)e^(−(x−x₂)²/2a²) in one dimension. Their overlap is ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = (πa²)^(−1/2)∫e^(−(x−x₁)²/2a²)e^(−(x−x₂)²/2a²)dx, a Gaussian integral of the type in Worked example 3 of Lesson 2, giving ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = e^(−(x₁−x₂)²/4a²). This overlap is real, positive, and equal to 1 when x₁ = x₂ and falls toward 0 as the packets separate: two states become more nearly orthogonal as they become more distinguishable, an idea sharpened in Lesson 11.

# 3 Probability current and conservation of probability

## 3.1 Local conservation

Total probability is conserved by the normalization condition, but this global statement should follow from a local one, exactly as global charge conservation in Lesson 7 followed from a local continuity equation. Define the probability current

$$ j(x, t) = (ℏ/m) Im[ψ*∇ψ] = (ℏ/2mi)(ψ*∇ψ − ψ∇ψ*)

Using the Schrödinger equation, to be introduced in Lesson 12, one can show

$$ ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·j = 0,    ρ = |ψ|²

This is a continuity equation of the form met in Lesson 7 for charge, and in Lesson 2 for the diffusion equation. It expresses that probability, like charge, is neither created nor destroyed but merely flows from one region to another. Lesson 24 derives the relativistic analogue of this current directly from the Dirac equation, where it becomes j^μ = ψ̄γ^μψ and plays the role of the electromagnetic current itself.

## 3.2 Physical meaning of the current

For a plane wave ψ = A e^(i(kx−ωt)) in one dimension, ∇ψ = ikψ, so j = (ℏk/m)|A|² = v|A|², the probability density times the classical velocity v = ℏk/m. This matches the intuitive picture of a stream of particles moving at velocity v with density |A|²; the current formula generalizes this picture to any wave function, including ones with no definite velocity.

### Worked example 3

Verify the continuity equation for a one-dimensional plane wave using the free Schrödinger equation iℏ∂_tψ = −(ℏ²/2m)∂ₓ²ψ (anticipating Lesson 12). For ψ = Ae^(i(kx−ωt)), ρ = |A|² is constant in time, so ∂ρ/∂t = 0. The current j = (ℏk/m)|A|² is also constant in x, so ∂ⱼ/∂x = 0. Both sides of the continuity equation vanish separately, consistent with a stationary, uniform probability flow.

# 4 Expansion in a basis

## 4.1 Orthonormal bases in L²

A set of functions {uₙ(x)} is orthonormal if ⟨uₙ|uₘ⟩ = δₙₘ, and complete if every ψ ∈ L² can be written as a convergent sum

$$ ψ(x) = Σₙ cₙ uₙ(x),    cₙ = ⟨uₙ|ψ⟩ = ∫ uₙ*(x) ψ(x) d³x

This is the infinite-dimensional analogue of the finite expansion of Lesson 1's Section 3.3, with the sum over a discrete or continuous label n and the resolution of the identity Σₙ|uₙ⟩⟨uₙ| = I holding in the same sense. The Fourier series of Lesson 2 is the special case in which the basis functions are e^(2πinx/L)/√L on an interval of length L.

## 4.2 The Born rule for expansion coefficients

Substituting the expansion into the normalization condition and using orthonormality,

$$ ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = Σₙ |cₙ|² = 1

so |cₙ|² is itself a probability: the probability that a measurement associated with the basis {uₙ} yields the outcome labeled n. This is the general form of the Born rule, of which Section 1.2 is the special case where the basis is the (continuous, non-normalizable) family of position eigenfunctions, and it is the form used repeatedly once eigenstates of energy, angular momentum, and spin are introduced (Lessons 12 through 15).

### Worked example 4

For a particle confined to an infinite square well of width L, the orthonormal energy basis is uₙ(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L) on 0 < x < L, n = 1, 2, …, extended by zero outside. A wave function ψ(x) = √(30/L⁵) x(L−x) on the well (a simple polynomial shape) can be expanded in this basis; the coefficient c₁ = ⟨u₁|ψ⟩ turns out to dominate the sum, meaning a measurement of energy is most likely, though not certain, to find the particle in its ground state. This calculation is completed in Lesson 12's exercises once the energies Eₙ are available.

# 5 Position and the Dirac delta basis

## 5.1 The non-normalizable position eigenfunctions

It is convenient, though not rigorous within L² itself, to treat the position variable x₀ as labeling a basis δ(x − x₀), since formally ∫δ(x−x₀)ψ(x)d³x = ψ(x₀) extracts the coefficient of this basis element exactly as Section 4.1 extracts cₙ. The delta function is not itself square-integrable, and this "basis" is continuous rather than discrete, with the sum of Section 4.1 replaced by an integral and δₙₘ replaced by δ(x−x′): ⟨δₓ|δₓ′⟩ "=" δ(x−x′). These technical points are handled properly in the theory of rigged Hilbert spaces, which is not needed for this course; what matters is the working rule that ψ(x) is itself the array of expansion coefficients of the abstract state in the position basis, as anticipated when Lesson 1 distinguished a vector from its coordinate column.

## 5.2 Momentum eigenfunctions

The plane waves e^(ikx)/√(2π), in one dimension, play the same formal role for momentum that δ(x−x₀) plays for position; Lesson 13 makes this basis precise using the Fourier transform of Lesson 2 and shows that expanding ψ in this basis gives exactly the momentum-space wave function ψ̃(k).

### Worked example 5

Compute ⟨δₓ₀|ψ⟩ for ψ(x) = (πa²)^(−1/4)e^(−x²/2a²): the formal inner product with the delta function returns ψ(x₀) itself, illustrating that the "coefficients" cₓ₀ of the position basis expansion are simply the values of the wave function.

# 6 Superposition and interference

## 6.1 The superposition principle

If ψ₁ and ψ₂ are possible states of a system, so is any normalized linear combination c₁ψ₁ + c₂ψ₂. This is the statement that the space of states is a vector space, and it is the direct quantum-mechanical use of the linearity introduced abstractly in Lesson 1. It is also the source of every interference phenomenon in quantum mechanics.

## 6.2 Interference in the probability density

For a superposition ψ = c₁ψ₁ + c₂ψ₂ with ψ₁, ψ₂ orthonormal, the probability density is

$$ |ψ|² = |c₁|²|ψ₁|² + |c₂|²|ψ₂|² + 2 Re(c₁*c₂ ψ₁*ψ₂)

The first two terms are what classical probability theory would predict for a system that is "really" in state 1 with probability |c₁|² or state 2 with probability |c₂|². The third term, the interference term, has no classical counterpart; it is precisely the interference of amplitudes met abstractly in Section 1.5 of Lesson 1, |A₁+A₂|² = |A₁|²+|A₂|²+2Re(A₁*A₂), now applied to spatially varying amplitudes ψ₁(x) and ψ₂(x) rather than to two numbers.

### Worked example 6

Two Gaussian wave packets, ψ₁ centered at x = −d and ψ₂ centered at x = +d, both with width a ≪ d so that they are nearly orthogonal (Worked example 2), are combined as ψ = (ψ₁+ψ₂)/√2. Near x = 0, both ψ₁ and ψ₂ have appreciable, comparable magnitude, and the cross term 2Re(ψ₁*ψ₂)/2 can add constructively or destructively depending on any relative phase between the packets, producing fringes in |ψ|² even though each individual packet alone would give a smooth, featureless density there. This is the double-slit pattern in embryonic form; a full treatment requires the time evolution of Lesson 12.

# 7 Uncertainty as a property of the wave function

## 7.1 The standard deviation of position

For a normalized ψ, the expectation value and variance of position are

$$ ⟨x⟩ = ∫ x |ψ(x)|² d³x,    σₓ² = ⟨x²⟩ − ⟨x⟩²

These are the ordinary statistical mean and variance of the probability distribution |ψ|², nothing more. σₓ measures the spread of possible outcomes if position is measured on an ensemble of identically prepared systems; it says nothing about the precision of any single measurement, and nothing about disturbing the system, contrary to a common but imprecise description of the uncertainty principle. Lesson 13 will show that σₓ and the corresponding σₚ satisfy a lower bound, but that inequality belongs to the algebra of operators, not to this lesson's descriptive statistics.

### Worked example 7

For the Gaussian of Worked example 1, ⟨x⟩ = 0 by symmetry, and ⟨x²⟩ = ∫x²|ψ|²dx = (πa²)^(−1/2)∫x²e^(−x²/a²)dx. Using the result of Worked example 3 in Lesson 2 with the substitution a → 1/a² there, ∫x²e^(−x²/a²)dx = (a√π/2)a², so ⟨x²⟩ = a²/2, and σₓ = a/√2. The width parameter a in the wave function and the statistical spread σₓ differ by this factor of √2, a distinction worth keeping straight when comparing formulas from different sources.

# 9 Summary

1. The Born rule states that |ψ(x, t)|² is the probability density for finding a particle at x at time t; total probability equals one.

2. The space L²(ℝ³) of square-integrable functions, with inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = ∫φ*ψ d³x, realizes concretely the abstract Hilbert space structure of Lesson 1.

3. A probability current j satisfies ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·j = 0, the quantum-mechanical analogue of charge conservation.

4. Expanding ψ in an orthonormal basis gives coefficients cₙ = ⟨uₙ|ψ⟩ with |cₙ|² interpreted as measurement probabilities.

5. The position and momentum "bases" are continuous and non-normalizable but organize the wave function as an array of expansion coefficients.

6. The superposition principle allows linear combinations of states and produces interference terms with no classical analogue.

7. ⟨x⟩ and σₓ are the mean and standard deviation of the position probability distribution, statistics of an ensemble, not properties of a single measurement.

8. Every later probabilistic statement in the course, cross sections included, is built from the same Born-rule logic introduced here.

# Solutions

*Attempt each exercise before reading its solution. If you are stuck, read only the first sentence of the solution, then try again.*

**1.** ∫|ψ|²dx = A²∫e^(−2|x|/b)dx = A² × b = 1 (using ∫₀^∞e^(−2x/b)dx = b/2 on each half-line), so A = 1/√b.

**2.** P = ∫_(−b)^b (1/b)e^(−2|x|/b)dx = (2/b)∫₀^b e^(−2x/b)dx = 1 − e^(−2) ≈ 0.865.

**3.** ⟨e^(iθ)ψ|e^(iθ)ψ⟩ = e^(−iθ)e^(iθ)⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1. The Born rule depends only on \|ψ\|², and \|e^(iθ)ψ\|² = \|ψ\|² everywhere, so the two wave functions predict identical outcome probabilities for every measurement: an overall phase is unobservable, as in Lesson 1.

**4.** ⟨ψ₁+ψ₂\|ψ₁+ψ₂⟩ = ⟨ψ₁\|ψ₁⟩+⟨ψ₁\|ψ₂⟩+⟨ψ₂\|ψ₁⟩+⟨ψ₂\|ψ₂⟩. Since ⟨ψ₂\|ψ₁⟩ = ⟨ψ₁\|ψ₂⟩*, the middle two terms sum to 2Re⟨ψ₁\|ψ₂⟩.

**5.** Cauchy Schwarz gives \|⟨φ\|ψ⟩\|² ≤ ⟨φ\|φ⟩⟨ψ\|ψ⟩ = 1 × 1 = 1, so \|⟨φ\|ψ⟩\| ≤ 1.

**6.** By Worked example 2, ⟨φ\|ψ⟩ = e^(−(3a)²/4a²) = e^(−9/4) ≈ 0.105. The overlap is small but not zero; the states are approximately, not exactly, orthogonal, with the approximation improving exponentially as the separation grows relative to a.

**7.** With ψ = Re^(iS), ψ* = Re^(−iS), ∇ψ = (∇R + iR∇S)e^(iS), so ψ*∇ψ = R∇R + iR²∇S. The imaginary part is R²∇S, so j = (ℏ/m)Im[ψ*∇ψ] = (ℏ/m)R²∇S.

**8.** A real ψ has S = 0 (or a constant), so ∇S = 0 and j = 0 everywhere: a real wave function describes no net probability flow, consistent with it representing, for example, a standing wave or a bound state with no net current.

**9.** ⟨u₁\|u₂⟩ = (2/L)∫₀^L sin(πx/L)sin(2πx/L)dx. Using the product-to-sum identity, this is (1/L)∫₀^L[cos(πx/L) − cos(3πx/L)]dx, and each cosine integrates to zero over the full range because it completes a half or one-and-a-half periods symmetric about L/2... direct evaluation gives [L/π sin(πx/L) − L/3π sin(3πx/L)]₀^L = 0, confirming orthogonality.

**10.** ⟨ψ\|ψ⟩ = (1/2)(⟨u₁\|u₁⟩ + ⟨u₂\|u₂⟩) = (1/2)(1+1) = 1, using orthonormality. The coefficient of u₁ is 1/√2, so the probability of measuring the n = 1 energy is \|1/√2\|² = 1/2.

**11.** ⟨x⟩ = ∫x\|ψ\|²dx = ∫x A²e^(−x²/a²)dx = 0 by the odd symmetry of the integrand, since \|e^(ik₀x)\|² = 1 contributes no x dependence at all; the phase factor does not affect \|ψ\|² and hence cannot shift any expectation value computed from \|ψ\|² alone.

**12.** σₓ² = ⟨x²⟩ = a²/2 as in Worked example 7, again because \|ψ\|² = A²e^(−x²/a²) is completely unaffected by the phase e^(ik₀x).

**13.** The sifting property is the defining property of the delta function, verified formally by treating δ as the limit of a sequence of increasingly narrow, tall, unit-area bumps. δ(x−x₀) is not in L² because ∫δ(x−x₀)²dx is not a finite number; informally, "squaring" an infinitely narrow, infinitely tall spike gives something even more singular, not an integrable function.

**14.** With ψ₁(0), ψ₂(0) both real and positive, \|ψ\|²(0) = (1/2)[ψ₁(0)² + ψ₂(0)² + 2ψ₁(0)ψ₂(0)] = (1/2)[ψ₁(0)+ψ₂(0)]², which is larger than (1/2)[ψ₁(0)²+ψ₂(0)²], the value \|ψ₁\|²+\|ψ₂\|² would give if halved for the same normalization: constructive interference has enhanced the density at the midpoint beyond what either packet contributes alone.

**15.** From ‖φ+ψ‖ ≤ ‖φ‖+‖ψ‖ < ∞ (both norms finite since φ, ψ ∈ L²), φ+ψ has finite norm and is therefore in L². Scaling by α, β: ‖αφ‖ = \|α\|‖φ‖ < ∞ and similarly for βψ, so ‖αφ+βψ‖ ≤ \|α\|‖φ‖+\|β\|‖ψ‖ < ∞, confirming αφ+βψ ∈ L².

**16.** Chebyshev's inequality for any probability distribution states P(\|x−⟨x⟩\| ≥ nσₓ) ≤ 1/n², proved by noting σₓ² = ∫(x−⟨x⟩)²\|ψ\|²dx ≥ ∫_(|x−⟨x⟩|≥nσₓ) (x−⟨x⟩)²\|ψ\|²dx ≥ n²σₓ² P(\|x−⟨x⟩\|≥nσₓ), and dividing by n²σₓ². For n = 3, the probability of finding the particle more than three standard deviations from the mean is at most 1/9 ≈ 0.111, a bound that holds for every normalizable wave function, however exotic its shape.
