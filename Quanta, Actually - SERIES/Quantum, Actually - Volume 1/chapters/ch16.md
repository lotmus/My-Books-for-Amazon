## 16. From Collective Phase Back to the Fields

?> If the story starts with phase and potential, where did the electric and magnetic fields go?

### From Individual Histories to Collective Phase

Now widen the picture. Instead of one electron summing over its possible histories, imagine a macroscopic coherent system (the superconductor from Chapter 13) whose microscopic state is a staggeringly complicated sum over the histories of an enormous number of particles, but whose *collective* low-energy behavior can be captured by a single macroscopic phase field, as Chapter 13 described.

The same basic structure from the last chapter reappears, essentially unchanged in outline: a phase shifts as you move through space; the electromagnetic potential modifies how it shifts; and the system's momentum and current end up tied to the combination of the phase gradient and the potential, the same *p ∝ ħ∇θ − qA* relationship from Chapter 14, now describing a collective variable shared by billions of particles instead of the private phase of one electron's wavefunction.

This is one of the more beautiful points of contact in the entire synthesis this book is attempting. At the microscopic level, quantum electrodynamics says that charged matter couples to the electromagnetic potential through the phase of individual quantum amplitudes, summed over every possible history. At the macroscopic level, a coherent quantum system carries a collective phase that couples to that very same potential, through what is structurally the same relationship. At bottom, this is one electromagnetic relationship viewed at two different scales, through two different mathematical machineries built for those scales, not two separate interactions that happen to look alike.

Calling the macroscopic phase description quantum electrodynamics wearing a disguise would overstate the case: enormous amounts of microscopic detail have been averaged over, discarded, or rendered irrelevant to get from one picture to the other. That discarding has a cost, and a story of its own, which this book takes up directly when it reaches renormalization. Think of it the way a weather forecaster relates to the underlying physics of individual air molecules: nobody doubts that a forecast obeys the same laws of thermodynamics and fluid motion that govern every molecule in the atmosphere, and nobody expects the forecaster to track any of those molecules individually to produce an accurate forecast.

Wind speed and barometric pressure are what survives, systematically, once the irrelevant microscopic detail has been accounted for and set aside. The collective phase of a superconductor survives that same kind of process from full quantum electrodynamics. But the macroscopic phase picture isn't borrowed from a different part of physics because the mathematics happens to look similar — it's built from the same underlying quantum structure, all the way down, with most of the microscopic detail integrated away rather than ignored.

That gives us a powerful organizing principle to carry forward: the electromagnetic potential couples to quantum phase, full stop — whether that phase belongs to a single quantum amplitude summed over the wild multiplicity of paths available to one electron, or to a macroscopic coherent state shared by a trillion of them. The scale changes enormously. The mathematics gets simpler in some ways as you move to the macroscopic case, and more subtle in others. But the underlying relationship — potential shapes phase, phase determines physical behavior — survives the crossing intact.

With that relationship established at both scales, return once more to the superconducting loop from Chapter 12 and look at it again, now with more machinery in hand. The collective phase has to remain self-consistent all the way around a closed path — the same requirement that produced flux quantization the first time around, but now derived from the more general phase-and-potential relationship developed across the last few chapters rather than asserted as a special case. Integrate that relationship around the loop, demand the phase return to an equivalent value, and the quantization condition falls out again, this time as a direct consequence of everything built up since Chapter 14 rather than as an isolated result.

The re-derivation itself matters less than what it reveals about how the different descriptions in this book connect to one another. At the microscopic level, quantum mechanics is a story about amplitudes and phase, summed over every possible history a particle could have lived. At the collective level, that same phase becomes a single, directly useful macroscopic variable, shared by an enormous coherent population of particles. At the classical level — the level of ordinary circuits, voltages, and currents — the electromagnetic response can be written down entirely in terms of fields, currents, inductances, and fluxes, with no explicit mention of quantum phase anywhere in sight.

The three levels are nested descriptions of one underlying structure, and the remarkable thing is that the same mathematical role — phase, organized through its electromagnetic connection — keeps showing up as the thread connecting them, whether or not the language at any given level bothers to mention it by name.

This is the point where the synthesis this book is attempting stops being a juxtaposition of two clever ideas and starts being something closer to a single argument. Feynman's path-integral picture insists on taking phase seriously for every conceivable history available to a quantum system, however microscopic. Mead's collective-electrodynamics picture insists on taking phase seriously the moment an enormous number of charges start behaving coherently, however macroscopic the resulting system becomes.

The two insights aim at the same target from two different distances and land in the same place: electromagnetism runs on phase, from a single electron's sum over histories to a superconducting ring you could hold in your hand.

One piece of the picture is still conspicuously missing, though, and it has been missing since Chapter 11. Everything so far has explained how *matter's* phase responds to an electromagnetic potential that was simply handed over as a given, already fixed, already sitting there in space waiting to be coupled to. Nothing has yet explained where the familiar electric and magnetic fields — the ones every physics student meets first, with their field lines and their right-hand rules — come from, or how Maxwell's own four famous equations fit into a story told almost entirely in the language of phase and potential. That gap is the next one to close.

### Where Did the Fields Go?

Where did **E** and **B** go? Five chapters of phase and potential, and the fields with the diagrams and the right-hand rules have barely appeared.

Nowhere. They have simply moved one step downstream. Start with the scalar and vector potentials, *φ* and **A**, exactly as before. From them, construct

*E = −∇φ − ∂A/∂t*

and

*B = ∇×A*.

These relationships define how the potentials and the familiar fields relate to one another, and nothing about the last five chapters asks you to give them up. What changes is not the mathematics but the interpretation of which side of the equation gets to be the starting point. Instead of treating **E** and **B** as the primary ingredients of electromagnetism, with the potentials introduced afterward as a convenient shortcut, this book has been treating the potentials as the primary objects and **E** and **B** as the gauge-invariant, locally measurable quantities you build from them.

This reordering pays off the moment you notice how naturally it lines up with the rest of physics. Quantum mechanics is most naturally expressed in terms of phase and potentials — that has been the entire argument since Chapter 11. Classical electromagnetism is most naturally expressed in terms of fields — that is how the subject is normally taught, and for good reason, since fields are what you measure with a voltmeter or a compass needle. The potential is what lets these two natural languages talk to each other without either one pretending to be a mere approximation of the other.

The bridge becomes especially elegant once relativity enters the picture properly. Rather than treating the scalar potential and the three components of the vector potential as four separate, unrelated functions, they combine into a single relativistic object, the four-potential. The electric and magnetic fields can then be packaged together into one corresponding object, the electromagnetic field tensor — and this is where a fact usually taught as a curiosity turns out to be structurally central. Electricity and magnetism turn out to be two aspects of one electromagnetic structure rather than two fundamentally separate forces that happen to share a name, and which aspect you see depends on how you, the observer, happen to be moving.

A magnetic field measured in one reference frame can appear partly as an electric field in another frame moving relative to the first. The split between "electric" and "magnetic" phenomena is, in this precise sense, partly a fact about the observer rather than a fact about the field alone. The four-potential is what makes that unity visible without any extra work.

A single moving charge makes the point concretely. Sit at rest next to an electron, and you measure a purely electric field radiating outward from it in every direction, with no magnetic field anywhere to speak of — nothing is moving relative to you, so there is nothing for a magnetic field to be "made of" in the usual textbook sense. Now let a second observer run past that same electron at high speed. From that observer's point of view, the electron itself is moving, which means it constitutes a current, and a moving charge produces a magnetic field.

Both observers are correctly describing the very same electron, at the very same instant, using the very same laws of physics — they simply disagree about how much of the electron's influence to call "electric" and how much to call "magnetic." What neither observer can disagree about is the field tensor built from the four-potential, which every observer, moving at whatever speed, correctly reconstructs into the identical electric and magnetic split once their own velocity is accounted for. The split is real and useful, but only feels fundamental from inside a single, stationary laboratory.

Quantum mechanics then adds the layer that has been this book's whole argument so far: the four-potential couples directly to charged quantum matter, entering the phase of every amplitude a charged particle carries. Classical fields are what falls out of that coupling once you stop tracking individual phases and look only at the aggregate. That gives us a single thread running through everything covered in Parts One through Three — quantum phase, connected to the four-potential, connected to the electromagnetic field — and the rest of this book is, in large part, an extended investigation of how far that thread can be followed before it frays.
