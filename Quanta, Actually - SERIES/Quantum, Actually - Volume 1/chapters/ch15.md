## 15. The Photon and the Path

### The Photon

"Photon" is one of those words that feels perfectly clear right up until someone asks you what it actually means. Call it a particle of light, and you've said something true. You have not said very much. A photon is, more precisely, a quantum excitation of the electromagnetic field — which is a more accurate sentence, and also one that immediately raises a new question: excitation of *what*, exactly? What is the electromagnetic field, such that it has excitations?

The electromagnetic field, in the quantum theory, is not a classical electric field with a small quantum packet riding on top of it like a cork bobbing on water. Quantum field theory changes the meaning of the word "field" itself. A quantum field is an object whose disturbances (its excitations) correspond to particles. The electron field has electron and positron excitations. The electromagnetic field has photon excitations. This single move buys an enormous amount of explanatory power: it lets the theory describe processes in which particles are created and destroyed.

An electron can emit a photon that did not exist a moment before. A photon can be absorbed and simply cease to exist. At high enough energies, matter can convert into other forms of matter entirely. The particle picture and the field picture read like two competing stories here; they are really two views of one quantum theory.

This is a good place to be honest about a temptation this book has been resisting since Chapter 11, and needs to keep resisting. It would be convenient to say that everything is really collective phase underneath, and that photons are just a convenient bookkeeping device for talking about phase changes — that once you've absorbed the argument of the last eight chapters, you no longer strictly need photons at all. That would be a mistake, and not a subtle one. Real photons are experimentally real. They carry measurable energy and momentum.

They travel through the vacuum of space for billions of years without decaying. They eject electrons from a metal surface on contact, one at a time, in the way a particle would — the very fact Einstein used, in 1905, to argue that light itself must be quantized in the first place.^3

Shine dim light on a metal plate, and classical wave theory predicts electrons should trickle out gradually as the wave's energy slowly accumulates, with brighter light simply speeding up the trickle. That is not what happens. Below a sharp threshold frequency, no electrons come out at all, no matter how bright the light; above it, electrons come out immediately, one per absorbed quantum, with an energy that depends on the light's color, not its intensity. Turning the brightness up only makes more electrons come out, not more energetic ones.

That pattern makes no sense for a continuous wave delivering its energy smoothly. It makes complete sense if light arrives in discrete packets, each carrying a fixed energy set by its frequency, and an electron either absorbs one whole packet or none at all. A single photon of visible light carries only around two electron-volts — an almost comically small amount of energy, and yet exactly enough, delivered all at once instead of trickled in, to knock a particular electron loose from a particular atom.

A photovoltaic solar cell runs on the same one-photon-one-electron logic, though the threshold there is a semiconductor's bandgap rather than a metal's work function: each photon above that energy frees one electron into the circuit. For light of a single color, that's also why a panel's output current climbs in step with how many photons arrive each second — which, for a fixed color, is what turning up the intensity means in the first place. Quantum electrodynamics describes all of this, and a great deal more, with a precision that borders on the absurd — predictions checked against experiment to better than one part in a billion.

This book's phase-centered reframing isn't trying to talk that precision away; it rests on top of it.

What the reframing does buy us is a sharper distinction, and it takes some care to state correctly. When two charged particles interact in a Feynman-diagram calculation, the diagram typically shows a wavy line connecting them, conventionally drawn as a photon being exchanged. It is tempting to read this literally: electron A emits a tiny photon, the photon flies across the gap, electron B catches it. As a rough mnemonic, that picture is harmless. Taken literally, it becomes actively misleading. The internal photon line in a diagram like this represents a mathematical contribution to a quantum amplitude (a term in a calculation), not an independently detectable particle in flight.

A *real* photon, the kind that can arrive at a detector, satisfies a strict relationship between its energy and momentum. An internal, *virtual* photon in a diagram is under no such obligation; it is an intermediate piece of bookkeeping inside a perturbative calculation, not a mini-projectile crossing the space between two electrons. Feynman diagrams are not, in this sense, tiny movies of what physically happened. They are diagrams of the terms in a sum, an extremely useful organizing scheme for an amplitude that is, underneath, the kind of object Chapter 11 introduced: a number with a size and a phase.

This is, if anything, a point in favor of the phase-centered picture rather than against it. The fundamental object being calculated is still an amplitude. The diagram is a bookkeeping device for organizing the contributions to that amplitude (a superb one, arguably the most successful bookkeeping device in the history of physics), but it is not handing you a photograph of a mechanism.

The *real* photon, the one that actually arrives, loses nothing in any of this. And there is an elegant bridge between the microscopic photon and the classical electromagnetic wave that this book has not yet earned the right to describe carefully, but can preview: a classical-looking beam of light (a radio wave, a laser beam) isn't so much a rival description competing with the photon picture as what an enormous, highly coherent collection of photons looks like from far enough away that counting them individually stops being useful.

The wave did not replace the photon. The photon did not replace the wave. One is the large-scale coherent limit of the other, in much the sense that a classical trajectory turned out, in Chapter 11, to be the large-scale coherent limit of a sum over quantum histories.

That word — *coherent* — keeps reappearing, and the rest of this chapter and the next lean on it hard.

:::qa QUESTION AND ANSWER
Q: Is a photon a tiny ball of light?
A: Not quite. A photon is a quantum excitation of the electromagnetic field: the smallest step by which that field's state can change at a given frequency. "Particle of light" is true as far as it goes. It explains why light is emitted and absorbed in whole lumps, but a little ball cannot explain why light also interferes.
Q: If phase does so much of the work, are photons just bookkeeping?
A: No, and the mistake would not be a subtle one. Real photons carry measurable energy and momentum and are counted one at a time by detectors. Collective phase and photons describe different situations, and the rest of this book needs both.
:::

### The Path Is Not a Track

Suppose an electron travels from point A to point B. The classical instinct is to ask which path it took, as though the answer were a single line you could, in principle, trace with a pencil. Feynman's path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics^4 asks a different question entirely: what amplitude is associated with *each* of the infinitely many possible paths the electron could have taken between A and B?

Every conceivable route — the straight line, the gentle curve, the path that loops backward on itself, the path that visits the Moon and comes back — contributes an amplitude to the total. Each amplitude has a phase, set by the *action* accumulated along that particular history: a quantity — roughly, the running difference between kinetic and potential energy, tallied up moment by moment along the path — that measures something like the total "cost," in a technical sense, of that specific trajectory. Add every contribution together, weighting each by its phase, and you get the full quantum amplitude for the electron to go from A to B.

The observable outcome depends on that combined sum, not on any single path considered in isolation.

Feynman arrived at this picture partly by taking a much older idea more seriously than anyone before him had. Paul Dirac had noted, in passing, that a quantum amplitude ought to be related to the classical action by something like the factor *exp(iS/ħ)* (the same expression Chapter 22 will meet again in a more formal setting, sitting directly inside the machinery of QED) without pushing the observation very far. Feynman, still a graduate student at the time, pushed it all the way: if every possible history really does contribute a phase set by its action, then summing over all of them, rather than singling out one, ought to reproduce the whole of quantum mechanics from scratch.

It did. The Schrödinger equation, the interference patterns, the uncertainty principle — all of it falls out of the sum-over-histories picture as a consequence, not an additional assumption bolted on afterward.

Here is where it gets interesting. Most of the wildly different paths in that infinite sum have wildly different actions, and so wildly different phases, and when you add a huge collection of essentially random phases together, the contributions cancel each other out almost completely — arrows pointing in every direction, canceling in aggregate. But paths that lie close to the single path a classical, Newtonian particle would have taken share almost the *same* action, and therefore almost the same phase, and phases that are nearly equal reinforce each other instead of canceling.

Add the same pair from Chapter 11. Two arrows of length 1, both pointing north: head to tail, the sum has length 2. That is a pair of neighboring paths, the ones whose phases nearly agree. One north and one south: the sum has length 0. Tip the second ten degrees off due south, and the sum is the 0.17 you already found, a wild pair that has not quite canceled. The classical trajectory is the neighborhood where your addition comes out at 2. Everywhere else, it comes out at 0, or at that 0.17.

The result is that the classical trajectory emerges as the trajectory around which quantum contributions pile up and survive, while everything else washes out, rather than being fed into the theory as a separate assumption. A "path," in this picture, was never really a physical track the electron slid along. It was always this: the one neighborhood of histories where the interference stopped canceling and started adding.

Now bring electromagnetism back into the picture, and the payoff of this whole formulation becomes concrete. The electromagnetic contribution to the action for a charged particle's history takes the schematic form *q times the integral of A along the path* — the same loop integral from Chapter 12, except now measured along one open history rather than around a closed circuit. The exact notation matters less than what it says in plain language: the electromagnetic potential contributes directly to the phase built up along a charged particle's history, on exactly the same footing as every other contribution to the action.

Everything from Chapters 11 and 12 clicks together at this point. The Aharonov–Bohm effect is no longer an isolated curiosity requiring its own special explanation — it is what you'd expect the moment you take path integrals seriously. The potential shifts the phase of every possible history. The phase shift changes how those histories interfere with one another. The changed interference changes what you observe at the far end. The path integral is the language in which all three of those steps fit together as one continuous statement instead of three separate facts that happen to be true.

And it reveals something the earlier, more piecemeal version of the argument only gestured at. The electromagnetic potential does more than dictate the force a particle feels at each instant — it reaches into the phase of every possible history that particle might have taken, before any single history has been singled out as "the" one that happened. That is a far deeper role for the potential to be playing than anything a force law, by itself, could ever capture.
