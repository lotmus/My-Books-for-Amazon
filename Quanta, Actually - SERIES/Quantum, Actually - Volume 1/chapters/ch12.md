## 12. The Loop, and Why the Potential Is Not Just Bookkeeping

?> Can a magnetic field an electron never touches still change what the electron does?

### The Loop

A loop is one of the simplest shapes in geometry, and one of the most revealing objects in physics. Take an electron and move it around a closed path until it returns to where it began. Classically, this is a non-event. The electron is back where it started; if its speed matches too, nothing about its state has changed, and there is nothing left to explain.

Quantum mechanics is not so easily satisfied. An electron is described by an amplitude, and an amplitude can return to its starting point with a different phase than it left with — the rotating arrow can complete a lap, or several laps, while its length stays exactly the same the entire time. For an everyday wave, a shift in phase is barely worth mentioning; it just moves where the peaks and troughs happen to sit. For a quantum amplitude, phase is what determines interference, so a phase shift picked up on one route, compared to a different phase shift picked up on another, can change everything downstream.

Now put a magnetic field in the picture. The phase a charged particle accumulates as it moves depends on the vector potential **A** along its path, and going all the way around a closed loop, the accumulated phase shift works out to be proportional to the *loop integral* of **A** — the potential, added up all the way around the circuit, in the same spirit as adding up small pieces of distance to get a total length.

A standard result from vector calculus, known as Stokes' theorem, converts that loop integral of **A** into something more familiar: the magnetic flux passing through the surface the loop encloses, the same flux you'd get by adding up how much magnetic field pokes through that surface at every point. So the phase a charged quantum state picks up on a round trip is tied directly to the magnetic flux enclosed by that trip — not to the magnetic field along the path itself, which is the detail that will matter enormously in the next section.

This is the bridge we have been looking for. The magnetic field has not vanished from the story — it shows up where it should, as the flux through the loop — but we have found a deeper reason it enters the phase of charged matter in the first place: through the potential, integrated around the path.

This is also the mechanism behind the Aharonov–Bohm shift from Figure 2. Split an electron's path in two, let the two halves recombine, and the phase difference between them is proportional to the flux Φ enclosed between the two paths — which is why the interference pattern slides continuously as Φ changes, with nothing more exotic required than a single electron's own wavefunction taking two routes at once. A superconductor, in the next section, adds one further ingredient on top of that same mechanism: millions of electrons sharing one phase instead of one electron carrying its own. That extra ingredient is what turns a continuous shift into discrete steps.

The loop gives us something more. A wave function has to be single-valued — it cannot arrive back at the same physical point disagreeing with itself. So the phase picked up on a full circuit is allowed to wind by a whole number of complete turns, but it cannot come back with some arbitrary leftover angle stuck on. That requirement is a quantization condition, and for a coherent quantum state of charge *q* traveling around a loop, it forces a combination of the enclosed flux and the loop's own current — the *fluxoid* — to come in discrete steps.

In a sufficiently thick or otherwise well-screened superconducting loop, the current's own contribution becomes negligible, and the fluxoid reduces to the enclosed flux itself, which is the simple loop picture this book uses from here on. In a superconductor the coherent charge carriers are Cooper pairs, with charge *q* = 2*e*, and that is what produces one of the cleanest quantized quantities in all of physics^2.

A Cooper pair is two electrons bound into one quantum state. In the ordinary low-temperature superconductors this book has in mind, vibrations of the crystal lattice carry a weak attraction. For electrons near the top of the filled levels, that attraction wins against their mutual repulsion. The two electrons have opposite spin and opposite momentum, so the pair has no net spin, charge 2*e*, and can share one phase with every other pair in the material. The microscopic account of that binding is the theory of Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer. This book uses what that theory hands over — the charge 2*e* and the shared phase — and leaves the binding calculation to the superconductivity texts in the Notes on Sources.

> **Equation (1).** The superconducting flux quantum:
> *Φ_0 = h/2e*

That unit is almost absurdly small: the flux quantum works out to roughly two femtowebers — on the order of a billionth of the magnetic flux you'd get from an ordinary refrigerator magnet held near a single square centimeter of space. Nobody trips over flux quantization by accident. It takes a superconductor, cooled far below the temperature where ice forms, before nature's bookkeeping becomes fine enough for a single human being to notice that, in the idealized loop picture used here, the flux through a loop of wire only ever changes by whole multiples of *h*/2*e*, never by a little more or a little less.

The same step does something different to a single electron, and the arithmetic is short enough to check. The phase a charge *q* picks up around a loop enclosing flux Φ is *q*Φ/*ħ*, with *ħ* = *h*/2π. A Cooper pair has *q* = 2*e*, so one flux quantum advances its shared phase by a full turn, 2π, which is why the trapped flux changes in steps of Φ₀. A single electron has *q* = *e*, so that same flux quantum advances its phase by π. The Aharonov–Bohm pattern for electrons therefore repeats every *h*/*e*, twice Φ₀. One quantum, two experiments, two different periods. The back-matter section Symbols, Numbers, and Distinctions keeps this conversion with the other letters.

Notice what we did *not* do to get there. We did not start by declaring that magnetic flux must come in discrete lumps and then go looking for a reason. We started from something much more basic: a quantum state has to be self-consistent when you follow it around a closed path. And the quantization fell out as close to inevitable. This is a pattern that will recur throughout this book: a rule that looks arbitrary, even magical, in one description turns out to be nearly forced in another.

The equations have not changed. Our reasons for believing them have. Keep an eye on this particular loop, too — the same closed circuit, the same consistency requirement, will reappear from several unrelated directions before this book is through, and each time, ask why the loop, of all shapes, keeps being the one that matters.

### Not Just Bookkeeping

:::qa QUESTION AND ANSWER
Q: Isn't the vector potential just a convenience? Couldn't all of this be done with **B** alone?
A: For classical electromagnetism, often yes: there is real freedom in how you organize the mathematics. Quantum mechanics removes that option. In the Aharonov–Bohm effect from the previous section, the electrons travel where the magnetic field is essentially zero, yet their interference pattern still shifts with the flux hidden inside the loop. A description built only from **B** where the electron actually is cannot produce that shift. The potential can.
Q: But **A** can be changed without changing anything you can measure. Doesn't that make it fictional?
A: It makes it a description with freedom in it, not a fiction. Latitude and longitude depend on where you put the origin, and every number changes if you move it, but geography is not made up. Potentials carry the same kind of freedom, called gauge freedom. What an experiment registers is always a gauge-invariant combination: a quantity that stays the same whichever equivalent description you chose.
Q: Which quantities are those?
A: Flux through a loop, an interference pattern, a measured energy, and the accumulated phase around a closed loop, the one behind flux quantization. A gauge transformation can shift the potential freely. It never shifts these.
:::

That freedom has a specific form, called a gauge transformation. Pick any smooth function of space and time, call it *χ*, and shift the potentials by

**A** → **A** + ∇*χ*,

*φ* → *φ* − ∂*χ*/∂*t*.

Chapter 11 already set **B** = ∇×**A** and **E** = −∇*φ* − ∂**A**/∂*t*. Put the shifted potentials into those two definitions and the pieces that contain *χ* cancel, so the fields come out untouched. Different potentials, same physics. What an experiment can actually register is a *gauge-invariant* combination: a quantity that stays fixed no matter which of these equivalent descriptions you picked. The accumulated phase around a closed loop, the one that produced flux quantization, is such a quantity.

The accumulated phase around the closed loop from the previous section also survives this whole exercise untouched: run the gauge transformation on **A**, and the loop integral of ∇*χ* around any closed path comes to exactly zero, since a gradient always integrates to the difference between its values at the start and end of a path, and a closed loop starts and ends at the same point. That's a preview of the general rule, not a coincidence: gauge-dependent quantities can shift freely under a gauge transformation, but the specific combinations that show up in an experiment — flux through a loop, an interference pattern, a measured energy — never do.

Once this settles, an economical way of thinking about electromagnetism comes into view: the electromagnetic potential is the connection that tells a charged quantum state how to compare its phase from one point to the next as it moves — a gauge-covariant relationship between neighboring points, not a phase sitting at one point that the potential simply pushes around. Electric and magnetic fields are what you get when you extract the gauge-invariant, locally measurable content out of that deeper relationship. They remain completely indispensable for calculating forces, energies, and the behavior of ordinary circuits.

But they no longer have to be where the story starts. The potential can be the starting point, and the fields the useful things you build from it — a reversal of the usual teaching order, and one of the more useful moves available to us.

Push that reversal one step further, into a word this book has been using constantly without pausing to define: *interaction* itself. Physicists rarely stop to ask what the word actually means. An electron interacts with another electron. An electron interacts with a photon. Matter interacts with the electromagnetic field. In classical mechanics the meaning seems obvious: a force acts, the force changes momentum, the trajectory bends. That picture is completely adequate for a huge range of everyday physics.

Quantum mechanics offers a different vocabulary for the same word. An interaction changes amplitudes. It changes phases. It reshapes the probabilities attached to the different ways a story can end. In quantum electrodynamics, the electromagnetic interaction is expressed as a direct coupling between charged matter and the electromagnetic potential — with the scalar potential *φ* and the vector potential **A** packaged together into a single relativistic object, the four-potential, that couples to the flow of charge and current. The packaging is not a notational convenience chosen for tidiness.

Special relativity mixes space and time together depending on how fast an observer is moving, and it mixes *φ* and **A** together the same way — a purely electric-looking potential in one observer's frame can pick up a vector-potential piece in another's, the way a purely spatial separation between two events can pick up a time-separation piece once a second observer starts moving. Bundling the two into one four-component object is simply refusing to let the mathematics pretend that space and time, or *φ* and **A**, were ever really separate in the first place.

The notation, past that point, is secondary. What matters is that the potential sits directly inside the quantum dynamics of charged matter, not off to the side as a tool for calculating forces after the fact.

That gives us a shared language for two situations that otherwise look nothing alike. On one side is the microscopic world of quantum electrodynamics: electrons, positrons, photons, amplitudes, interference, all handled relativistically. On the other is a coherent quantum system built from an enormous number of charged particles acting together. The mathematics of the two situations looks very different up close. But the phase is present in both, doing the same conceptual job in each — and when a huge number of particles become coherent, the phase stops being a private property that belongs to one electron's amplitude and starts being something else entirely: a *collective* physical variable, shared across the whole system.

This is where superconductivity enters the story in earnest. A superconductor is more than a material that happens to have zero electrical resistance, as though resistance had been dialed down to nothing by some clever trick. Underneath, it is a quantum system in which a vast number of microscopic degrees of freedom share a single coherent phase — a phase that is no longer buried three or four steps down inside a wavefunction nobody can see, but is instead a variable you can manipulate with a circuit, measure with a voltmeter, and quantize in units of *h*/2*e*, as we already saw earlier in this chapter.

The quantum world, in other words, has not been left behind once we start talking about wires and circuits. It has gotten enormous. And once quantum phase becomes something you can hold in your hand, electromagnetism starts to look like a very different subject than the one usually taught first.
