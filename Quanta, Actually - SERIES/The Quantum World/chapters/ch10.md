## 10. A Step Up: One Road Through the Map

?> Part One drew the map. What does it take to walk one road through it, from an electron's phase all the way to a circuit you can build?

### Why the Pace Changes Here

Part One was a survey. It moved quickly from the double slit to entanglement, from quantum fields to quarks, and from QED to cryptography and computing, and it kept the mathematics in the background. Chapter 7 told the story of light and matter the way Feynman liked to tell it: little arrows, little diagrams, and the most precise agreement between theory and experiment anywhere in science.

It also left a question unanswered. What, exactly, is the electromagnetic field? Is it a thing that fills space, or a convenient way of describing how charges affect one another? Part Two takes that one question and follows it all the way down, and then all the way back up to a wire on a laboratory bench.

That makes this a step up. The chapters that follow stay longer on a single idea, a few equations appear, and the argument builds, so a later chapter leans on an earlier one. Nothing in them needs more than the small toolkit below. If a page gets dense, the toolkit is the place to come back to.

### A Small Toolkit

**The arrow is a number.** Chapter 7 drew each possible history as a little stopwatch arrow. Physicists write the same arrow as a complex number,

> *ψ = Ae^iθ*.

*A* is the arrow's length, the amplitude. *θ* is the direction it points, the phase. Square the length of the final arrow and you get a probability. Turn every arrow by the same angle and nothing you can observe changes. What shows up in an experiment is how the arrows point *relative to one another*, which is why phase differences, not phases on their own, produce interference.

**Height and slope.** Picture a hilly landscape. The height at each point is a single number: that is a potential. The slope tells a rolling ball which way to go and how hard: that is a field. You can measure every height from sea level or from the valley floor, and the slopes do not change. Electricity works the same way. The electric potential *φ* is the height, and the electric field is the downhill slope, written **E** = −∇*φ*. The symbol ∇ ("nabla") is shorthand for "how fast, and in which direction, this changes from place to place."

**A potential with a direction.** Magnetism has a potential too, but it is a small arrow at every point rather than a single number. It is called the vector potential, **A**. The magnetic field measures how much **A** circulates around a tiny loop, written **B** = ∇×**A** ("curl"). The freedom to choose where sea level sits has a name, gauge freedom. Chapter 12 shows why quantum mechanics cares about **A** anyway.

**Two constants.** *ħ* ("h-bar") is Planck's constant *h* divided by 2π, Planck's constant measured in angles instead of full turns. The combination *h*/2*e*, about 2.07 × 10⁻¹⁵ webers, is the smallest amount of magnetic flux a superconducting loop can trap. It turns up again and again.

**The sentence before the formula.** Every equation in Part Two comes after a sentence that says what it means. If the symbols are new, hold on to the sentence. It carries the argument; the symbols only make it exact.

### A Different Way of Thinking

There is a peculiar moment that arrives when you have learned enough physics to become confused by it.

I remember roughly where I was sitting the first time this stopped feeling like a formality and started feeling like an actual problem: partway through a forty-year career spent around components that obey Maxwell's equations without ever once complaining, and it occurred to me, for the first time since university, that I could not actually say what a field *was*. Not vaguely — precisely. The question turned out to have a real answer. Part Two is the long way of giving it to you.

At first, everything seems beautifully straightforward. Objects have positions and velocities. Forces push and pull them. Gravity makes apples fall. Electricity pushes charges around. Magnetism bends moving charges. Light is an electromagnetic wave. Maxwell's equations tell us how the electric and magnetic fields behave, and everything seems to fit together with admirable efficiency.

Then quantum mechanics arrives, and after it quantum electrodynamics. Part One has already walked that road: an electron described by a wave that interferes with itself, light arriving in discrete quanta, particles emitting and absorbing photons, empty space that is no longer quite empty, and predictions that agree with experiment to better than one part in a billion.

At this point, you might reasonably expect the basic picture to have become clearer. Instead, it becomes stranger. What, exactly, is an electromagnetic field? Is it a physical thing filling space, or merely a convenient way of describing relationships between charges? What is a photon? Does it travel from one electron to another like a microscopic bullet? What is the electromagnetic potential, and why does quantum mechanics seem to care about it so much more than classical physics ever did? And perhaps the most uncomfortable question of all: what is actually fundamental here?

I am not going to hand you that answer here. The questions above are Part Two. The Lorentz force still predicts the motion of every charged object in the laboratory, and nothing in these pages retires it.

These aren't philosophical decorations hung around the equations after the fact — they shape how the equations themselves get understood.

Richard Feynman spent much of his career searching for ways of thinking about physics that made the structure of correct answers intelligible, not just the answers themselves. Carver Mead, shaped by years of conversation with Feynman at Caltech, pursued an unusually radical version of that same project within electrodynamics specifically. Rather than beginning with Maxwell's equations and quantizing the electromagnetic field afterward, Mead asked whether one could start much closer to the quantum phase of matter itself, and reconstruct electromagnetism outward from there.

The two approaches are not identical, and I won't pretend otherwise. They cannot simply be pasted together and declared one theory. Feynman's quantum electrodynamics is a relativistic quantum field theory, describing photons, charged particles, radiation, quantum corrections, and processes in which particles are created and destroyed — one of the most successful theories in the history of science, full stop. Mead's approach is a different kind of achievement, placing quantum phase, electromagnetic potentials, and coherent collective behavior at the center of the story, especially in systems like superconductors, where that phase becomes something you can measure with an ordinary instrument.

The path runs, roughly, like this. Begin with waves, and discover phase hiding inside them. Follow phase to the electromagnetic potential. Follow the potential to the interaction between charges, and from there to photons, to collective quantum matter, and eventually back out to classical electromagnetism — arriving, by the end, at quantum electrodynamics again, but carrying a very different intuition than the one Part Two started with.

The goal was to find a way of thinking in which several genuinely different descriptions of the same physical world begin to look like different windows onto one structure, rather than rival claims about what is really there.

### What's Mead, What's Feynman, What's New Here

A book synthesizing two very different thinkers owes its reader a clear account of where each one's actual contribution starts and stops, stated plainly before the synthesis itself gets underway rather than left to be inferred two hundred pages later.

I owe this book's relativistic backbone to Feynman. Quantum electrodynamics — the full machinery of quantized fields, Feynman diagrams, renormalization, radiative corrections calculated to a precision better than one part in a billion — is entirely his and his collaborators' achievement, decades of established, exhaustively tested physics that I explain rather than originate. His sum-over-histories picture, phase attached to every possible path rather than one chosen trajectory, is the conceptual engine I run on from Chapter 11 onward. And Feynman's own instinct, that an appealing explanation is worthless until the mathematics has been pushed hard enough to check it, is a habit of mind I've tried to imitate throughout.

I owe this book's starting point and its central nerve to Mead. The decision to treat quantum phase and the electromagnetic potential as primary, with the classical field emerging downstream rather than standing at the beginning, is his — laid out at length in *Collective Electrodynamics*, the book this one grew out of in the most literal sense. So is the insistence that a coherent quantum system, a superconductor above all, is where that phase-centered picture becomes something you can build a circuit around rather than argue about.

Where I push further than Mead's own text is mostly in scope: connecting his collective-phase argument explicitly, chapter by chapter, to the full relativistic QED machinery he treats mostly as background rather than as the destination.

Gauge theory, Noether's theorem, the geometry of connections and curvature — none of that belongs to either Feynman or Mead individually. It is the shared mathematical language of modern physics, older than both of their specific contributions, and I'm explaining that shared structure, not claiming to have discovered it.

What belongs specifically to me, then, is narrower than any of the physics I rely on: the decision to run the whole story in reverse teaching order — phase, then the potential, then the fields and the force — the extended argument that the same combination of phase gradient and potential shows up on both ends of that reversed story, and the sustained attempt to hold Feynman's machinery and Mead's intuition in the same frame without quietly pretending they were the same theory all along. Maxwell's equations are not the casualty of telling it backward.

They remain among the most beautiful results in the history of science, and arriving at them this way is meant to show where they sit. That's a conceptual synthesis, built entirely from established pieces, not a new discovery standing beside theirs — and the chapters ahead try never to let a sentence outrun that distinction.

Part One met Mead's idea only in passing, as a minority research program that Chapter 7 pointed toward. Here it gets the full hearing it asked for.

Part One gave you the essentials: electric and magnetic fields, and an electron described by a wave that interferes. The toolkit above supplies the rest. Part Two does not teach calculus, Maxwell's equations from nothing, or quantum field theory; the full calculation, from the Lagrangian to the loop corrections, is the job of the *Complete Quantum Electrodynamics Course* (Volume 2). It does stop to say what a phase, a potential, and a gauge transformation are doing.

There are three sensible ways to use what follows. Read it straight through: that is the argument in the order it was built. If fields and interference are already familiar and the destination you care about is the circuit, the spine is Chapters 11 through 14, then 22 and 24, then 28, then 31. The chapters between those are where the fields return, where the absorber question is kept in its place, and where QED's precision is earned, so that route is a tour of the spine.

If a formula stops you, "The Equations, Decoded" at the back of the book has the equation, "Symbols, Numbers, and Distinctions" has the letter and the unit convention, and the Glossary has the word.
