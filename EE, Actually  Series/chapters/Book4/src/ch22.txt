H1: Chapter 22. Amplifier Classes and Efficiency
P: Shrink a transistor's conduction from half the cycle to a third and its efficiency rises from 78.5 % to 89.7 %. The same transistor now delivers 39 % less power. Every amplifier class is a trade between how much of the supply becomes signal and how much signal the device can deliver, and the class letter tells you where on that trade you stand.
P: Where you meet this: Amplifier data sheets and application notes, the bias network on every power stage, and the efficiency number in every base-station or handset specification.
P: Chapter 21 fixed the load. This chapter fixes the bias, and with it the fraction of each RF cycle during which the transistor conducts. That choice, named by the amplifier's class, decides the efficiency: how much of the DC supply power becomes RF and how much becomes heat. In a base station that heat is the operator's largest electricity bill after air conditioning; in a phone it is battery life. The classes fall into two families: the current-source classes A, AB, B, and C, which trade linearity for efficiency by conducting for less of the cycle, and the switching and harmonic-tuned classes D, E, and F, which shape the voltage and current waveforms so that they do not overlap.
H2: 22.1 Efficiency measures
P: Drain efficiency is the RF output power divided by the DC supply power. Power-added efficiency subtracts the input drive, which matters when the gain is low, as it is at millimeter wave. The power dissipated in the device is the DC input plus the RF input minus the RF output, and it is the number the heat sink of Chapter 24 must handle.
EQ: ηD = Pout / PDC,    PAE = (Pout − Pin) / PDC,    Pdiss = PDC + Pin − Pout
H2: Worked Example 22.1
P: Problem. A 100 W amplifier has 15 dB of gain and 65% drain efficiency. Find the input power, DC power, PAE, and dissipation.
P: Solution. The gain is a factor of 31.6, so Pin = 100/31.6 = 3.16 W. PDC = 100/0.65 = 153.8 W. PAE = (100 − 3.16)/153.8 = 62.9%. The dissipation is 153.8 + 3.16 − 100 = 57.0 W. At 12 dB of gain and 70% drain efficiency, a 40 W millimeter-wave stage would have PAE = (40 − 2.52)/57.1 = 65.6%, already four and a half points below its drain efficiency; at lower gains the gap grows quickly.
H2: 22.2 Conduction angle: classes A, AB, B, and C
P: In class A the transistor is biased at half its maximum current and conducts for the whole cycle. Its peak theoretical efficiency is 50%, and it is the most linear. Lowering the bias reduces the conduction angle, the part of the cycle over which current flows. In class B, biased at the threshold, the device conducts for half the cycle, 180°, and the theoretical peak efficiency is π/4 = 78.5%. Class AB lies between. In class C the angle is less than 180° and the efficiency rises further, but the fundamental output falls and the gain falls with it. For a conduction angle α, with an ideal linear device and a harmonic short at the output, the peak efficiency is given below.
EQ: η(α) = (α − sin α) / [4 (sin(α/2) − (α/2) cos(α/2))]

P: The formula gives 50.0% for class A at 360°, 60.2% at 270°, 78.5% for class B at 180°, 89.7% at 120°, and 94.0% at 90°. The fundamental output power, relative to class A with the same peak current and voltage, is +0.55 dB at 270°, 0 dB at 180°, −2.1 dB at 120°, and −4.2 dB at 90°. Deep class C therefore buys efficiency with power and gain, and is used for constant-envelope signals such as FM broadcast, where linearity does not matter. Most linear amplifiers run in class AB, a little above the threshold, to keep the gain flat at low drive.
H2: Worked Example 22.2
P: Problem. A device biased for a 120° conduction angle delivers its maximum output with a drain efficiency at the theoretical limit. If the same device in class B delivers 100 W, what does it deliver in this class C condition, and how much dissipation is saved?
P: Solution. At 120° the output is 2.14 dB below class B, so 100 × 10^(−0.214) = 61.1 W. In class B the DC power is 100/0.785 = 127.3 W and the dissipation 27.3 W. At 120°, the DC power is 61.1/0.897 = 68.1 W and the dissipation 7.0 W. Efficiency rose from 78.5% to 89.7%, but the device now delivers 39% less power: class C is a trade, not a free gain.
P: Key idea. Efficiency comes from keeping voltage and current from being large at the same time. Reducing the conduction angle does it at a cost in power and gain; shaping the waveforms with harmonics or switching does it without that cost, in principle reaching 100%.
H2: 22.3 Switching and harmonic-tuned classes
P: If the transistor acts as a switch, it has either zero voltage across it or zero current through it, and an ideal switch dissipates nothing. Class D uses two switches and a filter; it is common at audio and low radio frequencies. Class E uses one switch with a shunt capacitor and a tuned load designed so that the voltage returns to zero, with zero slope, at the instant the switch closes; this removes the loss of discharging the capacitor and makes class E practical into the gigahertz range. Class F adds open circuits at the odd harmonics and short circuits at the even ones, so the drain voltage approaches a square wave and the current a half-sine; inverse class F swaps them. Real devices have finite on-resistance, output capacitance, and transition times, and practical class E and F amplifiers reach 70 to 85% drain efficiency at a few gigahertz. All of these are efficient only at full drive. Chapter 23 addresses what happens when the signal's envelope varies.
H2: 22.4 Device technologies at the load line
P: The classes above describe what the transistor does in each cycle; the technology decides how far it can swing while doing it. Every power amplifier in this book starts from the same load line, Ropt = (V − Vknee)²/(2P), whatever the device, so the technologies differ in the numbers that go into it: the supply the device survives, the current per millimeter of gate or square micrometer of emitter, the frequency at which it still has gain, and the heat it can shed. CMOS comes first here, because it is the technology of nearly every integrated radio, and the other families are described by what changes. Why each material behaves as it does is Book 3's subject, in its chapter "Materials for Speed, Power, and Heat"; Part III of this book designs with each of them.
P: The comparison is given as rows, one per technology, each in the same order: breakdown and supply, power density, speed, efficiency and linearity, cost and integration, and typical uses. The figures are typical of commercial processes in 2026 and vary by a factor of two or more between processes.
P: Bulk CMOS and RF SOI: thin-oxide core devices tolerate about 1 V and thick-oxide input-output devices 1.8 to 3.3 V, so a battery-powered stage needs cascodes or stacks, Chapter 42; current density is set by gate width, and power is limited by voltage rather than current; fT and fmax reach a few hundred gigahertz in advanced nodes; efficiency is moderate, 30 to 50 % for linear Wi-Fi stages at average power; the PA shares a die with the transceiver and the processor, which is the decisive advantage; Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, IoT radios such as the ESP32, and integrated millimeter-wave beamformers.
P: SiGe HBT: a fast silicon-germanium bipolar device in a BiCMOS process; high-speed devices with fT near 300 GHz and fmax near 500 GHz have a BVCEO of only about 1.5 V, while slower power variants tolerate several volts; very high gain per stage at millimeter waves; automotive radar at 77 GHz, optical transceivers, and millimeter-wave front ends, Chapter 46.
P: GaAs HBT (InGaP emitter): a collector breakdown BVCEO of about 15 to 20 V lets it run from a 3.4 to 4.5 V battery with wide margin for mismatch; fT of a few tens of gigahertz; good linearity and efficiency at a few watts; a separate die in a front-end module; the cellular handset power amplifier, Chapter 44.
P: GaAs pHEMT: a high-electron-mobility FET with very low noise, about 0.5 dB at 2 GHz in a packaged part, and low switch loss; power density near 1 W/mm; LNAs, switches, and microwave MMICs, Chapters 28 and 48.
P: GaN HEMT on silicon carbide or silicon: supplies of 28 to 50 V, power densities of about 4 to 6 W/mm at 28 V and up to about 10 W/mm at 50 V in commercial processes, and channel temperatures rated to about 225 °C; efficient and broadband at the high impedance its voltage gives; depletion-mode, so it needs a negative gate and a bias sequence; base stations, radar, satellite amplifiers, and broadband test amplifiers, Chapter 45.
P: LDMOS: a laterally diffused silicon MOSFET for 28 to 50 V supplies, rugged and cheap, with useful gain to about 3 to 4 GHz; broadcast, industrial heating, and older base stations, Chapter 20.
EQ: Ropt = (V − Vknee)² / (2P),    Ropt,2 / Ropt,1 = [(V₂ − Vk₂) / (V₁ − Vk₁)]²  at equal power
H2: Worked Example 22.3
P: Problem. Find Ropt for a 1 W stage in four technologies: a CMOS cascode on 3.3 V with a 0.5 V knee, a GaAs HBT on 3.4 V with a 0.3 V knee, and GaN on 28 V with a 3 V knee and on 48 V with a 4 V knee. Find the single-section L-network Q to 50 Ω for each, and the ratio of the GaN and CMOS loads.
P: Solution. CMOS: Ropt = 2.8²/2 = 3.92 Ω, a ratio of 12.8 to 50 Ω and Q = √(12.8 − 1) = 3.43. GaAs HBT: Ropt = 3.1²/2 = 4.81 Ω and Q = 3.07. GaN at 28 V: Ropt = 25²/2 = 312.5 Ω, above 50 Ω, so the network transforms up, with Q = √(312.5/50 − 1) = 2.29. GaN at 48 V: Ropt = 44²/2 = 968 Ω and Q = 4.28. At equal power the GaN load at 28 V is 312.5/3.92 = 80 times the CMOS load, the square of the ratio of the voltage swings; a figure of 10 sometimes quoted would compare supplies only √10 = 3.2 times apart. A 1 W GaN device is therefore an unusual part: GaN's voltage pays off at tens to hundreds of watts, where its load comes back down near 50 Ω, while a 1 W handset stage on a battery needs a large step-down ratio.

P: Bipolar version. Where the rest of this book designs a CMOS or FET stage, a bipolar stage changes in five ways, and each design in Part III closes with a short note on them.
LI: Drive and bias: an HBT is driven by base current, with collector current exponential in VBE; a FET is driven by gate voltage with a roughly square-law, and in short channels nearly linear, transfer. An HBT needs a current mirror or ballasted bias, a FET a gate voltage.
LI: Breakdown and swing: SiGe and GaAs HBTs tolerate more collector swing than thin-oxide CMOS, so they need less stacking; BVCBO, with the base driven from a low impedance, is the limit that matters under drive, and it is well above BVCEO.
LI: Knee: VCE,sat of 0.2 to 0.3 V is comparable to or below a FET's knee, and the load-line formula is the same.
LI: Linearity: the exponential base-emitter junction gives different AM-AM and AM-PM curves, and HBT stages often need less predistortion at moderate back-off.
LI: Heat: an HBT's VBE falls about 1 to 2 mV per kelvin at constant current, so hot fingers hog current; multi-finger HBTs need emitter or base ballast, Chapter 44, while FETs share current more evenly because their mobility falls with temperature.
P: Key idea. One load line serves every technology; what changes is the voltage it may swing. At equal power the optimum load scales with the square of that swing, which is why a battery-powered stage must transform down by a large ratio and a 48 V GaN stage meets 50 Ω only at hundreds of watts.
BENCH: Measure efficiency the way your customer will: at the average power of the real signal, not at saturation with a single tone. Give a class AB stage's bias temperature compensation, because the quiescent current that's right at 25 °C can double on a hot heat sink. Check the bias network for low-frequency resonances too. A large bypass capacitor and a long bias line can resonate, and under modulation the resonance shows up as sidebands.
