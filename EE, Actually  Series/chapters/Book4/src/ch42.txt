PART: Part III. Device Technologies and Power-Amplifier Design
H1: Chapter 42. CMOS Power Amplifiers: Beating the Breakdown Limit
P: A thick-oxide transistor in a CMOS process survives a few volts across its drain, yet a Wi-Fi chip in a phone delivers more than half a watt from it. No single transistor in that amplifier ever sees the full swing. The trick is to share the voltage among devices, add the power of several stages on chip, and choose the load by measurement instead of by formula.
P: Where you meet this: Wi-Fi and Bluetooth chips, IoT radios such as the ESP32 family, cellular transceivers with integrated drivers, and millimeter-wave beamformer ICs with an amplifier behind every antenna element.
P: Chapter 21 set the optimum load from the supply, the knee, and the power, and Chapter 22 showed which classes make the most of it. A CMOS designer meets both with a handicap: the transistors that are fast enough are the ones that break first. This chapter collects the techniques that push a CMOS amplifier's output power up, groups them by the limit each one beats, and finishes with a complete 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi amplifier in which every number is declared. Bipolar and compound-semiconductor amplifiers follow in Chapters 43 to 45.
H2: 42.1 What limits the output power
P: Five limits set how much power a CMOS amplifier can deliver. The supply voltage is bounded by breakdown and by reliability: a thin-oxide core device tolerates about 1 V, and even a thick-oxide input-output device only 2 to 3 times its nominal supply at the drain peak before oxide stress and hot carriers shorten its life, Book 3's "What Breaks a Chip: ESD, Latch-up, and Aging". The drain current is bounded by the device width and the current density the metal can carry. The load impedance follows from the first two through Ropt = (V − Vknee)²/(2P), and a low load means a high-ratio, lossy match. Efficiency and heat close the list, because a watt that does not leave as RF must leave through the die.
P: The techniques that beat these limits overlap, and the clearest way to hold them is by the limit each one attacks.
LI: Voltage: cascodes and stacks of devices, so that each sees part of the swing; thick-oxide and extended-drain devices with higher breakdown; and RF SOI, whose buried oxide removes the junction limits of a stack, Chapter 46.
LI: Current and size: wider devices, laid out as many short fingers to keep gate resistance and heat under control, with neutralization of the gate-drain capacitance to keep a large device stable.
LI: Load: load-pull to find the true optimum, and impedance transformation by L, pi, or transformer networks; on-chip transformers match, combine, and convert differential to single-ended at once.
LI: Combining: differential outputs, transformer and Wilkinson combiners, and corporate trees; current reuse, in which stacked stages share one DC current, belongs here too.
LI: Efficiency: the switching and harmonic-tuned classes of Chapter 35, the Doherty of Chapter 23, outphasing and envelope tracking of Chapter 36, and dynamic bias that lowers the quiescent current at low power.
LI: Heat and package: many fingers, copper pillars, thermal vias, and flip-chip mounting move heat out; advanced packages shorten the lossy path to the antenna.
LI: Process and system: RF CMOS, RF SOI, BiCMOS, and SiGe options trade cost for headroom, and multiple amplifiers and antennas add power in space, within the limits that regulators place on the total.
P: Five of these carry most commercial Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and cellular CMOS amplifiers: cascode stacking, differential outputs, transformer power combining, load-pull-optimized matching, and envelope tracking or a dynamic supply. The rest of the chapter follows that spine.
H2: 42.2 Sharing the voltage: cascodes and stacks
P: In a cascode, a common-gate device sits on top of the common-source device and holds the lower drain near its own gate voltage minus a threshold, so the lower device sees only part of the swing. Stacking extends the idea to N devices, with the upper gates driven through capacitors so that each gate follows its own source and every device sees about 1/N of the drain swing. If each device may see a peak of Vmax, the stack's drain may swing to N·Vmax, and with the swing centered on the supply, the supply can rise to about N·Vmax/2. The load line then uses the larger swing, and the optimum load rises with its square.
EQ: Vdrain,peak ≤ N Vmax,    VDD ≈ N Vmax / 2,    Ropt = (VDD − Vknee)² / (2P)
H2: Worked Example 42.1
P: Problem. A process offers core devices whose drain may safely peak at 2.4 V, with a 0.2 V knee for one device. Find the supply and Ropt for a 1 W stage built from one device and from a stack of three, taking the stack's knee as 0.6 V. Compare the single-section Q to 50 Ω.
P: Solution. One device: VDD = 2.4/2 = 1.2 V, Ropt = (1.2 − 0.2)²/2 = 0.50 Ω, a ratio of 100 to 50 Ω and a single-section Q of √99 = 9.9. Three devices: VDD = 3 × 2.4/2 = 3.6 V, Ropt = (3.6 − 0.6)²/2 = 4.5 Ω, a ratio of 11.1 and Q = 3.18. Stacking three devices raised the load nine times and cut the match's Q by a factor of three. A Q near 10 would lose a large fraction of a watt in on-chip inductors with Q of 10 to 20, so the single device is impractical at 1 W, and the stack is what makes a watt possible from a fine-line process.
P: Stacks have their own costs. Each added device adds a knee and parasitic capacitance at its source, which shifts the phase of its swing relative to the others and spoils the equal voltage sharing unless the gate capacitors are tuned for it. In bulk CMOS the source-to-body junctions of the upper devices limit the stack height; RF SOI removes them, which is why switch stacks of ten and more devices are routine there, Chapter 46. Thick-oxide devices and extended-drain, LDMOS-like devices offer the other route to voltage: fewer devices, each with more breakdown, at the cost of speed.
H2: 42.3 Differential outputs and combining on chip
P: A differential pair of amplifiers suppresses even harmonics, rejects supply and ground bounce, and doubles the voltage across a floating load. How much power that buys depends on what is held constant. With the same supply and the same load per side, a differential stage is two amplifiers and gives 3 dB more. With the full differential swing across the same total load, the voltage doubles and the power quadruples, 6 dB, but each device then carries twice the current. Combining several amplifiers adds 10 log N decibels, minus the combiner's loss, which on chip is often 1 dB or more: four amplifiers through a 1 dB combiner give 5.0 dB, not 6.
H2: Worked Example 42.2
P: Problem. A single-ended stage swings 2.8 V peak into 20 Ω. Find its power. Then find the power of a differential pair of the same stages, first with each side driving its own 20 Ω, and then with both driving a single 20 Ω load connected between the drains. Find the peak current per device in each case.
P: Solution. Single-ended: P = 2.8²/(2 × 20) = 196 mW, with a peak current of 2.8/20 = 0.14 A. Two sides, each into 20 Ω: 392 mW, 3.0 dB more, with 0.14 A per device. Both into one 20 Ω load between the drains: the differential swing is 5.6 V, so P = 5.6²/(2 × 20) = 784 mW, 6.0 dB more than one side, but the load current is 5.6/20 = 0.28 A and each device must carry it. The second arrangement is the same as halving the load seen by each device to 10 Ω. The '+6 dB of a differential amplifier' therefore assumes double the current; at the same current per device, the gain is 3 dB.
P: A transformer combines amplifiers and matches them at the same time. In series combining, the secondaries of N transformers are connected in series, so their voltages add; each primary then sees the load divided among N secondaries and transformed by the turns ratio n, RL/(N n²). In parallel combining the currents add instead. Either way, the transformer also converts the differential amplifier output to the single-ended antenna port, and its magnetizing inductance can resonate the devices' output capacitance. Distributed amplifiers, which absorb the device capacitances into artificial lines as in Chapter 11, are used at millimeter waves mainly for bandwidth; the power they add is a secondary benefit.
EQ: series combining: Rprimary = RL / (N n²),    combined power ≈ N P₁ × 10^(−Lcomb/10)
H2: 42.4 Large devices: fingers, gate resistance, and neutralization
P: Output power needs width, and a wide device is laid out as many parallel fingers. Each finger's gate is a resistive strip contacted at one end, with a resistance of about R□Wf/(3L) for a finger of width Wf and length L, the factor 3 coming from the distributed RC line. N fingers in parallel divide it by N, so at a fixed total width W = N Wf, the gate resistance is R□Wf²/(3WL): halving the finger width quarters it. Low gate resistance keeps fmax high and the input loss low, and many short fingers also spread the heat. The cost is the capacitance of the wiring that connects them.
P: A large device's gate-drain capacitance Cgd feeds the output back to the input, lowering the reverse isolation and inviting oscillation, and the Miller effect multiplies it at the input. In a differential stage, a pair of cross-coupled capacitors equal to Cgd, each from one drain to the opposite gate, injects an equal and opposite current and cancels the feedback. Neutralization raises the stable gain of a large stage by several decibels and is standard in CMOS amplifiers from Wi-Fi to millimeter waves.
EQ: Rg ≈ R□ Wf / (3 L N) = R□ Wf² / (3 W L),    Cneutralization = Cgd
H2: 42.5 A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi amplifier, designed
P: The design below uses the five techniques of Section 42.1 to reach 28 dBm of saturated output at the antenna port from a 3.3 V supply, enough for an OFDM average of 20 dBm with 8 dB of back-off. The numbers are idealized: class B current waveforms in each device, a stated knee, and a combiner loss of 1 dB. A real design starts from them and then moves the load by load-pull and simulation.
H2: Worked Example 42.3
P: Problem. Two differential cascode cores of thick-oxide devices run from 3.3 V with a knee of 0.5 V per side and are combined by a series transformer with 1 dB of loss into 50 Ω. Find the power per core, the differential and per-side optimum loads, the turns ratio, the drain efficiency at saturation, and the per-side load to present if each drain has 1.5 pF of output capacitance at 2.45 GHz. Check the top device's peak drain-source voltage against an example reliability limit of 5.0 V, at 1:1 and at a 3:1 VSWR.
P: Solution. 28 dBm after 1 dB means 29 dBm, 794 mW, from the cores, 397 mW (26.0 dBm) each. Each side swings V1 = 3.3 − 0.5 = 2.8 V, so the differential amplitude is 5.6 V and Rdiff = 5.6²/(2 × 0.397) = 39.5 Ω, 19.7 Ω per side. With two secondaries in series on 50 Ω, each primary sees 50/(2n²), so n² = 50/(2 × 39.5) = 0.633 and n = 0.80, a 1:0.8 transformer. Per side the fundamental current is 0.397/2.8 = 0.142 A; in class B the DC current is (2/π) × 0.142 = 0.090 A per side, so a core draws 0.181 A, 0.596 W, and its drain efficiency is 0.397/0.596 = 66.6 %, the class B figure times the knee factor 2.8/3.3. After the combiner the amplifier delivers 631 mW from 1.19 W, 52.9 %. To absorb 1.5 pF, each side must see the load whose admittance plus jωC is 1/19.7 Ω: Z = 1/(1/19.74 − j0.0231) = 16.3 + j7.4 Ω, the starting point for load-pull; equivalently, 2.81 nH of magnetizing inductance per side resonates the capacitance. The drain of the top device peaks at 3.3 + 2.8 = 6.1 V; with its gate held at 3.3 V its source sits near 2.8 V, so it sees about 3.3 V and the lower device at most 2.8 V, inside the limit. At a 3:1 VSWR the swing can grow by 1 + |Γ| = 1.5 to 4.2 V, the drain peaks near 7.5 V, and the top device sees about 4.7 V, under 5.0 V with little margin. The design therefore needs a VSWR detector that lowers the drive or the supply, or a third device in the stack.

P: Bipolar version. Built in SiGe or GaAs HBTs, the same amplifier needs less stacking, because BVCBO of a few volts to well over 10 V covers the swing; the bias moves from gate voltages to ballasted current mirrors with a temperature-tracking reference; each finger gets an emitter or base ballast resistor against current hogging; and the lower knee, 0.2 to 0.3 V, raises the per-side load slightly for the same power. The transformer combiner and the load-pull procedure are unchanged.
H2: 42.6 Efficiency at the average power
P: At the 20 dBm OFDM average the ideal class B efficiency of each core falls to 66.6 % × 10^(−8/20) = 26.5 %, and after the combiner to about 21 %. Three techniques recover part of it. Average-power tracking lowers the supply for the whole transmission when the requested power is low, so the swing again reaches close to the rail. Envelope tracking follows the envelope itself, Chapter 36. Dynamic bias lowers the quiescent current of a class AB stage at low drive. In CMOS a Doherty or outphasing combiner can be built from the same transformers, at the cost of area and bandwidth; at the power levels of Wi-Fi, average-power tracking with a buck converter is the common choice.
H2: 42.7 Reliability and ruggedness
P: A CMOS amplifier fails slowly before it fails suddenly. Hot carriers injected into the gate oxide during the high-voltage, high-current part of each cycle shift the threshold and lower the current over months; time-dependent dielectric breakdown accumulates while the gate-drain voltage is high. Both depend steeply on the peak voltage, so the reliability limit is set on the peak drain-source and gate-drain voltages over the worst load the antenna presents, not on the supply. Designers simulate every phase of the mismatch circle, add peak detectors at the drains, and fold back the drive or the supply when a peak approaches the limit, the protection schemes of Chapter 31.
H2: 42.8 More amplifiers and more antennas
P: A phased array or a MIMO radio adds power in space: N amplifiers, each feeding its own antenna, combine coherently in the beam direction, and each amplifier stays small enough for CMOS. Regulators limit the conducted power and the EIRP, and the array gain counts toward the EIRP, so the combined emission is limited however legal each amplifier is alone. The RF exposure limits of Chapter 33 apply to the combined beam, and certification, Book 7's subject, measures it as such.
P: Key idea. A CMOS amplifier reaches useful power by never asking one transistor for the whole swing: stack the voltage, split the power into differential halves and several cores, add them in a transformer that also matches, set the load by load-pull, and let the supply follow the average power.
BENCH: Simulate the peak drain-source and gate-drain voltage of every device in the stack, not only the top one, at every phase of a 3:1 mismatch and at the highest supply, and keep each below its reliability limit. Measure the combiner's loss on its own, because a transformer that loses 1.5 dB instead of 1.0 takes 12 % of the output. Check stability with the neutralization capacitors at their process corners: a few femtofarads of mismatch is the difference between a neutralized stage and an oscillator.
P: On the Job
P: RFIC PA designers turn power, efficiency, and linearity specifications into transistor sizes, stacks, transformers, and CMOS or SOI layouts. They use circuit and electromagnetic simulators, collaborate with layout engineers, and validate returned silicon with load-pull and ruggedness tests. Useful preparation includes RFIC design, semiconductor devices, electromagnetic simulation, and laboratory measurement. Graduate study can help for research-heavy roles; requirements vary by employer, and relevant industry experience matters too.
