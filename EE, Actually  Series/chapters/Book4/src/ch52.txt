H1: Chapter 52. Antennas and the Sky: LoRa, GPS, Satellites, and the Hydrogen Line
P: The International Space Station passes overhead several times a day, and its amateur radio can be heard with a handheld receiver and a stick antenna, its frequency sliding by a few kilohertz as it goes. The hydrogen in our galaxy glows at 1420 MHz, faintly enough that a small dish and a careful receiver are needed, and brightly enough that a backyard telescope can map the spiral arms.
P: Where you meet this: LoRa and IoT gateways, GPS receivers and their antennas, amateur and weather-satellite stations, model rocket and balloon telemetry, and amateur radio astronomy.
P: This chapter turns the antennas of Chapters 7, 13, and 16 and the link budget of Chapter 30 into projects. A NanoVNA, Chapter 16's instrument at the price of a meal, is the measuring tool throughout.
H2: 52.1 Trimming a dipole with a NanoVNA
P: A half-wave dipole is about 95 % of a free-space half wavelength long, because the end effect of a wire of finite thickness shortens it. For the 2 m amateur band at 145.8 MHz that is 0.95 × 1.028 m = 0.977 m tip to tip. Cut it a little long, measure the frequency of minimum SWR with the NanoVNA, and shorten both halves by the fraction the resonance sits low: a minimum at 141.4 MHz, 3 % low, means removing 3 % of the length.
H2: 52.2 Antennas for LoRa and GPS readers
P: A 915 MHz LoRa reader with a quarter-wave whip needs 299.79/915/4 = 8.19 cm of wire over a ground plane at least that large in radius; shorter ground planes raise the resonant frequency and lower the efficiency, and the NanoVNA shows both. A GPS receiver needs a right-hand circularly polarized antenna for L1 at 1575.42 MHz. The ceramic patch common in GPS modules is shortened by its high-permittivity substrate: with εr ≈ 20, the half-wave patch is c/(2f√εr) = 21 mm across, and fringing makes it somewhat smaller in practice. Two orthogonal modes fed 90° apart, or a nearly square patch with trimmed corners, make the polarization circular.
H2: Worked Example 52.1
P: Problem. GPS L1 C/A signals arrive at a minimum of −128.5 dBm. With a 3 dBic patch and a receiver noise figure of 2 dB, find C/N0. Then find the link margin of a 915 MHz LoRa telemetry link from a rocket at 10 km, with +20 dBm transmitted, dipole-class antennas of 2.15 dBi at both ends, and a receiver sensitivity of −132 dBm at spreading factor 10 and 125 kHz.
P: Solution. GPS: C/N0 = −128.5 + 3 − (−174 + 2) = 46.5 dB-Hz, enough to track, though a receiver indoors loses 20 dB or more. LoRa: the free-space loss at 10 km and 915 MHz is 20 log(10⁴) + 20 log(9.15×10⁸) − 147.55 = 111.7 dB, so the received power is 20 + 4.3 − 111.7 = −87.4 dBm and the margin to −132 dBm is 44.6 dB. The margin is needed: a rocket's whip has a null along its axis, which points at the ground station at launch and during descent, and spin and polarization mismatch cost more. Check the legal limits for the band and the antenna gain before flying.
H2: 52.3 Satellites overhead
P: A satellite in low Earth orbit moves at about 7.7 km/s, and the component along the line of sight changes from about +7 km/s at rise to −7 km/s at set, so its signal shifts by f × v/c: at 145.8 MHz, a 7.2 km/s radial velocity is a 3.50 kHz shift, and at 436.795 MHz, the downlink of the long-lived amateur satellite SO-50, it is 10.5 kHz. Receivers retune during the pass, by hand or by software that computes the orbit from published two-line element sets. When active, the ISS voice downlink at 145.800 MHz and SO-50's 436.795 MHz downlink can be received with a handheld radio and small directional antenna pointed by hand; verify current ARISS/AMSAT status before a live pass; weather-satellite images near 137 MHz need a turnstile or quadrifilar antenna and an SDR. NOAA ended operational POES data delivery on June 16, 2025; NOAA-15 and NOAA-19 were decommissioned on August 19 and August 13, 2025, respectively. NOAA left APT/LRPT enabled but said not to use it for operational purposes, so do not assume a live POES signal is available. Some Meteor-M spacecraft have carried digital LRPT broadcasts near 137 MHz; spacecraft and downlink availability can change, so check current operator information before planning a live capture. The receiver method remains useful with a recorded pass or simulated signal.
EQ: Doppler shift Δf = f · vradial / c;   145.8 MHz at 7.2 km/s → 3.50 kHz
H2: 52.4 A backyard radio telescope
P: Neutral hydrogen emits at 1420.405751768 MHz, the 21 cm line. A 1.2 m dish has a beamwidth of about 70λ/D = 12.3°, a low-noise amplifier at the feed and an SDR record the spectrum, and the galaxy's rotation Doppler-shifts the line by up to a few hundred kilohertz, which maps the spiral arms as the sky drifts through the beam. The signal is a small rise of the system noise, so the radiometer equation sets what can be seen: the smallest detectable temperature change is the system temperature divided by the square root of the bandwidth-time product.
EQ: ΔTmin = Tsys / √(B τ):   100 K, 2.4 MHz, 10 s → 0.020 K
H2: Worked Example 52.2
P: Problem. A backyard system has Tsys = 100 K, a 2.4 MHz bandwidth, and integrates for 10 s. Find ΔTmin. Then find the frequency shift of hydrogen gas moving away at 100 km/s, and the beamwidth of the 1.2 m dish.
P: Solution. ΔTmin = 100/√(2.4×10⁶ × 10) = 100/4,899 = 0.020 K. The shift is 1420.4 MHz × 10⁵/3×10⁸ = 474 kHz, to a lower frequency, so the 2.4 MHz bandwidth holds the whole line. The beamwidth is 70 × 0.211/1.2 = 12.3°. The hydrogen line from the galactic plane raises the antenna temperature by tens of kelvins in a beam this wide, far above 0.02 K, which is why the project works with modest equipment; the difficulty is interference and the stability of the gain over the integration.

BENCH: Measure an antenna where it will be used. A whip measured on the bench without its ground plane, or a patch measured without the enclosure lid, resonates somewhere else in the finished product. Hold the NanoVNA's cable away from the antenna with a ferrite choke or clamp-on beads at the feed, and repeat the measurement with your hand near the cable: if the reading moves, the cable is part of the antenna.
