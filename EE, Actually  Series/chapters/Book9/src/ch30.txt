H1: Chapter 30. Transmitter Spectrum: Channel Power, ACLR, Emission Masks, and CCDF
P: Two labs measure the same base-station amplifier and disagree on its adjacent channel leakage by 6 dB. Both used good analyzers. One drove its mixer at −25 dBm and measured its own noise floor in the adjacent channel; the other drove it at −5 dBm and measured its own distortion. The amplifier was somewhere in between. ACLR measures the device only when the analyzer is set up to be better than the device, and that takes arithmetic before it takes a button.
P: Where you meet this: Cellular base-station and handset test, Wi-Fi and broadcast transmitter compliance, power-amplifier and predistortion development, production test of radio modules, and regulatory approval.
H2: 30.1 Channel power
P: A modulated signal's power is the integral of its spectral density over the channel bandwidth. The analyzer sweeps (or FFTs) with an RBW much smaller than the channel, uses an rms (average) detector, and sums the bins with a correction for the RBW filter's noise bandwidth; the result agrees with a thermal power sensor within the analyzer's absolute accuracy (Chapter 23). Some standards apply a filter shape (a root-raised-cosine with α = 0.22 for WCDMA) rather than a rectangle; choose the standard's setup, not a generic one.
H2: 30.2 ACLR and ACPR
P: The adjacent channel leakage ratio (ACLR, called ACPR in older documents) is the power in the adjacent channel divided by the power in the main channel, both integrated over their bandwidths. Limits come from the standards: LTE base stations need 45 dB, handsets 30 to 33 dB, WCDMA base stations 45 and 50 dB at the first and second adjacent channels. Three analyzer contributions can dominate: its noise floor in the adjacent channel, its own third-order distortion, and its phase noise spreading the main channel into the adjacent one. Optimize the mixer level (attenuation) so that noise and distortion are balanced, switch on noise correction, and check the analyzer on a clean source.
H2: Worked Example 30.1: Does the analyzer have the range?
P: Problem. An analyzer's noise floor is −150 dBm/Hz at its mixer and its own ACLR (from distortion and phase noise) is −55 dBc at a mixer level of −10 dBm, for a 3.84 MHz WCDMA channel. What noise-limited ACLR can it measure? If the device is at −45 dBc, what does the analyzer read?
P: Solution. Noise in 3.84 MHz: −150 + 10 log(3.84e6) = −84.2 dBm, 74.2 dB below −10 dBm: noise is not the limit. The analyzer's distortion at −55 dBc adds to the device's −45 dBc. If uncorrelated, they add in power: 10 log(10^(−4.5) + 10^(−5.5)) = −44.6 dBc, an error of 0.4 dB. If they are correlated and in phase (both from third-order distortion of the same signal), they add as voltages: 20 log(10^(−2.25) + 10^(−2.75)) = −42.6 dBc, 2.4 dB worse. A margin of 10 dB is therefore not enough for 0.5 dB accuracy; aim for 15 to 20 dB, which is what the mixer-level optimization buys.

H2: 30.3 Spectrum emission mask and spurious emissions
P: The spectrum emission mask (SEM) checks the region beyond the adjacent channels against limits that change with offset, each segment with its own measurement bandwidth (for example 30 kHz near the carrier and 1 MHz farther out). The analyzer measures with a narrower RBW and integrates to the specified bandwidth: a limit in 1 MHz measured with a 100 kHz RBW is compared after adding 10 log(1 MHz/100 kHz) = 10 dB of integration. Spurious emissions out to several GHz use the techniques of Chapter 21: preselection, notch filters for the carrier, and the attenuation test for analyzer-generated products.
H2: 30.4 CCDF and crest factor
P: The complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF) plots the probability that the instantaneous power exceeds the average by x dB. It tells the amplifier designer how much headroom the signal needs: OFDM signals reach about 9 to 11 dB above average at 0.01 % probability, single-carrier QPSK with α = 0.22 about 4 to 6 dB. Crest-factor reduction and digital predistortion change the CCDF, so measure it at the amplifier's input and output: a compressed amplifier shows the right-hand tail pulled in, as in Figure 30.1 (right). Analyzers measure CCDF from gap-free I/Q in a bandwidth that must cover the signal; an RBW or span too narrow underreads the peaks.
H2: Worked Example 30.2: Backoff from the CCDF
P: Problem. An amplifier saturates at 46 dBm. The OFDM signal has 9.7 dB of peak-to-average ratio at 0.01 %, and the ACLR limit is 45 dB. With the listing's amplifier model, how much backoff is needed, and what average power results?
P: Solution. Without linearization, staying out of clipping at 0.01 % needs 9.7 dB below saturation: 36.3 dBm average. The model gives ACLR −44.7 dB at 10.3 dB backoff and −45.4 dB at 10.5 dB; with 1 dB of production margin, run 11.5 dB backoff, 34.5 dBm (2.8 W) from a 40 W amplifier. That 7 % ratio of average to saturated power is why predistortion and crest-factor reduction exist: they buy back several dB.
P: Listing 30.1 — Channel power, ACLR, and CCDF of an OFDM signal through a Rapp amplifier model, computed by integrating the spectral density over the channels as an analyzer does (Python with NumPy; the command-line argument sets the backoff; analyzer personalities provide the same numbers on real hardware).
P: """Channel power, ACLR, and CCDF of an OFDM-like signal through an amplifier model, computed the way asignal analyzer does it: integrate the power spectral density over each channel bandwidth; count how oftenthe instantaneous power exceeds the average by x dB.On a real analyzer use its measurement personalities (Keysight X-Series: CONF:ACP / READ:ACP?, andCONF:PST / READ:PST? for CCDF; R&S FSx: CALC:MARK:FUNC:POW:SEL ACP; check the guide); this listingshows what those numbers mean and lets you predict them.Run:  python l30_1_aclr_ccdf.py  [backoff_dB]"""import mathimport sysimport numpy as npdef ofdm(nsym=200, nfft=1024, used=600, osr=4, seed=30):    """Baseband OFDM with QPSK on `used` subcarriers, oversampled by osr. Returns samples and fs/bw ratio."""    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)    out = []    for _ in range(nsym):        X = np.zeros(nfft * osr, complex)        k = np.r_[1:used // 2 + 1, -used // 2:0]        X[k] = (rng.choice([-1, 1], used) + 1j * rng.choice([-1, 1], used)) / math.sqrt(2)        out.append(np.fft.ifft(X))    x = np.concatenate(out)    return x / math.sqrt(np.mean(np.abs(x) ** 2)), used / (nfft * osr)  # occupied bw as fraction of fsdef rapp_pa(x, backoff_db=8.0, p=2.0):    """Rapp AM/AM model: saturates smoothly at |x| = 1; input scaled so the average is backoff_db below."""    xin = x * 10 ** (-backoff_db / 20)    a = np.abs(xin)    return xin / (1 + a ** (2 * p)) ** (1 / (2 * p))def psd(x, nseg=4096):    w = np.hanning(nseg); segs = x[: x.size // nseg * nseg].reshape(-1, nseg)    s = np.mean(np.abs(np.fft.fft(segs * w, axis=1)) ** 2, axis=0)    return np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fftfreq(nseg)), np.fft.fftshift(s)def aclr(x, bw):    """Main channel power and adjacent channel ratios (lower, upper) in dB; channel spacing = bw * 1.1."""    f, s = psd(x)    band = lambda c: s[np.abs(f - c) <= bw / 2].sum()    main = band(0.0)    return 10 * math.log10(band(-1.1 * bw) / main), 10 * math.log10(band(1.1 * bw) / main)def ccdf(x, probs=(1e-2, 1e-3, 1e-4)):    """Power level (dB above average) exceeded with each probability."""    p = np.abs(x) ** 2 / np.mean(np.abs(x) ** 2)    return [10 * math.log10(np.quantile(p, 1 - q)) for q in probs]if __name__ == '__main__':    bo = float(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 8.0    x, bw = ofdm()    print('input  CCDF 1 %%/0.1 %%/0.01 %%: %.1f / %.1f / %.1f dB' % tuple(ccdf(x)))    y = rapp_pa(x, bo)    print('output CCDF 1 %%/0.1 %%/0.01 %%: %.1f / %.1f / %.1f dB' % tuple(ccdf(y)))    lo, hi = aclr(y, bw)    print('backoff %.1f dB: ACLR lower %.1f dB, upper %.1f dB' % (bo, lo, hi))
P: Common misconception. The analyzer's ACLR equals its noise floor. Distortion and phase noise often limit first; lowering the attenuation to beat the noise can make the reading worse. Sweep the attenuation and find the setting where the reading is best: that is where noise and distortion balance.
BENCH: Measure the generator first. Play the same waveform from a good vector generator straight into the analyzer at the device's output level; if that reads −60 dBc, you have a 15 dB margin for a −45 dBc device. If it reads −50 dBc, find out why before testing anything.
