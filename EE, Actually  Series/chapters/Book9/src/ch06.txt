H1: Chapter 6. Source-Measure Units and Parametric Analyzers
P: A diode's datasheet says its leakage is 1 nA at 20 V. To check it you need a 20 V source that can measure a nanoampere through the same wires, and a forward curve from 1 nA to 10 mA on the same part, and the same instrument must also sink current when it tests a battery. A power supply and an ammeter can do some of this badly. A source-measure unit does all of it, through one pair of terminals.
P: Where you meet this: Device characterization in semiconductor labs, sensor and LED testing, battery and solar cell measurements, and production test of components.
H2: 6.1 What an SMU is
P: A source-measure unit (SMU) is a precision voltage or current source and a precision voltmeter and ammeter combined, with fast switching between sourcing and limiting. Source a voltage and it measures the current, while a compliance (limit) on the current protects the device; source a current and it measures the voltage, with a voltage compliance. Its ranges reach far lower than a power supply's: a typical bench SMU such as the Keithley 2450 sources up to 200 V and 1 A (20 W) and has current ranges down to 10 nA, with 10 fA rms noise on that range. It operates in all four quadrants: positive and negative voltage, and current flowing out of or into the instrument. In quadrants 1 and 3 it delivers power; in 2 and 4 it absorbs it, as when it discharges a battery or tests a solar cell.

H2: 6.2 Sweeps, compliance, and four-wire sensing
P: The SMU's strength is the sweep: step the source through a list, linear or logarithmic, measure at each step, and store the result, with a delay and an integration time per point. Compliance is not a fault but a mode: when the device draws more current than the limit, the SMU holds the limit and reports that it is in compliance, so check that flag in every result. At currents above about 10 mA, use four-wire (remote) sensing, exactly as with a power supply (Chapter 5) and an ohmmeter (Chapter 12). At low currents the problems change: leakage through cables and fixtures, settling of the cable capacitance, and noise; Chapter 14 covers them, and the cure begins with the SMU's guarded triaxial output.
P: Listing 6.1 — A logarithmic forward-current sweep of a diode from 1 nA to 10 mA with four-wire sensing and a 2 V compliance, and a fit of the ideality factor, saturation current, and series resistance (Python with PyVISA and NumPy; runs in simulation with --sim).
P: """Forward I-V of a diode with an SMU: logarithmic current sweep, voltage compliance, four-wire sensing,then a fit of the ideality factor n, saturation current Is, and series resistance Rs.SCPI as in the Keithley 2450 SCPI command set; other SMUs differ, so check the manual.Run:  python l06_1_smu_sweep.py --sim      python l06_1_smu_sweep.py USB0::0x05E6::0x2450::04412345::INSTR"""import argparseimport mathimport numpy as npVT = 0.025852                    # thermal voltage kT/q at 300 K (V)class SimSMU:    """A diode with Is = 2.5 nA, n = 1.75, Rs = 2 ohm, plus 20 uV of measurement noise."""    def __init__(self):        self.i = 0.0        self.rng = np.random.default_rng(6)    def write(self, cmd):        if cmd.startswith(':SOUR:CURR '):            self.i = float(cmd.split()[1])    def query(self, cmd):        if cmd == '*IDN?':            return 'SIM,SMU,0,1.0'        v = 1.75 * VT * math.log(self.i / 2.5e-9 + 1) + 2.0 * self.i        return '%.7e' % (min(v, 2.0) + self.rng.normal(0, 20e-6))def main():    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser()    ap.add_argument('resource', nargs='?')    ap.add_argument('--sim', action='store_true')    a = ap.parse_args()    smu = SimSMU() if a.sim else __import__('pyvisa').ResourceManager().open_resource(a.resource)    print(smu.query('*IDN?'))    for cmd in ('*RST', ':SOUR:FUNC CURR', ':SOUR:CURR:VLIM 2', ':SENS:FUNC "VOLT"',                ':SENS:VOLT:RSEN ON', ':SENS:VOLT:NPLC 1', ':OUTP ON'):        smu.write(cmd)    currents = np.logspace(-9, -2, 36)        # 1 nA to 10 mA, 5 points per decade    volts = []    for i in currents:        smu.write(':SOUR:CURR %.4e' % i)        volts.append(float(smu.query(':MEAS:VOLT?')))    smu.write(':OUTP OFF')    v = np.array(volts)    fit = (currents >= 1e-7) & (currents <= 1e-4)    # ideal region: above leakage, below Rs    slope, icept = np.polyfit(v[fit], np.log(currents[fit]), 1)    n = 1 / (slope * VT)    i_s = math.exp(icept)    v_ideal = n * VT * math.log(currents[-1] / i_s + 1)    rs = (v[-1] - v_ideal) / currents[-1]    print('points %d, V(10 mA) = %.4f V' % (len(v), v[-1]))    print('n = %.3f, Is = %.2f nA, Rs = %.2f ohm' % (n, i_s * 1e9, rs))    print('slope %.1f mV per decade' % (n * VT * math.log(10) * 1e3))if __name__ == '__main__':    main()
H2: Worked Example 6.1: Diode parameters from a sweep
P: Problem. Listing 6.1 sweeps 36 points from 1 nA to 10 mA. In simulation the diode has Is = 2.5 nA, n = 1.75, and Rs = 2 Ω. What should the fit return, and why is the fit window 100 nA to 100 µA?
P: Solution. The listing reports 36 points, V(10 mA) = 0.7077 V, and the fit n = 1.746, Is = 2.45 nA, Rs = 2.05 Ω, within the noise of the simulated meter. On a log-current plot the ideal diode is a straight line of 1.75 × 25.85 mV × ln 10 = 104 mV per decade (Book 3, Chapter 5, has the physics). The fit window avoids both ends: below 100 nA the current is close to Is, so the "+1" in the diode law bends the line; above 100 µA the series resistance adds a growing IR drop (20 mV at 10 mA). At 10 mA the ideal diode would need 0.688 V; the extra 20 mV is the 2 Ω.
H2: 6.3 Accuracy at low current
P: Low-current accuracy specifications have a large fixed term. For the 2450, the 10 nA measure range is ±(0.10 % of reading + 50 pA), the 100 nA range ±(0.060 % + 100 pA), the 1 µA range ±(0.025 % + 300 pA). The fixed term decides the result for small currents, so the lowest range that holds the current is the right one, even if autoranging would choose otherwise during a sweep.
H2: Worked Example 6.2: Measuring 1 nA of leakage
P: Problem. Measure a leakage current of 1 nA with the ranges above. Which range, and how good is the result?
P: Solution. 10 nA range: 0.10 % × 1 nA + 50 pA = 51 pA, so ±5.1 %. 100 nA range: 0.6 pA + 100 pA = 100.6 pA, ±10 %. 1 µA range: 300 pA, ±30 %. Use the 10 nA range; the result is 1.00 nA ± 0.05 nA, before you add the errors of the fixture (Chapter 14), which at this level are often larger than the instrument's.
H2: 6.4 Pulsed I-V and parameter analyzers
P: A power transistor measured at 10 W in DC heats up within milliseconds, so its curve describes a hot device at an unknown temperature. Pulsed I-V applies each point as a short pulse, often 50 µs to 1 ms, at a low duty cycle and measures near the end of the pulse, so the junction stays close to the chuck or case temperature. A semiconductor parameter analyzer is a mainframe with several SMUs, capacitance-voltage units, and pulse units, synchronized and driven by characterization software (Keysight's B1500A and Keithley's 4200A-SCS are examples), usually on a wafer prober. Its high-power relative, the curve tracer, and the bench walk-throughs that turn curves into device parameters are the subject of Chapter 7.
H2: Worked Example 6.3: Why pulse
P: Problem. A power MOSFET is measured at 5 V and 2 A, 10 W, with a junction-to-case thermal resistance of 20 K/W. Compare DC with 100 µs pulses at 0.1 % duty, where the transient thermal impedance at 100 µs is 0.5 K/W.
P: Solution. In DC the junction rises 10 W × 20 K/W = 200 K above the case, which would change every parameter and probably destroy the part. In the pulse, the rise by the end of 100 µs is 10 W × 0.5 K/W = 5 K, and the average power is 10 mW, adding 0.2 K. The pulsed curve describes a device within about 5 K of the case, which is what a datasheet curve means.
BENCH: In every SMU result, read the compliance flag before the number. A sweep that hit compliance at its high end returns a neat curve that flattens at the limit, and it looks like device saturation. One extra column in the data file, compliance yes or no, prevents a wrong paper.
