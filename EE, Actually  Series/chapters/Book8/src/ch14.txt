H1: Chapter 14. Low Power and Sleep
P: The garden node was designed to last most of a year on one 18650 cell. It died in nine days. The firmware did sleep, exactly as written, for 597 seconds out of every 600, and in those seconds the chip drew a few microamps. The board around it did not sleep: its voltage regulator, its power LED, and its USB bridge together drew 13.5 mA, around the clock. Low-power firmware starts with a current measurement of the whole board, and only then with code.
P: Where you meet this: Every battery or solar product: sensor nodes, trackers, wildlife cameras, remote controls, wearables, and the "it lasted a week instead of a year" support ticket.
H2: 14.1 Where the energy goes
P: A duty-cycled device spends a short time awake, measuring and transmitting at tens or hundreds of milliamps, and a long time asleep at microamps. Its average current is the time-weighted mean of the two, and the battery life is the usable capacity divided by that average. The first-order arithmetic is this chapter's; usable capacity, temperature, self-discharge, and the regulator's efficiency are Book 6's, and they always shorten the result.
EQ: I_avg = (I_active t_active + I_sleep t_sleep) / T,    life ≈ C / I_avg
P: Two terms dominate in practice. The active energy is the active current times the active time, and the active time is usually dominated by waiting: for Wi-Fi to associate, for DHCP, for a server to answer. The sleep current is whatever the whole board draws while the chip sleeps, and on development boards that is often a thousand times the chip's own figure.
P: Listing 14.1 — A first-order power-budget calculator for the three cases of Worked Examples 14.1 to 14.3 (Python, runs on the PC).
P: """Average current and battery life of a duty-cycled node (first-order; Book 6 adds the rest)."""def average_ma(active_ma, active_s, sleep_ma, period_s):    sleep_s = period_s - active_s    return (active_ma * active_s + sleep_ma * sleep_s) / period_sdef life_days(capacity_mah, avg_ma):    return capacity_mah / avg_ma / 24CASES = [    ("module, 3 s Wi-Fi",      80, 3.0, 0.020),    ("dev board, 3 s Wi-Fi",   80, 3.0, 13.5),    ("module, 0.8 s Wi-Fi",    80, 0.8, 0.020),]for name, a_ma, a_s, s_ma in CASES:    avg = average_ma(a_ma, a_s, s_ma, 600)    print(f"{name:22s} average {avg:7.3f} mA  life {life_days(3000, avg):7.1f} days")
H2: Worked Example 14.1: The node on a bare module
P: Problem. An ESP32-S3 module wakes every 10 minutes, spends 3 s connecting to Wi-Fi and sending one MQTT message at an average of 80 mA, and sleeps at 20 µA (the chip's deep sleep of about 7 to 8 µA, plus the BME280, the regulator's quiescent current, and leakage). Find the average current and the life on a 3000 mAh 18650 cell.
P: Solution. Active: 80 mA × 3 s/600 s = 0.400 mA. Sleep: 0.020 mA × 597/600 = 0.020 mA. Average 0.420 mA. Life 3000/0.420 = 7140 h = 298 days, before Book 6's corrections. The active part is 95 % of the energy, so the active time is where to work next.
H2: Worked Example 14.2: The same firmware on a development board
P: Problem. The same firmware runs on a development board whose sleep current measures 13.5 mA (its AMS1117-type linear regulator alone is specified at about 5 mA of quiescent current; the power LED and the USB-to-UART bridge take the rest). Find the life.
P: Solution. Average = 0.400 + 13.5 × 597/600 = 0.400 + 13.43 = 13.83 mA. Life = 3000/13.83 = 217 h = 9.0 days. The firmware is identical; the board decides. For battery work use a bare module or a board designed for low power, with a low-quiescent regulator, no always-on LED, and a USB bridge that is unpowered when USB is unplugged. Measure the sleep current before writing any power-saving code.

H2: 14.2 Sleep modes
P: Microcontrollers offer a ladder of sleep modes. In light sleep the CPU stops but RAM and state are kept, and the chip resumes where it stopped; on the ESP32-S3 it draws a few hundred microamps. In deep sleep almost everything is switched off, the chip draws a few microamps, and waking up is a reset: the program starts again from app_main, and only data in the small RTC memory survives. Wake-up sources are a timer, a level on an RTC-capable pin (the PIR sensor), a touch pad, or the ultra-low-power coprocessor, which can read a sensor while the main cores sleep. The RP2040 has a dormant mode woken by a pin or the real-time clock; the nRF52840 sleeps between BLE events automatically under its SoftDevice or Zephyr stack.
P: Listing 14.2 — Deep sleep on the ESP32-S3 with a timer and a PIR wake-up, and counters kept in RTC memory (ESP-IDF, C).
P: // ESP32-S3 sensor node: wake every 10 minutes or on PIR motion, do the work, sleep.// RTC_DATA_ATTR variables survive deep sleep; everything else restarts from scratch.#include <stdint.h>#include "esp_attr.h"                  // RTC_DATA_ATTR#include "esp_sleep.h"#include "esp_log.h"#include "driver/gpio.h"#define PIR_GPIO     GPIO_NUM_5            // an RTC-capable pin (GPIO 0..21 on the S3)#define INTERVAL_US  (10ULL * 60 * 1000000)static const char *TAG = "node";RTC_DATA_ATTR static uint32_t boots;RTC_DATA_ATTR static uint32_t motion_events;void app_main(void){    boots++;    esp_sleep_wakeup_cause_t cause = esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause();    if (cause == ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_EXT0) motion_events++;    ESP_LOGI(TAG, "boot %lu, cause %d, motion %lu", (unsigned long)boots, (int)cause,             (unsigned long)motion_events);    // ... read the BME280, send one MQTT or LoRa message (Chapters 17 and 18) ... H2: Worked Example 14.3: Shortening the active time
P: Problem. Storing the Wi-Fi channel and access point address in RTC memory, using a static IP address instead of DHCP, and sending without waiting for a reply shorten the active time from 3 s to 0.8 s at the same 80 mA. Find the new average and life for the bare module.
P: Solution. Active: 80 × 0.8/600 = 0.107 mA. Sleep: 0.020 mA. Average 0.127 mA, life 3000/0.127 = 23 700 h = 987 days, 2.7 years, more than three times longer. At this point the sleep current is 16 % of the total and the cell's own self-discharge (Book 6) becomes comparable, so further firmware work has little effect. Switching the radio to LoRa, with no association at all, goes further (Chapter 18).

P: Key idea. Measure the board's sleep current first, then shorten the time awake. Firmware can only save what the hardware lets it save, and most of a duty-cycled node's energy goes into waiting while awake.
P: Common misconception. Deep sleep is like pausing the program. On the ESP32 family, waking from deep sleep is a reset: global variables start from their initial values, peripherals must be configured again, and only RTC memory survives. Code written as if execution continued after esp_deep_sleep_start never runs.
P: AI Pair-Programming Note
P: Assistants produce sleep code that compiles but leaves the drain on: pins left driving LEDs or sensors, pull-ups fighting a pin held low, Wi-Fi not stopped before light sleep, or a wake-up pin that is not RTC-capable. Ask for a list of every pin's state during sleep as part of the answer, and check it against the schematic.
BENCH: A multimeter cannot follow a current that jumps from 80 mA to 20 µA in a millisecond; its average is meaningless and its burden voltage can brown out the board on the jump. Use a power profiler (Nordic's Power Profiler Kit II and Joulescope are common) or a shunt resistor with a scope, and record a whole cycle. The area under the curve is the energy, and the flat part is the sleep current you must explain.
