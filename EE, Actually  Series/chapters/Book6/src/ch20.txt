H1: Chapter 20. Charging Supplies and Solid-State Pulsed Power
P: Since the 1940s, radar modulators have used a trick that looks like free energy: charge a capacitor from a 5 kV supply through an inductor and a diode, and it stops at 10 kV. Nothing is created; the inductor swings the charge past the supply voltage and the diode keeps it from swinging back. Charge it through a resistor instead, and half the energy taken from the supply turns into heat.
P: Where you meet this: Every radar modulator, every pulsed-laser and X-ray power supply, every defibrillator's charging circuit, every capacitor-charging supply, and every solid-state Marx generator.
P: Chapter 8 introduced shelf C, high voltage and pulsed power: storing energy in capacitors and releasing it in a Marx generator or a pulse-forming line. This chapter covers the two engineering problems that sit on either side of that release. Before the pulse, a charging supply must refill the capacitor bank at the pulse rate, efficiently. During the pulse, accelerator projects have evaluated series-parallel semiconductor stacks as replacements for thyratrons. This can reduce tube replacement and adjustment, but each stack must meet the application's pulse voltage and current while controlling voltage sharing, current sharing, timing, parasitics, and thermal stress.
H2: 20.1 The average power of a repetitive system
P: Each pulse draws the energy ½CV² from the bank, and the charging supply must replace it before the next pulse. Its average output power is therefore that energy times the pulse repetition frequency. The charging supply's peak power can be far larger if the recharge time is short compared with the pulse period, but a supply that charges over the whole period sees a steady load. Radar transmitters, medical linear accelerators, and industrial pulsed systems all run at repetition rates where this average power is tens to hundreds of kilowatts.
EQ: Pavg = ½ C V² × PRF
H2: Worked Example 20.1
P: Problem. A 1 µF bank is charged to 40 kV and discharged 100 times a second. Find the energy per pulse and the charging supply's average power.
P: Solution. E = ½ × 10⁻⁶ × (4 × 10⁴)² = 800 J per pulse, and Pavg = 800 × 100 = 80 kW. A supply that is 90% efficient draws 88.9 kW from the line and dissipates 8.9 kW, by Chapter 1's waste formula.
H2: 20.2 Why resistive charging wastes half
P: Charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage through a resistor delivers ½CV² to the capacitor and dissipates exactly the same amount in the resistor, regardless of its value: resistive charging is at best 50% efficient. Command-charging and constant-current supplies avoid this by charging the capacitor with a controlled current from a switching converter, often a resonant series converter whose output naturally behaves as a current source, so that the loss is set by the converter's efficiency rather than by the charging physics. At 80 kW, that difference is the difference between 8.9 kW and 80 kW of waste.
P: Key idea. A pulsed system's charging supply must deliver the pulse energy times the repetition rate, and it must charge with a controlled current, because charging a capacitor from a voltage source through a resistor always wastes as much energy as it stores.
H2: 20.3 Series stacks of switches
P: A single IGBT or SiC MOSFET blocks a kilovolt or a few; a 40 kV modulator needs dozens in series. Semiconductor stacks switch in nanoseconds to microseconds, run at high repetition rates, and last far longer than gas switches, but they must share the voltage evenly at all times. In the off state, differences in leakage current unbalance the voltages, and static sharing resistors across each device swamp those differences. During switching, differences in timing unbalance the transient voltage, and RC snubbers and synchronized gate drives, delivered through isolated drivers of the kind in Chapter 19 that must each withstand the full stack voltage at their position, keep the devices together. Solid-state Marx generators, in which each stage is a capacitor and a semiconductor switch, combine the two ideas.
EQ: Rshare ≤ ΔVallowed / ΔIleak
H2: Worked Example 20.2
P: Problem. A 40 kV stack uses devices operated at 600 V each, half their 1.2 kV rating. How many are needed? If leakage may differ by 100 µA between devices and the imbalance must stay below 60 V, find the sharing resistor and its loss.
P: Solution. 40,000/600 = 66.7, so 67 devices. Rshare ≤ 60 V/100 µA = 600 kΩ, and each resistor dissipates 600²/600 kΩ = 0.6 W, about 40 W for the stack, a small price for safety.
H2: 20.4 Safety on shelf C
P: The energies of pulsed-power systems are lethal. A 1 µF bank at 40 kV stores 800 J, and even a bank that has been switched off can recharge itself from dielectric absorption. Standard practice includes interlocked enclosures, automatic dump switches and bleeder resistors that discharge the bank when a door opens, visible grounding sticks applied before work begins, and clearances designed for the full voltage plus transients. The insulation design of Chapter 8, with its margin for partial discharge and surface tracking, is part of the same discipline.
H2: 20.5 Resonant charging
P: Section 20.2 showed that charging a capacitor through a resistor wastes as much energy as it stores. Resonant charging avoids the waste by charging through an inductor. A DC supply at voltage V connected through an inductor L to an empty capacitor C drives a half-cycle of a sine-shaped current; at its end, after π√(LC), the capacitor has reached 2V and the current has fallen to zero, where a series diode holds it. Ideally no energy is lost, and the capacitor reaches twice the supply voltage. Line-type modulators for radar, Chapter 8, have used resonant charging since the 1940s. The charging time sets the maximum repetition rate, and a de-Q'ing circuit or a regulated command-charge supply trims the final voltage to the 0.1 % accuracy that a stable pulse amplitude requires.
EQ: V_C,final = 2 V,   t_charge = π √(L C)

H2: 20.6 The capacitor-charging power supply
P: Modern pulsed systems charge their capacitors with a dedicated switching converter, a capacitor-charging power supply, rated not in watts but in joules per second, the average power it can push into a capacitor that starts each cycle empty. Inside is usually a series-resonant converter whose output current is nearly constant regardless of the capacitor voltage, which is exactly what a capacitor being charged from zero needs: constant current charges it linearly, and the converter delivers its full power except near the start. Commercial units from a few hundred J/s to 30 kJ/s and beyond serve lasers, pulsed magnets, and medical accelerators, with output voltages of a few kilovolts to 50 kV.
H2: Worked Example 20.3
P: Problem. A 5 kV supply charges a 1 µF capacitor through a 10 mH inductor and a diode. Find the final voltage, the charging time, and the maximum repetition rate if the discharge and recovery take 200 µs.
P: Solution. The capacitor reaches 10 kV after π√(10 × 10⁻³ × 10⁻⁶) = 314 µs. With 200 µs more per cycle, the period is at least 514 µs, so the rate is at most about 1.9 kHz. Each pulse delivers ½ × 10⁻⁶ × (10⁴)² = 50 J, and at 1 kHz the supply provides 50 kW on average.
H2: Worked Example 20.4
P: Problem. A capacitor-charging supply rated 5 kJ/s charges a 2 µF capacitor to 20 kV. Find the charging time and the highest repetition rate.
P: Solution. The energy per pulse is ½ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × (2 × 10⁴)² = 400 J. At 5 kJ/s the capacitor charges in 0.08 s, so the supply supports 12.5 pulses per second at most, slightly fewer once the discharge time is included.
H2: 20.7 Solid-state Marx generators and inductive adders
P: Two solid-state topologies dominate modern pulsed power at tens of kilovolts. The solid-state Marx generator, the circuit of Section 8.5 with IGBTs or SiC MOSFETs instead of spark gaps, charges its stage capacitors in parallel through diodes and erects them in series by turning its switches on together. Each switch blocks only one stage voltage, the pulse width is set by when the switches turn off, and the stages can be fired in sequence to shape the pulse. The inductive adder instead places each stage's switch and capacitor on the primary of its own one-turn transformer core; a single secondary conductor threads all the cores, and the stage voltages add along it while every primary sits near ground potential. Accelerator kicker magnets and klystron modulators use both for their microsecond pulses with nanosecond edges.
H2: Worked Example 20.5
P: Problem. An inductive adder has 20 stages, each driving its core with 1 kV. Find the output voltage, and the volt-second product each core must support for a 2 µs pulse.
P: Solution. The stage voltages add to 20 kV on the secondary. Each core sees its own primary's 1 kV for 2 µs, 2 mV·s, and its cross-section and flux swing must support that without saturating: for a core with 1 T of usable flux swing, a cross-section of 2 × 10⁻³ V·s/1 T = 20 cm².
H2: Worked Example 20.6
P: Problem. A Tesla coil is a pair of loosely coupled resonant circuits, and its numbers follow from this chapter. Treat it as physics and calculation only. Its secondary has L = 50 mH and, with its top load, about 20 pF. Find its resonant frequency, the primary inductance that tunes a 30 nF primary capacitor to the same frequency, the energy per discharge with the primary charged to 10 kV, and the ideal secondary voltage if all of that energy reached the secondary capacitance.
P: Solution. f = 1/(2π√(0.05 × 20 × 10⁻¹²)) = 159 kHz. The primary needs L = 1/((2πf)² × 30 nF) = 33 µH. Each discharge starts with E1 = ½ × 30 nF × (10 kV)² = 1.5 J. In the ideal lossless, tuned model, transferring all of E1 to 20 pF gives V2 = √(2E1/C2) = 10 kV × √(30 nF/20 pF) = 387 kV. This is an energy-conservation ceiling, not a predicted operating voltage. Coupling k changes the energy-exchange rate and the coupled resonant frequencies; coupling alone does not dissipate energy. Real peak voltage depends on tuning, circuit Q, driver or spark-gap loss and quench timing, corona, and streamer loading. The resonant charger in Section 20.5 instead reaches 2V for its stated source-driven LC initial condition; that result does not follow from a C1/C2 ratio.
P: Safety: high voltage
P: Tesla coils, Marx generators, neon-sign transformers, and microwave-oven transformers can kill. Their primary capacitors hold lethal energy after the power is off and can recharge from dielectric absorption, so they are discharged with a grounding stick and kept shorted. The sparks make ozone and nitrogen oxides, the radio-frequency output causes deep burns that do not hurt at first, and the broadband interference reaches radio services for kilometers. This book gives the physics and the numbers, not construction steps; Section 20.4 lists the practice that professional laboratories follow.
P: Pulsed power concentrates energy in time. Chapter 21 turns to a source that delivers its energy slowly, unpredictably, and at a voltage that depends on the weather: the solar array.
BENCH: Treat every capacitor bank as charged until a meter, applied after the grounding stick, says otherwise. Check that the dump or bleeder circuit discharges the bank within the time the safety standard requires. When a series stack of switches fails, look for unequal voltage sharing first; the balancing networks age.
