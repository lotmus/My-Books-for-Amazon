H1: Chapter 13. Inverters and Motor Drives
P: Feed a three-phase inverter from an 800 V bus and plain sine-wave modulation gives at most 0.612 × 800 = 490 V rms line to line. Add a third harmonic to all three references, which cancels between the lines, and the same switches on the same bus deliver 0.707 × 800 = 566 V, 15.5 % more, for the price of a few lines of code.
P: Where you meet this: Every electric vehicle's traction inverter, every industrial motor drive, every heat-pump compressor, every robot joint's servo drive, and every ESC that spins a drone's or radio-control model's brushless motor.
P: The converters of Chapters 3 to 5 produce DC. An inverter produces AC from DC: it drives the motors of electric vehicles, pumps, and fans, and it connects solar panels and batteries to the grid of Chapter 7. A large share of the world's electricity passes through motor drives, so their efficiency matters as much as a data center's. This chapter covers the three-phase bridge, pulse-width modulation of a sine wave, the voltage a given DC bus can deliver, and the basics of the motor the inverter drives.
H2: 13.1 The three-phase bridge
P: A three-phase inverter has three half-bridges, one per phase, each a pair of switches between the DC bus rails. Each switches at tens of kilohertz with a duty cycle that varies sinusoidally at the output frequency, so the average voltage of each phase follows a sine wave; the motor's inductance filters the switching ripple out of the current. Silicon IGBTs dominate industrial drives up to megawatts, and silicon carbide MOSFETs have taken over electric-vehicle traction inverters, where their lower switching loss allows higher frequencies and efficiencies of 98% to 99%.

H2: 13.2 How much AC voltage from a DC bus
P: With sinusoidal PWM, the modulation index m is the ratio of the sine reference's peak to the carrier's, and at m = 1 each phase's fundamental has a peak of Vdc/2. The line-to-line RMS voltage is then (√3/(2√2)) m Vdc = 0.612 m Vdc. Adding a third harmonic to all three references, or using space-vector modulation, lets the line voltage reach the full bus, extending the usable modulation by 2/√3 = 1.155, a 15.5% increase, without overmodulation, because the added common-mode component cancels between lines.
EQ: VLL,rms = 0.612 m Vdc (sinusoidal PWM, m ≤ 1);   maximum with SVPWM: Vdc/√2 = 0.707 Vdc

H2: Worked Example 13.1
P: Problem. An electric-vehicle inverter runs from a 400 V bus. Find the largest line-to-line RMS voltage with sinusoidal PWM and with space-vector PWM, and the current for a 50 kW motor at a power factor of 0.9 with space-vector PWM. Find the loss at 98% efficiency.
P: Solution. Sinusoidal PWM: 0.612 × 400 = 244.9 V rms. Space-vector: 400/√2 = 282.8 V rms, 15.5% more. The motor current is 50,000/(√3 × 282.8 × 0.9) = 113.4 A rms. At 98% efficiency the inverter's waste is 50,000 × (1/0.98 − 1) = 1.02 kW, which the coolant loop must remove.
P: Key idea. An inverter synthesizes a sine wave by modulating the duty cycle of each phase. Space-vector or third-harmonic modulation gets 15.5% more line voltage from the same DC bus, which means less current for the same power.
H2: 13.3 The motor the inverter drives
P: An AC motor's speed is tied to the frequency: a synchronous or permanent-magnet machine turns at 120f/p revolutions per minute, with p poles, and an induction motor runs slightly slower, the difference being its slip. The motor's back-EMF rises with speed, so at high speed the inverter runs out of voltage and must weaken the field, trading torque for speed. Field-oriented control transforms the three phase currents into a rotating frame aligned with the rotor, where torque and flux are controlled by two independent DC-like currents, the way a DC motor's armature and field are. This is the control in nearly every modern drive.
EQ: nsync = 120 f / p  rpm
H2: Worked Example 13.2
P: Problem. Find the synchronous speed of a four-pole motor at 60 Hz, and of an eight-pole traction motor at 200 Hz.
P: Solution. 120 × 60/4 = 1800 rpm, and 120 × 200/8 = 3000 rpm. A traction motor reaching 15,000 rpm with eight poles needs an output frequency of 1000 Hz, so the inverter's switching frequency must be well above that, typically 10 to 20 kHz, to keep the current smooth.
H2: 13.4 Common-mode voltage and bearing currents
P: The fast edges of a PWM inverter produce a common-mode voltage on the motor's windings that couples through the motor's stray capacitances to its frame and bearings. The resulting currents can pit the bearings and fail the motor in months, and they radiate conducted emissions that Book 7's tests catch. Designers add common-mode chokes, filter the output with dv/dt or sine-wave filters, ground the shaft, or use insulated bearings. Faster SiC edges make the problem harder, so the benefit of lower switching loss must be weighed against the cost of filtering.
H2: 13.5 Field-oriented control
P: A three-phase motor's torque depends on the angle between the stator field and rotor. Field-oriented control measures rotor angle and transforms the phase currents into a rotating frame using Clarke and Park transforms; the resulting id and iq are DC-like quantities, with id aligned to rotor flux and iq perpendicular to it. For a permanent-magnet synchronous machine, T = (3/2)p[psi_f iq + (Ld - Lq)id iq]. In an approximately nonsalient surface-PM motor with id = 0, this reduces to T = (3/2)p psi_f iq. With the amplitude-invariant Park transform used here, id and iq have the same amplitude as phase-current peaks; do not read iq as RMS current. Interior-PM saliency, current limits, saturation, and voltage limits can change the practical torque available.
EQ: T = (3/2) p [ψ_f i_q + (L_d − L_q)i_d i_q]
H2: 13.6 The 800 V traction inverter
P: An electric car's traction inverter converts battery DC into three-phase motor AC at powers up to several hundred kilowatts. For the same power, doubling pack voltage from 400 to 800 V halves current; in an unchanged cable, I^2R loss falls to one quarter. At the same current ceiling, an 800 V system can roughly double transferred power if the battery, cable, connector, and charger are all rated for the voltage. Actual charging power is limited by the complete vehicle-station path. Some production EVs, including Hyundai's IONIQ 5 and Porsche's Taycan, use 800 V-class architectures. A CCS fast charger is not inherently fixed at 800 V: ABB lists a 150 to 920 V DC output range for its Terra 360. See Appendix C for product documentation.
P: An 800 V-class battery calls for inverter devices rated above the pack's maximum voltage and switching transients; 1200 V-class SiC MOSFET modules are used in some systems. SiC can reduce switching loss, especially at higher switching rates, but it does not guarantee a fixed efficiency or range gain. Results depend on device sizing, topology, switching strategy, cooling, and operating point; IGBTs remain a valid choice in some designs.
H2: Worked Example 13.3
P: Problem. An approximately nonsalient surface-PM motor has id = 0, 4 pole pairs, and flux linkage psi_f = 0.08 Wb. With the amplitude-invariant Park transform, find torque at iq = 200 A peak and mechanical power at 6000 rpm.
P: Solution. T = (3/2) x 4 x 0.08 Wb x 200 A peak = 96 N m. At 6000 rpm, omega = 6000 x 2 pi/60 = 628 rad/s, so ideal mechanical power is T omega = 96 x 628 = 60.3 kW. The corresponding balanced phase-current RMS is 200/sqrt(2) = 141 A when id = 0. This torque-speed point is ideal; the drive must also meet its current, voltage, thermal, and speed limits.
H2: Worked Example 13.4
P: Problem. A 250 kW drive runs from a 400 V and from an 800 V battery. Find the DC current in each case and the ratio of cable losses for the same cable.
P: Solution. 250 kW/400 V = 625 A and 250 kW/800 V = 312.5 A. The cable loss scales with I², so the 800 V system loses a quarter as much in the same cable, or can use a cable with half the copper cross-section for the same loss.
H2: 13.7 Dead time and its distortion
P: Each leg of an inverter needs a dead time between turning one switch off and the other on, or both would conduct and short the DC bus. During the dead time the load current flows through one of the diodes, and which one depends on the current's direction, not on the command. The leg's output voltage therefore differs from the commanded value by an error of about Vdc × td × fsw, positive or negative with the sign of the current. The error is small in volts but appears as a step at every zero crossing of the current, distorting it with low-order harmonics and producing torque ripple and audible noise in motors at low speed. Drives measure or estimate the current's sign and add a compensating voltage, and the short dead times that SiC and GaN switches allow, tens of nanoseconds instead of microseconds, make the problem much smaller.
EQ: ΔV ≈ Vdc × td × fsw
H2: Worked Example 13.5
P: Problem. An 800 V inverter switches at 10 kHz with a dead time of 500 ns. Find the average voltage error, and compare it with a SiC design at 20 kHz with 100 ns.
P: Solution. ΔV = 800 × 500 × 10⁻⁹ × 10⁴ = 4.0 V, about 1 % of a 400 V phase output, enough to distort the current visibly at low speed. The SiC design gives 800 × 100 × 10⁻⁹ × 2 × 10⁴ = 1.6 V, less than half, despite switching twice as fast.

BENCH: Judge a drive by its motor current, not its voltage: the voltage is a pulse train, the current shows the quality of the control. Measure phase voltages with a differential probe rated for the bus, never with a ground-referenced probe. Frosted or fluted bearing races in an inverter-fed motor point to common-mode voltage driving current through the bearings.
