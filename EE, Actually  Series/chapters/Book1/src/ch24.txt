H1: Chapter 24 — Calculus for Electrical Engineering
P: A capacitor's current depends on how fast its voltage changes, which is why a fast logic edge can draw a surprising spike of current. 'How fast' is a derivative. This chapter keeps calculus to the two ideas engineers use every day, rates and accumulations.
P: Where you meet this: Capacitor and inductor behavior, amplifier slew rate, energy as the integral of power, and battery fuel gauges that integrate current over time (coulomb counters).
P: Calculus has a reputation it does not deserve. Stripped of the machinery, it consists of two ideas: a derivative measures how fast something is changing, and an integral measures how much of something has accumulated. Everything else is technique for computing them. This chapter builds both ideas from the circuit behavior that motivates them, and covers the specific results the rest of this series relies on.
H2: 24.1 The Derivative as a Rate
P: The derivative of a quantity with respect to time is its rate of change: how fast it is going up or down at a particular instant. Written dv/dt, it is the slope of the graph of v against t at that moment.
P: Circuit theory is full of derivatives because two of the three basic components are defined by them. A capacitor's current is proportional to the rate of change of its voltage, and an inductor's voltage is proportional to the rate of change of its current:
EQ: i = C·dv/dt     and     v = L·di/dt
P: Read these as physical statements rather than formulas. A capacitor passes current only while its voltage is changing — hold the voltage constant and the current stops, which is why a capacitor blocks DC. An inductor develops voltage only while its current is changing — hold the current constant and the voltage vanishes, which is why an inductor is a short circuit to DC.
FIG: fig24_1.png | Figure 24.1 — Differentiating a sinusoid shifts its phase by 90 degrees; integrating shifts it by -90 degrees. | A sinusoid, its derivative, and its integral are plotted together; the derivative leads by 90 degrees and the integral lags by 90 degrees.
P: The derivatives you need are few:
LI: A constant has derivative zero. Nothing changing means no rate of change.
LI: A straight line v = kt has derivative k, the constant slope.
LI: A power tⁿ has derivative n·t^(n−1).
LI: The exponential e^(kt) has derivative k·e^(kt) — it reproduces itself, scaled. This is the defining property from Chapter 19.
LI: Sine and cosine swap: the derivative of sin(ωt) is ω·cos(ωt), and the derivative of cos(ωt) is −ω·sin(ωt). Note that each differentiation brings down a factor of ω and advances the phase by 90°, which is exactly the jω of Chapter 23 seen without complex notation.
MIS: The derivative of sin(ωt) is ω·cos(ωt), not cos(ωt). The factor of ω is dropped constantly and the consequences are severe, because ω is often a large number — at mains frequency it is 377, and in RF work it is in the billions. Omitting it does not make an answer slightly wrong; it makes it wrong by orders of magnitude. The factor appears because of the chain rule: differentiating the inside function ωt gives ω, which multiplies the result.
H2: 24.2 The Integral as an Accumulation
P: Integration is the reverse of differentiation: given a rate, it recovers the total accumulated. Geometrically it is the area under a curve.
P: Rearranging the capacitor relation shows the accumulation directly. If i = C·dv/dt, then the voltage is the accumulated current divided by capacitance:
EQ: v(t) = (1/C)·∫ i dt
P: This says a capacitor's voltage is proportional to the total charge that has flowed into it — the integral of current is charge, which is exactly the statement that a coulomb is an ampere-second. The capacitor is a device that remembers how much current has passed, which is why it is a memory element and why its state must be specified as an initial condition when solving a circuit.
P: The integrals worth knowing are the derivatives read backwards: the integral of tⁿ is t^(n+1)/(n+1), the integral of e^(kt) is e^(kt)/k, the integral of cos(ωt) is sin(ωt)/ω, and the integral of sin(ωt) is −cos(ωt)/ω. Each integration divides by ω and retards the phase by 90°, which is the 1/jω of capacitive impedance.
H2: 24.3 Definite Integrals, Averages, and RMS
P: A definite integral evaluates the accumulation between two limits, and dividing by the width of the interval gives the average value:
EQ: average of f over [a, b] = (1/(b−a))·∫ f dt
P: This is where the RMS definition of Chapter 20 comes from, and it is worth seeing the calculation once. The mean square of a sinusoid over one period is
EQ: mean of V_m²sin²(ωt) = V_m²·(mean of sin²)
P: and the double-angle identity from Chapter 20 gives sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2. The cosine term averages to zero over a whole number of cycles, leaving a mean of exactly one half. So the mean square is V_m²/2, and the root mean square is V_m/√2.
P: That is the entire derivation of the 0.707 factor, and it makes plain why the factor is specific to sinusoids: it came from the average of sin², and a different waveform has a different average of its square.
WE: Worked Example 24.1
P: A capacitor of 4.7 µF carries a current that rises linearly from 0 to 10 mA over 5 ms, then stops. Find the voltage across it at the end of that interval, assuming it started at 0 V.
P: The current is i(t) = kt with k chosen so that i = 10 mA at t = 5 ms: k = 0.010/0.005 = 2 A/s.
P: The voltage is the integral of current divided by capacitance:
P: v(t) = (1/C)·∫₀^t kt dt = (1/C)·k·t²/2
P: Evaluating at t = 5 ms:
P: v = (1/4.7 × 10⁻⁶)(2)(0.005²/2) = (1/4.7 × 10⁻⁶)(2)(1.25 × 10⁻⁵) = (2.5 × 10⁻⁵)/(4.7 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.32 V
P: A useful cross-check: the average current over the ramp is half the peak, or 5 mA, sustained for 5 ms, giving a total charge of 25 µC. Then v = Q/C = 25 × 10⁻⁶ / 4.7 × 10⁻⁶ = 5.32 V. The two routes agree, and the second is often quicker — for a linear ramp, the area under the curve is a triangle, and you do not need the integral at all.
KEY: In sinusoidal steady state, d/dt becomes ×jω and ∫dt becomes ÷jω. This is the whole reason Chapter 23 works. Once you accept it, no differential equation ever needs to be solved to analyze a linear circuit at a single frequency — the calculus has been done in advance, once, and packaged into the factor jω.
H2: 24.4 Differential Equations, Briefly
P: A differential equation relates a quantity to its own derivatives. The RC circuit produces the simplest interesting example. Kirchhoff's voltage law around a charging loop gives
EQ: RC·dv/dt + v = V_s
P: and the solution is the exponential of Chapter 19, v(t) = V_s(1 − e^(−t/RC)). You are not expected to derive that here. What matters is recognizing the pattern: a first-order differential equation produces an exponential response with a single time constant, and a second-order one produces either two exponentials or a damped oscillation, depending on the sign of the discriminant met in Chapter 16.
P: This is why the phasor method is such a relief. Differential equations must be solved for the complete response, including the transient. But if all you need is the steady state — the behavior after the transients have died away, which is what most AC analysis concerns — the phasor method delivers it algebraically. Transient analysis, where the differential equation genuinely must be solved, is taken up properly in Part III.
BENCH: Estimate derivatives from what a scope shows you: i = C·Δv/Δt. A 100 pF load swung through 3.3 V in 1 ns draws about 0.33 A during the edge, which is why fast logic needs decoupling capacitors right at its power pins.
