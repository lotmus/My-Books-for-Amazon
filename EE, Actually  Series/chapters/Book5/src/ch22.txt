PART: Part IV. Silicon, Sensing, Light, and a Network of Your Own
H1: Chapter 22. The Modem in Silicon: DSP in FPGAs and ASICs
P: The Wi-Fi radio inside a small ESP32 module performs billions of multiplications every second while it receives, and its processor cores never see a single sample. The arithmetic of Chapters 2 to 11 runs in dedicated logic, built from the same adders, multipliers, and registers that an FPGA offers by the thousand. Where the arithmetic runs decides the power, the cost, and whether the product can exist at all.
P: Where you meet this: Every Wi-Fi, cellular, and Bluetooth chip, every software-defined radio with an FPGA behind its converters, base-station radios, radar sensors, video encoders in cameras and phones, and test instruments.
P: Earlier chapters counted multiplications per second for each block: 307 million for the direct-form decimating filter of Worked Example 8.5, 5.9 billion for a half-band stage at 491.52 MS/s, 64 billion add-compare-select operations for a gigabit Viterbi decoder. This chapter asks what executes them. It compares processors, FPGAs, and ASICs by throughput and energy, shows how the word lengths of Chapter 9 become hardware, introduces the CORDIC rotator that most oscillators and phase detectors use, and ends with the economics that decide when a design deserves its own chip. The hardware description languages that describe such logic are Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, chapter "HDL: Software That Becomes Hardware"; the fabrication of the chip is Book 3, Semiconductor Physics and Devices.
H2: 22.1 Where the arithmetic runs
P: Four kinds of hardware run a modem. A microcontroller or application processor executes instructions one or a few at a time; each instruction pays for fetching, decoding, and moving operands through a register file, and that overhead, not the arithmetic, dominates its energy. A digital signal processor adds wide multiply-accumulate units and special addressing for filters and FFTs. An FPGA is a grid of lookup tables, registers, block memories, and hard multiplier blocks whose connections are configured at power-up, so the designer lays the data flow out in space instead of time: a 64-tap filter can be 64 multipliers working at once. An ASIC fixes that layout in silicon and removes the configurability, which makes each operation cheaper still.
P: Mark Horowitz's widely quoted figures for a 45 nm process give the scale. A 32-bit integer multiply costs about 3 pJ and an 8-bit add 0.03 pJ, while executing one instruction on a simple processor core costs around 70 pJ of overhead before the arithmetic starts, and reading a word from external DRAM costs well over a nanojoule. Newer processes lower all of these, but the ratios survive: dedicated logic spends its energy on arithmetic, a processor on bookkeeping. That is why the physical layer of every radio chip is hardwired, while the processor beside it runs the protocol and the application. An FPGA sits between the two: its configurable wiring costs several times the energy of an ASIC's fixed wiring, but it computes in parallel and can be changed after the board is built.
EQ: throughput = (number of multipliers) × (clock rate);   power ≈ (operations per second) × (energy per operation)
H2: Worked Example 22.1
P: Problem. An 802.11a receiver at 20 MHz uses a 32-tap channel filter on complex samples at 40 MS/s with real coefficients, a 64-point FFT every 4 µs, and a one-tap equalizer on 52 subcarriers per symbol. Count the real multiplications per second, the number of hard multipliers needed at a 200 MHz clock, and the arithmetic energy at 3 pJ per multiplication against 70 pJ per instruction on a processor.
P: Solution. The filter needs 2 × 32 × 40 × 10⁶ = 2.56 × 10⁹ real multiplications per second. A radix-2 64-point FFT has (64/2) × 6 = 192 complex multiplications, 768 real ones, every 4 µs, 0.19 × 10⁹ per second. The equalizer needs 52 complex multiplications per symbol, 208 real ones every 4 µs, 0.05 × 10⁹ per second. The total is 2.80 × 10⁹ per second, so at a 200 MHz clock about 14 multipliers working in parallel do it. At 3 pJ each the arithmetic costs 8.4 mW; as instructions at 70 pJ it would cost 196 mW before any memory traffic, more than the whole radio's receive budget in a battery device. The Viterbi decoder adds 3.5 × 10⁹ add-compare-select operations per second at 54 Mbit/s, also in dedicated logic.
H2: 22.2 Word lengths become wires
P: Chapter 9 chose word lengths for one filter. A whole receiver chooses them stage by stage, and every bit costs area and power in every stage that carries it. The guiding number is the signal-to-quantization-noise ratio of Chapter 2, about 6 dB per bit, reduced by the signal's peak-to-average ratio because full scale must hold the peaks. A datapath needs only enough bits to keep its quantization noise well below the noise the signal already carries: 10 dB below adds 0.4 dB to the total. An 8-bit datapath that is wasteful for a 4096-QAM signal is ample for a GNSS signal buried under the noise, which is why GPS receivers famously work with 1- or 2-bit converters.
P: Three effects make words grow inside the datapath. A multiplication of a B1-bit by a B2-bit number has B1 + B2 bits. A sum of N terms can grow by log₂N bits. And an FFT of N points can grow by log₂N bits for a pure tone, because all of the signal can land in one bin. The designer either keeps the growth, scales by one half at each FFT stage and accepts a little more rounding noise, or uses block floating point, in which a whole block shares one exponent. Each choice is checked with a bit-true model: a floating-point reference, a fixed-point model that reproduces every rounding of the hardware, and a test that the two agree within the noise budget.
EQ: SQNR ≈ 6.02B + 1.76 − (PAPR − 3) dB;   FFT growth ≤ log₂N bits
H2: Worked Example 22.2
P: Problem. An OFDM signal with a peak-to-average ratio of 12 dB at the chosen full scale arrives with an SNR of 30 dB. How many bits must the datapath carry so that its quantization noise lies 10 dB below the signal's own noise, and how much does that noise then add?
P: Solution. The quantization SNR must reach 30 + 10 = 40 dB. From 6.02B + 1.76 − (12 − 3) ≥ 40, B ≥ (40 − 1.76 + 9)/6.02 = 7.85, so 8 bits. Noise 10 dB below the existing noise raises the total by 10 log₁₀(1 + 0.1) = 0.41 dB. A 12-bit converter therefore gives a 30 dB signal four bits of room, which the receiver spends on the strong neighbors that arrive with it.
H2: 22.3 The CORDIC rotator
P: Several blocks of a modem rotate a complex number by an angle: the numerically controlled oscillator of Section 2.8, the frequency correction of Chapter 10, and every phase detector that needs the angle of a sample. Multipliers and a sine table can do it, but the CORDIC algorithm, published by Jack Volder in 1959 for aircraft navigation computers, does it with shifts and adds. It rotates by a fixed sequence of angles arctan(2^−i), each a shift of the other coordinate, choosing at each step whether to turn forward or back. Each iteration adds about one bit of angular accuracy. The rotations stretch the vector by a constant 1.6468 after many steps, which a single multiplication by 0.6073 removes, or which is absorbed into a later gain. Run in reverse, driving the y coordinate to zero, the same structure computes the magnitude and the angle of a sample, which is how FM discriminators and carrier loops are built in FPGAs.
EQ: x' = x − d y 2^−i,  y' = y + d x 2^−i,  z' = z − d arctan 2^−i;   gain K = Π √(1 + 2^−2i) → 1.6468

H2: Worked Example 22.3
P: Problem. A CORDIC must produce a phase accurate to 16 bits for an NCO running at 245.76 MS/s. How many iterations does it need, and what latency does a fully pipelined version have?
P: Solution. After n iterations the residual angle is at most about arctan(2^−(n−1)); 16-bit accuracy needs a final step of about 2^−15 rad, arctan(2^−15) = 3.05 × 10⁻⁵ rad, so 16 iterations. Pipelined with one iteration per clock at 245.76 MHz, it produces one result per clock with a latency of 16 cycles, 65 ns, harmless in a receiver whose filters delay the signal by microseconds. Each stage needs two adders, a third for the angle, and no multiplier.
H2: 22.4 Folding, pipelining, and the clock
P: An FPGA's multipliers run at a fixed clock, often 400 to 700 MHz in a modern device, while signals arrive at whatever rate the converter sets. If the clock is faster than the sample rate, one multiplier can serve several taps in turn, folding the filter in time; if it is slower, the filter must be unfolded into parallel copies that each handle a share of the samples. Long adder trees and accumulators are cut into stages with registers between them, pipelining, so that no path between two registers is longer than one clock period. Timing closure, making every such path fit, is the daily work of an FPGA designer. The AMD DSP48E2 slice of Section 9.5 includes a pre-adder for symmetric filters, the 27 × 18 multiplier, and the 48-bit accumulator in one pipelined block.
H2: Worked Example 22.4
P: Problem. A 64-tap symmetric FIR filter processes 245.76 MS/s complex data, and the FPGA's DSP slices run at 491.52 MHz with a pre-adder. How many slices does it need?
P: Solution. The pre-adder sums each symmetric pair of samples first, so each output needs 32 multiplications per rail. At 245.76 MS/s that is 32 × 245.76 × 10⁶ = 7.86 × 10⁹ multiplications per second per rail. Each slice does 491.52 × 10⁶ per second, so 7.86 × 10⁹/4.9152 × 10⁸ = 16 slices per rail, 32 for I and Q. A mid-range FPGA with several hundred slices holds a dozen such filters; a decimating polyphase form, Chapter 9, would cut the count by the decimation factor.
H2: 22.5 Converters on the same die
P: The newest radio platforms put the converters beside the logic. AMD's Zynq UltraScale+ RFSoC family integrates multi-gigasample ADCs and DACs, hardened digital down- and up-converters with NCOs and half-band chains of the kind built in Chapter 9, programmable logic, and Arm processor cores on one chip. A designer can sample a carrier in the second or third Nyquist zone, Section 2.5, decimate it in hardened blocks, and run the modem in the fabric, with no high-speed interface between separate chips. Phased-array radars, 5G radio units, and test instruments use them. The jitter limit of Section 2.3 then becomes a requirement on the clock that feeds the chip, and the synthesizer that provides it is treated in Book 4, RF, Microwave, and Transceivers, chapter "Frequency Synthesis: Fractional-N and DDS".
H2: 22.6 FPGA or ASIC: the economics
P: An ASIC costs a large fixed sum before the first chip exists, the non-recurring engineering: design and verification staff, licenses, masks, and test development. After that each chip is cheap. An FPGA has almost no fixed cost but a much higher price per unit and higher power. The break-even volume is the fixed cost divided by the difference in unit costs. Mask sets for an advanced process cost tens of millions of dollars, so only products sold in the millions, phones, Wi-Fi chips, and video codecs, justify the newest nodes; mature nodes cost far less, and many radar, sensor, and industrial radios are built there. Prototypes often go through a shared multi-project wafer, where many designs split one mask set. For students and hobbyists, shared-shuttle programs such as Tiny Tapeout put a small design on real silicon for a modest fee; the price and the area offered change with each shuttle, so check the program's current terms rather than a number in a book. The fabrication steps themselves are Book 3, Semiconductor Physics and Devices.
EQ: break-even volume = NRE / (unit cost FPGA − unit cost ASIC)
H2: Worked Example 22.5
P: Problem. Take, as illustrative values, an FPGA design at 80 dollars per unit against an ASIC at 8 dollars per unit after 3 million dollars of non-recurring engineering. Find the break-even volume, and the total cost of each at 10 000 and at 200 000 units.
P: Solution. The break-even volume is 3 × 10⁶/(80 − 8) = 41 667 units. At 10 000 units the FPGA costs 0.80 million dollars and the ASIC 3.08 million; at 200 000 units the FPGA costs 16.0 million and the ASIC 4.6 million. The ASIC also draws a fraction of the power, which can decide the question below the break-even volume when the product runs from a battery. Its risk is the schedule: a mistake costs a new mask set and months, while an FPGA bitstream is fixed in an afternoon.

H2: 22.7 Cameras and video codecs in hardware
P: Video is the largest data stream most embedded products handle, and it shows the same split between processor and logic. A camera sensor delivers raw pixels; a hardware encoder compresses them with block transforms, motion estimation, and entropy coding defined by H.264, H.265, or AV1, and each of those steps is a fixed data flow of the kind dedicated logic does best. A phone encodes 4K video in hardware at a few hundred milliwatts. A hobby board such as the ESP32-S3 with an OV2640 camera relies on the sensor's own JPEG encoder, because the processor could not compress the raw stream in time, and the Wi-Fi link of Chapter 15 could not carry it uncompressed.
H2: Worked Example 22.6
P: Problem. An OV2640 sensor delivers 1600 × 1200 pixels at 15 frames per second in RGB565, 2 bytes per pixel. Find the raw bit rate. A 1080p30 stream in YUV 4:2:0, 1.5 bytes per pixel, is encoded by a hardware H.264 encoder to 6 Mbit/s. Find its raw rate and the compression ratio.
P: Solution. 1600 × 1200 × 2 bytes × 15 = 57.6 MB/s, 461 Mbit/s, far beyond what the ESP32-S3's Wi-Fi delivers in practice, a few to a few tens of megabits per second. The 1080p30 stream is 1920 × 1080 × 1.5 × 30 = 93.3 MB/s, 746 Mbit/s raw, and 746/6 = 124 times smaller after encoding. Only dedicated logic sustains that compression at video rate within a phone's power budget.
BENCH: When an FPGA filter's output disagrees with the floating-point model, compare them sample by sample, not by spectrum. A difference of exactly one least significant bit on some samples is rounding and is expected; a difference that grows with the signal is an overflow or a missing bit of growth; a difference that appears only on the first samples after reset is an uninitialized register. The bit-true model is the instrument here, and it should be written before the hardware.
P: On the Job
P: Who uses this: FPGA and ASIC digital designers, the digital-hardware field of the industry, at makers of base stations, radar, test instruments, network equipment, and wireless chips. Typical devices are AMD Zynq UltraScale+ RFSoC and Versal, Intel Agilex, Lattice parts for small designs, and custom ASICs in every phone. The tools are MATLAB or Python bit-true models, Vivado or Quartus, simulators such as Questa or Verilator, and cocotb test benches. A realistic task: port a floating-point channel filter to fixed point, choose the word lengths from the noise budget, show that the hardware matches the bit-true model on a million samples, and close timing at 491.52 MHz.
