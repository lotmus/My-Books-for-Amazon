H1: Chapter 19 — Digital Control: Sample, Hold, and Delay
P: Move a control loop from analog parts into a microcontroller and it often gets slightly worse, not better. The cause is delay: sampling and holding the output costs on average half a sample period, plus whatever time the code takes. This chapter puts a number on that cost.
P: Where you meet this: Firmware control loops in motor drives, power supplies, drones and anything with a microcontroller in the loop.
P: The controllers of Chapters 17 and 18 were written as continuous equations, and for decades they were built that way, from op-amps, resistors, and capacitors. Today nearly every controller is a few lines of code running in a microcontroller: it reads a sensor through an analog-to-digital converter, computes a command, and writes it to a PWM generator or a digital-to-analog converter, then waits for the next tick. The arithmetic is easy. What changes is time. The controller sees the plant only at sampling instants, and it answers each one late. This chapter shows how to account for that lateness and how to translate a continuous design into code that behaves like it.
H2: 19.1 What Happens in One Period
P: In each sampling period T, a digital controller does four things in order: the converter samples the sensor, the processor computes the new command, the output stage updates, and the output holds that value until the next update. That last step is called a zero-order hold, because it holds a constant (zeroth-order) value between samples, and it turns a sequence of numbers into a staircase waveform the plant can respond to.
FIG: fig19_1.png | Figure 19.1 — A sampled-and-held output follows the signal in steps, and on average it lags by half a sample period. That half period is phase the loop has to pay for. | A blue sine wave over 2 seconds with a red staircase that samples it every 0.1 seconds and holds each value until the next sample. A dashed red sine, the same signal delayed by half a sample period, runs through the middle of the steps.
P: Seen from the plant, the staircase is a delayed copy of the smooth command a continuous controller would have produced. A zero-order hold contributes, on average, half a sample period of delay. If the processor computes during one period and applies the result at the start of the next, as is common because it makes the timing deterministic, that adds a full period more.
EQ: Total delay ≈ T/2 (hold) + T_compute,   phase lag at ω: φ = ω × delay (radians)
P: A delay is unlike a pole. It does not reduce the gain at all, and its phase lag grows without limit as frequency rises. That is why sampling sets a ceiling on how fast a loop can be, however clever the controller.
H2: 19.2 The Phase Cost at Crossover
WE: Worked Example 19.1
PROB: The PI design of Worked Example 18.1 (crossover 100 rad/s, phase margin 90° in continuous form) is implemented on the 5 ms joint tracker declared in Chapter 10, with the command applied one period after the sample is taken. Estimate the total delay, the phase lost at crossover, and the remaining phase margin. Repeat for a crossover of 50 rad/s.
SOL: The hold contributes 2.5 ms and the one-period compute delay 5 ms, a total of 7.5 ms. At 100 rad/s the lag is 100 × 0.0075 = 0.75 rad ≈ 43°, so the phase margin falls from 90° to about 47°. A simulation of the sampled loop shows a step overshoot of about 27%, somewhat more than Chapter 18's two-pole estimate for 47° would suggest, because a delay is not a pole. Halving the crossover to 50 rad/s (K_p = 2.5 V per rad/s, K_i = 25 V per rad) halves the lag to about 21°, leaves a margin near 69°, and the simulated step shows no measurable overshoot, at the cost of a loop half as fast.
FIG: fig19_2.png | Figure 19.2 — Step response of the motor-speed PI loop (crossover 100 rad/s): continuous controller, sampled at 1 kHz, and sampled at 200 Hz with a one-period compute delay. | Three sampled-data PI step responses compare a continuous controller with 1 kHz and 200 Hz sampling; the slower case includes a full-period delay.
KEY: Every sample period of delay costs ω_c × T radians of phase at crossover. Choose the sample rate from the phase margin you can afford to lose, not from the Nyquist rate of the signal.
P: The example also shows where the rule of thumb in Chapter 17 comes from. A 200 Hz loop is 1257 rad/s in angular terms. A crossover of 100 rad/s is about one twelfth of that, and it already costs over 40 degrees. Faster sampling is the direct cure: at 1 kHz with the same one-period compute delay, the total delay is 1.5 ms, the lag at 100 rad/s is under 9 degrees, and the response in Figure 19.2 is nearly indistinguishable from the continuous one.
H2: 19.3 Turning the PI Controller into Code
P: The continuous PI law u = K_p e + K_i ∫e dt becomes a difference equation by replacing the integral with a running sum. The simplest, called the backward-Euler form, adds the present error times the period to an accumulator each sample:
EQ: I[k] = I[k−1] + T · e[k],    u[k] = K_p · e[k] + K_i · I[k]
P: In code this is three lines: compute the error, update the accumulator, compute and clamp the command. The clamp is where anti-windup from Chapter 18 lives: if the computed command exceeds the actuator's limit, apply the limit and do not add this sample's error to the accumulator. Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, writes this loop as real C and MicroPython, and Section 19.6 gives the hardware numbers that decide whether it can keep up.
WE: Worked Example 19.2
PROB: Implement the PI controller of Worked Example 18.1 (K_p = 5 V per rad/s, K_i = 50 V per rad) at T = 5 ms. The present error is 0.4 rad/s, and the accumulator holds 0.30 rad. Find the new accumulator value and the command.
SOL: The accumulator gains T × e = 0.005 × 0.4 = 0.002 rad, becoming 0.302 rad. The command is 5 × 0.4 + 50 × 0.302 = 2.0 + 15.1 = 17.1 V. That exceeds the 12 V driver limit, so the output is clamped to 12 V and, under conditional integration, the accumulator is restored to 0.30 rad. Note that K_i T = 0.25 V per (rad/s) is the gain the integrator applies to one sample's error. If the sample rate changes, K_i T changes with it unless K_i is stated in true per-second units, which is why this book writes the gains with units.
H2: 19.4 Other Discretizations
P: Backward Euler is simple and robust, but it is only an approximation of the continuous integral, and its accuracy depends on the sample rate being well above the loop's bandwidth. The trapezoidal, or Tustin, method averages the present and previous error in the integral, which matches the continuous frequency response more closely up to about a quarter of the sample rate. For the derivative term, the backward difference (e[k] − e[k−1])/T is the usual choice, always followed by the low-pass filter Chapter 18 recommended, because a raw difference of noisy samples is mostly noise.
H2: 19.5 The Converter Front End
P: The analog-to-digital converter sees only the samples, and the sampling theorem applies: any signal content above half the sample rate folds back into the band below it as an alias. A motor current sensor sampled at 200 Hz that carries a 10 kHz PWM ripple can alias that ripple into a slow wobble the controller dutifully tries to correct. An analog anti-alias filter before the converter prevents this, but its phase lag at the loop's crossover is part of the loop too and must be counted along with the hold and the compute delay. A common compromise samples the sensor much faster than the control rate, averages several samples in software, and runs the control law on the average.
P: Quantization matters as well. A 12-bit converter spanning a 24 rad/s speed range resolves steps of about 6 mrad/s. An integrator acting on errors smaller than one step can drift between two codes and produce a small, persistent oscillation called a limit cycle. Adequate resolution, a small dead band around zero error, or added dither are the usual remedies.
MIS: Sampling well above the Nyquist rate of the signal is not enough for a control loop. Nyquist guarantees that a signal can be reconstructed from its samples, not that a loop acting on those samples is stable. A loop's sample rate is set by the phase delay it can tolerate at crossover, and that usually means ten to thirty times the closed-loop bandwidth.
H2: 19.6 PID on a Pico or a Teensy
P: The code version of the loop runs on the boards of the reader lab. A Raspberry Pi Pico (the RP2040: two Cortex-M0+ cores at 125 MHz by default, with no floating-point unit) and a Teensy 4.x (a 600 MHz Cortex-M7 with a hardware floating-point unit) bracket the range. The Pico is inexpensive and has unusually flexible PWM and programmable I/O blocks; the Teensy has the speed to run a floating-point PID loop at tens of kilohertz. The ESP32-S3 of Book 1's kit sits between them. Three hardware choices decide how well any of them closes a loop.
LI: A fixed sample rate. Run the control law from a hardware timer interrupt, not from a loop that runs as fast as it can; the delay arithmetic of Section 19.2 assumes a constant T.
LI: PWM resolution. A PWM counter clocked at f_clk and wrapping at f_PWM has f_clk/f_PWM steps. On the RP2040 at 125 MHz, a 20 kHz PWM, above hearing, has 6250 steps, about 12.6 bits; at 100 kHz it has only 1250.
LI: Arithmetic. Without a floating-point unit, floating-point math runs in software and is many times slower; fixed-point integer math is the usual answer on the Pico, and Book 8's chapter Fixed-Point Math and DSP (Chapter 15) shows how.
P: The code itself, the timer setup, the interrupt, and the clamp and anti-windup of Section 19.3 written as real C and MicroPython, lives in Book 8: Chapter 7 for timers and PWM, Chapter 12 for structuring a control loop as a state machine, and Chapter 13 for running it beside other tasks under a real-time operating system. This book keeps the numbers that decide whether that code can work.
WE: Worked Example 19.3
PROB: A motor loop on a Pico uses 20 kHz PWM from the 125 MHz system clock into a 12 V H-bridge, and runs a PI law at 1 kHz from a timer interrupt. The law takes 60 µs in software floating point. Find the number of PWM steps and the average voltage per step, the fraction of each control period the law consumes, and the loop delay and its phase cost at a 20 Hz crossover.
SOL: Steps = 125 MHz/20 kHz = 6250, which is log₂ 6250 = 12.6 bits, and each step is 12 V/6250 = 1.92 mV of average bridge voltage. The control period is 1 ms, so the law uses 60/1000 = 6% of it. The command is applied at the next sample, so the delay is T/2 + T = 1.5 ms, which costs 2π × 20 × 0.0015 = 0.19 rad, about 11°, at a 20 Hz crossover. Check: a Teensy 4.x with its floating-point unit runs the same law in a few microseconds, so the loop could sample ten times faster and cut the delay to 0.15 ms; on the Pico the same gain comes from fixed-point code.
KEY: A digital loop needs a fixed sample rate, enough PWM steps, and arithmetic that fits inside the period. Count all three before writing the code.
BENCH: As a rule of thumb, sample a control loop at least 10 to 20 times faster than its crossover frequency, and keep the computation delay fixed by updating the output at a fixed point in each period. Jitter in the loop timing behaves like a varying delay, and it is easier to prevent than to analyze.
