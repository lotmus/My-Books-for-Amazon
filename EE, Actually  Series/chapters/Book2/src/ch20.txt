H1: Chapter 20 — The Instrumentation Amplifier
P: A strain gauge on a bridge produces a few millivolts of signal riding on 2.5 V of common-mode voltage, with mains hum on top. The instrumentation amplifier's job is to amplify the millivolts and throw the rest away.
P: Where you meet this: Strain gauges and load cells, thermocouples, ECG electrodes, current shunts on the high side, and bridge sensors of all kinds.
P: Chapter 15 measured the errors an op-amp adds to a signal that arrives referenced to ground. Many of the signals worth measuring do not. A strain-gauge bridge, a current-sense resistor in the high side of a supply, and a thermocouple bonded to a grounded machine frame all present a small difference between two wires, and both wires sit at a large voltage that carries no information. This chapter builds the amplifier for that job in two steps: first the single op-amp difference amplifier, whose weakness is resistor matching, then the three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier that fixes it.
H2: 20.1 Differential and Common-Mode Signals
P: Any pair of input voltages V₊ and V₋ can be rewritten as two new quantities. The differential voltage is V_d = V₊ − V₋, and it carries the measurement. The common-mode voltage is V_cm = (V₊ + V₋)/2, the level both wires share. An ideal differential amplifier responds only to V_d. A real one responds a little to V_cm as well, and the figure of merit is the ratio of the two gains.
EQ: V_out = A_d · V_d + A_cm · V_cm,    CMRR = A_d / A_cm,    CMRR(dB) = 20 log₁₀(A_d / A_cm)
WE: Worked Example 20.1
PROB: A 350 Ω strain-gauge bridge is excited from 5 V and has a sensitivity of 2 mV/V at full load. Find the full-scale differential signal and the common-mode voltage at the bridge outputs.
SOL: Full scale is 2 mV/V × 5 V = 10 mV differential. Each bridge output sits at half the excitation, so V_cm = 2.5 V. The information is 10 mV riding on 2.5 V, a ratio of 250 to 1. An amplifier that converted even 1% of the common-mode level into output would produce 25 mV of error, more than twice the full-scale signal.
H2: 20.2 The Difference Amplifier and Its Matching Problem
P: One op-amp and four resistors make a difference amplifier. R₁ runs from V₋ to the inverting input, R₂ from the inverting input to the output, R₃ from V₊ to the non-inverting input, and R₄ from the non-inverting input to a reference node, usually ground. When R₂/R₁ = R₄/R₃, the output is exactly (R₂/R₁)·V_d and the common-mode level cancels. Superposition gives the general result, which shows where the cancellation fails.
EQ: A_cm = (R₁R₄ − R₂R₃) / (R₁(R₃ + R₄))
P: The cancellation is a subtraction of two nearly equal products, so it depends on matching, not on the op-amp. If each resistor can deviate from its nominal value by a fractional tolerance t, the worst case puts the errors in the directions that add, and for a difference gain G the common-mode rejection falls to roughly the following.
EQ: CMRR_worst ≈ (1 + G) / (4t)
WE: Worked Example 20.2
PROB: A unity-gain difference amplifier is built from four 0.1% resistors. Find its worst-case CMRR, and the output error it produces on the 2.5 V common-mode level of Worked Example 20.1.
SOL: CMRR ≈ (1 + 1)/(4 × 0.001) = 500, or 54.0 dB. The common-mode gain is A_d/CMRR = 1/500 = 0.002, so the 2.5 V common-mode level produces 2.5 × 0.002 = 5 mV at the output. Against a 10 mV full-scale signal at unity gain, that is a 50% error before any amplification is applied. Tighter resistors help in proportion: 0.01% parts give 74.0 dB and 0.5 mV.
P: The difference amplifier has a second weakness. Its input resistances are the resistors themselves, of order R₁ and R₃ + R₄, so the source must drive them. A 10 kΩ input loading a bridge whose output resistance is 175 Ω (two 350 Ω arms in parallel) attenuates the signal by 10k/10.175k = 0.983, a 1.7% gain error that changes with the bridge resistance. And unequal source resistances on the two inputs add directly to R₁ and R₃, which destroys the matching that set the CMRR.
MIS: A precision op-amp does not rescue a difference amplifier. Its CMRR of 120 dB applies to the op-amp's own inputs. The circuit's rejection is set by the four resistors and by any resistance in series with the inputs or the reference node, and with ordinary 1% parts at a gain of ten it is about 49 dB no matter which op-amp is used.
H2: 20.3 The Three-Op-Amp Instrumentation Amplifier
P: The instrumentation amplifier puts a follower-like stage in front of each input of a difference amplifier and lets the two front-end stages share one gain resistor. Each input drives a non-inverting input, so the source sees the op-amps' input impedance, typically gigaohms. Each front-end op-amp has a feedback resistor R from its output to its inverting input, and a single resistor R_G connects the two inverting inputs. Because each inverting input follows its own non-inverting input, the whole differential voltage appears across R_G, and the current it drives flows through both resistors R.
EQ: V₁ − V₂ = (1 + 2R/R_G) · V_d,    G = (1 + 2R/R_G) · (R₂/R₁)
FIG: fig20_1.png | Figure 20.1 — The classic three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier. One resistor, R_G, sets the gain; the difference stage on the right throws away the common-mode voltage. | Schematic. Input V minus drives the non-inverting input of op-amp A1; input V plus drives the non-inverting input of A2. Between the outputs of A1 and A2 is a chain of three resistors: R, R G and R. The inverting inputs of A1 and A2 connect to the two ends of R G. The outputs of A1 and A2 feed a difference amplifier A3 through resistors R2; A3 has a feedback resistor R3 and a matching R3 from its non-inverting input to ground. Formula: V out equals (1 plus 2R over R G) times (R3 over R2) times (V plus minus V minus).
P: The common-mode level does not drive any current through R_G, because both ends of R_G move together. The front end therefore passes V_cm through at unity gain while multiplying V_d by its gain G₁ = 1 + 2R/R_G. The difference stage that follows sees a signal that has already grown by G₁ against an unchanged common-mode level, so the rejection of the complete amplifier is approximately the front-end gain times the rejection of the difference stage.
EQ: CMRR_total ≈ G₁ · CMRR_diff
WE: Worked Example 20.3
PROB: An instrumentation amplifier uses R = 25 kΩ in each front-end stage and a unity-gain difference stage with 0.1% resistors. Find the R_G that gives a total gain of 100, the resulting CMRR, and the output error from the bridge's 2.5 V common-mode level.
SOL: With the difference stage at unity gain, 1 + 2R/R_G = 100, so R_G = 50 kΩ/99 = 505 Ω; the nearest 0.1% E192 value is 505 Ω. The difference stage alone gives 54.0 dB from Worked Example 20.2, and the front-end gain of 100 adds 40 dB, so CMRR ≈ 50,000, or 94.0 dB. The common-mode gain is 100/50,000 = 0.002 and the output error is 2.5 V × 0.002 = 5 mV. The full-scale output is now 100 × 10 mV = 1 V, so the error is 0.5% of full scale instead of 50%, and it is a fixed offset that a one-time zero calibration removes.
P: Integrated instrumentation amplifiers laser-trim the difference stage, so their datasheets quote CMRR directly, typically 80–90 dB at a gain of one and 110–130 dB at a gain of 100 or more. The rise with gain is the mechanism above. A discrete build from good parts can match this only with resistor networks whose elements are matched to each other, which is why precision designs buy the amplifier rather than build it.
KEY: An instrumentation amplifier rejects common-mode voltage by amplifying the difference before it subtracts. The front end raises the signal against an untouched common-mode level, so every factor of gain placed in the front end multiplies the rejection of the imperfect difference stage that follows.

H2: 20.4 Headroom Inside the Amplifier
P: The front-end outputs carry the common-mode level plus half the amplified difference each, V_cm ± G₁V_d/2. Those internal nodes are invisible on the package, but they clip like any other op-amp output. A part can show a perfectly legal output voltage while one of its internal amplifiers is saturated, and the output is then wrong. Datasheets publish the allowed region as a plot of common-mode voltage against output voltage, usually a hexagon or diamond, and that plot, not the input common-mode specification alone, is the test of a design.
WE: Worked Example 20.4
PROB: A 5 V single-supply instrumentation amplifier with rail-to-rail front-end outputs (allow 0.1 V from each rail) is set to a front-end gain of 100. It senses a 20 mV differential signal at a common-mode level of 4.0 V. Check the internal headroom.
SOL: The front-end outputs are 4.0 ± (100 × 0.020)/2 = 4.0 ± 1.0 V, so one internal node must reach 5.0 V. The allowed maximum is 4.9 V, so that amplifier saturates. Three fixes are possible: lower the front-end gain and take the remaining gain later, bring the common-mode level toward mid-supply, or run the part from a higher supply. With the common-mode level at 2.5 V the nodes span 1.5 to 3.5 V and the design is comfortable.
H2: 20.5 The Reference Pin
P: The difference stage's reference node, brought out as the REF pin, sets the output level that corresponds to zero differential input. On a single supply it is usually driven to mid-supply so that the output can swing both ways. The REF pin is the bottom end of resistor R₄, so any source resistance driving it adds to R₄ and unbalances the bridge of four resistors. Applying the common-mode formula with all resistors equal to R and a source resistance R_s in series with R₄ gives A_cm = R_s/(2R + R_s).
WE: Worked Example 20.5
PROB: A difference stage uses four matched 10 kΩ resistors. The REF pin is driven from a divider whose Thévenin resistance is 10 Ω. Find the CMRR this alone allows.
SOL: A_cm = 10/(20,000 + 10) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴, and with unity differential gain the CMRR is 2,001, or 66.0 dB. Ten ohms in the wrong place undoes a 0.01% resistor network, which would otherwise give about 74 dB. The REF pin must be driven by a low-impedance source, typically a follower whose closed-loop output resistance is milliohms.
MIS: The REF pin is not a ground connection that can be returned anywhere. Its current is the output current of the difference stage divided among the resistors, and any resistance or ground-return voltage between REF and the point the load measures from appears directly in the output.
H2: 20.6 Choosing Between Them
P: Use a difference amplifier when the signal source is low impedance, the common-mode voltage may exceed the supply rails (high-side current sensing, where an integrated difference amplifier with a divider ahead of its inputs tolerates tens of volts), and the required CMRR is modest. Use an instrumentation amplifier when the source is high impedance or unbalanced, the signal is small, or the rejection must exceed about 70 dB. In both, keep the two input paths symmetrical: equal source resistances, equal filter components, and matched layout, because any asymmetry converts common-mode voltage into a differential error before the amplifier can reject it.

H2: 20.8 The Charge Amplifier for Piezoelectric Sensors
P: A piezoelectric sensor (an accelerometer, a knock sensor, a hydrophone, a guitar's under-saddle pickup) produces charge, not voltage: q = S·a, where S is its sensitivity in picocoulombs per unit of input. Read with a voltage amplifier, that charge divides between the sensor's own capacitance and the cable's, so the reading changes whenever the cable is moved or replaced. A charge amplifier removes the dependence. The sensor feeds the inverting input, and the feedback element is a capacitor C_f (Figure 20.3). The virtual ground keeps the sensor and cable at 0 V, so their capacitance never charges, and all of q flows onto C_f: V_out = −q/C_f. A large resistor R_f across C_f gives the op-amp's bias current a path and sets a low-frequency corner f_L = 1/(2πR_fC_f); below it the amplifier forgets, which is why piezo sensors cannot measure a static force.
FIG: fig20_3.png | Figure 20.3 — A charge amplifier. The piezo sensor is a charge source in parallel with its own and the cable's capacitance; the virtual ground keeps that capacitance at 0 V, so V_out = −q/C_f, and R_f sets the low-frequency corner. | Schematic: a piezo sensor modeled as a current source with a parallel capacitance feeds the inverting input of an op-amp, with a 1 nanofarad capacitor and a 1 gigaohm resistor in parallel as feedback.
WE: Worked Example 20.6
PROB: An accelerometer has S = 10 pC/g. The charge amplifier uses C_f = 1 nF and R_f = 1 GΩ. Find the sensitivity in mV/g, the output for a 5 g shock, and the low-frequency corner.
SOL: V/a = S/C_f = 10 pC/1 nF = 10 mV/g, so a 5 g shock gives 50 mV (inverted). f_L = 1/(2π × 10⁹ Ω × 10⁻⁹ F) = 0.159 Hz. Check: a longer cable adds capacitance at the inverting input, which changes the noise gain but not V_out = −q/C_f; with a voltage amplifier, doubling the total capacitance would halve the reading. The op-amp must be a low-bias-current FET-input or CMOS part, because 1 pA through 1 GΩ is already 1 mV of offset.
H2: 20.9 Measuring Mains Current: the Home Energy Dashboard
P: The Home Energy Dashboard of Book 1 reads the current into a house with a clip-on current transformer (CT), which needs no break in the wiring and isolates the mains from the electronics. A CT with N secondary turns delivers I_p/N into a burden resistor R_b, and the burden turns that current into a voltage. A single-supply converter cannot read a negative voltage, so the burden's other end sits at a mid-supply bias of V_DD/2, made by a resistor divider and a capacitor or, better, by the follower of Chapter 12.
WE: Worked Example 20.7
PROB: A 100 A : 50 mA CT (N = 2000) feeds a 3.3 V converter biased at 1.65 V. The largest current to measure is 100 A rms. Choose the burden resistor, and find the primary current per count of a 12-bit converter.
SOL: The peak primary current is 100√2 = 141.4 A, so the peak secondary current is 141.4/2000 = 70.7 mA. The swing must stay within ±1.65 V, so R_b ≤ 1.65/0.0707 = 23.3 Ω; choose 22 Ω, which gives ±1.56 V at 100 A. One count of a 12-bit converter is 3.3/4096 = 0.81 mV, which is 0.81 mV/22 Ω = 36.6 µA of secondary current, or 73 mA of primary current. Check: at 100 A rms the burden dissipates (0.050 A)² × 22 Ω = 55 mW, comfortable for a 0.25 W part. Never unclip the burden while the CT sits on a live conductor: an open secondary can rise to a dangerous voltage.
KEY: Match the front end to what the sensor delivers: a voltage to an instrumentation amplifier, a current to a transimpedance amplifier, a charge to a charge amplifier.
BENCH: Check the internal headroom, not only the input range. In a three-op-amp in-amp, the first-stage outputs carry the common-mode voltage plus the amplified difference, and they can clip while the output looks fine. Many manufacturers publish 'diamond plot' tools for exactly this. And drive the reference pin from a low impedance; a resistor divider there spoils CMRR.
P: On the Job
P: Test and measurement engineers live in this chapter. A production tester, a strain-gauge rig, or a sensor qualification lab is a row of instrumentation amplifiers, references, and converters, and the daily work is chasing a reading that is a few counts wrong to a ground loop, a common-mode problem, or an unmatched filter. At work the instrumentation amplifier is a single chip with laser-trimmed resistors; this chapter is how you know what its CMRR figure will and will not protect you from.
