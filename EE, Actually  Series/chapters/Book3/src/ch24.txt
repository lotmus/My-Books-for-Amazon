H1: Chapter 24. Transistor Datasheets in SPICE: BJT, MOSFET, and JFET
P: A 2N3904 model can reproduce the transistor's gain at 10 mA within a few percent and still be the wrong model for your design, because your design runs the transistor at 20 µA, at 85 °C, or switching in 30 ns.
P: Where you meet this: Discrete transistor circuits, gate-driver and power-stage simulation, thermal design, and every board-level design review that leans on a vendor model.
P: The method of Chapter 23 carries over unchanged: map each parameter to a measurement, fit in an order that keeps them apart, validate on data the fit never saw, and record the range. What changes is the number of parameters and the number of ways a transistor can leave its fitted range.
H2: 24.1 The bipolar transistor: Gummel-Poon from a data sheet
P: SPICE's bipolar model is the Gummel-Poon model of 1970. Its core is the transport of Chapter 8: a collector current Is exp(VBE/VT), a forward gain Bf, and a reverse gain Br. To that it adds what Chapter 9 measured: the Early voltage Vaf; a second, non-ideal base current with saturation current Ise and emission coefficient Ne, which lowers β at low current; and a knee current Ikf beyond which high injection lowers β again. Rb, Re, and Rc add the ohmic resistances; Cje, Cjc, Vje, Vjc, Mje, and Mjc the junction capacitances; Tf the forward transit time that sets fT, with Itf, Vtf, and Xtf shaping its growth at high current; and Tr the reverse transit time that sets the storage time when the transistor saturates. Xtb and Xti carry β and Is over temperature.
P: A widely distributed 2N3904 card reads Is = 6.734 fA, Bf = 416.4, Ne = 1.259, Ise = 6.734 fA, Ikf = 66.78 mA, Vaf = 74.03 V, Br = 0.7371, Rb = 10 Ω, Rc = 1 Ω, Cje = 4.493 pF, Cjc = 3.638 pF, Tf = 301.2 ps, and Tr = 239.5 ns, plus temperature and shaping parameters. A Bf of 416 is not the β you will measure. It is the ceiling that the Ise term pulls down at low current and the Ikf term pulls down at high current. The data sheet's hFE limits are what the card must respect: at VCE = 1 V, a minimum of 40 at 0.1 mA, 70 at 1 mA, 100 to 300 at 10 mA, 60 at 50 mA, and 30 at 100 mA.
H2: Worked Example 24.1
P: Simulate the card's hFE at VCE = 1 V at the five data sheet currents and compare it with the limits. Then find the upper bound on fT that Tf alone sets.
P: ngspice gives about 96, 133, 160, 130, and 100 at 0.1, 1, 10, 50, and 100 mA (Figure 24.1). Every value is inside the limits, and the peak near 10 mA sits where the data sheet places its 100-to-300 window. Tf alone gives fT ≤ 1/(2π × 301.2 ps) = 528 MHz; the junction capacitances, charged through 1/gm, lower it further. The data sheet guarantees at least 300 MHz at 10 mA and 20 V. The card describes a typical part, as a library card should. A design that needs gain must still work with the minimum, 100 at 10 mA: scale Bf down for a worst-case run or, better, bias so that gain does not matter, as Chapter 9 advised. Below about 10 µA, where the data sheet gives no limits at all, Ise and Ne are extrapolating; measure if your circuit lives there.

P: VBIC and HICUM, the successors to Gummel-Poon for RF and SiGe transistors, add self-heating, a parasitic substrate transistor, and a better transit-time model. A foundry's SiGe PDK uses one of them; a discrete small-signal transistor is usually well served by Gummel-Poon inside the data sheet's range.
H2: 24.2 The MOSFET: level 1, and why BSIM exists
P: Chapter 10's square law is SPICE's level 1 MOSFET: a threshold Vto, a transconductance parameter Kp (μCox, multiplied by W/L on the instance line), the channel-length modulation Lambda, and the body-effect pair Gamma and Phi. For a discrete MOSFET with W and L set to 1, Kp is the whole device's K, and three data sheet numbers fix the model.
H2: Worked Example 24.2
P: Fit a level 1 model to the onsemi 2N7000 typical values: VGS(th) = 2.1 V at ID = 1 mA with VDS = VGS; forward transconductance 320 mS at ID = 200 mA; RDS(on) = 1.2 Ω at VGS = 10 V and 0.5 A. Then compare the fitted model with the data sheet's typical 1.8 Ω at VGS = 4.5 V and 75 mA, and its typical on-state current of 600 mA at VGS = 4.5 V and VDS = 10 V.
P: In saturation gm = √(2 K ID), so K = gm²/(2 ID) = 0.32²/0.4 = 0.256 A/V². The threshold criterion puts 1 mA at 2.1 V: 1 mA = (K/2)(2.1 − Vto)², so 2.1 − Vto = √(2×10⁻³/0.256) = 0.088 V and Vto = 2.01 V. The data sheet's threshold is not the model's Vto; it is the gate voltage at which a stated current flows. In the linear region at 10 V the channel alone gives 1/(K(VGS − Vto)) = 1/(0.256 × 7.99) = 0.49 Ω against 1.2 Ω measured, and the remaining 0.71 Ω goes into a drain resistance Rd. With Rd, ngspice reproduces 1.04 mA at 2.1 V and 1.21 Ω at 10 V. At 4.5 V it gives 2.31 Ω against the typical 1.8 Ω, 28 % high, and 0.79 A of on-state current against the typical 0.6 A. Three numbers fixed three parameters exactly; the two the fit did not use show the square law's limits. Real mobility falls as the gate field rises, so a single K cannot match both the low-drive and the high-drive end.
P: That failure, multiplied by every short-channel effect of Chapter 11, is why BSIM exists: mobility degradation, velocity saturation, drain-induced barrier lowering, and dozens more effects, each with parameters fitted across sizes and temperatures. Nobody fits BSIM by hand; it arrives with the PDK. A discrete power MOSFET gets something in between, a subcircuit.
H2: 24.3 The power MOSFET subcircuit
P: A power MOSFET data sheet carries much more than a threshold and an on-resistance, and every line has a test condition that matters. VGS(th) is quoted at a small current, 250 µA for the IRFZ44N, so it says nothing about the gate voltage needed for full current. RDS(on) is quoted at one gate voltage, one drain current, and 25 °C, 17.5 mΩ maximum at 10 V and 25 A for this part, and its curves against VGS and temperature show how fast it grows, often by 1.5 to 2 times from 25 to 150 °C in silicon. The forward transconductance gfs is quoted at a large current, 19 S minimum at 25 A. The capacitances Ciss, Coss, and Crss are measured at VGS = 0 and a stated VDS, here 1470, 360, and 88 pF typical at 25 V and 1 MHz, and all three fall steeply as VDS rises, Crss most of all.
P: The gate-charge curve tells the switching story in one figure (Figure 24.2). With the gate driven by a constant current, VGS first rises while the input capacitance charges, Qgs. At the plateau the drain voltage falls, and the whole gate current goes into the gate-drain capacitance, the Miller effect of Chapter 13 made visible: VGS stays flat while Qgd flows. Then the gate charges on to the drive voltage, completing Qg. The IRFZ44N data sheet gives Qg = 63 nC, Qgs = 14 nC, and Qgd = 23 nC, all maximum values at ID = 25 A, VDS = 44 V, and VGS = 10 V.
P: A level 1 MOSFET cannot hold all of this, so vendors ship a subcircuit: a MOSFET core, often level 1 or level 3, with resistances for the package and the drift region, voltage-dependent capacitors for Cgd and Cds (sometimes diodes used as capacitors), and a separate body diode with its own Is, N, Rs, Tt, and BV. Some models add a thermal node whose voltage is the junction temperature, so that RDS(on) rises as the device heats. LTspice's VDMOS element packs much of this into one model line. Read the subcircuit's header: a good vendor states which curves were fitted, and at which temperature.
H2: Worked Example 24.3
P: A gate driver with a 10 V output drives the IRFZ44N through a total gate resistance of 12 Ω, the data sheet's switching test value. Assume the Miller plateau sits at 5.0 V (an assumed value for this example; read the real one from the data sheet's gate-charge figure). Estimate the time the drain voltage takes to fall, the gate energy per cycle, and the gate-drive power at 100 kHz.
P: On the plateau the gate current is (10 − 5)/12 = 0.42 A, so the drain transition takes Qgd/Ig = 23 nC/0.42 A = 55 ns, in line with the data sheet's typical rise time of 60 ns measured with the same 12 Ω (VDD = 28 V, ID = 25 A). Each cycle the driver delivers Qg × V = 63 nC × 10 V = 0.63 µJ, all of it dissipated in the driver and the gate resistance over one turn-on and one turn-off. At 100 kHz that is 63 mW. Every number used the maximum Qg, so the estimate is conservative; the plateau voltage, which moves with drain current and temperature, is the number to read carefully.

P: The body diode and the safe operating area mark the subcircuit's limits. The body diode's reverse recovery, which matters in every half bridge, is fitted with Tt and sometimes with a separate recovery model; check it against the data sheet's Qrr and trr at the stated di/dt. The safe operating area, the region of current and voltage the device survives for a given pulse length, is not in the model at all. A simulator will run a MOSFET at 50 A and 40 V for a millisecond without complaint; the data sheet's SOA curve, not the simulation, says whether the part survives.
H2: 24.4 Temperature, TNOM, and thermal networks
P: Every model card is fitted at a nominal temperature, TNOM, which is 27 °C by default in SPICE. The simulator scales Is with Eg and Xti, β with Xtb, and resistances with linear and quadratic coefficients when the card provides them. If the vendor fitted at 25 °C and the card does not say so, the 2 K difference is harmless. If the card has no temperature parameters and you simulate at 125 °C, the result is a 25 °C device in a 125 °C simulation.
P: Junction temperature itself comes from a thermal network. The data sheet's transient thermal impedance curve Zth(t) shows how the junction heats after a power step, and vendors fit it with a Foster network, a chain of RC pairs whose responses add, Zth(t) = Σ Ri(1 − exp(−t/τi)) with τi = Ri Ci. A Foster network fits the curve, but its internal nodes have no physical meaning, so it cannot be extended with a heat sink. A Cauer network, a ladder of series resistances and shunt capacitances to ground, has nodes that correspond to physical layers, die, solder, and case, and can be joined to a heat-sink model. Both are ordinary circuits in SPICE, with watts as amperes and kelvin as volts.
EQ: Zth(t) = Σ Ri [1 − exp(−t/τi)],   ΔTj = P × Zth(t)
H2: Worked Example 24.4
P: A Foster fit to a transistor's Zth curve has two pairs, R1 = 0.4 K/W with τ1 = 0.5 ms and R2 = 1.2 K/W with τ2 = 10 ms (example values, 1.6 K/W in total to the case). Find the junction temperature rise above the case for a single 1 ms pulse of 50 W and for a continuous 50 W.
P: Zth(1 ms) = 0.4 × (1 − e⁻²) + 1.2 × (1 − e^(−0.1)) = 0.4 × 0.865 + 1.2 × 0.095 = 0.346 + 0.114 = 0.460 K/W, so ΔT = 50 × 0.460 = 23 K. Continuous: 50 × 1.6 = 80 K. A 1 ms pulse sees less than a third of the steady thermal resistance, which is why the SOA allows far more power in short pulses. The 1.6 K/W total matches the IRFZ44N's rating, 94 W at a 25 °C case with a 175 °C maximum junction, (175 − 25)/94 = 1.6 K/W; the two time constants are example values, not that part's.
H2: 24.5 JFETs and the special diodes
P: SPICE's JFET is a square law with three main parameters: Vto, the pinch-off voltage (negative for an n-channel part), Beta, the transconductance parameter, and Lambda. The saturation current is ID = Beta (VGS − Vto)², so the data sheet's IDSS, the current at VGS = 0, gives Beta = IDSS/Vto². JFET data sheets state IDSS and VGS(off) as wide ranges: for the J310, IDSS from 24 to 60 mA and VGS(off) from −2.0 to −6.5 V. A single card is one point inside that box, and a circuit that depends on IDSS needs a trim or a bias that does not care.
H2: Worked Example 24.5
P: Fit Beta for a J310-like part with IDSS = 40 mA and Vto = −4 V, a point inside the data sheet ranges, and find ID and gm at VGS = −1 V. Then find ID at the same bias for the corner parts IDSS = 24 mA with Vto = −2 V and IDSS = 60 mA with Vto = −6.5 V.
P: Beta = 0.040/16 = 2.5 mA/V². At VGS = −1 V, ID = 2.5×10⁻³ × (−1 + 4)² = 22.5 mA, and gm = 2 Beta (VGS − Vto) = 2 × 2.5×10⁻³ × 3 = 15 mS. The first corner has Beta = 0.024/4 = 6 mA/V² and ID = 6×10⁻³ × 1² = 6 mA; the second has Beta = 0.060/42.25 = 1.42 mA/V² and ID = 1.42×10⁻³ × 5.5² = 43 mA. The same fixed bias gives anywhere from 6 to 43 mA across the data sheet box, which is why JFET bias circuits use source resistors or current sources rather than a fixed gate voltage.
P: The special diodes reuse the level 1 diode card with the emphasis moved. A Zener is a diode whose BV and Ibv carry the design, with the breakdown slope setting the rz of Chapter 6; the temperature coefficient of the Zener voltage needs its own parameter or a subcircuit. An LED is a level 1 diode with a large N and an Is small enough to put its forward voltage near 2 to 3 V; its light output is not in the model, so a behavioral source proportional to current models the optical side. A Schottky diode has a large Is, an N near 1.0 to 1.1, almost no Tt because it stores no minority charge, and a strong temperature dependence of leakage, set by giving Eg the value of the barrier height. The op-amp, which needs a macromodel rather than a device card, is treated in Book 2, Circuits, Components, and Control, Sections 15.7, 15.8, and 16.8.
P: Key idea. Each transistor card is a fit to the data sheet's typical curves at their test conditions. Check it at the data sheet's own points, know which condition each number was measured under, and add from the data sheet what the card cannot carry: worst case, temperature, and the safe operating area.

BENCH: Before a model enters a design review, simulate the data sheet's own test circuits with it: the hFE sweep, the threshold condition, the gate-charge circuit, and the switching test with its stated gate resistance. If the model cannot reproduce the page it came from, nothing it says about your circuit is worth a margin. Then simulate once more at the hot corner with the minimum gain or the maximum on-resistance, because the typical card is the part you will not always get.
P: On the Job
P: A characterization lab is where these models start. Its engineers run source-measure units, curve tracers, capacitance meters, gate-charge and switching testers, and thermal-impedance setups, usually in automated sequences that sweep dozens of parts over temperature overnight and log every point with its test condition. Much of that automation is written in LabVIEW and sequenced with TestStand; Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, covers both in its chapter "LabVIEW and TestStand: Graphical Test for the Characterization Lab", and the instruments in "Source-Measure Units and Parametric Analyzers". The lab's output is the data sheet's typical curves, the minimum and maximum limits from statistics over many lots, and the raw data from which the part's SPICE models are fitted.
