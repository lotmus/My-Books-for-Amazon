TITLE: Global U(1) Symmetry
TAGLINE: The phase symmetry of the complex scalar and Dirac fields, the conserved charge as generator, charge selection rules, several species, and the obstruction to making the symmetry local
NUM: 31
NEXT: Part V turns to quantum field theory. Lesson 32 explains why particles become fields: the limits of single-particle relativistic quantum mechanics, the need for a framework in which particle number can change, and the route from the classical fields of Part IV to quantum operators.

# How to use this lesson

This lesson closes Part IV by studying in detail the one symmetry that quantum electrodynamics is built on: the multiplication of a charged field by a constant phase. Lesson 28 proved that a symmetry gives a conserved current, Lesson 29 organized symmetries into groups, and Lesson 30 computed the energy-momentum and electric currents. Here the phase symmetry is examined on its own terms. Its current and charge are written down for the complex scalar and the Dirac field, the charge is shown to generate the symmetry and to impose a selection rule on every process, the pairing of particles with antiparticles is read off from the signs of the charge, and the freedom to assign separate phase symmetries to separate species is examined.

The lesson then asks what the symmetry does not do. It does not make the field interact with the electromagnetic potential, and it does not survive being made local. The calculation that shows the second point, in Section 5, is the first step of the argument that Part VI completes. Expect seven to nine focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Write the phase transformation of a charged field and verify that the Dirac and complex scalar Lagrangian densities are invariant.

- Compute the Noether current and charge for both fields, with the sign convention that makes the charge of a particle positive.

- Show that the charge generates the phase rotation and derive the commutator [Q, φ] = −qφ.

- Derive the charge selection rule (Q_f − Q_i − q_O)⟨f|O|i⟩ = 0 and apply it to interaction operators.

- Interpret the sign of the charge density of positive- and negative-frequency modes as the charge of particles and antiparticles.

- Explain why several Dirac fields have separate conserved numbers and why QED conserves electron number and muon number separately.

- Compute the change of the Dirac Lagrangian density under a position-dependent phase and identify it with (∂_μα)j^μ.

- Preview how a compensating field cancels this change and produces the interaction −j^μA_μ.

# 1 The transformation and its current

## 1.1 The Dirac field

The Dirac Lagrangian density ℒ = ψ̄(iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ is invariant under the multiplication of the field by a constant phase.

$$ ψ → e^(−iqα) ψ,    ψ̄ → e^(+iqα) ψ̄

Every term in ℒ is a bilinear ψ̄Γψ in which the two phases cancel, and the constant α commutes with the derivative. The sign in the exponent is chosen so that the Noether current comes out with a positive coefficient. For an infinitesimal α = ε, Δψ = −iqψ and Δψ̄ = iqψ̄, and with ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μψ) = iψ̄γ^μ and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μψ̄) = 0 (Lesson 27, Section 3), the current of Lesson 28, Section 2, is

$$ j^μ = (iψ̄γ^μ)(−iqψ) = q ψ̄γ^μψ

This is the current of Lesson 24 multiplied by the charge q. The opposite choice of sign in the exponent, used in Lesson 28, Section 4.3, gives −qψ̄γ^μψ. The overall sign of a Noether current is a convention, and the convention adopted here, and from now on, is that the charge of a particle is positive.

## 1.2 The charge

$$ Q = ∫ d³x j⁰ = q ∫ d³x ψ†ψ

The density j⁰ = qψ†ψ is positive definite (Lesson 24, Section 3), so a state built from particles has Q of one sign, and it is conserved in time by Noether's theorem. Lesson 24's positive probability density and Lesson 30's electric charge are the same object seen from two sides. After quantization, ∫d³x ψ†ψ becomes the number of particles minus the number of antiparticles, so that Q = q(N_particles − N_antiparticles) (Lesson 36).

## 1.3 The complex scalar

For ℒ = ∂_μφ*∂^μφ − m²φ*φ the same transformation φ → e^(−iqα)φ, φ* → e^(iqα)φ* has Δφ = −iqφ and Δφ* = iqφ*. With ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ* and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ*) = ∂^μφ,

$$ j^μ = ∂^μφ*(−iqφ) + ∂^μφ(iqφ*) = iq (φ*∂^μφ − φ∂^μφ*)

which is q times the Klein–Gordon current of Lesson 18, Section 3.1, with the sign that was chosen there. Its charge density is ρ = iq(φ*φ̇ − φφ̇*), and Q = ∫d³x ρ.

### Worked example 1

Compute the charge density of plane waves. For the positive-frequency wave φ = Ne^(−iEt+ip·x), φ̇ = −iEφ and φ̇* = iEφ*, so φ*φ̇ − φφ̇* = −iE|N|² − iE|N|² = −2iE|N|², and ρ = iq(−2iE|N|²) = 2qE|N|², positive for q > 0. For the negative-frequency wave φ = Ne^(+iEt+ip·x), the signs of φ̇ and φ̇* reverse and ρ = −2qE|N|². The two signs are exactly those found in Lesson 18, Worked example 3, and it is no longer a problem: positive-frequency modes carry charge +q and negative-frequency modes carry charge −q. The sign that spoiled the probability interpretation of the Klein–Gordon equation is the sign of the charge of an antiparticle.

# 2 The charge as generator

## 2.1 The commutator with the field

By Lesson 28, Section 5.2, the Noether charge generates the symmetry through the Poisson bracket, {φ, Q} = Δφ = −iqφ. Under canonical quantization the bracket becomes a commutator, [f, g] = i{f, g} with ℏ = 1 (Lesson 6, Section 7.1), so [φ, Q] = i(−iqφ) = qφ, that is

$$ [Q, φ] = −q φ

The field φ lowers the charge by q: acting on a state of definite charge, φ produces a state whose charge is smaller by q, either by removing a particle of charge q or by adding an antiparticle of charge −q. For the Dirac field the same relation holds, and it can be checked directly from the anticommutation relations (Exercise 4). The conjugate field φ* raises the charge by q.

## 2.2 The unitary operator

Define U(α) = e^(iαQ). Differentiating UφU⁻¹ with respect to α gives iU[Q, φ]U⁻¹ = −iq UφU⁻¹, whose solution with the initial condition U(0) = 1 is

$$ U(α) φ U(α)⁻¹ = e^(−iqα) φ

The operator e^(iαQ) implements the phase rotation on the quantum field, which confirms that Q is the generator of the symmetry in the sense of Lesson 29, Section 1.2, and that the sign convention of Section 1.1 is the one that makes U = e^(iαQ) act as ψ → e^(−iqα)ψ. Because Q commutes with the Hamiltonian, so does U, and the symmetry is respected by time evolution.

## 2.3 The selection rule

Let O be an operator with [Q, O] = q_O O, so that O changes the charge by q_O. Take its matrix element between states of definite charge, Q|i⟩ = Q_i|i⟩ and ⟨f|Q = Q_f⟨f|. Then

$$ ⟨f|[Q, O]|i⟩ = (Q_f − Q_i)⟨f|O|i⟩ = q_O ⟨f|O|i⟩

so that (Q_f − Q_i − q_O)⟨f|O|i⟩ = 0. The matrix element vanishes unless the charge of the final state equals the charge of the initial state plus the charge carried by the operator. A neutral operator, q_O = 0, can only connect states of the same charge. Every interaction term of a charge-conserving Lagrangian density is neutral (Lesson 29, Section 5), so every process it drives conserves charge. This is the quantum statement of charge conservation at each vertex of a Feynman diagram.

### Worked example 2

Apply the rule to two operators. The operator ψ̄γ^μψ has [Q, ψ̄γ^μψ] = 0, since ψ̄ carries charge +q and ψ carries −q, so it connects only states of equal charge: an electron can scatter to an electron but not to a positron. The operator ψ alone has q_O = −q and can connect a state of charge Q to one of charge Q − q, for instance the vacuum to a state containing a single antiparticle, or a one-electron state to the vacuum. That ψ can change the charge is why it cannot appear alone in the Lagrangian density.

# 3 Particles and antiparticles

## 3.1 Charge conjugation

For the complex scalar, charge conjugation exchanges φ and φ*, and the Lagrangian density ∂_μφ*∂^μφ − m²φ*φ is unchanged by the exchange. The current changes sign: iq(φ*∂^μφ − φ∂^μφ*) → iq(φ∂^μφ* − φ*∂^μφ) = −j^μ (Exercise 6). So charge conjugation maps a state of charge Q to one of charge −Q, and the antiparticle of a particle of charge q has charge −q. For the Dirac field the same result holds after quantization, where the anticommuting character of the field operators supplies the sign (Lesson 37). Charge conjugation is a symmetry of QED, as stated in Lesson 23, Section 5.2.

## 3.2 Pair creation

A neutral operator can create a particle only together with an antiparticle, so that the net charge is unchanged. The photon has no charge, so the process γ → e⁻ + e⁺, allowed by the selection rule, has initial charge 0 and final charge −e + e = 0, while γ → e⁻ + e⁻ would change the charge by −2e and is forbidden (Exercise 13). This is the systematic form of the argument of Lesson 23, Section 4.2, and of Lesson 23, Exercise 15.

## 3.3 What the symmetry does not fix

The symmetry holds for any value of q. Nothing in the Lagrangian density relates the charge of one field to the charge of another. That the charges of the electron and of the proton are equal in magnitude, so that atoms are neutral to better than one part in 10²⁰, is a striking fact that the U(1) symmetry of QED does not explain. It is an input to the theory, not an output (Exercise 15).

# 4 Several species

## 4.1 One symmetry for each field

Suppose the theory contains two Dirac fields, an electron field ψ_e and a muon field ψ_μ, with

$$ ℒ = ψ̄_e(iγ^μ∂_μ − m_e)ψ_e + ψ̄_μ(iγ^μ∂_μ − m_μ)ψ_μ

The Lagrangian density is invariant under ψ_e → e^(−iα_e)ψ_e and ψ_μ → e^(−iα_μ)ψ_μ with independent constants α_e and α_μ, since each field appears only in its own bilinears. There are therefore two independent U(1) symmetries, with conserved charges N_e = ∫ψ_e†ψ_e d³x and N_μ = ∫ψ_μ†ψ_μ d³x: the number of electrons minus positrons and the number of muons minus antimuons. The group of the theory is U(1) × U(1), of dimension two, and by Lesson 29, Section 2, it has two conserved charges. The electric charge is the particular combination Q = −e(N_e + N_μ), obtained when both fields have charge −e.

## 4.2 Consequences for QED

The QED interaction, in which each species couples to the photon through its own current, −q_fψ̄_fγ^μψ_fA_μ, mixes no fields, so the separate conservation of N_e and N_μ persists once the photon is included. A decay such as μ⁻ → e⁻ + γ would change N_μ from 1 to 0 and N_e from 0 to 1, violating each separately while conserving electric charge. It cannot arise from the QED Lagrangian density, and no such decay has been observed at branching fractions above about 10⁻¹². The processes that do occur, such as e⁺e⁻ → μ⁺μ⁻ (Lesson 56) and eμ → eμ scattering (Lesson 54), each conserve N_e and N_μ separately.

### Worked example 3

Check that e⁺e⁻ → μ⁺μ⁻ respects every charge. In the initial state N_e = (+1) + (−1) = 0, since the electron counts +1 and the positron −1, and N_μ = 0. In the final state N_e = 0 and N_μ = (−1) + (+1) = 0, taking the μ⁻ as the particle of the muon field and the μ⁺ as its antiparticle. The electric charge is (−e) + (+e) = 0 before and after. The process is allowed, but only because the two pairs are created and destroyed as pairs; the interaction converts an electron-positron pair into a muon-antimuon pair through a virtual photon that carries no charge.

# 5 What the global symmetry does not do

## 5.1 No interaction

A free Dirac field with a conserved charge does not interact with anything. The phase symmetry by itself makes no statement about the electromagnetic field, and a theory of noninteracting electrons has the same U(1) symmetry as QED. Symmetry constrains the form of interactions but does not by itself create them. What creates them in this course is a stronger requirement, that the symmetry be local.

## 5.2 The local phase

Let the parameter depend on position, ψ → e^(−iqα(x))ψ and ψ̄ → e^(iqα(x))ψ̄. The mass term ψ̄ψ is unchanged, but the derivative acts on the phase.

$$ ∂_μ(e^(−iqα)ψ) = e^(−iqα)(∂_μψ − iq(∂_μα)ψ)

so that ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ → ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ + q(∂_μα)ψ̄γ^μψ, and to first order in α the Lagrangian density changes by

$$ δℒ = (∂_μα) j^μ

with j^μ = qψ̄γ^μψ. This is the general result of Lesson 28, Section 3.1, δS = ∫(∂_με)j^μ, verified explicitly for the Dirac field. The Dirac Lagrangian density is not invariant under a local phase rotation, and the failure is proportional to the gradient of α and to the very current that the global symmetry conserves.

## 5.3 The cure, previewed

The change can be cancelled by an interaction with a vector field. Add to ℒ the term −j^μA_μ = −qψ̄γ^μψA_μ, the coupling of Lesson 26, Section 6.3, and let A_μ transform as A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μα when the phase of ψ rotates. Then the interaction changes by −j^μ∂_μα, which cancels δℒ exactly. Combining the two terms, the invariant Lagrangian density is ψ̄iγ^μD_μψ − mψ̄ψ with

$$ D_μ = ∂_μ + iqA_μ,    D_μψ → e^(−iqα) D_μψ

as Exercise 10 verifies. The transformation A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μα is the gauge transformation of the electromagnetic potential, written in the sign convention of Lesson 8 (with χ = −α). Requiring the phase symmetry to be local therefore forces the existence of the electromagnetic field, with exactly the coupling that the classical charged particle has. Lessons 39 through 41 carry out this argument carefully, and Lesson 42 assembles the result.

## 5.4 Why local is natural

A global symmetry says that a phase convention for ψ must be chosen the same way at every point of the universe. In a relativistic theory, in which simultaneity is relative and no influence travels faster than light (Lesson 3), a convention that must be coordinated instantaneously across space is not natural. A local symmetry says that the phase may be chosen independently at each point; the price is a field that carries the information about how phases at neighboring points are related, which is what A_μ does.

### Worked example 4

Check the cancellation in the simplest case. Take α small and A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μα. The interaction term −qψ̄γ^μψA_μ changes by −qψ̄γ^μψ∂_μα = −(∂_μα)j^μ. The kinetic term changes by +(∂_μα)j^μ from Section 5.2. The sum is zero, and the full Lagrangian density ψ̄(iγ^μD_μ − m)ψ is invariant under the local transformation, to first order in α and in fact exactly, as Exercise 10 shows. The interaction term appears not as an additional postulate but as the unique change needed to preserve a symmetry.

# 6 What Part IV has established

Lesson 25 introduced the field as a system with infinitely many degrees of freedom and showed that a free field is a set of independent oscillators. Lesson 26 constructed the Lagrangian densities of the scalar, Dirac, and electromagnetic fields from the requirements of Lorentz invariance and locality. Lesson 27 derived their equations of motion, which are the Klein–Gordon equation, the Dirac equation, and the Maxwell equations, and solved the sourced scalar field to show how mass sets the range of a force. Lesson 28 proved that continuous symmetries yield conserved currents, Lesson 29 organized the symmetries of relativistic theories, and Lesson 30 computed the conserved currents: energy, momentum, angular momentum, and charge. This lesson has examined the U(1) phase symmetry and shown that its localization forces the electromagnetic interaction.

What remains open is that all of this is classical. The fields are ordinary functions, the oscillators of Lesson 25 have continuous energies, and nothing yet explains why the excitations of a field come in discrete units with definite energy and momentum, or how particles can be created and destroyed. Part V answers these questions by quantizing the fields, using the canonical machinery of Lessons 6 and 11 applied to each oscillator.

# 8 Summary

1. The phase transformation ψ → e^(−iqα)ψ leaves the Dirac and complex scalar Lagrangian densities invariant for constant α.

2. The Noether current is j^μ = qψ̄γ^μψ for the Dirac field and iq(φ*∂^μφ − φ∂^μφ*) for the complex scalar; the charge is Q = ∫d³x j⁰.

3. The charge generates the symmetry: [Q, φ] = −qφ and U(α) = e^(iαQ) implements φ → e^(−iqα)φ.

4. Charge conservation is the selection rule (Q_f − Q_i − q_O)⟨f|O|i⟩ = 0, and interaction terms are neutral.

5. Positive-frequency modes carry charge +q and negative-frequency modes charge −q, which is the charge of the antiparticle.

6. Each Dirac field has its own phase symmetry, so QED conserves electron number and muon number separately.

7. The symmetry allows any value of q and does not, by itself, produce any interaction.

8. Under a local phase, δℒ = (∂_μα)j^μ; adding −j^μA_μ with A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μα cancels it, giving the covariant derivative D_μ = ∂_μ + iqA_μ.

# Solutions

**1.** For constant α, ψ̄ψ → e^(iqα)e^(−iqα)ψ̄ψ = ψ̄ψ, and ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ → e^(iqα)e^(−iqα)ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ, so ℒ is invariant. For α(x), ∂_μ(e^(−iqα)ψ) = e^(−iqα)(∂_μψ − iq(∂_μα)ψ), so ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ → ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ + ψ̄iγ^μ(−iq∂_μαψ) = ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ + q(∂_μα)ψ̄γ^μψ. The mass term is unchanged, so δℒ = q(∂_μα)ψ̄γ^μψ.

**2.** ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ* and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ*) = ∂^μφ. With Δφ = −iqφ and Δφ* = iqφ*, j^μ = ∂^μφ*(−iqφ) + ∂^μφ(iqφ*) = iq(φ*∂^μφ − φ∂^μφ*). For φ = Ne^(−iEt+ip·x), ρ = j⁰ = iq(φ*φ̇ − φφ̇*) = iq(−iE − iE)|N|² = 2qE|N|². For φ = Ne^(iEt+ip·x), φ̇ = iEφ, and ρ = iq(iE + iE)|N|² = −2qE|N|².

**3.** From Lesson 26, Exercise 6, with α replaced by −qα (small), ∂_μφ*∂^μφ → ∂_μφ*∂^μφ + i∂^μ(−qα)(φ∂_μφ* − φ*∂_μφ) + O(α²) = ∂_μφ*∂^μφ + iq∂^μα(φ*∂_μφ − φ∂_μφ*) + O(α²). The mass term is unchanged. So δℒ = ∂_μα · iq(φ*∂^μφ − φ∂^μφ*) = (∂_μα)j^μ, with j^μ the current of Solution 2.

**4.** Using [AB, C] = A{B, C} − {A, C}B with A = ψ_b†(y), B = ψ_b(y), C = ψ_a(x): [ψ_b†(y)ψ_b(y), ψ_a(x)] = ψ_b†(y){ψ_b(y), ψ_a(x)} − {ψ_b†(y), ψ_a(x)}ψ_b(y) = 0 − δ_(ab)δ³(x − y)ψ_b(y). Summing over b and integrating over y, [Q, ψ_a(x)] = −qψ_a(x). Let f(α) = e^(iαQ)ψe^(−iαQ). Then df/dα = ie^(iαQ)[Q, ψ]e^(−iαQ) = −iqf, with f(0) = ψ, so f = e^(−iqα)ψ.

**5.** For states of definite charge, ⟨f|[Q, O]|i⟩ = ⟨f|QO − OQ|i⟩ = (Q_f − Q_i)⟨f|O|i⟩. Since [Q, O] = q_OO, this equals q_O⟨f|O|i⟩, and (Q_f − Q_i − q_O)⟨f|O|i⟩ = 0. For O = ψ̄γ^μψ, [Q, O] = 0 because ψ̄ has charge +q and ψ has charge −q, so q_O = 0 and Q_f = Q_i. For O = ψ, [Q, ψ] = −qψ gives q_O = −q, so Q_f = Q_i − q.

**6.** Under φ ↔ φ*, ∂_μφ*∂^μφ → ∂_μφ∂^μφ* = ∂_μφ*∂^μφ and φ*φ → φφ* = φ*φ, so ℒ is invariant. The current iq(φ*∂^μφ − φ∂^μφ*) becomes iq(φ∂^μφ* − φ*∂^μφ) = −iq(φ*∂^μφ − φ∂^μφ*) = −j^μ.

**7.** Each field has a symmetry ψ_f → e^(−iα_f)ψ_f with Noether charge N_f = ∫d³x ψ_f†ψ_f, that is N_e and N_μ. The electric charge is Q = −e(N_e + N_μ) when both fields have charge −e. In μ⁻ → e⁻γ, the initial state has N_μ = 1 and N_e = 0, and the final state has N_μ = 0 and N_e = 1, so both N_e and N_μ change by one unit and neither is conserved separately. The electric charge is −e before and after, and N_e + N_μ = 1 before and after, so those are conserved. The QED vertex ψ̄_fγ^μψ_fA_μ has the same species on both fermion legs, so it cannot change N_e or N_μ.

**8.** The term φψ̄₁ψ₂ has total charge q_φ − q₁ + q₂, since ψ̄₁ carries −q₁ and ψ₂ carries +q₂ (ψ̄ carries the opposite charge of ψ). Setting this to zero, q_φ = q₁ − q₂ = 0 − (−1) = +1, so φ carries charge +e.

**9.** From i∂_tψ = (−iα·∇ + βm)ψ, ∂_tψ = −α·∇ψ − iβmψ. The Hermitian conjugate, using α and β Hermitian, is ∂_tψ† = −∇ψ†·α + iψ†βm. Then ∂_t(ψ†ψ) = (∂_tψ†)ψ + ψ†∂_tψ = −∇ψ†·αψ + imψ†βψ − ψ†α·∇ψ − imψ†βψ = −(∇ψ†·αψ + ψ†α·∇ψ) = −∇·(ψ†αψ). Hence ∂_t(ψ†ψ) + ∇·(ψ†αψ) = 0, and ψ†αψ = ψ̄γψ is the spatial current of Lesson 24.

**10.** D_μψ → (∂_μ + iq(A_μ + ∂_μα))(e^(−iqα)ψ) = e^(−iqα)[∂_μψ − iq(∂_μα)ψ + iqA_μψ + iq(∂_μα)ψ] = e^(−iqα)(∂_μ + iqA_μ)ψ, the terms in ∂_μα cancelling. Since ψ̄ → e^(iqα)ψ̄, the combination ψ̄iγ^μD_μψ → ψ̄iγ^μD_μψ is invariant, and so is the mass term. Expanding, ψ̄iγ^μ(∂_μ + iqA_μ)ψ = ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ − qψ̄γ^μψA_μ, so the interaction is −qψ̄γ^μψA_μ = −j^μA_μ.

**11.** All observables are built from bilinears such as ψ̄Γψ in which ψ and ψ̄ appear together, so a constant phase cancels and no measurement can detect it. A position-dependent phase does not cancel in the derivative, because ∂_μ(e^(−iqα)ψ) contains the extra term −iq(∂_μα)ψ, which acts like a momentum shift proportional to the gradient of the phase. Expectation values that involve derivatives, such as the momentum density or the kinetic term of ℒ, therefore change by a term proportional to ∂_μα, and physics is altered unless a compensating field is included.

**12.** The photon field A_μ enters ℒ = −(1/4)F_μνF^(μν) only through F_μν and carries no phase that rotates under the U(1) of the Dirac field, so [Q, A_μ] = 0 and the photon is neutral; equivalently, no Noether current is built from A_μ. The free Lagrangian density is quadratic in A_μ, so the equation of motion ∂_μF^(μν) = 0 is linear and photon waves superpose without scattering. Interactions between photons arise only indirectly, through charged particles, in the loop diagrams of Lesson 62.

**13.** γ → e⁻e⁺: initial charge 0, final charge −e + e = 0, allowed. γ → e⁻e⁻: initial 0, final −2e, forbidden. e⁻ → e⁻γ: initial −e, final −e + 0 = −e, allowed by charge conservation (it is forbidden for a free electron by energy-momentum conservation, but it occurs as a virtual process inside larger diagrams).

**14.** With [ψ] = 3/2 (Lesson 26, Section 7), ψ̄γ^μψ has dimension 3/2 + 3/2 = 3, and q is dimensionless, so [j^μ] = 3. The volume element d³x has dimension −3, so Q = ∫d³x j⁰ has dimension 3 − 3 = 0, dimensionless.

**15.** The U(1) symmetry is present for any value of q for each field, so nothing in the Lagrangian density relates the charge of the electron field to that of a proton field, which is not even a field of the theory. Yet measurements of the neutrality of atoms and bulk matter show that the charge of the proton and the charge of the electron are equal in magnitude to better than one part in 10²⁰. The symmetry says charge is conserved, not why charges are what they are.

**16.** Write φ = Σ_k(e^(ik·x)/√V)c_k(t) with c_k(t) = (2ω_k)^(−1/2)(a_ke^(−iω_kt) + b₋ₖ*e^(iω_kt)), obtained by collecting the terms with the same spatial dependence, since the b* term at −k has the same e^(ik·x) as the a term at k. Orthogonality of the e^(ik·x) gives Q = iqΣ_k(c_k*ċ_k − c_kċ_k*). With c_k* = (2ω)^(−1/2)(a_k*e^(iωt) + b₋ₖe^(−iωt)) and ċ_k = (2ω)^(−1/2)(−iωa_ke^(−iωt) + iωb₋ₖ*e^(iωt)), c_k*ċ_k = (i/2)(|b₋ₖ|² − |a_k|²) − Im(a_k*b₋ₖ*e^(2iωt)). The second term is real, so c_k*ċ_k − c_kċ_k* = 2i Im(c_k*ċ_k) = i(|b₋ₖ|² − |a_k|²). Then Q = iq Σ_k i(|b₋ₖ|² − |a_k|²) = qΣ_k(|a_k|² − |b₋ₖ|²), and relabeling k → −k in the b sum gives Q = qΣ_k(|a_k|² − |b_k|²).
