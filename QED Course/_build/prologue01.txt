# How to use this prologue

This opening follows the physics of Richard Feynman's 1985 lectures QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter, rewritten in this course's language and without copying that book. No Dirac matrices yet. No Lagrangian. The objects are photons, detectors that click, and arrows whose squared lengths are probabilities. Lesson 1 will name those arrows complex numbers. Expect four to six hours.

## Learning objectives

- State that light is detected as individual photons, each producing at most one click in an ideal detector.

- Distinguish a probability (a real number between 0 and 1) from an amplitude (an arrow in a plane).

- Compute a probability as the square of an arrow's length.

- Combine alternative ways an event can happen by adding arrows, then squaring.

- Identify the rotating of an arrow with the passage of time, at a rate set by the color of the light.

# 1 Light arrives in lumps

A dim source, a photomultiplier, a click. Turn the source down and the clicks do not get quieter; they get rarer. Each click is the same size. That is the experimental fact this course starts from: light is detected in identical lumps, photons. Maxwell's wave picture of Lesson 7, which Prologue 5 will put beside this one, still describes the average of many clicks. It does not describe the click.

### Worked example 1

If a detector covering a screen records 100 clicks in a run, and a small patch of the screen recorded 4 of them, what probability does the theory have to explain? P=4/100=0.04 for a photon that has left the source to be found in that patch. The theory's job is to compute 0.04, not to tell a story about a tiny billiard ball bouncing into the patch.

# 2 Arrows, not percentages, are added

When an event can happen in more than one way, you do not add the probabilities of the ways. You draw an arrow for each way, add the arrows tip-to-tail, and square the length of the resultant. Only that last square is a probability.

FIG:
  way A:  -->           resultant:  -->-->  (if A and B point the same way)
  way B:  -->           P = (2 × length)²  if the two arrows are equal and aligned
                        P = 0              if they are equal and opposite
ENDFIG

Two equal arrows that point the same way double the length and quadruple the probability. Two equal arrows that point opposite ways cancel, and the event does not happen. That is interference, and it is why this course will spend Lesson 1 on complex numbers: an arrow in a plane is a complex number.

## 2.1 Where the direction comes from

For a photon, the arrow associated with a path rotates as time along that path passes. Blue light rotates faster than red. Two paths that take different times arrive with arrows pointing different ways, even if each path, taken alone, would have been perfectly ordinary.

### Worked example 2

A way with arrow length 0.2 has probability 0.04 if it is the only way. A second, independent way of the same length, pointing the same direction, makes a resultant of length 0.4 and P=0.16. The same two ways pointing opposite give P=0. That 0-to-16% range, for two 4% ways, is Prologue 2's glass.

# 3 What this opening is not claiming

The arrows are not little pointers living in space next to the photon. They are the theory's bookkeeping for "how much this way contributes." Lesson 1 will call the length r and the direction e^{iθ}. The path integral of Lesson 79 is the same idea written as an integral over field histories. Nothing in the later algebra replaces the rule: add first, square last.

# Summary

1. Light is detected as photons.

2. A probability is the square of an arrow's length.

3. Alternative ways add as arrows, not as probabilities.

4. An arrow's direction records time along a path, at a rate set by color.

5. Maxwell's wave language is postponed to Prologue 5 as an alternative for many photons, not as a replacement for the click.

# Solutions

**1.** Each photon is identical; intensity is a rate of arrivals.

**2.** (1/2)²=1/4.

**3.** Aligned: length 1, P=1. Opposite: length 0, P=0.

**4.** Probabilities of alternatives are not additive when the alternatives are not distinguished by the experiment. Amplitudes are.

**5.** The color (frequency) of the light.

**6.** Length and direction in a plane are the modulus and argument of a complex number.

**7.** When so many photons arrive that only averages matter: radio waves, sunlight on a solar cell's power output, Lesson 7's energy density.

**8.** They form a closed triangle; resultant length 0; P=0.
