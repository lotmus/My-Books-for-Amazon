# How to use this lesson

Lesson 32 introduced the ladder operators a_p and a_p† as the coefficients of the mode expansion and derived what was needed from their commutation relations: the Hamiltonian, the momentum, and the one-particle states. This lesson studies the operators in their own right. It proves the properties of the number operator and the ladder action for any number of modes, sets out the relation between the continuum normalization used in field theory and the discrete normalization of a box, shows how ordinary wave packets arise as superpositions of one-particle states, and introduces coherent states, the quantum states whose expectation values reproduce the classical fields of Part IV.

The last section examines what the operators do inside an interaction term. A product such as a_p†a_q†a_r annihilates one particle and creates two; each monomial in the interaction Hamiltonian is a definite process, and reading the interaction this way is the first step toward the Feynman diagrams of Part VII. Expect seven to nine focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Prove that the eigenvalues of the number operator are the nonnegative integers and derive the action of the ladder operators on the states |n⟩.

- Construct the occupation number states for several modes and count them.

- Relate the box normalization of states to the relativistic normalization and identify N as the total number of quanta.

- Write a wave-packet state as a superposition of one-particle states and compute its norm, energy, and momentum.

- Define coherent states, compute their number distribution, and show that the expectation value of the field in a coherent state is a classical solution.

- Derive the time evolution of a coherent state under the free Hamiltonian.

- Show that the number operator generates a phase rotation of the ladder operators and identify it with the U(1) charge.

- Read the terms of a cubic interaction as processes that change the number of particles, and decide which can conserve energy and momentum.

# 1 The algebra of one mode

## 1.1 The number operator

For a single mode, with [a, a†] = 1 as in Lesson 11, Section 4.1, define N = a†a. The commutators follow from the algebra.

$$ [N, a] = −a,    [N, a†] = a†

Suppose |n⟩ is a normalized eigenstate, N|n⟩ = n|n⟩. Then N(a|n⟩) = (n − 1)(a|n⟩) and N(a†|n⟩) = (n + 1)(a†|n⟩): the operator a lowers the eigenvalue by one and a† raises it by one. The norms are fixed by the algebra.

$$ ‖a|n⟩‖² = ⟨n|a†a|n⟩ = n,    ‖a†|n⟩‖² = ⟨n|aa†|n⟩ = ⟨n|(N + 1)|n⟩ = n + 1

The first norm must be nonnegative, so n ≥ 0. If n is not an integer, repeated application of a would produce a state with negative eigenvalue and negative norm, a contradiction; so the sequence must terminate, which requires it to reach exactly zero, and n must be a nonnegative integer. The lowest state |0⟩ has a|0⟩ = 0 and is the vacuum, and the higher states follow from it.

$$ |n⟩ = (a†)ⁿ |0⟩ / √(n!),    a|n⟩ = √n |n − 1⟩,    a†|n⟩ = √(n + 1) |n + 1⟩

The normalization of |n⟩ follows by induction (Exercise 2): ‖(a†)ⁿ|0⟩‖² = n‖(a†)ⁿ⁻¹|0⟩‖², so it equals n!.

### Worked example 1

Verify the norm of the two-quantum state. |2⟩ = (a†)²|0⟩/√2, and ⟨2|2⟩ = (1/2)⟨0|a²(a†)²|0⟩. Using a(a†)²|0⟩ = ([a, (a†)²] + (a†)²a)|0⟩ = 2a†|0⟩ (since [a, (a†)²] = 2a† and a|0⟩ = 0), then a(2a†|0⟩) = 2|0⟩, so ⟨0|a²(a†)²|0⟩ = 2 and ⟨2|2⟩ = (1/2)(2) = 1, as required.

## 1.2 Matrix elements

The ladder relations give all the matrix elements needed. In particular ⟨n|a†a|n⟩ = n, ⟨n|aa†|n⟩ = n + 1, and ⟨n|(a + a†)²|n⟩ = ⟨n|(a² + a†² + aa† + a†a)|n⟩ = 2n + 1, since a² and a†² change n by two and have vanishing diagonal elements. The last is the quantity used for the position spread of the oscillator in Lesson 11, Worked example 4, and it is the origin of the vacuum fluctuation of the field at n = 0, where the value is 1 rather than 0 (Exercise 15).

# 2 Many modes

## 2.1 Independent oscillators

For a field in a box, each allowed momentum k has its own ladder operators, and operators for different modes commute.

$$ [a_k, a_k′†] = δ_(kk′),    [a_k, a_k′] = [a_k†, a_k′†] = 0

A state of the field is specified by the occupation numbers n_k of all modes, and is built from the vacuum.

$$ |{n_k}⟩ = Π_k [ (a_k†)^(n_k) / √(n_k!) ] |0⟩

with the vacuum annihilated by every a_k. Because the operators for different modes commute, the order of the factors does not matter, and the state is symmetric under the interchange of quanta: the quanta are bosons. The number operators N_k = a_k†a_k commute with each other, so the occupation numbers can all be specified at once, and a one-body operator that is a sum over modes, such as H = Σ_kω_kN_k or P = Σ_kkN_k, acts diagonally.

## 2.2 Counting states

A state with N quanta distributed among M modes is specified by the occupation numbers n₁, …, n_M with Σn_k = N. The number of such states is the number of ways to place N indistinguishable quanta into M boxes, C(N + M − 1, N) (Exercise 4). For N = 2 and M = 3 this gives C(4, 2) = 6 states: three with both quanta in one mode and three with one quantum in each of two different modes. The count is the counting of the symmetric states of two bosons in a three-dimensional single-particle space, in agreement with Lesson 16.

# 3 Normalizations

## 3.1 Box and continuum

The continuum operators of Lesson 32 and the box operators are related by a_p = √V a_k, with ∫d³p/(2π)³ → (1/V)Σ_k and (2π)³δ³(p − q) → Vδ_(kk′) (Lesson 32, Exercise 16). The box state |k⟩_box = a_k†|0⟩ has unit norm, while the relativistic state of Lesson 32, Section 4.3, is

$$ |p⟩ = √(2E_p) a_p†|0⟩ = √(2E_pV) |k⟩_box,    ⟨p|p⟩ = 2E_p V

so the relativistic normalization corresponds to 2E_p particles per unit volume, matching the Dirac spinors of Lesson 19, Section 7, normalized to u†u = 2E_p. The choice between the two is a matter of convenience: unit normalization in a box is best for counting states, and relativistic normalization is best for Lorentz-covariant calculations.

## 3.2 The total number

The number operator of the field is the sum over modes.

$$ N = ∫ d³p/(2π)³ a_p†a_p

It commutes with H and P, so the number of quanta is conserved in the free theory, and [N, a_p†] = a_p†, so a_p† raises N by one. On a one-particle state N|p⟩ = |p⟩. A state built from n creation operators has N = n regardless of the momenta.

# 4 Wave-packet states

## 4.1 The state

A one-particle state with a definite wave function in momentum space is a superposition of the momentum eigenstates.

$$ |ψ⟩ = ∫ d³p/(2π)³ ψ̃(p) a_p†|0⟩

Its norm is ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = ∫∫ψ̃*(q)ψ̃(p)⟨0|a_qa_p†|0⟩ = ∫d³p/(2π)³|ψ̃(p)|², so the state is normalized when the momentum-space function is normalized in the sense of Lesson 12, Section 1.2. The number operator gives N|ψ⟩ = |ψ⟩, and

$$ ⟨H⟩ = ∫ d³p/(2π)³ E_p |ψ̃(p)|²,    ⟨P⟩ = ∫ d³p/(2π)³ p |ψ̃(p)|²

exactly the expectation values for a particle whose momentum distribution is |ψ̃|², with the relativistic energy E_p (Exercise 5). If ψ̃ is sharply peaked at p₀, the state describes a particle moving with velocity p₀/E_{p₀}, a wave packet of the type constructed in Lesson 11, Section 3.

## 4.2 The wave function of the state

The field operator gives the position-space function of the state, ⟨0|φ(x)|ψ⟩ = ∫d³p/((2π)³√(2E_p)) ψ̃(p) e^(−ip·x), obtained directly from ⟨0|φ(x)a_p†|0⟩ = e^(−ip·x)/√(2E_p), which follows from ⟨0|a_qa_p†|0⟩ = (2π)³δ³(q − p). It satisfies the Klein–Gordon equation, since each plane wave does. It should not be read as a probability amplitude for finding the particle at x. Its square is not the probability density that integrates to one, because the normalization of the state involves ∫d³p|ψ̃|² without the factor 1/(2E_p) that appears here, and, as Lesson 31 showed, a relativistic particle cannot be localized more sharply than its Compton wavelength. Position measurements in field theory are measurements of the field, not of a position operator, and their interpretation requires the analysis of Part VII.

### Worked example 2

Compute the norm and energy of a state with a sharply peaked wave packet, ψ̃(p) proportional to a narrow Gaussian centered at p₀ with width Δp ≪ |p₀|, normalized so that ∫d³p/(2π)³|ψ̃|² = 1. The norm is 1 by construction. The energy is ⟨H⟩ = ∫d³p/(2π)³E_p|ψ̃|² ≈ E_{p₀} + (1/2)(∂²E/∂p²)(Δp)² + ⋯, so to leading order the energy is that of a particle of momentum p₀, with a correction from the spread that is the relativistic version of the spreading of Lesson 11, Section 3.2. The expectation of the number operator is exactly 1: the packet contains one particle however it is shaped.

# 5 Coherent states

## 5.1 Definition

The number states |n⟩ have definite particle number but no definite phase, and their expectation values of the field ⟨n|φ|n⟩ vanish, since φ changes n by ±1. A classical field has a definite value, not a definite number of quanta. The quantum states that come closest to classical waves are the eigenstates of the annihilation operator.

$$ a|α⟩ = α|α⟩,    |α⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2) Σ_(n=0)^∞ (α^n/√(n!)) |n⟩

for any complex α. To verify that this is an eigenstate, apply a using a|n⟩ = √n|n − 1⟩: a|α⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2)Σ_(n≥1)(α^n/√((n−1)!))|n − 1⟩ = α|α⟩ (Exercise 7). The state is normalized, since Σ_n|α|^(2n)/n! = e^(|α|²).

## 5.2 The number distribution

The probability of finding n quanta is |⟨n|α⟩|² = e^(−|α|²)|α|^(2n)/n!, a Poisson distribution with mean ⟨N⟩ = ⟨α|a†a|α⟩ = |α|², and variance ΔN² = ⟨N²⟩ − ⟨N⟩² = |α|² also. The relative spread ΔN/⟨N⟩ = 1/|α| decreases as the mean number grows, so a state with many quanta is nearly sharp in the classical sense.

### Worked example 3

Find the number statistics of a coherent state with |α|² = 4. The mean number is 4 and the standard deviation is 2. The probability of n = 4 is e^(−4)4⁴/4! ≈ 0.195, and the distribution has appreciable weight from n = 1 to n = 8. For |α|² = 10⁶ the mean is 10⁶ and the standard deviation is 10³, a relative spread of 10⁻³: for the macroscopic number of photons in a laser beam the fluctuations are relatively tiny, and the beam is described by a classical wave.

## 5.3 The classical limit of the field

In a coherent state of the modes of the field, a_p|α⟩ = α_p|α⟩ for every p, the expectation value of the field is obtained by replacing the operators by their eigenvalues.

$$ ⟨α|φ(x)|α⟩ = ∫ d³p/(2π)³ (1/√(2E_p)) ( α_p e^(−ip·x) + α_p* e^(ip·x) )

This is a classical solution of the Klein–Gordon equation, of exactly the form of the mode expansion of Lesson 24, Section 4.4, with the classical amplitude a_p replaced by the eigenvalue α_p. The expectation value of the normal-ordered Hamiltonian is ⟨:H:⟩ = ∫d³p/(2π)³E_p|α_p|², the classical energy H = Σω|a|² of Lesson 24. A coherent state is therefore a quantum state whose expectation values follow a classical solution. The classical fields of Part IV are recovered as coherent states of many quanta, and the connection explains how a classical electromagnetic wave, which has a definite amplitude and phase, arises from photons that individually have neither.

## 5.4 Displacement operator

The coherent state is obtained from the vacuum by the unitary displacement operator D(α) = exp(αa† − α*a). Since [αa†, −α*a] = |α|² is a number, the identity e^(A+B) = e^Ae^Be^(−[A,B]/2) applies, giving D(α) = e^(−|α|²/2)e^(αa†)e^(−α*a), and, using a|0⟩ = 0,

$$ D(α)|0⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2) e^(αa†) |0⟩ = |α⟩

The vacuum, displaced in the complex plane of the mode amplitude, is the coherent state (Exercise 8). This is the quantum analogue of shifting the oscillator from the origin of phase space.

# 6 Time dependence and interactions

## 6.1 Time evolution of a coherent state

Under the free Hamiltonian H = ωa†a (dropping the zero-point term), e^(−iHt) acts on a number state as a phase e^(−inωt), so

$$ e^(−iHt)|α⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2) Σ_n ((αe^(−iωt))^n/√(n!)) |n⟩ = |αe^(−iωt)⟩

A coherent state remains coherent, and its amplitude rotates in the complex plane at the frequency ω, exactly as the classical amplitude a(t) = a(0)e^(−iωt) does (Lesson 24, Section 4.4). The expectation value of the field is a classical wave for all time, and the state does not spread, unlike the free-particle wave packet of Lesson 11: the coherent state is a stationary shape in the phase space of the oscillator.

## 6.2 The number operator and the U(1) symmetry

Since [N, a_p] = −a_p, the unitary operator e^(iθN) acts by e^(iθN)a_pe^(−iθN) = e^(−iθ)a_p. Rotating the phase of every annihilation operator by e^(−iθ) is a phase rotation of the field. For a complex field, with the a quanta of charge q and the b quanta of charge −q, the charge is Q = q(N_a − N_b) (Lesson 32, Section 8.2), and e^(iθQ/q) generates the U(1) transformation of Lesson 30, Section 2.2. The conservation of particle number in a free theory is the U(1) symmetry of the phase of the mode amplitudes.

## 6.3 Interactions as number-changing processes

An interaction Hamiltonian is a polynomial in the field, and each monomial in the ladder operators corresponds to a definite process. Take the cubic interaction H_int = (g/3!)∫d³x φ³, whose classical form appears in the theory with Lagrangian density including −(g/3!)φ³. Expanding φ³ in the modes, the terms are of four kinds.

@widths 2160 3024 4608
| Operator content | Process | Number change |
| a†a†a† | Vacuum to three particles | +3 |
| a†a†a | One particle to two | +1 |
| a†aa | Two particles to one | −1 |
| aaa | Three particles to vacuum | −3 |

Each monomial also has a definite momentum structure, set by the integral ∫d³x e^(i(±p±q±r)·x) = (2π)³δ³(±p ± q ± r), which enforces conservation of momentum. Whether the process conserves energy is a separate question, and in the free theory it cannot in these cases (Exercise 11): a single particle of mass m cannot decay into two of mass m, because two particles have invariant mass at least 2m > m, and three particles cannot appear from the vacuum. The interaction contributes only through virtual processes at second order, in which a particle is emitted and reabsorbed, and these produce the scattering 2 → 2 of Part VII.

### Worked example 4

Decide whether the term a_p†a_q†a_r in H_int can describe a real process for identical particles of mass m. The term annihilates a particle of momentum r and creates two of momenta p and q, with conservation of momentum r = p + q. The energy would have to satisfy E_r = E_p + E_q. But the invariant mass squared of the two final particles is (E_p + E_q)² − |p + q|² ≥ 4m² (for two particles of mass m it is at least (2m)² at threshold), while the initial particle has E_r² − |r|² = m². Since 4m² > m², no choice of momenta satisfies both conditions: the decay is kinematically forbidden. The operator still acts, but only in intermediate states that do not conserve energy, which Part VII treats through propagators.

# 8 Summary

1. The number operator N = a†a has nonnegative integer eigenvalues; the states |n⟩ = (a†)ⁿ|0⟩/√n! satisfy a|n⟩ = √n|n − 1⟩ and a†|n⟩ = √(n + 1)|n + 1⟩.

2. For many modes, operators of different modes commute, occupation number states are symmetric under interchange, and N quanta in M modes give C(N + M − 1, N) states.

3. The box state has unit norm and the relativistic state |p⟩ = √(2E_p)a_p†|0⟩ has norm 2E_pV; the number operator of the field is N = ∫d³p/(2π)³a_p†a_p.

4. A wave-packet state ∫d³p/(2π)³ψ̃(p)a_p†|0⟩ has norm ∫|ψ̃|², one particle, and energy and momentum given by averages over |ψ̃|².

5. Coherent states, a|α⟩ = α|α⟩, have a Poisson number distribution with mean |α|² and relative spread 1/|α|.

6. The expectation value of the field in a coherent state is a classical solution of the field equation, with energy equal to the classical energy.

7. A coherent state stays coherent under free evolution, |α⟩ → |αe^(−iωt)⟩, and the number operator generates the phase rotation of the ladder operators.

8. Each monomial in the ladder operators of an interaction Hamiltonian is a process with a definite change of particle number, and the cubic terms cannot conserve energy for real particles of one mass.

# Solutions

**1.** Suppose N|n⟩ = n|n⟩ with |n⟩ normalized. Then [N, a] = −a gives N(a|n⟩) = (n − 1)a|n⟩, so a|n⟩ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue n − 1 unless it vanishes. Its norm is ‖a|n⟩‖² = ⟨n|a†a|n⟩ = n ≥ 0, so n ≥ 0. Repeated application produces eigenvalues n − 1, n − 2, …, and if n were not an integer the sequence would eventually reach a negative eigenvalue and a state of negative norm, a contradiction. The sequence can terminate only by reaching a state with a|·⟩ = 0, whose eigenvalue is zero, so n − k = 0 for some integer k, and n is a nonnegative integer.

**2.** For n = 0, ‖|0⟩‖² = 1 = 0!. Suppose ‖(a†)^(n−1)|0⟩‖² = (n − 1)!. Then ‖(a†)ⁿ|0⟩‖² = ⟨0|aⁿ(a†)ⁿ|0⟩, and using a(a†)ⁿ|0⟩ = n(a†)^(n−1)|0⟩ (since [a, (a†)ⁿ] = n(a†)^(n−1)), the remaining a^(n−1) then gives n⟨0|a^(n−1)(a†)^(n−1)|0⟩ = n‖(a†)^(n−1)|0⟩‖² = n(n − 1)! = n!. So (a†)ⁿ|0⟩/√(n!) has unit norm.

**3.** a†a|n⟩ = n|n⟩ gives ⟨n|a†a|n⟩ = n. aa† = a†a + 1, so ⟨n|aa†|n⟩ = n + 1. For (a + a†)² = a² + a†² + aa† + a†a, the matrix elements of a² and a†² between |n⟩ and |n⟩ vanish because they change n by ±2, and ⟨n|(aa† + a†a)|n⟩ = (n + 1) + n = 2n + 1.

**4.** Placing N identical quanta into M distinguishable modes is the stars and bars problem: N stars and M − 1 bars can be arranged in C(N + M − 1, N) ways. For N = 2, M = 3: C(4, 2) = 6. For N = 2, M = 4: C(5, 2) = 10.

**5.** ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = ∫∫d³pd³q/(2π)⁶ψ̃*(q)ψ̃(p)⟨0|a_qa_p†|0⟩ = ∫∫ψ̃*(q)ψ̃(p)(2π)³δ³(p − q)d³pd³q/(2π)⁶ = ∫d³p/(2π)³|ψ̃|². Since [N, a_p†] = a_p†, N|ψ⟩ = |ψ⟩ and ⟨N⟩ = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩. With [H, a_p†] = E_pa_p†, H|ψ⟩ = ∫d³p/(2π)³ψ̃(p)E_pa_p†|0⟩, so ⟨H⟩ = ∫d³p/(2π)³E_p|ψ̃|².

**6.** Each plane wave e^(−ip·x) with E_p² = |p|² + m² satisfies (□ + m²)e^(−ip·x) = (−E_p² + |p|² + m²)e^(−ip·x) = 0, so the superposition does too. Its modulus squared |⟨0|φ(x)|ψ⟩|² is not a probability density because the normalization of the state is ∫d³p/(2π)³|ψ̃|² (Exercise 5) whereas ∫d³x|⟨0|φ(x)|ψ⟩|² = ∫d³p/((2π)³2E_p)|ψ̃|², which differs by the momentum-dependent factor 1/(2E_p). The two normalizations therefore disagree for any packet with a spread of energies. Moreover, a relativistic particle has no sharp position (Lesson 31).

**7.** Norm: ⟨α|α⟩ = e^(−|α|²)Σ_n|α|^(2n)/n! = e^(−|α|²)e^(|α|²) = 1. Eigenvalue: a|α⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2)Σ_n(α^n/√(n!))√n|n − 1⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2)Σ_(n≥1)(α^n/√((n−1)!))|n − 1⟩. Relabeling m = n − 1, this is αe^(−|α|²/2)Σ_m(α^m/√(m!))|m⟩ = α|α⟩.

**8.** Take A = αa† and B = −α*a. Then [A, B] = −|α|²[a†, a] = |α|², a number. So D(α) = e^(A+B) = e^Ae^Be^(−|α|²/2) = e^(−|α|²/2)e^(αa†)e^(−α*a). Acting on |0⟩, e^(−α*a)|0⟩ = |0⟩ since a|0⟩ = 0, and e^(αa†)|0⟩ = Σ_n(α^n/n!)(a†)ⁿ|0⟩ = Σ_n(α^n/√(n!))|n⟩. Hence D(α)|0⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2)Σ_n(α^n/√(n!))|n⟩ = |α⟩.

**9.** Since a_p|α⟩ = α_p|α⟩ and ⟨α|a_p† = α_p*⟨α|, ⟨α|φ(x)|α⟩ = ∫d³p/(2π)³(2E_p)^(−1/2)(α_pe^(−ip·x) + α_p*e^(ip·x)), the classical mode expansion with a_p → α_p. For the normal-ordered Hamiltonian, ⟨α|∫E_pa_p†a_p|α⟩ = ∫d³p/(2π)³E_p|α_p|², which is the continuum form of the classical H = Σω|a|² of Lesson 24, Section 4.4.

**10.** With H = ωa†a, e^(−iHt)|n⟩ = e^(−inωt)|n⟩, so e^(−iHt)|α⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2)Σ_n(α^n/√(n!))e^(−inωt)|n⟩ = e^(−|α|²/2)Σ_n((αe^(−iωt))^n/√(n!))|n⟩. Since |αe^(−iωt)|² = |α|², the prefactor is the correct normalization, and the result is |αe^(−iωt)⟩. The complex amplitude α rotates in the complex plane at frequency ω, which is the classical motion a(t) = a(0)e^(−iωt), a circle in the phase space of the oscillator.

**11.** The terms a†a†a† and aaa would create or destroy three particles from or into the vacuum, requiring 3E = 0 or a total energy of zero from particles of positive energy, impossible. The term a†a†a (1 → 2) requires E_r = E_p + E_q with r = p + q, but the pair's invariant mass is at least 2m, while the initial particle has mass m, so it is impossible. The term a†aa (2 → 1) is the reverse process, requiring two particles of mass m to have total invariant mass m, and is also impossible. None of the terms can conserve energy and momentum for real particles of one mass.

**12.** With φ = ∫(2E_p)^(−1/2)(a_pe^(−ip·x) + b_p†e^(ip·x)), Cφ(x)C⁻¹ = ∫(2E_p)^(−1/2)(b_pe^(−ip·x) + a_p†e^(ip·x)) = φ†(x), since φ† = ∫(2E_p)^(−1/2)(a_p†e^(ip·x) + b_pe^(−ip·x)). For the charge, CQC⁻¹ = q∫d³p/(2π)³(b_p†b_p − a_p†a_p) = −Q.

**13.** [N, a_p†] = ∫d³q/(2π)³[a_q†a_q, a_p†] = ∫d³q/(2π)³a_q†(2π)³δ³(q − p) = a_p†. Then Na_p†a_q†|0⟩ = ([N, a_p†] + a_p†N)a_q†|0⟩ = a_p†a_q†|0⟩ + a_p†[N, a_q†]|0⟩ + a_p†a_q†N|0⟩ = a_p†a_q†|0⟩ + a_p†a_q†|0⟩ + 0 = 2a_p†a_q†|0⟩.

**14.** Let f(θ) = e^(iθN)a_pe^(−iθN). Then df/dθ = ie^(iθN)[N, a_p]e^(−iθN) = −if, so f(θ) = e^(−iθ)a_p. This is a phase rotation of the annihilation operator, of the same form as U(α)φU(α)⁻¹ = e^(−iqα)φ of Lesson 30, Section 2.2, with N playing the role of Q/q for the a quanta; the U(1) charge is q times the difference of the numbers of a and b quanta.

**15.** ⟨0|φ(x)²|0⟩ = ∫d³pd³q/(2π)⁶(4E_pE_q)^(−1/2)⟨0|a_pa_q†|0⟩e^(−ip·x+iq·x) = ∫d³p/((2π)³2E_p). For m = 0, E_p = |p| and the integral is (4π/(2π)³)∫₀^Λp²dp/(2p) = (1/(4π²))∫₀^Λp dp = Λ²/(8π²), which diverges as Λ → ∞. The field at a point has infinite fluctuations in the vacuum, which is why field operators are defined by smearing over a small region.

**16.** For |α⟩, ⟨a†a†aa⟩ = ⟨α|a†²a²|α⟩ = |α|⁴ (a² → α² on the right and a†² → α*² on the left), and ⟨a†a⟩ = |α|², so g² = 1. For |n⟩, a²|n⟩ = √(n(n−1))|n − 2⟩, so ⟨n|a†²a²|n⟩ = n(n − 1) and g² = n(n − 1)/n² = 1 − 1/n < 1. A coherent state has g² = 1: the photon counts follow a Poisson distribution, uncorrelated. A number state has g² < 1, antibunched, since photons arrive with fewer coincidences than a random process, which has no classical analogue. (A thermal state has g² = 2, bunched.) The correlation g² distinguishes fields with the same mean intensity and is the basis of the Hanbury Brown and Twiss experiment.
