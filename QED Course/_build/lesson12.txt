# How to use this lesson

Lessons 9 through 11 worked almost entirely in position space, with ψ(x, t) as the primary object. This lesson makes the alternative, equally valid description in momentum space precise, using the Fourier transform of Lesson 2, and shows that position and momentum are two representations of the same abstract state, related exactly as two coordinate systems for the same vector were related in Lesson 1. The lesson closes the loop opened in Lesson 10: the uncertainty relation ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2 is now derived directly from the properties of the Fourier transform, giving it a second, independent justification.

This is a shorter lesson than most, because its content is largely a direct transcription of Lesson 2's Fourier analysis into the bra-ket language of Lessons 9 and 10. What is new is the physical interpretation and a handful of results, the momentum-space Schrödinger equation and the free-particle propagator among them, that are used repeatedly later. Expect five to seven focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Define the momentum-space wave function ψ̃(p) as the Fourier transform of ψ(x) and state its probabilistic interpretation.

- Show that ⟨p̂⟩ and ⟨p̂²⟩ can be computed either in position space or directly from ψ̃(p).

- Write the free Schrödinger equation in momentum space and solve it.

- Derive the position-momentum uncertainty relation directly from the properties of the Fourier transform.

- Construct the free-particle propagator and use it to evolve a wave packet.

- State the momentum-space representation of the position operator and verify [x̂, p̂] = iℏ in that representation.

- Compute the momentum-space wave function of the harmonic oscillator ground state.

- Recognize position and momentum representations as two bases for the same Hilbert space, connecting back to Lesson 1.

# 1 The momentum-space wave function

## 1.1 Definition

Following the Fourier convention of Lesson 2 (with an ℏ inserted to convert wave number to momentum),

$$ ψ(x) = ∫ (dp/2πℏ) ψ̃(p) e^(ipx/ℏ),    ψ̃(p) = ∫ dx ψ(x) e^(−ipx/ℏ)

The function ψ̃(p) is called the momentum-space wave function, or the wave function in the momentum representation. It carries exactly the same information as ψ(x); nothing has been lost or gained, only reorganized, precisely as a vector in Lesson 1 was unchanged by a change of basis, only its coordinate column changed.

## 1.2 The Born rule in momentum space

The Parseval relation of Lesson 2, adapted to this convention, states ∫|ψ(x)|²dx = ∫(dp/2πℏ)|ψ̃(p)|². If ψ is normalized, this common value is 1, and

$$ dP(p) = |ψ̃(p)|² dp/(2πℏ)

is the probability of finding the particle's momentum in a small interval dp around p, upon measurement. This is the Born rule of Lesson 9, applied not to position but to momentum, and it identifies |ψ̃(p)|²/(2πℏ) as the momentum-space probability density. The measure dp/(2πℏ) rather than dp is the price of the Fourier convention adopted in Lesson 2, and it will reappear as d³p/(2πℏ)³ in every mode sum of quantum field theory.

### Worked example 1

Find ψ̃(p) for the Gaussian ψ(x) = (πa²)^(−1/4)e^(−x²/2a²) of Lesson 9. This is the Gaussian transform computed in Worked example 6 of Lesson 2, with k = p/ℏ, so ψ̃(p) = (πa²)^(−1/4)∫e^(−x²/2a²)e^(−ipx/ℏ)dx. Completing the square as in that example gives

$$ ψ̃(p) = (4πa²)^(1/4) e^(−a²p²/2ℏ²)

itself a Gaussian, with width ℏ/a in momentum, inversely proportional to the width a in position, exactly the reciprocal relationship noted in Lesson 2. Exercise 1 checks that this ψ̃(p) is correctly normalized against the measure dp/(2πℏ).

# 2 Expectation values in momentum space

## 2.1 Momentum operator as multiplication

In the momentum representation, the momentum operator acts simply by multiplication, p̂ψ̃(p) = p ψ̃(p), the mirror image of position acting by multiplication in the position representation (Lesson 10, Section 2.1). Consequently,

$$ ⟨p̂⟩ = ∫ (dp/2πℏ) ψ̃*(p) p ψ̃(p),    ⟨p̂²⟩ = ∫ (dp/2πℏ) ψ̃*(p) p² ψ̃(p)

exactly as position expectation values were computed by multiplying by x in Lesson 9, now with the roles of x and p exchanged, a symmetry built into the Fourier transform.

## 2.2 Position operator in momentum space

Position must act as a derivative in momentum space, by the same argument that made momentum a derivative in position space (Lesson 10, Section 2.2), with a compensating sign.

$$ x̂ ψ̃(p) = iℏ (dψ̃/dp)

This can be verified by inserting the Fourier representations of ψ and ψ̃ into the definitions and integrating by parts, exactly as in Lesson 2's Section 5.3, where a space derivative in x became a factor of ik, and here a derivative in p, up to sign and the appropriate ℏ, reproduces multiplication by x.

### Worked example 2

Verify [x̂, p̂] = iℏ in the momentum representation. Acting on ψ̃(p), [x̂,p̂]ψ̃ = iℏ d/dp(pψ̃) − p(iℏ dψ̃/dp) = iℏψ̃ + iℏp ψ̃′ − iℏp ψ̃′ = iℏψ̃, so [x̂,p̂] = iℏ, the same result found in position space in Lesson 10, as it must be: the commutator is a statement about the abstract operators, independent of which representation is used to compute it, exactly as a matrix commutator in Lesson 1 was unchanged by a change of basis.

# 3 The free Schrödinger equation in momentum space

## 3.1 Transforming the equation

Starting from iℏ∂_tψ = −(ℏ²/2m)∂ₓ²ψ and using the rule of Lesson 2 that a space derivative becomes multiplication by ip/ℏ under the Fourier transform (adjusting the convention there to match this lesson's momentum variable),

$$ iℏ ∂ψ̃/∂t = (p²/2m) ψ̃(p, t)

For each value of p, this is an ordinary first-order equation in t of the type solved in Lesson 2, Section 6.1, with immediate solution

$$ ψ̃(p, t) = ψ̃(p, 0) e^(−ip²t/2mℏ)

The free Schrödinger equation, a partial differential equation in position space, becomes a decoupled family of ordinary differential equations in momentum space, one for each momentum, each evolving with a pure phase. This is the deepest reason the momentum representation is convenient for free (and, after Lesson 45, for asymptotic) particles: the dynamics is diagonal.

## 3.2 Reconstructing the position-space packet

Substituting the solution back into the inverse Fourier transform reproduces the wave-packet formula quoted without derivation in Lesson 11's Section 3.1.

$$ ψ(x, t) = ∫ (dp/2πℏ) ψ̃(p, 0) e^(i(px − p²t/2m)/ℏ)

The spreading of a wave packet, discussed qualitatively in Lesson 11, is now visible directly: each momentum component contributes a phase px/ℏ that advances at a rate p/mℏ per unit time (matching the group velocity p/m) plus a quadratic correction −p²t/2mℏ² that varies across the spread of momenta present, causing the components to fall out of phase with each other, which is precisely spreading.

### Worked example 3

Carry through the Gaussian wave packet evolution promised in Worked example 3 of Lesson 11. With ψ̃(p, 0) = (4πℏ²/a²)^(1/4)e^(−a²p²/2ℏ²)e^(−ik₀ax) (a carrier added as a phase, equivalently a shift p → p−ℏk₀ in the Gaussian), the time-evolved ψ̃(p,t) has an extra phase e^(−ip²t/2mℏ). This is again a Gaussian in p, but with a complex width parameter a² → a² + iℏt/m, whose real part gives the position-space spreading a(t) = a√(1+(ℏt/ma²)²) quoted in Lesson 11, now derived rather than merely stated.

# 4 The uncertainty relation from the Fourier transform

## 4.1 A general property of the transform

For any function and its Fourier transform related as in Section 1.1, the product of their widths is bounded below, a purely mathematical fact about Fourier pairs, independent of any physics. Concretely, define σₓ² = ⟨x²⟩−⟨x⟩² using |ψ(x)|² and σₚ² = ⟨p²⟩−⟨p⟩² using |ψ̃(p)|²/2πℏ. The Fourier transform relates σₓ and σₚ through the same operator algebra used in Lesson 10's general uncertainty proof, since x̂ and p̂ are represented as multiplication and differentiation in the two pictures precisely so that [x̂,p̂] = iℏ holds; the general proof of Lesson 10, Exercise 16, therefore applies without modification and gives

$$ σₓ σₚ ≥ ℏ/2

This is the same inequality obtained abstractly in Lesson 10 from Cauchy Schwarz, now understood concretely: it is the statement that a function cannot be made arbitrarily narrow in both position and momentum space simultaneously, the general mathematical fact about Fourier pairs already encountered for classical wave packets in Lesson 2.

## 4.2 The minimum-uncertainty packet is Gaussian

Among all normalized functions, the Gaussian saturates the inequality of Section 4.1 with equality; this was checked numerically for a specific case in Worked example 4 of Lesson 10 and is now understood structurally: the Gaussian is the unique fixed point (up to translation and momentum boost) of the Fourier transform's width-reciprocity, the same special role it plays in Lesson 2's discussion of the transform. Any other normalized shape has σₓσₚ strictly greater than ℏ/2.

### Worked example 4

Confirm that the Gaussian of Worked example 1 saturates the bound. From Lesson 9, σₓ = a/√2. From ψ̃(p) = (4πℏ²/a²)^(1/4)e^(−a²p²/2ℏ²), the same Gaussian computation with a replaced by ℏ/a gives σₚ = ℏ/(a√2), matching Exercise 3 of Lesson 10. Then σₓσₚ = (a/√2)(ℏ/(a√2)) = ℏ/2 exactly, confirming the Gaussian saturates the inequality.

# 5 The free-particle propagator

## 5.1 Definition

The propagator K(x, t; x′, 0) is defined so that ψ(x, t) = ∫dx′ K(x,t;x′,0)ψ(x′,0): it is the amplitude for a particle to travel from x′ at time 0 to x at time t. Inserting the momentum-space solution of Section 3.1 and the completeness relation for plane waves,

$$ K(x, t; x′, 0) = ∫ (dp/2πℏ) e^(ip(x−x′)/ℏ) e^(−ip²t/2mℏ) = √(m/2πiℏt) e^(im(x−x′)²/2ℏt)

The Gaussian integral needed to evaluate this is exactly of the type in Lesson 2's Section 2.3, continued to an imaginary coefficient; the manipulation is made fully rigorous by treating t as having a small negative imaginary part, a device that reappears, for a closely related reason, in the Feynman propagator of Lesson 45.

## 5.2 Interpretation

|K(x,t;x′,0)|² is independent of x−x′, reflecting that a truly free particle, once its position is perfectly known at t=0 (a delta function, not normalizable, but a useful idealization), has completely undetermined position at any later time, an extreme instance of the spreading of Section 3.2 and of the uncertainty relation of Section 4: perfect initial localization means infinite momentum spread, by Section 4.1, and infinite momentum spread means the particle could be found anywhere at the very next instant.

### Worked example 5

Verify K solves the free Schrödinger equation in the variable (x,t) for fixed (x′,0), away from t=0. Direct differentiation of K = √(m/2πiℏt) e^(im(x−x′)²/2ℏt) gives ∂ₜK and ∂ₓ²K; substituting into iℏ∂ₜK = −(ℏ²/2m)∂ₓ²K and canceling the common exponential factor reduces the check to an identity among the prefactor's t-dependence and the exponent's x and t dependence, confirmed by the same algebra used to verify a Gaussian solves the diffusion-type equation of Lesson 2, Section 7.4, continued to imaginary diffusion constant.

# 6 The harmonic oscillator in momentum space

## 6.1 Ground-state wave function

The position-space ground state of the harmonic oscillator satisfies âφ₀ = 0, that is, √(mω₀/2ℏ)(x̂+ip̂/mω₀)φ₀ = 0, giving the first-order equation ℏφ₀′ = −mω₀xφ₀, solved by the Gaussian φ₀(x) = (mω₀/πℏ)^(1/4)e^(−mω₀x²/2ℏ).

## 6.2 Fourier transform of the ground state

Since a Gaussian transforms to a Gaussian (Section 1.1, Worked example 1), φ̃₀(p) is also Gaussian; direct computation, or the general reciprocity a → ℏ/a of Section 1, gives

$$ φ̃₀(p) = (πmℏω₀)^(−1/4) e^(−p²/2mℏω₀)

This is exactly what is obtained by solving âφ̃₀ = 0 directly in the momentum representation, using x̂ = iℏd/dp there: √(mω₀/2ℏ)(iℏφ̃₀′ + ip/(mω₀) φ̃₀) = 0, the momentum-space mirror of the position-space calculation, confirming that the abstract ground state |0⟩ has a definite, computable representative in either basis, exactly as an abstract vector in Lesson 1 has a definite coordinate column in any basis once one is chosen.

### Worked example 6

Verify σₓσₚ = ℏ/2 for the oscillator ground state directly from φ₀ and φ̃₀. From φ₀(x), matching the form of Worked example 1 with a² = ℏ/mω₀, σₓ = √(ℏ/2mω₀); from φ̃₀(p), matching the same form with the reciprocal width, σₚ = √(mℏω₀/2). The product is σₓσₚ = ℏ/2: the oscillator ground state, like the free Gaussian packet, saturates the uncertainty bound, a fact used repeatedly when the oscillator ground state is reinterpreted as the vacuum of a quantum field in Lesson 32.

# 7 Position and momentum as two bases

## 7.1 The abstract picture

Everything in this lesson can be summarized in the language of Lesson 1. The physical state is an abstract vector |ψ⟩ in Hilbert space. Position space represents it by the array of coefficients ψ(x) = ⟨x|ψ⟩ in the (continuous, non-normalizable) position basis {|x⟩}; momentum space represents it by ψ̃(p) = ⟨p|ψ⟩ in the momentum basis {|p⟩}, up to the 2πℏ normalization convention of Section 1.1. The two bases are related by ⟨x|p⟩ = e^(ipx/ℏ), the plane wave, exactly analogous to a change-of-basis matrix in Lesson 1's Section 4.4, generalized to a continuous index.

## 7.2 Why this matters going forward

Choosing a representation is a matter of convenience, never of physics: any calculation can be done in either, and the answer, an expectation value or a probability, is representation-independent, since it is built from the abstract inner product ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩. Field theory calculations from Lesson 32 onward will move freely between a field's values at each spacetime point and its Fourier components at each four-momentum, using exactly the reasoning of this lesson, generalized from one particle in three dimensions to a field with infinitely many degrees of freedom.

### Worked example 7

Compute ⟨p|x⟩ and confirm it is the complex conjugate of ⟨x|p⟩. Since ⟨p|x⟩ = ⟨x|p⟩* by the conjugate symmetry of the inner product (Lesson 1, Section 3.1), and ⟨x|p⟩ = e^(ipx/ℏ), ⟨p|x⟩ = e^(−ipx/ℏ), consistent with the two Fourier transform conventions of Section 1.1 differing by exactly this complex conjugation between the forward and inverse transforms.

Recognizing the family resemblance between the two propagators, nonrelativistic here and relativistic there, is one of the more satisfying continuities of the course.

# 9 Summary

1. The momentum-space wave function ψ̃(p) carries the same information as ψ(x); the two are related by the Fourier transform of Lesson 2.

2. |ψ̃(p)|²/(2πℏ) is the probability density for momentum, exactly parallel to the position-space Born rule.

3. Momentum acts by multiplication and position by iℏd/dp in the momentum representation, the mirror image of position space.

4. The free Schrödinger equation becomes a decoupled family of first-order equations in momentum space, each evolving by a pure phase.

5. The uncertainty relation σₓσₚ ≥ ℏ/2 follows from the general reciprocity of Fourier transform widths; the Gaussian saturates it.

6. The free-particle propagator K(x,t;x′,0) gives the amplitude to travel from x′ to x in time t and is the ancestor of the Feynman propagator.

7. The harmonic oscillator ground state has explicit, mutually consistent representations in both position and momentum space.

8. Position and momentum representations are two bases for the same abstract Hilbert space vector, exactly as in Lesson 1, with no physical content favoring either.

# Solutions

**1.** ψ̃(p) = (πa²)^(−1/4)∫e^(−x²/2a²)e^(−ipx/ℏ)dx = (πa²)^(−1/4)√(2πa²) e^(−a²p²/2ℏ²) = (4πa²)^(1/4)e^(−a²p²/2ℏ²), matching the lesson body. Checking normalization: ∫(dp/2πℏ)\|ψ̃\|² = (4πa²)^(1/2)/(2πℏ) ∫e^(−a²p²/ℏ²)dp = (4πa²)^(1/2)/(2πℏ) × √π ℏ/a = 1, confirming the normalization.

**2.** ψ̃(p) = ∫₀^L √(2/L)sin(πx/L)e^(−ipx/ℏ)dx. Writing sin(πx/L) as a combination of e^(±iπx/L), the integral picks out momenta near p = ±πℏ/L, giving a two-lobed distribution peaked at these values; these are the two momenta ±ℏk with k = π/L whose superposition, as noted in Worked example 3 of Lesson 9's discussion of real wave functions, forms the standing wave in the well.

**3.** By the symmetry \|ψ̃(p)\|² = \|ψ̃(−p)\|² (since the two dominant momenta ±πℏ/L appear with equal weight, reflecting the standing wave having no net direction of travel), ⟨p̂⟩ = ∫(dp/2πℏ)p\|ψ̃(p)\|² vanishes by the antisymmetry of the integrand under p → −p, matching the zero probability current found in Lesson 9, Exercise 8, for a real wave function.

**4.** ∂ψ/∂p′ inside the integral is not directly x; instead consider iℏ∂ψ̃/∂p = iℏ∫dx ψ(x)(−ix/ℏ)e^(−ipx/ℏ) = ∫dx (xψ(x))e^(−ipx/ℏ), which is exactly the Fourier transform of xψ(x). Since the Fourier transform of xψ(x) is, by definition, the momentum-space representative of x̂ψ(x), this confirms x̂ acts as iℏd/dp on ψ̃(p).

**5.** ∂ₜψ̃ = ψ̃(p,0)(−ip²/2mℏ)e^(−ip²t/2mℏ) = −(ip²/2mℏ)ψ̃(p,t). Multiplying by iℏ gives iℏ∂ₜψ̃ = (p²/2m)ψ̃(p,t), as required.

**6.** ψ(x,0) = ∫_(−P)^P (dp/2πℏ) C e^(ipx/ℏ) = (C/2πℏ) × (ℏ/ix)[e^(iPx/ℏ)−e^(−iPx/ℏ)] = (C/πx)sin(Px/ℏ), a sinc-function shape, spread over a spatial scale ~ ℏ/P: a sharply confined momentum distribution (all \|p\| < P) produces a spatially extended wave function, the reciprocal relationship of Section 4 in an extreme, non-Gaussian example.

**7.** As t → 0⁺, the phase m(x−x′)²/2ℏt varies extremely rapidly with x for any x ≠ x′, causing the integral defining ψ(x,t) = ∫K ψ(x′,0)dx′ to oscillate to near-zero contribution except where x is very close to x′, while the prefactor √(m/2πiℏt) grows without bound; this is exactly the qualitative behavior expected of a function tending to δ(x−x′), consistent with ψ(x,0⁺) → ψ(x,0).

**8.** ψ(x,t) = ∫dx′ K(x,t;x′,0)δ(x′−x₀) = K(x,t;x₀,0): for a perfectly localized initial state, the evolved wave function is exactly the propagator itself, confirming the interpretation of K as "the wave function that started as a delta function at x₀."

**9.** [x̂,p̂]ψ̃ = iℏ d/dp(pψ̃) − p(iℏ dψ̃/dp) = iℏψ̃ + iℏp ψ̃′ − iℏp ψ̃′ = iℏψ̃, giving [x̂,p̂] = iℏ, matching Worked example 2 (this is the same calculation, repeated here as a standalone check).

**10.** For the Lorentzian ψ̃(p), \|ψ̃(p)\|² ∝ 1/(1+(pb/ℏ)²)², and ⟨p²⟩ is computed by an integral of this form; the result (a standard integral) gives σₚ finite but with σₓσₚ coming out larger than ℏ/2 (specific evaluation shows the exponential wave function, unlike the Gaussian, is not a minimum-uncertainty state, consistent with only Gaussians saturating the bound as stated in Section 4.2).

**11.** In momentum space, â = √(mω₀/2ℏ)(x̂+ip̂/mω₀) = √(mω₀/2ℏ)(iℏd/dp + ip/mω₀). Setting âφ̃₀ = 0 gives iℏφ̃₀′ = −(ip/mω₀)φ̃₀, that is φ̃₀′/φ̃₀ = −p/(mℏω₀), integrating to φ̃₀(p) ∝ e^(−p²/2mℏω₀), matching the quoted result after normalization.

**12.** ⟨p\|p′⟩ = ∫dx e^(−ipx/ℏ)e^(ip′x/ℏ) = ∫dx e^(i(p′−p)x/ℏ). Substituting k = (p′−p)/ℏ into Lesson 2's ∫dk e^(ikx)... rearranged, ∫dx e^(i(p′−p)x/ℏ) = 2πℏ δ(p′−p), using the delta-function identity of Lesson 2 with the variable rescaled by ℏ.

**13.** (∫dp/2πℏ \|p⟩⟨p\|)ψ acting in position space gives ∫dp/2πℏ e^(ipx/ℏ)⟨p\|ψ⟩ = ∫dp/2πℏ e^(ipx/ℏ)ψ̃(p) = ψ(x) by the inverse Fourier transform formula of Section 1.1, confirming the resolution of the identity.
