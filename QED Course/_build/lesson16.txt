# How to use this lesson

Quantum mechanics treats two particles of the same species, two electrons, or two photons, as fundamentally indistinguishable, not merely difficult to tell apart in practice. This lesson works out the mathematical consequence of that indistinguishability: the symmetrization postulate, which splits every particle in nature into one of exactly two classes, bosons and fermions, with sharply different statistical behavior. The Pauli exclusion principle, without which no periodic table and no stable matter would exist, is a direct corollary.

This is the last lesson of Part II, and it closes the nonrelativistic quantum mechanics needed for the rest of the course. The exchange effects developed here reappear with full force once the Dirac field is quantized in Lesson 35, where anticommutation relations, rather than an ad hoc symmetrization rule, will be shown to build fermionic antisymmetry into the algebra of the theory from the start. Expect six to eight focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- State the physical meaning of indistinguishability and the exchange operator that formalizes it.

- Derive the symmetrization postulate and the two allowed particle statistics, bosons and fermions.

- Construct properly (anti)symmetrized two-particle states from single-particle states.

- Derive the Pauli exclusion principle for fermions and explain why it has no bosonic counterpart.

- Compute the exchange energy for two identical particles and distinguish it from any classical interaction.

- Construct symmetrized and antisymmetrized states of N identical particles, including the Slater determinant.

- Explain the connection between spin and statistics as an empirical rule at this stage of the course.

- Recognize identical-particle antisymmetry as the property that will reappear, built into the algebra, when the Dirac field is quantized.

# 1 Indistinguishability and the exchange operator

## 1.1 What indistinguishability means

Two electrons cannot be labeled, tagged, or tracked as "electron A" and "electron B" in the way two classical billiard balls can, even in principle: there is no experiment that could determine which of two electrons in a helium atom is "the same one" that was there a moment ago, because the electrons have no individual identity beyond their species. This is stronger than the classical statement that identical objects are hard to tell apart; it is the claim that the question "which one is which" has no physical meaning at all.

## 1.2 The exchange operator

For a two-particle state ψ(1,2), where the arguments label which particle's coordinates (position, spin, or both) occupy which slot, define the exchange operator

$$ P̂₁₂ ψ(1,2) = ψ(2,1)

Since indistinguishability requires every observable prediction to be unchanged by relabeling which particle is called "1" and which is called "2," the exchange operator must commute with the Hamiltonian of any system of identical particles: [Ĥ, P̂₁₂] = 0, exactly the compatibility condition of Lesson 10, Section 5.1, and by that lesson's reasoning, energy eigenstates can be chosen to be simultaneous eigenstates of P̂₁₂.

## 1.3 The eigenvalues of the exchange operator

Applying P̂₁₂ twice returns the original labeling, P̂₁₂²ψ(1,2) = ψ(1,2), so P̂₁₂² = I. The eigenvalues of P̂₁₂ therefore satisfy λ² = 1, giving λ = ±1, exactly the same algebra as the parity operator of Lesson 10, Exercise 13. A state with λ = +1 is called symmetric under exchange; a state with λ = −1 is called antisymmetric.

### Worked example 1

Verify P̂₁₂² = I directly. Applying P̂₁₂ once to ψ(1,2) gives ψ(2,1); applying it again exchanges the labels once more, giving ψ(1,2) back, confirming that two exchanges is the identity operation, exactly as two reflections (Lesson 10, Exercise 13) or two applications of any order-2 symmetry return the original state.

# 2 The symmetrization postulate

## 2.1 Statement

Every particle in nature is either a boson, whose multi-particle wave functions are always symmetric under exchange of any two identical particles, or a fermion, whose multi-particle wave functions are always antisymmetric under such an exchange. This is an empirical postulate, not a derivation from the preceding sections alone (which only established that ±1 are the possible eigenvalues, not that a given species is locked into one or the other); Section 6 states the deeper, relativistic reason, the spin-statistics theorem, that connects the choice to the particle's spin.

## 2.2 Symmetrized and antisymmetrized states

Given two single-particle states |a⟩ and |b⟩ (a and b distinct), the only two-particle states consistent with the symmetrization postulate are

$$ |ψ_S(1,2)⟩ = (1/√2) [ |a⟩₁|b⟩₂ + |b⟩₁|a⟩₂ ],    |ψ_A(1,2)⟩ = (1/√2) [ |a⟩₁|b⟩₂ − |b⟩₁|a⟩₂ ]

for bosons and fermions respectively, where the subscript indicates which particle occupies which single-particle state. Direct application of P̂₁₂ confirms P̂₁₂ψ_S = +ψ_S and P̂₁₂ψ_A = −ψ_A, as required. If a = b (both particles assigned the same single-particle state), ψ_S is still well-defined and normalizable, but ψ_A vanishes identically, the algebraic seed of the exclusion principle developed in Section 3.

### Worked example 2

Verify P̂₁₂ψ_A = −ψ_A. Applying the exchange operator to |ψ_A(1,2)⟩ = (1/√2)[|a⟩₁|b⟩₂−|b⟩₁|a⟩₂] swaps the particle labels, giving (1/√2)[|a⟩₂|b⟩₁−|b⟩₂|a⟩₁] = (1/√2)[|b⟩₁|a⟩₂−|a⟩₁|b⟩₂] = −ψ_A(1,2), confirming the antisymmetric eigenvalue −1.

# 3 The Pauli exclusion principle

## 3.1 Statement and derivation

Setting a = b in the antisymmetrized combination of Section 2.2 gives ψ_A = (1/√2)[|a⟩₁|a⟩₂−|a⟩₁|a⟩₂] = 0: two fermions cannot occupy the same single-particle quantum state. This is the Pauli exclusion principle, and it follows directly and only from the antisymmetry required of fermions; there is no analogous restriction for bosons, whose symmetric wave function ψ_S remains perfectly well-defined and normalizable when a = b, and indeed bosons show the opposite tendency, examined in Section 4.

## 3.2 Consequences

The exclusion principle is the reason atoms have shell structure rather than every electron collapsing into the lowest orbital, the reason ordinary matter occupies space and resists compression (electron degeneracy pressure, ultimately what supports white dwarf stars against gravitational collapse), and the reason chemistry has the periodic structure it does. Every one of these macroscopic facts is a direct, large-scale consequence of the single algebraic statement ψ_A(a,a) = 0.

### Worked example 3

Two noninteracting fermions are placed in a one-dimensional infinite well (Lesson 9, Worked example 4). If both were allowed in the ground state φ₁, the naive lowest energy would be 2E₁. The exclusion principle forbids this; the true ground state places one fermion in φ₁ and the other in φ₂, giving total energy E₁+E₂ = (1+4)π²ℏ²/(2mL²) = 5π²ℏ²/(2mL²), the antisymmetrized combination ψ_A = (1/√2)[φ₁(x₁)φ₂(x₂)−φ₂(x₁)φ₁(x₂)]. This is a direct, computable illustration of how the exclusion principle raises the ground-state energy of a many-fermion system above what noninteracting classical intuition would suggest.

# 4 Exchange energy

## 4.1 Setup

Consider two identical particles with a genuine interaction, such as the Coulomb repulsion between two electrons, Ĥ = Ĥ₀(1)+Ĥ₀(2)+V(|x₁−x₂|), where Ĥ₀ is the one-particle Hamiltonian for each and V is the interparticle interaction. Treating V as a perturbation (Lesson 15) evaluated on the properly symmetrized or antisymmetrized states of Section 2.2 gives an energy shift with two distinct pieces.

## 4.2 Direct and exchange integrals

$$ ⟨ψ_(S,A)|V|ψ_(S,A)⟩ = J ± K,    J = ∫|φₐ(x₁)|²|φᵦ(x₂)|²V(|x₁−x₂|)d³x₁d³x₂,    K = ∫φₐ*(x₁)φᵦ*(x₂)V(|x₁−x₂|)φᵦ(x₁)φₐ(x₂)d³x₁d³x₂

with the + sign for the symmetric spatial state and the − sign for the antisymmetric spatial state (the sign convention depends on whether the spatial part of the full wave function, including spin, is symmetric or antisymmetric, as clarified in Section 4.3). J is the direct integral, with a straightforward classical-electrostatics interpretation as the interaction energy of two charge distributions |φₐ|² and |φᵦ|²; K is the exchange integral, with no classical counterpart whatsoever, arising purely from the interference between the two terms of the (anti)symmetrized wave function, exactly the kind of cross term encountered abstractly in Lesson 9, Section 6.2.

## 4.3 Combining spatial and spin symmetry

For spin-1/2 fermions, the full two-electron wave function is a product of a spatial part and a spin part, and it is the full product that must be antisymmetric overall; since the spin singlet of Lesson 14, Section 6.2, is antisymmetric and the spin triplet is symmetric, an antisymmetric spatial wave function must pair with the symmetric spin triplet, and a symmetric spatial wave function must pair with the antisymmetric spin singlet. This coupling of spatial and spin symmetry is what allows an interaction, V, which does not itself depend on spin, nonetheless to produce a spin-dependent energy, since the sign of K in Section 4.2 depends on which spatial symmetry, and hence which spin state, is being considered.

### Worked example 4

Explain, using Sections 4.2 and 4.3, why the two electrons in a helium atom's ground state (both spatially in the 1s orbital) must be in the spin singlet. If both electrons occupy the same spatial orbital φₐ = φᵦ, the spatial wave function is automatically symmetric under exchange (swapping identical factors does nothing), and by Section 4.3 this symmetric spatial state must pair with the antisymmetric spin singlet; the spin triplet would require an antisymmetric spatial wave function, but with a = b that vanishes identically, exactly as in Section 3.1. This is the exclusion principle, reinterpreted through the lens of combined spatial-spin antisymmetry: it is not that "no two electrons with the same spin can share an orbital" as an independent rule, but a direct consequence of Section 3.1 applied to the full two-particle state.

# 5 More than two particles

## 5.1 General N-particle symmetrization

For N identical bosons, the wave function must be symmetric under exchange of any pair of particles; for N identical fermions, it must be antisymmetric under exchange of any pair. Given N single-particle states φ₁,…,φₙ (one particle per state, since repeats will vanish for fermions by Section 3.1's argument extended to N particles), the properly antisymmetrized N-fermion state is constructed as a determinant.

## 5.2 The Slater determinant

$$ ψ_A(1,…,N) = (1/√(N!)) det [ φᵢ(xⱼ) ]

where the (i,j) entry of the N×N matrix is the ith single-particle state evaluated for the jth particle's coordinates. Exchanging any two particles corresponds to exchanging two columns of the determinant, which by a standard property of determinants multiplies the whole expression by −1, automatically enforcing antisymmetry for every pair, not merely for a specially chosen one. If any two of the single-particle states φᵢ coincide, two rows of the determinant become identical and it vanishes identically, reproducing the N-particle Pauli exclusion principle directly from a familiar fact of linear algebra (Lesson 1, Section 4.3): a matrix with two identical rows has zero determinant.

### Worked example 5

Write the Slater determinant for two fermions explicitly and confirm it reproduces ψ_A of Section 2.2. For N=2, ψ_A(1,2) = (1/√2!) det[[φₐ(x₁),φᵦ(x₁)],[φₐ(x₂),φᵦ(x₂)]] = (1/√2)[φₐ(x₁)φᵦ(x₂)−φᵦ(x₁)φₐ(x₂)], exactly matching Section 2.2 with the labeling of particles and states made explicit; the Slater determinant is simply the systematic, scalable generalization of the two-particle antisymmetrization to any number of fermions.

## 5.3 Bosons and permanents

For N identical bosons, the analogous symmetrized construction replaces the determinant with a permanent, the same sum of products over all permutations but with every term added rather than alternately signed. Unlike fermions, any number of bosons can share the same single-particle state, and indeed bosons show a statistical preference for doing so (a phenomenon underlying Bose-Einstein condensation), the polar opposite of fermionic exclusion; this course, being centered on the electron and photon, uses the boson case chiefly in Lesson 34's construction of the multi-photon Fock space, where the number of photons occupying a single mode is unrestricted.

# 6 Spin and statistics

## 6.1 The empirical rule at this stage

Every particle with half-integer spin (electrons, protons, neutrons, quarks) is observed to be a fermion, and every particle with integer spin (photons, and the pion of Lesson 17's exercises) is observed to be a boson. At the level of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics developed in this course so far, this connection is an empirical fact, stated but not derived; nothing in Sections 1 through 5 forced spin-1/2 particles specifically to be fermions.

## 6.2 Where the connection is actually proved

The spin-statistics theorem, which proves that a consistent, causal, Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory of a half-integer-spin field necessarily requires anticommutation relations (and hence Fermi statistics), while an integer-spin field necessarily requires commutation relations (and hence Bose statistics), is a genuinely relativistic, field-theoretic result. It cannot be proven within nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, and this course does not attempt a full proof; the structural reason, that a relativistically causal theory of a half-integer-spin field is inconsistent unless its quanta anticommute, becomes visible once the Dirac field is quantized in Lesson 36, where the choice of anticommutators rather than commutators is shown to be forced, not assumed, by the requirement of a sensible, positive-energy theory.

### Worked example 6

State, without proof, what would go wrong if the electron field of Lesson 35 were quantized with commutation relations instead of anticommutation relations. The resulting theory would either fail to have a lowest-energy (ground) state, since the field equation's negative-frequency solutions would then correspond to negative-energy excitations that could be created without bound (a difficulty already visible classically for the Dirac equation in Lesson 22), or would fail to respect the exclusion principle central to atomic structure; Lesson 36 shows explicitly that anticommutation relations resolve both problems simultaneously, providing the promised structural reason behind the empirical rule of Section 6.1.

In Lesson 54, electron-electron scattering will be found to receive contributions from two Feynman diagrams differing by an exchange of the two outgoing electron lines, related to each other by exactly the antisymmetrization studied here, and the relative minus sign between them, forced by fermionic statistics, is directly responsible for physical effects, such as the vanishing of the scattering amplitude in certain kinematic configurations, that have no analogue for otherwise identical bosonic scattering processes.

# 8 Summary

1. Identical particles cannot be individually labeled; the exchange operator P̂₁₂ commutes with the Hamiltonian and has eigenvalues ±1.

2. The symmetrization postulate assigns every particle species to one of two classes: bosons (symmetric wave functions) or fermions (antisymmetric wave functions).

3. Antisymmetrized two-fermion states vanish if both particles occupy the same single-particle state, giving the Pauli exclusion principle.

4. Interacting identical particles acquire a purely quantum exchange energy, K, in addition to the classical-looking direct energy, J, with the relative sign fixed by spatial symmetry.

5. Overall antisymmetry for fermions couples spatial and spin symmetry, so a spin-independent interaction can still produce spin-dependent energies.

6. The Slater determinant generalizes two-fermion antisymmetrization to N fermions and automatically enforces the exclusion principle via a vanishing determinant.

7. Bosons show the opposite statistical tendency, favoring shared occupation of a single state, described by permanents rather than determinants.

8. The connection between spin and statistics is empirical at the level of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics; it is proved as a structural necessity only once fields are quantized relativistically (Lesson 36).

# Solutions

**1.** ψ_sym = (1/2)[ψ(1,2)+ψ(2,1)] satisfies P̂₁₂ψ_sym = (1/2)[ψ(2,1)+ψ(1,2)] = ψ_sym (eigenvalue +1), and ψ_antisym = (1/2)[ψ(1,2)−ψ(2,1)] satisfies P̂₁₂ψ_antisym = (1/2)[ψ(2,1)−ψ(1,2)] = −ψ_antisym (eigenvalue −1); their sum is ψ(1,2), confirming the decomposition. Only ψ_sym is physically realized for bosons and only ψ_antisym for fermions; the other component of a generic ψ is simply discarded as unphysical for identical particles.

**2.** ⟨ψ_S\|ψ_S⟩ = (1/2)[⟨a\|a⟩⟨b\|b⟩+⟨a\|b⟩⟨b\|a⟩+⟨b\|a⟩⟨a\|b⟩+⟨b\|b⟩⟨a\|a⟩] = (1/2)[1+0+0+1] = 1, using orthonormality ⟨a\|b⟩=0. The same calculation with the two cross terms subtracted instead of added gives ⟨ψ_A\|ψ_A⟩ = (1/2)[1−0−0+1] = 1.

**3.** ⟨ψ_S\|ψ_A⟩ = (1/2)[⟨a\|a⟩⟨b\|b⟩−⟨a\|b⟩⟨b\|a⟩+⟨b\|a⟩⟨a\|b⟩−⟨b\|b⟩⟨a\|a⟩] = (1/2)[1−0+0−1] = 0.

**4.** Three fermions must occupy three distinct levels, φ₁, φ₂, φ₃, giving total energy (1+4+9)π²ℏ²/(2mL²) = 14π²ℏ²/(2mL²). Unrestricted distinguishable particles could all sit in φ₁, giving 3π²ℏ²/(2mL²), far lower; the exclusion principle raises the achievable ground-state energy substantially, exactly the mechanism behind electron degeneracy pressure mentioned in Section 3.2.

**5.** With three identical columns [φ(x₁),φ(x₂),φ(x₃)] appearing three times in the 3×3 matrix (once for each of the three particles all assigned the same single-particle state φ), the determinant has (at least) two identical columns and vanishes by the standard determinant property, generalizing the two-row argument of Section 5.2 to three particles directly.

**6.** J = ∫\|φₐ\|²\|φᵦ\|²V d³x₁d³x₂ is a weighted average of V(\|x₁−x₂\|) with manifestly nonnegative weights \|φₐ\|²\|φᵦ\|² ≥ 0, so J > 0 whenever V > 0 everywhere. K = ∫φₐ*φᵦ*Vφᵦφₐ d³x₁d³x₂ involves the product φₐ*(x₁)φᵦ(x₁) and its counterpart at x₂, which need not be positive or even real-valued pointwise (though the full integral is real by Hermiticity), so its sign depends on the detailed overlap structure of φₐ and φᵦ, not on the sign of V alone.

**7.** With K > 0, the antisymmetric spatial state (energy J−K, from Section 4.2's − sign) is lower than the symmetric spatial state (energy J+K); by Section 4.3, the antisymmetric spatial state pairs with the symmetric spin triplet, so the triplet configuration is energetically favored, exactly the statement of Hund's rule that electrons in different orbitals prefer parallel spins.

**8.** If φᵢ = φⱼ for some i≠j, then row i and row j of the matrix [φₖ(xₗ)] are identical (both equal φᵢ evaluated at each xₗ), and a determinant with two identical rows is zero, a standard fact of linear algebra following from the antisymmetry of the determinant under row exchange: swapping two identical rows both flips the sign and leaves the determinant unchanged, forcing it to equal its own negative, hence zero.

**9.** Exchanging particles 2 and 3 corresponds to exchanging columns 2 and 3 of the 3×3 matrix [φᵢ(xⱼ)]; by the defining antisymmetry of the determinant under column exchange, this multiplies the determinant by −1, confirming ψ_A(1,3,2) = −ψ_A(1,2,3), the required antisymmetry under this particular pairwise exchange (and, by an identical argument, under exchanging any other pair).

**10.** ψ_S(1,2) = (1/√2)[\|a⟩₁\|b⟩₂+\|b⟩₁\|a⟩₂]. Since the two particles are indistinguishable, the question "which one is particle 1" is not physically meaningful; operationally, a measurement of which single-particle state is occupied by each of the two particles will find one particle in \|a⟩ and the other in \|b⟩ with certainty (probability 1), since ψ_S is entirely supported on the subspace where one particle is in each state, never both in the same one.

**11.** The properly normalized state is simply \|a⟩₁\|a⟩₂ (no antisymmetrization needed or possible, since exchanging identical labels here changes nothing at all): this state trivially satisfies P̂₁₂ψ = +ψ, the symmetric eigenvalue required for bosons, and it is perfectly normalizable, unlike the fermionic case where the analogous construction identically vanishes.

**12.** Since P̂₁₂² = I is forced by the physical requirement that two exchanges restore the original labeling (Section 1.3), any eigenvalue λ of P̂₁₂ must satisfy λ² = 1 (applying P̂₁₂ twice to an eigenstate gives λ²ψ = ψ), and the only solutions of λ²=1 over the complex numbers are λ = +1 and λ = −1; no other value is algebraically possible.

**13.** The helium ground state is realized in the spin singlet, as derived in Worked example 4, because both electrons occupy the same (1s) spatial orbital, forcing a symmetric spatial wave function that must pair with the antisymmetric singlet. No first-excited configuration exists with both electrons still in 1s, because the exclusion principle (Section 3.1) forbids the only alternative spin state (the triplet) once both electrons share a spatial orbital; the first excited states of helium instead promote one electron to a higher orbital (such as 2s), reopening the possibility of either singlet or triplet spin states for that configuration.

**14.** Such a particle would directly violate the spin-statistics theorem of Section 6.2, which is not an empirical accident but a consequence, proved from first principles in a relativistic quantum field theory, of requiring a causal, stable (positive-energy), Lorentz-invariant description of an integer-spin field; observing a genuine violation would indicate that one of these foundational requirements, causality, stability, or Lorentz invariance, fails for the new particle, a far more serious conclusion than an isolated surprising fact, hence the demand for extraordinary scrutiny of any such claimed observation.

**15.** J = ∫\|φₐ(x₁)\|²\|φᵦ(x₂)\|²V(\|x₁−x₂\|)d³x₁d³x₂ contains no reference to spin at all; it depends only on the spatial orbitals φₐ, φᵦ and the (assumed spin-independent) interaction V, so its numerical value is identical regardless of whether the accompanying spin state is a singlet or a triplet, confirming that only the exchange term K, through the spatial symmetry it multiplies, carries any spin dependence in this model.

**16.** Filling levels n=1 through n=N, the total energy is Σₙ₌₁^N n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²) = [N(N+1)(2N+1)/6]π²ℏ²/(2mL²), using the standard sum of squares. For large N this is approximately (π²ℏ²/2mL²)(N³/3), scaling as N³, dramatically steeper than the N¹ scaling (Nπ²ℏ²/2mL², all particles in the ground level) that unrestricted bosons could achieve; this N³ scaling is the one-dimensional toy model underlying the far more physically significant degeneracy pressure of a three-dimensional electron gas.
