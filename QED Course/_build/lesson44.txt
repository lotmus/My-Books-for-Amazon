# How to use this lesson

The interaction term eψ̄γ^μψA_μ has appeared since Lesson 39, and the value +ieγ^μ has been quoted as its associated vertex factor since as early as Lesson 22, always as a forward reference to be justified later. This lesson is where that justification is finally given. It requires one new piece of machinery, a first, minimal look at time-dependent perturbation theory applied to a quantum field, developed here just far enough to extract a single result: the first-order transition amplitude generated by the interaction term, evaluated between an incoming electron, an incoming photon, and an outgoing electron. Reading the mode expansions of Lessons 35 and 37 against this amplitude produces the vertex factor directly, with no unexplained sign or factor of i left over. The lesson closes with an equally important, often-skipped point: why this single vertex can never be a complete physical process on its own. This is the last lesson of Part VI. Expect five to seven hours.

## Learning objectives

- Derive the first-order term of the interaction-picture time-evolution operator, S^(1)=−i∫d⁴x ℋ_int(x), from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

- Combine this with ℋ_int=−ℒ_int (Lesson 39) to write S^(1)=ie∫d⁴x ψ̄γ^μψA_μ for the electron.

- Identify which piece of each field's mode expansion is responsible for annihilating an incoming electron, creating an outgoing electron, and annihilating an incoming photon.

- Substitute these pieces into S^(1), carry out the spacetime integral, and read off the vertex factor +ieγ^μ together with the momentum-conserving delta function.

- Show, using the on-shell conditions for the external electron and photon, that a single vertex requires p·k=0, forcing the photon's energy to vanish in the electron's rest frame.

- Explain why this kinematic constraint means the vertex is only ever a building block of a larger diagram, never a complete process by itself.

- State the vertex factor from memory and explain, in outline, the derivation that produces it.

# 1 A first look at time-dependent perturbation theory

## 1.1 The interaction picture

Split the full Hamiltonian as H=H_0+H_int, with H_0 the free (quadratic, already-diagonalized) part governing the mode expansions of Lessons 35 and 37, and H_int=∫d³x(−ℒ_int) the interaction. Define interaction-picture states by |ψ(t)⟩_I = e^(iH_0t)|ψ(t)⟩_S, removing the trivial free-field time evolution so that only the interaction drives any further change. Differentiating and using the ordinary time-dependent Schrödinger equation i∂_t|ψ⟩_S=(H_0+H_int)|ψ⟩_S for the Schrödinger-picture state,

$$ i ∂_t |ψ(t)⟩_I = H_int,I(t) |ψ(t)⟩_I , qquad H_int,I(t) = e^(iH_0t) H_int e^(−iH_0t)

an equation with exactly the same form as the ordinary Schrödinger equation, but with only the interaction, not the full Hamiltonian, generating the (now generally nontrivial) time dependence of the state.

## 1.2 The first-order S-matrix element

Writing |ψ(t)⟩_I=U_I(t,t_0)|ψ(t_0)⟩_I, the equation of Section 1.1 becomes an integral equation, U_I(t,t_0)=1−i∫_(t_0)^t dt′ H_int,I(t′)U_I(t′,t_0) (Exercise 1 derives this by direct integration). Solved iteratively, the first-order term (obtained by replacing U_I under the integral by its zeroth-order value, 1) is

$$ U_I^((1))(t,t_0) = − i ∫_(t_0)^t dt′ H_int,I(t′)

Taking t_0→−∞ and t→+∞, the standard scattering setup, and defining the S-matrix S=U_I(+∞,−∞), the first-order term is

$$ S^((1)) = − i ∫ d⁴x ℋ_int(x)

using H_int(t)=∫d³x ℋ_int(x,t) to combine the time integral and the implicit spatial integral into a single four-dimensional one. This is the one new formula this lesson needs; Part VII extends the same iterative construction to all orders and develops the machinery (Wick's theorem applied to the full, interacting fields) needed to evaluate it systematically for arbitrarily many vertices.

### Worked example 1

Using ℋ_int=−ℒ_int (Lesson 39, Worked example 4) and ℒ_int=eψ̄γ^μψA_μ for the electron (Lesson 39, Section 2.2), rewrite S^(1) directly in terms of the QED interaction Lagrangian. S^(1)=−i∫d⁴x(−eψ̄γ^μψA_μ)=ie∫d⁴x ψ̄γ^μψA_μ, the expression Section 3 evaluates between specific external states.

# 2 Reading external particles off the mode expansions

## 2.1 An incoming electron

The Dirac field's mode expansion (Lesson 35) is ψ(x)=∫d³p/(2π)³(2E_p)^(−1/2)Σ_s[b_s(p)u_s(p)e^(−ipx)+d_s†(p)v_s(p)e^(ipx)]. Acting on a one-electron state |p,s⟩ (normalized so that b_s(p)|p,s⟩ removes the particle, leaving the vacuum, with the momentum-space delta function collapsing the integral), only the b_s(p)u_s(p)e^(−ipx) term contributes; the d_s†(p) term would create, not annihilate, a particle, and has nothing to act on in an initial state containing no antiparticle. The net effect, standard in this kind of calculation, is that ψ(x) contracted against an incoming electron of momentum p, spin s, contributes exactly u_s(p)e^(−ipx), with the momentum integral's measure and normalization factors cancelling against the state's own relativistic normalization.

## 2.2 An outgoing electron

Similarly, ψ̄(x)=ψ†(x)γ⁰ contains a term ∫d³p′/(2π)³(2E_p′)^(−1/2)Σ_s′ b_s′†(p′)ū_s′(p′)e^(ip′x); contracted against an outgoing electron of momentum p′, spin s′ (i.e. this term is responsible for creating that particle in the final state), ψ̄(x) contributes ū_s′(p′)e^(ip′x).

## 2.3 An incoming photon

Lesson 37 established ⟨0|A^i(x)|k,λ⟩=ε_λ^i(k)e^(−ikx) for the photon field in Coulomb gauge. Contracted against an incoming photon of momentum k, polarization λ, A_μ(x) contributes ε_λ,μ(k)e^(−ikx) by the identical logic to Section 2.1: the annihilation piece of the field's mode expansion is the only one that can remove a photon from the initial state.

### Worked example 2

Explain why the specific process chosen here, e⁻(p)+γ(k)→e⁻(p′), uses the b(p) piece of ψ and not the d(p) piece. The b operators are associated with the electron (the particle, in the charge convention fixed since Lesson 22), and the d operators with the positron (the antiparticle); since the process being evaluated involves an electron in the initial state, not a positron, only the b(p) piece of ψ has a nonzero matrix element with that initial state.

# 3 Assembling the amplitude

## 3.1 Substitution

Substituting the three contractions of Section 2 into Worked example 1's S^(1),

$$ ⟨p′,s′| S^((1)) |p,s;k,λ⟩ = ie ∫ d⁴x [ ū_s′(p′) e^(ip′x) ] γ^μ [ u_s(p) e^(−ipx) ] [ ε_λ,μ(k) e^(−ikx) ]

The spinor and polarization factors carry no x-dependence and pull outside the integral, leaving only the exponential factors and the integral over x.

## 3.2 Momentum conservation

$$ ∫ d⁴x , e^(i(p′−p−k)x) = (2π)⁴ δ⁴(p′−p−k)

the standard Fourier representation of the delta function, already used in this form for wave packets earlier in the course. Assembling everything,

$$ ⟨p′,s′| S^((1)) |p,s;k,λ⟩ = ie , ū_s′(p′) γ^μ u_s(p) , ε_λ,μ(k) , (2π)⁴δ⁴(p′−p−k)

Writing this as i(2π)⁴δ⁴(p′−p−k)ℳ, the standard convention separating the universal momentum-conserving delta function from the process-specific amplitude ℳ, ℳ = e ū(p′)γ^μu(p)ε_μ(k). Stripping the external wavefunctions ū(p′), u(p), ε_μ(k), which will be replaced by whatever specific particles occupy each leg in a larger diagram, the object attached to this vertex, for every process built from it, is

$$ i e γ^μ

exactly the vertex factor previewed without derivation since Lesson 22, and confirmed in the sign-fix review of Lessons 4–33: this lesson supplies the derivation those earlier forward references assumed.

### Worked example 3

Explain why the factor of i in front of eγ^μ, rather than eγ^μ alone, is not an arbitrary convention but a direct consequence of Section 1.2's S^(1)=−i∫d⁴xℋ_int. The i in the vertex factor traces directly to the explicit −i in the definition of S^(1), itself forced by the −i in front of H_int(t) in the interaction-picture Schrödinger equation of Section 1.1, ultimately the same −i that appears in the ordinary time-dependent Schrödinger equation i∂_t|ψ⟩=H|ψ⟩; nothing about the vertex's own definition introduces this factor independently.

# 4 Why the vertex alone is not a complete process

## 4.1 The kinematic constraint

The delta function of Section 3.2 enforces p′=p+k for on-shell external particles, with p²=p′²=m² (both electron states on the mass shell) and k²=0 (an on-shell, massless photon). Squaring p′=p+k, p′²=p²+2p·k+k², giving m²=m²+2p·k+0, so

$$ p · k = 0

## 4.2 What this means physically

Evaluated in the electron's rest frame, p=(m,0), so p·k=mω−0=mω, where ω is the photon's energy in that frame (Exercise 11 works through this explicitly). Setting p·k=0 forces ω=0: a photon of zero energy, not a genuine physical photon at all. Since p·k is itself a Lorentz scalar, this conclusion (that no nonzero-energy photon can be absorbed in a single-vertex process consistent with both energy and momentum conservation for on-shell particles) holds in every frame, not only the electron's rest frame.

### Worked example 4

State what this implies about processes such as Compton scattering (γ+e⁻→γ+e⁻), which certainly do occur physically. Since a single vertex cannot, by Section 4.2, connect on-shell initial and final particles consistently, any real, observable process built from the interaction term must involve at least two vertices, with an intermediate particle connecting them that is not required to be on its mass shell (an internal, or virtual, line); this is exactly the structure of the Feynman diagrams Part VII constructs, with the single vertex derived in this lesson serving as their fundamental, repeated building block rather than as a diagram in its own right.

# 5 What remains for Part VII

The vertex factor ieγ^μ, derived here from a single application of first-order perturbation theory, is a fixed, universal object: the same factor multiplies every occurrence of an electron-photon coupling in every diagram, regardless of which larger process it appears in, exactly the property that makes Feynman-diagram bookkeeping possible in the first place. What is not yet available is a systematic way to combine two or more such vertices, evaluate the resulting sum over intermediate (virtual) momenta, and extract a physical cross section or decay rate; that machinery, extending Section 1's first-order calculation to all orders and organizing the result diagrammatically, is the subject of Part VII, starting with Lesson 45.

# 7 Summary

1. First-order time-dependent perturbation theory in the interaction picture gives S^(1)=−i∫d⁴xℋ_int(x)=ie∫d⁴xψ̄γ^μψA_μ for the electron.

2. Contracting ψ, ψ̄, and A_μ against an incoming electron, an outgoing electron, and an incoming photon respectively picks out u(p)e^(−ipx), ū(p′)e^(ip′x), and ε_μ(k)e^(−ikx) from their mode expansions.

3. Substituting and integrating over x gives ie ū(p′)γ^μu(p)ε_μ(k)(2π)⁴δ⁴(p′−p−k).

4. Stripping the external wavefunctions and delta function leaves the vertex factor, +ieγ^μ, matching every earlier forward reference in the course.

5. The on-shell conditions for a single vertex force p·k=0, which in the electron's rest frame requires a zero-energy photon.

6. Consequently, no genuine physical process is described by a single vertex alone; real processes require at least two vertices connected by an internal, off-shell line.

7. This vertex factor is universal, reused unchanged in every diagram built from ℒ_QED, a property Part VII's diagrammatic methods depend on.

8. Part VI is now complete: the QED Lagrangian, its field tensor, its coupling constant, and its fundamental vertex have all been assembled from first principles.

# Solutions

**1.** Integrating i∂_tU_I(t,t_0)=H_int,I(t)U_I(t,t_0) from t_0 to t, using U_I(t_0,t_0)=1, gives U_I(t,t_0)−1=−i∫_(t_0)^t dt′H_int,I(t′)U_I(t′,t_0), i.e. U_I(t,t_0)=1−i∫_(t_0)^t dt′H_int,I(t′)U_I(t′,t_0), the stated integral equation, exact and not yet perturbative.

**2.** Under the integral, U_I(t′,t_0) itself equals 1 plus a term already proportional to H_int; replacing it by exactly 1 discards all terms with two or more powers of H_int, keeping only the single-power (first-order) term. This is justified when H_int is weak, in the specific, quantitative sense established in Lesson 43: the dimensionless coupling e≈0.303 (and more precisely α≈1/137 controlling probabilities) is small enough that each additional power of H_int contributes a strongly suppressed correction.

**3.** U_I^(1)(+∞,−∞)=−i∫_(−∞)^(+∞)dt′H_int,I(t′)=−i∫_(−∞)^(+∞)dt′∫d³xℋ_int(x,t′)=−i∫d⁴xℋ_int(x), combining the separate time and space integrals into the single four-dimensional integral by definition of d⁴x=dt d³x.

**4.** S^(1)=−i∫d⁴xℋ_int(x)=−i∫d⁴x(−ℒ_int(x))=i∫d⁴xℒ_int(x)=i∫d⁴x(eψ̄γ^μψA_μ)=ie∫d⁴xψ̄γ^μψA_μ, using ℋ_int=−ℒ_int and ℒ_int=eψ̄γ^μψA_μ in turn.

**5.** The d operators are associated with the positron (Lesson 22's antiparticle assignment for the Dirac field, carried through Lessons 29 and 35); d_s†(p) creates a positron, not an electron, so it has zero matrix element with an initial state containing a single electron and no positron, leaving only the b_s(p) term (which annihilates an electron) to contribute.

**6.** The relevant term is ∫d³p′/(2π)³(2E_p′)^(−1/2)b_s′†(p′)ū_s′(p′)e^(ip′x) inside the full mode expansion of ψ̄(x)=ψ†(x)γ⁰; its exponential factor is e^(+ip′x) (a positive exponent, in contrast with the e^(−ipx) of the annihilated incoming electron), consistent with b_s′†(p′) creating, rather than annihilating, a particle.

**7.** Section 2.3 uses ⟨0|A^i(x)|k,λ⟩=ε_λ^i(k)e^(−ikx) because the photon field A_μ(x), sandwiched between the vacuum (representing "nothing left over" on that leg after the photon is absorbed) on the left and the incoming one-photon state |k,λ⟩ on the right, is exactly the matrix element describing the removal (annihilation) of that photon from the initial state; ⟨k,λ|A^i(x)|0⟩ would instead describe creating a photon, the matrix element relevant to an outgoing, not incoming, photon leg.

**8.** e^(ip′x)e^(−ipx)e^(−ikx)=e^(i(p′−p−k)x), combining the three exponents by the ordinary rule for multiplying exponentials with the same base; ∫d⁴x of this single exponential is, by the standard Fourier representation of the delta function (used already for wave packets earlier in the course), (2π)⁴δ⁴(p′−p−k).

**9.** ie ū(p′)γ^μu(p)ε_μ(k), with the external spinors ū(p′), u(p) and polarization vector ε_μ(k) removed (since these depend on the specific momenta and spins of whatever particles occupy the legs of a larger diagram, not on the vertex itself), leaves ieγ^μ as the fixed, reusable factor; this value was first quoted, as a forward reference pending this derivation, in Lesson 22.

**10.** Section 1.2 derived S^(1)=−i∫d⁴xℋ_int directly from iterating the interaction-picture equation i∂_t|ψ⟩_I=H_int,I|ψ⟩_I, itself following from the ordinary time-dependent Schrödinger equation i∂_t|ψ⟩=H|ψ⟩; the −i is inherited at every step from the i already present on the left-hand side of the Schrödinger equation, not introduced as an independent choice when the vertex factor was written down.

**11.** With p=(m,0,0,0) and k=(ω,k) (k the photon's three-momentum, |k|=ω since the photon is massless), and the metric diag(+,−,−,−), p·k=p^0k^0−p·k(three-vector part)=m·ω−0·k=mω, using that the electron's three-momentum is zero in its own rest frame.

**12.** Setting p·k=mω=0 (Section 4.1's result) with m≠0 (the electron has nonzero mass) forces ω=0. A photon is defined by its energy-momentum relation ω=|k| for a massless particle; ω=0 forces the photon's entire four-momentum to vanish, which is not a physically meaningful particle (no energy, no momentum, indistinguishable from no photon being present at all), so no genuine, detectable incoming photon can be absorbed by a free electron in a single-vertex process.

**13.** Compton scattering has a real photon and a real (on-shell) electron in both the initial and final state; Section 4.1 showed a single vertex cannot connect on-shell states in this way, so the process must proceed through at least two vertices, with an intermediate line between them (an electron propagator, in the diagrammatic language of Part VII) that does not need to satisfy the same on-shell condition as an external particle.

**14.** ψ̄(x)=ψ†(x)γ⁰ contains a d_s(p) term (unlike ψ(x), which contains d_s†(p), a creation operator), so it is ψ̄(x), not ψ(x), that can annihilate an incoming positron; that term contributes v̄_s(p)e^(−ipx). For an outgoing positron, the relevant term is instead the d_s′†(p′) piece of ψ(x) itself, contributing v_s′(p′)e^(ip′x). This is the standard, and easily misremembered, feature of antiparticle legs: an incoming antiparticle is read off from ψ̄ with a barred spinor v̄, and an outgoing antiparticle from ψ with an unbarred spinor v, the reverse of the particle assignment in Sections 2.1–2.2.

**15.** The vertex factor ieγ^μ was derived in Section 3.3 only after every momentum- and spin-dependent object (the spinors and polarization vector) had been explicitly stripped away, leaving a factor built purely from the fixed constants i, e, and the numerical gamma matrices; since none of these depend on p, p′, or k, the identical factor ieγ^μ can be inserted at any vertex in any diagram, with the specific external or internal momenta at that vertex supplied separately by whatever lines connect to it, exactly the modularity that makes Feynman-diagram bookkeeping tractable for arbitrarily complicated processes.

**16.** The three inputs were: first, the formula S^(1)=−i∫d⁴xℋ_int from first-order time-dependent perturbation theory (Section 1), which depends only on there being some interaction Hamiltonian and not on the specific field content, and so would be unchanged for a scalar field; second, the specific mode-expansion contractions of Section 2, which for a scalar field would use the scalar field's own creation and annihilation operators and plane-wave factors e^(∓ipx) with no spinor u(p) or ū(p′) attached, since a scalar particle carries no spin degrees of freedom; third, the interaction term itself, eψ̄γ^μψA_μ for the Dirac field (Lesson 39), which for a charged scalar field would instead be the different interaction term derived for the complex scalar in Lesson 38's Solutions 9–10, −iqA^μ(φ*∂_μφ−φ∂_μφ*), producing a different-looking (momentum-dependent, rather than a fixed γ^μ) vertex factor, since that interaction term itself involves derivatives of the scalar field rather than a fixed bilinear like ψ̄γ^μψ.
