# How to use this lesson

Every dynamical law in quantum electrodynamics is a differential equation, and every measurable prediction is an integral. The Schrödinger, Dirac, and Maxwell equations are statements about derivatives; amplitudes, cross sections, and loop corrections are integrals over positions or momenta. This lesson assembles the calculus needed to read those statements and to manipulate them with confidence.

The treatment is operational. Proofs are sketched only where the idea behind the proof later becomes a calculational tool. Work every example by hand, because the manipulations here, differentiating exponentials, integrating by parts, expanding to first order, and transforming between position and momentum descriptions, are repeated hundreds of times in later lessons. A learner who has met single-variable calculus before should budget eight to twelve focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Differentiate and integrate exponential, trigonometric, and Gaussian functions fluently, including complex exponentials.

- Use integration by parts and recognize when boundary terms vanish.

- Compute partial derivatives, gradients, divergences, curls, and Laplacians in Cartesian coordinates.

- Expand functions in Taylor series and control the size of the neglected terms.

- Move between a function and its Fourier transform, and use the transform to turn derivatives into multiplications.

- Solve linear ordinary differential equations with constant coefficients by the exponential ansatz.

- Recognize the wave equation, find its plane wave solutions, and read off a dispersion relation.

- Connect each of these tools to a specific step in later QED calculations.

# 1 Derivatives

## 1.1 The derivative as a linear approximation

The derivative f′(x) is the coefficient of the linear term when f is expanded around x. This is the definition to keep in mind, because it is the form in which derivatives enter physics: a small displacement produces a proportional small change.

$$ f(x + h) = f(x) + f′(x) h + O(h²)

The rules of differentiation are all consequences of this linear structure.

- Linearity: (αf + βg)′ = αf′ + βg′ for constants α and β.

- Product rule: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′.

- Chain rule: d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x)) g′(x).

- Quotient rule: (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′)/g², which follows from the product and chain rules applied to f·g⁻¹.

## 1.2 The exponential function

The exponential is the function that equals its own derivative. For any constant k, real or complex,

$$ d/dx e^(kx) = k e^(kx)

In the language of Lesson 1, e^(kx) is an eigenfunction of the derivative operator with eigenvalue k. This single fact explains why exponentials dominate physics: whenever an equation is linear with constant coefficients, an exponential trial solution converts calculus into algebra.

With k = iω purely imaginary, the Euler formula of Lesson 1 gives e^(iωt) = cos ωt + i sin ωt. Differentiating an oscillation is therefore multiplication by iω: the amplitude is scaled by ω and the phase is advanced by a quarter turn. A second derivative multiplies by −ω².

$$ d/dt e^(iωt) = iω e^(iωt),    d²/dt² e^(iωt) = −ω² e^(iωt)

### Worked example 1

Let φ(x, t) = e^(i(kx − ωt)), a plane wave moving in the positive x direction. By the chain rule, ∂φ/∂x = ik φ and ∂φ/∂t = −iω φ. The rule to memorize is that on a plane wave each space derivative produces a factor ik and each time derivative produces a factor −iω. In quantum mechanics these factors become momentum and energy, which is why the operators −i∂ₓ and i∂ₜ will represent p and E.

# 2 Integrals

## 2.1 The fundamental theorem

An integral of a derivative depends only on the values at the endpoints.

$$ ∫ₐᵇ f′(x) dx = f(b) − f(a)

In field theory the endpoints are usually at infinity, or on the boundary of a region of spacetime, and the fields are assumed to vanish there. This assumption turns many integrals of total derivatives into zero, and it is invoked, sometimes silently, every time a term is discarded from an action.

## 2.2 Integration by parts

Apply the fundamental theorem to the product rule (uv)′ = u′v + uv′ and rearrange.

$$ ∫ₐᵇ u v′ dx = [uv]ₐᵇ − ∫ₐᵇ u′ v dx

Integration by parts moves a derivative from one factor to the other at the cost of a sign and a boundary term. When the boundary term vanishes, a derivative acting to the right becomes minus a derivative acting to the left. This mechanism makes the momentum operator Hermitian (Lesson 10) and produces the Euler–Lagrange equations for fields (Lesson 26).

### Worked example 2

Evaluate ∫₀^∞ x e^(−x) dx. Take u = x and v′ = e^(−x), so that v = −e^(−x). The boundary term [−x e^(−x)]₀^∞ vanishes at both ends, because e^(−x) decays faster than x grows. The remaining integral is ∫₀^∞ e^(−x) dx = 1, so the answer is 1. Repeating the argument n times gives

$$ ∫₀^∞ xⁿ e^(−x) dx = n!

which defines the Gamma function Γ(n + 1) = n! and extends the factorial to non-integer arguments. Dimensional regularization (Lesson 66) relies on exactly that extension.

## 2.3 The Gaussian integral

$$ ∫₋∞^∞ e^(−ax²) dx = √(π/a),    a > 0

The proof squares the integral, writes the product as a double integral over the plane, and switches to polar coordinates, where the radial integral is elementary. Completing the square extends the formula to a linear term in the exponent.

$$ ∫₋∞^∞ e^(−ax² + bx) dx = √(π/a) e^(b²/(4a))

The result holds for complex b, and for complex a with positive real part. The Gaussian integral is the most frequently used integral in quantum theory: oscillator ground states, wave packets, the path integral (Lesson 79), and the loop integrals that follow the introduction of Feynman parameters (Lesson 66) all reduce to it.

### Worked example 3

Evaluate ∫₋∞^∞ x² e^(−ax²) dx. Differentiate the Gaussian formula with respect to the parameter a. On the left, d/da brings down a factor −x²; on the right, d/da √(π/a) = −(1/2)√π a^(−3/2). Comparing the two sides,

$$ ∫₋∞^∞ x² e^(−ax²) dx = √π / (2a^(3/2))

Differentiating under the integral sign with respect to a parameter is a standard technique. Later it generates the numerators of propagators and the moments of wave packets without any new integration.

# 3 Functions of several variables

## 3.1 Partial derivatives

For a function f(x, y, z, t), the partial derivative ∂f/∂x is computed by treating y, z, and t as constants. For smooth functions the order of mixed partial derivatives does not matter: ∂ₓ∂ᵧf = ∂ᵧ∂ₓf. The total change of f under a small displacement collects one term from each variable.

$$ df = (∂f/∂x) dx + (∂f/∂y) dy + (∂f/∂z) dz + (∂f/∂t) dt

If the point moves along a path x(t), y(t), z(t), the chain rule gives the total time derivative

$$ df/dt = ∂f/∂t + (dx/dt) ∂f/∂x + (dy/dt) ∂f/∂y + (dz/dt) ∂f/∂z

The partial derivative ∂f/∂t is the rate of change at a fixed point; the total derivative df/dt is the rate of change seen by an observer riding along the path. Hamiltonian mechanics (Lesson 6) and the continuity equation (Lesson 23) depend on keeping the two apart.

## 3.2 Gradient, divergence, curl, and Laplacian

| Gradient ∇φ | (∂ₓφ, ∂ᵧφ, ∂_zφ) | Scalar to vector; direction and rate of steepest increase |
| Divergence ∇·A | ∂ₓAₓ + ∂ᵧAᵧ + ∂_zA_z | Vector to scalar; net outflow per unit volume |
| Curl ∇×A | (∂ᵧA_z − ∂_zAᵧ, ∂_zAₓ − ∂ₓA_z, ∂ₓAᵧ − ∂ᵧAₓ) | Vector to vector; local circulation |
| Laplacian ∇²φ | ∂ₓ²φ + ∂ᵧ²φ + ∂_z²φ | Scalar to scalar; how φ differs from its local average |

Two identities follow from the commuting of mixed partial derivatives and are used constantly.

$$ ∇ × (∇φ) = 0,    ∇ · (∇ × A) = 0

Every gradient is curl free, and every curl is divergence free. The converse statements hold in ordinary space: a curl-free field is a gradient and a divergence-free field is a curl. These are the facts that allow the electric and magnetic fields to be written in terms of potentials (Lesson 8).

## 3.3 Integral theorems

The divergence theorem converts a volume integral of a divergence into a flux through the bounding surface, and the Stokes theorem converts a surface integral of a curl into a circulation around the bounding curve.

$$ ∫_V ∇·A d³x = ∮_S A·dS,    ∫_S (∇×A)·dS = ∮_C A·dl

Both are higher-dimensional versions of the fundamental theorem: the integral of a derivative lives on the boundary. They convert the differential form of the Maxwell equations into the integral form (Lesson 7), and the four-dimensional divergence theorem is what allows total derivatives to be dropped from an action.

### Worked example 4

Let φ = x² + y² + z² = r². Then ∇φ = (2x, 2y, 2z) = 2r, and ∇²φ = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6. For the vector field A = (x, y, z), the divergence is ∇·A = 3 and the curl vanishes, since ∂ᵧA_z − ∂_zAᵧ = 0 and likewise for the other components. Check the divergence theorem on a sphere of radius R: the volume integral is 3 × (4/3)πR³ = 4πR³, and the flux is the surface value |A| = R times the area 4πR², also 4πR³.

# 4 Taylor expansions

## 4.1 The Taylor series

Repeating the linear approximation to higher order gives the Taylor series.

$$ f(x + h) = f(x) + f′(x) h + f″(x) h²/2! + f‴(x) h³/3! + ⋯ = Σₙ f⁽ⁿ⁾(x) hⁿ/n!

The expansions about zero that recur throughout the course are

$$ e^x = 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 + ⋯

$$ sin x = x − x³/6 + ⋯,    cos x = 1 − x²/2 + x⁴/24 − ⋯

$$ (1 + x)^α = 1 + αx + α(α − 1)x²/2 + ⋯,    ln(1 + x) = x − x²/2 + ⋯

Setting x = iθ in the exponential series and separating real and imaginary parts recovers the Euler formula, which is the cleanest proof of it.

## 4.2 Controlled approximation

In physics a Taylor expansion is always a statement about a small dimensionless parameter. Writing (1 + x)^α ≈ 1 + αx is legitimate when |x| ≪ 1, and the error is of order x². The discipline is to name the expansion parameter, state which power is kept, and estimate the first neglected term. Perturbation theory (Lesson 15) and the entire perturbative structure of QED, an expansion in the fine structure constant α ≈ 1/137, rest on expansions of this kind.

### Worked example 5

Expand the relativistic energy E = √(m²c⁴ + p²c²) for small momentum. Factor out the rest energy and use the binomial series with α = 1/2 and x = p²/(m²c²).

$$ E = mc² √(1 + p²/(m²c²)) = mc² + p²/(2m) − p⁴/(8m³c²) + ⋯

The first term is the rest energy, the second is the Newtonian kinetic energy, and the third is the leading relativistic correction, which contributes to the fine structure of hydrogen. The expansion parameter is (v/c)², so the series is useful precisely when the particle is slow.

# 5 Fourier analysis

## 5.1 Superposition of exponentials

Because exponentials are eigenfunctions of the derivative, any function written as a superposition of exponentials is easy to differentiate. Fourier analysis asserts that essentially every function met in physics can be written that way. For a function with period L,

$$ f(x) = Σₙ cₙ e^(2πinx/L),    cₙ = (1/L) ∫₀^L f(x) e^(−2πinx/L) dx

The coefficient formula follows from the orthogonality relation (1/L)∫₀^L e^(2πi(n−m)x/L) dx = δₙₘ. In the language of Lesson 1, the exponentials form an orthonormal basis of a space of functions, with the integral serving as inner product. Lesson 9 makes that function space precise.

## 5.2 The Fourier transform

Let the period grow without bound. The sum over n becomes an integral over a continuous wave number k, and the pair of formulas becomes

$$ f(x) = ∫ (dk/2π) f̃(k) e^(ikx),    f̃(k) = ∫ dx f(x) e^(−ikx)

The factor 2π is placed with dk throughout this course. This convention matches the momentum measures d³p/(2π)³ of field theory and keeps the position-space formulas free of constants. The Parseval relation ∫|f(x)|² dx = ∫(dk/2π)|f̃(k)|² states that the transform preserves the inner product, so it is a unitary change of basis in the sense of Lesson 1.

## 5.3 Derivatives become multiplications

Differentiate the inverse transform under the integral sign. Each derivative of e^(ikx) produces a factor ik, so

$$ f′(x) = ∫ (dk/2π) (ik) f̃(k) e^(ikx),  that is,  (f′)~(k) = ik f̃(k)

A differential equation with constant coefficients becomes an algebraic equation in k. In quantum mechanics this is the statement that momentum acts as −i∂ₓ in position space and as multiplication by p in momentum space (Lesson 12). In field theory it is the reason propagators are written as simple functions of momentum (Lesson 45).

## 5.4 The delta function

Substituting the transform into the inverse transform and exchanging the order of integration gives

$$ ∫ (dk/2π) e^(ik(x − y)) = δ(x − y)

The Dirac delta δ(x − y) vanishes for x ≠ y, has unit integral, and obeys the sifting property ∫ f(y) δ(x − y) dy = f(x). It is not a function but a distribution, an object defined by what it does inside an integral, and it should be used only there. Its three-dimensional version δ³(x) is a product of three one-dimensional deltas. Delta functions will appear in the commutation relations of quantized fields (Lesson 32) and in the factors (2π)⁴δ⁴(pₒᵤₜ − pᵢₙ) that enforce energy and momentum conservation in every amplitude (Lesson 50).

### Worked example 6

Transform the Gaussian f(x) = e^(−x²/(2σ²)). The transform integral is ∫ e^(−x²/(2σ²) − ikx) dx, which is the completed-square formula with a = 1/(2σ²) and b = −ik.

$$ f̃(k) = √(2πσ²) e^(−σ²k²/2)

A Gaussian of width σ in x becomes a Gaussian of width 1/σ in k. The product of the two widths is fixed: a function that is sharply localized in position is broad in wave number. This is the mathematical content of the uncertainty principle, before any physics is added.

# 6 Ordinary differential equations

## 6.1 First order linear equations

The simplest differential equation states that the rate of change of y is proportional to y.

$$ dy/dx = k y  ⇒  y(x) = y(0) e^(kx)

Real negative k describes decay, imaginary k describes oscillation, and complex k with negative real part describes damped oscillation. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation for an energy eigenstate is exactly this equation with k = −iE/ℏ (Lesson 11).

With a source term on the right, dy/dx − ky = g(x), the solution is the homogeneous solution plus a particular one, found with the integrating factor e^(−kx).

$$ y(x) = C e^(kx) + ∫^x e^(k(x − x′)) g(x′) dx′

The kernel e^(k(x − x′)) is the response at x to a unit source at x′. It is the first example of a Green's function, and the propagators of Lesson 45 are Green's functions of the free field equations.

## 6.2 Second order equations with constant coefficients

For a y″ + b y′ + c y = 0, try y = e^(λx). Every derivative brings down a factor λ, and after dividing by the exponential the differential equation becomes the characteristic equation.

$$ a λ² + b λ + c = 0

With two distinct roots λ₁ and λ₂ the general solution is y = A e^(λ₁x) + B e^(λ₂x); a repeated root λ gives y = (A + Bx) e^(λx). Because the equation is linear, any combination of solutions is a solution. The number of free constants equals the order of the equation, and they are fixed by initial conditions or boundary conditions.

## 6.3 The harmonic oscillator

$$ y″ + ω²y = 0  ⇒  λ = ±iω  ⇒  y = A e^(iωt) + B e^(−iωt) = C cos ωt + D sin ωt

This equation is the prototype for the whole course. Every mode of a free field is a harmonic oscillator (Lesson 32), and quantizing that oscillator produces the creation and annihilation operators of Lesson 33. The two exponential solutions with frequencies +ω and −ω are the classical ancestors of the positive and negative frequency parts of a quantum field.

### Worked example 7

Solve y″ + 2y′ + 5y = 0. The characteristic equation λ² + 2λ + 5 = 0 has roots λ = −1 ± 2i. Therefore

$$ y = e^(−t)(A cos 2t + B sin 2t)

a damped oscillation: the real part of λ sets the decay rate and the imaginary part sets the frequency. A resonance in particle physics has exactly this structure, with a complex energy whose imaginary part is the decay width (Lesson 59).

# 7 Partial differential equations

## 7.1 The wave equation

$$ ∂²φ/∂t² = c² ∇²φ

The wave equation governs vibrating strings, sound, and light. In units with c = 1, which the course adopts from Lesson 3 onward, it reads ∂ₜ²φ − ∇²φ = 0, and the operator □ = ∂ₜ² − ∇² is called the d'Alembertian. Adding a mass term turns the wave equation into the Klein–Gordon equation (Lesson 17).

## 7.2 Plane wave solutions and the dispersion relation

Try a plane wave φ = e^(i(k·x − ωt)). By Worked example 1, each time derivative gives −iω and each space derivative gives ik, so ∂ₜ² gives −ω² and ∇² gives −|k|². Substituting,

$$ −ω² φ = −c²|k|² φ  ⇒  ω = c|k|

The relation between frequency and wave number required for the plane wave to solve the equation is called the dispersion relation. For the Klein–Gordon equation it becomes ω² = |k|² + m², which with ℏ = c = 1 is the relativistic energy relation E² = |p|² + m² in disguise. Reading a dispersion relation off a field equation is a skill used in every later part of the course.

## 7.3 The general solution by superposition

Because the wave equation is linear, plane waves with every allowed k can be added. A real solution is

$$ φ(x, t) = ∫ (d³k/(2π)³) [a(k) e^(i(k·x − ωt)) + a*(k) e^(−i(k·x − ωt))],    ω = c|k|

In one dimension this collapses to the d'Alembert form φ = F(x − ct) + G(x + ct): an arbitrary shape moving to the right plus an arbitrary shape moving to the left. The coefficient functions a(k) will become the annihilation operators of the quantum field (Lesson 32). The quantum theory inherits the structure of the classical solution almost unchanged, which is why this expansion deserves careful attention now.

## 7.4 Diffusion and Schrödinger type equations

An equation that is first order in time behaves differently. The diffusion equation ∂ₜφ = D∇²φ, tested with a plane wave, gives ω = −iDk², so every mode decays. Replacing the real constant D by the imaginary combination iℏ/2m gives the free Schrödinger equation.

$$ iℏ ∂ₜψ = −(ℏ²/2m) ∇²ψ  ⇒  ℏω = ℏ²|k|²/2m,  that is,  E = |p|²/2m

The factor i converts decay into oscillation, which is one of the reasons quantum mechanics needs complex numbers (Lesson 1). Being first order in time also means that ψ at one instant determines ψ at all later times; a second order equation needs both the field and its time derivative. This difference between the Schrödinger and wave equations is the tension examined in Lesson 18.

### Worked example 8

Verify that φ = sin(kx) cos(ωt) solves the one-dimensional wave equation when ω = ck. Two time derivatives give ∂ₜ²φ = −ω²φ, and two space derivatives give ∂ₓ²φ = −k²φ. The equation ∂ₜ²φ = c²∂ₓ²φ therefore holds exactly when ω² = c²k². This standing wave is a superposition of a right-moving and a left-moving plane wave. If the string is fixed at x = 0 and x = L, only k = nπ/L is allowed; counting such modes in a cavity is the first step in quantizing the electromagnetic field (Lesson 37).

# 8 A first look at the calculus of variations

A functional assigns a number to a whole function. The action S[y] = ∫ₐᵇ L(y, y′) dx is the example that matters. The function y is stationary if the first order change of S vanishes under every small deformation y → y + εη with η(a) = η(b) = 0.

$$ δS = ε ∫ₐᵇ [(∂L/∂y) η + (∂L/∂y′) η′] dx

Integrate the second term by parts. The boundary term vanishes because η vanishes at the endpoints, and what remains is proportional to η. Since η is arbitrary, the coefficient must vanish.

$$ d/dx (∂L/∂y′) − ∂L/∂y = 0

This is the Euler–Lagrange equation. Applied to L = (1/2)y′² − (1/2)ω²y², it gives y″ + ω²y = 0, the harmonic oscillator of Section 6.3. The derivation is integration by parts followed by the fundamental lemma that a function integrating to zero against every test function is zero. Lesson 5 builds classical mechanics on this equation, and Lesson 26 extends it to fields.

# 10 Summary

1. The derivative is a linear approximation, and the exponential is its eigenfunction, so linear problems with constant coefficients reduce to algebra.

2. Integration by parts transfers derivatives between factors; vanishing boundary terms are a recurring physical assumption that should be stated, not assumed.

3. The Gaussian integral, together with differentiation with respect to parameters, covers most integrals met in quantum theory.

4. Gradient, divergence, curl, and Laplacian are the vector derivatives; the identities curl grad = 0 and div curl = 0 underlie the electromagnetic potentials.

5. A Taylor expansion is a controlled approximation in a named small parameter.

6. The Fourier transform turns derivatives into factors of ik and produces the delta function.

7. Linear ordinary differential equations are solved by the exponential ansatz; the harmonic oscillator is the prototype for every field mode.

8. The wave equation is solved by plane waves obeying a dispersion relation, and superposition builds every other solution.

# Solutions

**1.** f′ = e^(−x²)(1 − 2x²); g′ = −e^(−γt)(γ cos ωt + ω sin ωt); h′ = 2x/(1 + x²).

**2.** Each derivative multiplies e^(kx) by k, so y‴ = k³e^(kx). The equation y‴ = y requires k³ = 1, whose roots are k = 1, e^(2πi/3), and e^(−2πi/3). The real root gives e^x; the complex pair combine into e^(−x/2) cos(√3 x/2) and e^(−x/2) sin(√3 x/2), a damped oscillation.

**3.** ∫₀^∞ x² e^(−x) dx = [−x² e^(−x)]₀^∞ + 2∫₀^∞ x e^(−x) dx = 0 + 2 × 1 = 2, in agreement with 2! = 2.

**4.** With a = 2 and b = 3, the completed-square formula gives √(π/2) e^(9/8).

**5.** Differentiate √(π/a) = √π a^(−1/2) twice with respect to a: the result is √π (3/4) a^(−5/2). Two derivatives of the integral bring down (−x²)² = x⁴, so ∫ x⁴ e^(−ax²) dx = 3√π/(4a^(5/2)). For a = 1 this is (3/4)√π ≈ 1.329.

**6.** ∇φ = (yz, xz, xy). Each second partial derivative in ∇² is zero, so ∇²φ = 0. The x component of the curl is ∂ᵧ(xy) − ∂_z(xz) = x − x = 0; the other two components vanish the same way.

**7.** ∇·A = ∂ₓ(−y) + ∂ᵧ(x) = 0. The curl is (∂ᵧ0 − ∂_z x, ∂_z(−y) − ∂ₓ0, ∂ₓx − ∂ᵧ(−y)) = (0, 0, 2). The field describes rigid rotation about the z axis with unit angular velocity, and the curl is twice the angular velocity.

**8.** 1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + ⋯. With α = −1/2 and x = −v²/c², the binomial series gives (1 − v²/c²)^(−1/2) = 1 + v²/(2c²) + 3v⁴/(8c⁴) + ⋯.

**9.** cos x ≈ 1 − x²/2 + x⁴/24. At x = 0.2: 1 − 0.02 + 0.0000667 = 0.98007. The first neglected term, −x⁶/720 ≈ −9 × 10⁻⁸, does not affect the fifth decimal place.

**10.** Split the integral at zero: ∫₀^∞ e^(−(a + ik)x) dx + ∫₋∞^0 e^((a − ik)x) dx = 1/(a + ik) + 1/(a − ik) = 2a/(a² + k²).

**11.** Two factors of ik give (ik)² = −k². Transforming the equation gives −k²f̃ − m²f̃ = −1, so f̃(k) = 1/(k² + m²). By Exercise 10 with a = m, the inverse transform is f(x) = e^(−m\|x\|)/(2m). This is the Green's function of the operator −d²/dx² + m², and its momentum-space form 1/(k² + m²) is the ancestor of the propagators of Lesson 45.

**12.** The particular solution is the constant y = 2, the homogeneous solution is C e^(−3x), and y(0) = 0 gives C = −2. Hence y = 2(1 − e^(−3x)).

**13.** The characteristic roots are λ = 1 and λ = 2, so y = A e^x + B e^(2x). The conditions A + B = 0 and A + 2B = 1 give B = 1 and A = −1: y = e^(2x) − e^x.

**14.** Try y = Y cos Ωt: (ω² − Ω²)Y = F, so Y = F/(ω² − Ω²). The general solution adds C cos ωt + D sin ωt. As Ω → ω the response grows without bound: resonance. Denominators of the form 1/(ω² − Ω²), continued to complex frequency, are precisely the propagators met later in the course.

**15.** On the plane wave, ∂ₜ² gives −ω² and ∇² gives −\|k\|², so the equation becomes (−ω² + \|k\|² + m²)φ = 0, which has a nonzero solution exactly when ω² = \|k\|² + m². For F(x − ct), write u = x − ct: ∂ₜF = −cF′(u), ∂ₜ²F = c²F″(u), and ∂ₓ²F = F″(u), so ∂ₜ²F = c²∂ₓ²F.

**16.** Under y → y + εη, δS = ε∫ₐᵇ [(∂L/∂y)η + (∂L/∂y′)η′] dx = ε[(∂L/∂y′)η]ₐᵇ + ε∫ₐᵇ [∂L/∂y − d/dx(∂L/∂y′)] η dx. The boundary term vanishes because η(a) = η(b) = 0, and the integral vanishes for every η only if ∂L/∂y − d/dx(∂L/∂y′) = 0. For L = (1/2)m y′² − V(y), ∂L/∂y′ = m y′ and ∂L/∂y = −V′(y), so m y″ = −V′(y): Newton's second law for a particle in the potential V.

# Mastery checklist

- I can differentiate any product or composition of exponentials, powers, and trigonometric functions without hesitation.

- I know that on a plane wave ∂ₓ gives ik and ∂ₜ gives −iω, and I can read a dispersion relation off a linear field equation.

- I can integrate by parts, state the boundary term, and say why it vanishes.

- I can evaluate any Gaussian integral, including ones with a linear term or with polynomial prefactors.

- I can compute gradient, divergence, curl, and Laplacian in Cartesian coordinates and use the two vanishing identities.

- I can expand a function to a stated order in a named small parameter and estimate the first neglected term.

- I can transform a function to wave-number space with the course convention and recognize the delta function when it appears.

- I can solve a linear constant-coefficient ordinary differential equation with the exponential ansatz and fit the initial conditions.
