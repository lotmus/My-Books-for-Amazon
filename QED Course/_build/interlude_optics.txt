# How to use this interlude

Prologue 2 measured that a glass surface reflects about four photons in a hundred, drew the reflection as an arrow of length 0.2, and promised that the 0.2 would later come "from the dielectric of a real material." Lesson 7 has now produced electromagnetic waves, and Lesson 8 the potentials. This interlude keeps that promise. It takes Lesson 7's plane waves into matter and derives, with no quantum mechanics, the refractive index, Snell's law, total internal reflection, the Fresnel coefficients that give the 0.2, thin-film interference, the diffraction limit of a lens and the blue of the sky.

It is optional for a reader who already knows undergraduate optics; skim the worked examples and Section 7, then continue with Lesson 9. For everyone else it is the classical picture that the photon arrows of the prologues must reproduce when photons are many. Expect three to four hours.

## Learning objectives

- Show that a linear medium turns Lesson 7's wave equation into one with phase speed c/n, and relate n to the medium's response to the field.

- Explain normal dispersion with a driven-electron model and read off why violet bends more than red.

- Derive Snell's law from phase matching at a boundary and, independently, from Fermat's principle of stationary time.

- Compute critical angles and the decay length of the evanescent wave beyond them.

- Derive the normal-incidence Fresnel coefficient r=(n₁−n₂)/(n₁+n₂) and recover Prologue 2's arrow of length 0.2 for glass.

- Locate Brewster's angle and explain why reflected glare is polarized.

- Compute fringe spacings for two slits and thin films, including the half-wave sign from reflection.

- Apply the diffraction limit θ≈1.22λ/D to an eye and a telescope.

- Derive the 1/λ⁴ law of Rayleigh scattering from dipole radiation and state what classical optics cannot explain.

# 1 Light in a medium

## 1.1 The refractive index

In a linear, non-magnetic, uncharged medium the polarization of the material is proportional to the field, so ε₀ in Lesson 7's equations becomes ε₀ε_r. Repeating the curl-of-curl step of Lesson 7, Section 5.1, gives

$$ ∇²E − (n²/c²) ∂²E/∂t² = 0,    n = √(ε_r μ_r)

A plane wave e^(i(k·x−ωt)) solves it with |k| = nω/c. The frequency is fixed by the source and is the same on both sides of any boundary; the phase speed is v = c/n and the wavelength shrinks to λ = λ₀/n. Air has n=1.0003, water 1.33, crown glass about 1.52, diamond 2.42.

The slowing has a microscopic picture that matters for QED. The incident wave drives the bound electrons, each driven electron radiates, and in the forward direction the radiated waves lag the drive by a quarter period. Their sum with the incident wave is a wave whose phase advances more slowly: the "slower light" is the original wave plus the forward scattering of the electrons, which is how Feynman explains refraction in QED, using the electron–photon action of Prologue 3.

## 1.2 Dispersion

Model each bound electron as a damped oscillator of natural frequency ω_j, driven by the field (the Lorentz model). Solving the driven oscillator of Lesson 2 for N electrons per unit volume gives

$$ n²(ω) = 1 + (N e²/(ε₀ m)) Σ_j f_j/(ω_j² − ω² − iγ_j ω)

with oscillator strengths f_j and damping rates γ_j. Glass and water have their strong resonances in the ultraviolet, so across the visible band ω < ω_j and n grows with ω. This is normal dispersion: water has n≈1.331 for red and n≈1.343 for violet light, so violet bends more. Near a resonance the imaginary part of n describes absorption.

### Worked example 1

Yellow sodium light has λ₀=589 nm. Find its frequency and its wavelength in water (n=1.333). The frequency is f=c/λ₀=(2.998×10⁸ m/s)/(589×10⁻⁹ m)=5.09×10¹⁴ Hz, in air and in water alike. The wavelength in water is λ=λ₀/n=589/1.333≈442 nm. A diver sees the same yellow, because the eye responds to frequency, not to the wavelength in the medium.

# 2 Boundaries: Snell's law

## 2.1 Phase matching

Let a plane wave meet a flat boundary between media n₁ and n₂. The boundary conditions that follow from the integral forms of Lesson 7's equations (tangential E continuous and, for non-magnetic media, tangential B continuous) must hold at every point of the surface and at every instant. That is possible only if the incident, reflected and transmitted waves have the same frequency and the same component of k along the surface. With |k| = nω/c on each side,

$$ n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

which is Snell's law. The reflected wave has the same |k| as the incident one, so its angle equals the angle of incidence.

## 2.2 Fermat's principle

The same law follows from asking which path between a point A in medium 1 and a point B in medium 2 takes the least time. If the path crosses the boundary at a distance x along it, the travel time is

$$ T(x) = (n₁/c) √(a² + x²) + (n₂/c) √(b² + (L − x)²)

and dT/dx = 0 gives n₁ x/√(a²+x²) = n₂ (L−x)/√(b²+(L−x)²), which is n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Prologue 2, Section 3, explains why the stationary-time path wins: arrows on neighboring paths near it point the same way and add, and the rest cancel. Fermat's principle is the classical shadow of that sum over paths, and Lesson 79 makes it the stationary-phase limit of the path integral.

## 2.3 Total internal reflection

Going from the denser medium into the less dense one, sin θ₂ = (n₁/n₂) sin θ₁ reaches 1 at the critical angle

$$ sin θ_c = n₂/n₁

Beyond θ_c no transmitted wave can match the tangential k, and all of the power is reflected. The field on the far side does not vanish; it decays as e^(−κz) with

$$ κ = (ω/c) √(n₁² sin²θ₁ − n₂²)

an evanescent wave that carries no power on average but can leak through a second, nearby boundary. This is frustrated total internal reflection, the optical twin of quantum tunneling.

### Worked example 2

Find the critical angles of water, crown glass and diamond against air, and the escape angle of a ray leaving water at 30°. Water: θ_c=arcsin(1/1.333)=48.6°. Glass (n=1.52): arcsin(1/1.52)=41.1°. Diamond: arcsin(1/2.42)=24.4°, which is why a cut diamond traps light and returns it through the top. For the ray at 30° in water, sin θ₂=1.333×0.5=0.667, so θ₂=41.8°: the ray bends away from the normal, and an eye tracing it back straight places a submerged object higher than it is.

# 3 How much reflects: the Fresnel equations

## 3.1 Normal incidence

At normal incidence write the incident, reflected and transmitted fields as E₀, rE₀ and tE₀. Continuity of E gives 1 + r = t. For a plane wave in a non-magnetic medium the magnetic field is proportional to nE, and it reverses with the direction of travel, so continuity of tangential B gives n₁(1 − r) = n₂ t. Solving the pair,

$$ r = (n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂),    t = 2n₁/(n₁ + n₂)

The reflected power fraction is R = r², and the transmitted fraction is T = (n₂/n₁) t², the factor n₂/n₁ coming from the different energy flux per unit field in the two media. One checks R + T = 1.

### Worked example 3

Derive Prologue 2's arrow for glass. With n₁=1 and n₂=1.5, r=(1−1.5)/(1+1.5)=−0.2, so R=0.04: four photons in a hundred, an arrow of length 0.2. The transmitted fraction is T=1.5×(2/2.5)²=0.96, and 0.04+0.96=1. The minus sign is a half-turn of the arrow on reflection from the denser side; from inside the glass, going back towards air, r=(1.5−1)/(2.5)=+0.2 with no half-turn. This is Prologue 2's 0.2, now derived from ε_r=n²=2.25, as that prologue promised.

## 3.2 Oblique incidence and Brewster's angle

At oblique incidence the two polarizations differ. With the field perpendicular to the plane of incidence (s) and in it (p),

$$ r_s = (n₁ cos θ₁ − n₂ cos θ₂)/(n₁ cos θ₁ + n₂ cos θ₂)

$$ r_p = (n₂ cos θ₁ − n₁ cos θ₂)/(n₂ cos θ₁ + n₁ cos θ₂)

The overall sign of r_p depends on how the p field's direction is defined, and textbooks differ; R_p = r_p² does not. The p coefficient vanishes when n₂ cos θ₁ = n₁ cos θ₂. Combined with Snell's law this means θ₁ + θ₂ = 90°, and

$$ tan θ_B = n₂/n₁

At Brewster's angle the reflected light is purely s-polarized. For water θ_B=arctan 1.333=53.1°, for glass 56.3°. Light glancing off a lake or a road is therefore mostly horizontally polarized, and polarizing sunglasses, whose transmission axis is vertical, remove it.

# 4 Interference

## 4.1 Two beams

Two waves of the same frequency with amplitudes E₁, E₂ and a phase difference δ add to an intensity

$$ I = I₁ + I₂ + 2 √(I₁ I₂) cos δ

The last term is interference; it is the classical form of adding two arrows before squaring, Prologue 2's rule. For Young's two slits a distance d apart, a point at height y on a screen at distance L ≫ d sees a path difference d y/L, so bright fringes are spaced by

$$ Δy = λ L/d

## 4.2 Thin films

A film of index n and thickness d in air, viewed at normal incidence, returns two main waves: one from the front surface with r=(1−n)/(1+n)<0, and one from the back surface with r=(n−1)/(n+1)>0, delayed by the round trip. Their phase difference is

$$ δ = 4π n d/λ₀ + π

where the π is the relative half-turn of Section 3.1. The film is bright where 2nd = (m + 1/2)λ₀ and dark where 2nd = mλ₀, for m = 0, 1, 2, … A film much thinner than the wavelength (m=0, d→0) is dark for every color, which is the black spot that appears on a soap bubble just before it bursts.

### Worked example 4

A glass sheet n=1.5 with both surfaces counted. Prologue 2 said that two arrows of length 0.2 give a reflection between 0 and 0.16 depending on thickness. Including the transmission factor 0.96 for the back-surface wave (it crosses the front surface twice), the two-arrow maximum is (0.2+0.96×0.2)²=0.154. Summing all multiple reflections inside the sheet (the Airy formula, Exercise 12) gives a maximum of 4r²/(1+r²)²=0.16/1.0816=0.148, and still exactly 0 at the dark thicknesses. The arrow rule of the prologue is the first term of this series.

# 5 Diffraction and the resolution limit

## 5.1 Far-field diffraction

Treat each point of an aperture as a source of secondary waves (Huygens and Fresnel). Far away, the amplitude in a direction θ is the sum of those waves with phases k x sin θ across the aperture, so it is the Fourier transform of the aperture, the transform of Lesson 2. For a slit of width a the first zeros are at a sin θ = ±λ, and in general at a sin θ = mλ with m ≠ 0. For a circular aperture of diameter D the first zero of the transform, a Bessel function, lies at

$$ sin θ₁ = 1.22 λ/D

Two point sources closer in angle than θ₁ blur into one (Rayleigh's criterion). No lens design can beat this for a given aperture; only a larger D or a shorter λ can.

### Worked example 5

The eye in daylight has a pupil of D≈3 mm; take λ=550 nm. Then θ₁=1.22×550×10⁻⁹/(3×10⁻³)=2.2×10⁻⁴ rad, about 0.8 arcminute, close to the one-arcminute acuity of normal vision. Two car headlights 1.5 m apart are resolved out to 1.5/(2.2×10⁻⁴)≈6.7 km. The Hubble Space Telescope (D=2.4 m) reaches 1.22×550×10⁻⁹/2.4=2.8×10⁻⁷ rad, about 0.06 arcsecond.

# 6 Scattering and polarization of skylight

An electron bound far below its resonance (ω ≪ ω_j) responds to the driving field with a dipole moment p independent of frequency. An oscillating dipole radiates the time-averaged power

$$ P = p₀² ω⁴/(12π ε₀ c³)

so molecules scatter in proportion to ω⁴, or 1/λ⁴. Blue light at 450 nm is scattered (700/450)⁴≈5.9 times more strongly than red at 700 nm, and the sky away from the Sun is blue; at sunset the long path through air removes the blue from the direct beam. Violet is scattered even more but is weaker in sunlight and in the eye's response. A dipole does not radiate along its own axis, so light scattered through 90° is strongly polarized, which is the polarization pattern of the blue sky. Water droplets in clouds are much larger than λ, the 1/λ⁴ law does not apply, and clouds are white.

What classical optics cannot do is just as instructive. It predicts a smooth reflected intensity, never the clicks of single photons that Prologue 1 started from. It cannot produce stimulated emission, the photon-cloning process behind the laser, which needs a quantized field (Lesson 37). And its dielectric constant is put in by hand; QED computes the response of the electrons, and of the vacuum, from e and m.

# 8 Summary

1. In a linear medium Lesson 7's waves travel at c/n with n=√(ε_r μ_r); the frequency is unchanged at a boundary and the wavelength becomes λ₀/n.

2. Driven bound electrons give n(ω); below ultraviolet resonances n rises with frequency, so violet bends more than red.

3. Phase matching along a boundary, or Fermat's stationary time, gives n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂; beyond sin θ_c = n₂/n₁ reflection is total and an evanescent field remains.

4. The normal-incidence Fresnel coefficient r=(n₁−n₂)/(n₁+n₂) gives r=−0.2 and R=0.04 for glass, deriving Prologue 2's arrow; r_p vanishes at tan θ_B = n₂/n₁.

5. Interference adds amplitudes before squaring: Young's fringes are spaced by λL/d, and a thin film is bright where 2nd=(m+1/2)λ₀.

6. Diffraction limits any aperture to sin θ₁=1.22λ/D, and dipole radiation makes molecular scattering grow as ω⁴, which colors the sky.

# Solutions

**1.** In the medium, ∇·E=0, ∇·B=0, ∇×E=−∂B/∂t and ∇×B=μ₀ε₀ε_r ∂E/∂t. Taking the curl of the third, ∇×(∇×E)=∇(∇·E)−∇²E=−∇²E=−∂(∇×B)/∂t=−(ε_r/c²)∂²E/∂t², so ∇²E−(n²/c²)∂²E/∂t²=0 with n²=ε_r. A plane wave e^(i(k·x−ωt)) gives −k²+n²ω²/c²=0, so |k|=nω/c.

**2.** f=c/λ₀=2.998×10⁸/(633×10⁻⁹)=4.74×10¹⁴ Hz; v=c/n=1.97×10⁸ m/s; λ=633/1.52=416 nm.

**3.** With γ=0, n²=1+A/(ω₀²−ω²) with A=Ne²f/(ε₀m)>0. For ω<ω₀ the denominator is positive, so n²>1, and d(n²)/dω=2Aω/(ω₀²−ω²)²>0, so n increases with ω.

**4.** The boundary conditions must hold at every instant at each point of the surface. Fields oscillating at different frequencies on the two sides would match at one instant and fail at the next, so all three waves share ω. The wavelength then follows from |k|=nω/c and differs because n does.

**5.** dT/dx=(n₁/c)x/√(a²+x²)−(n₂/c)(L−x)/√(b²+(L−x)²). The first fraction is sin θ₁ and the second is sin θ₂, so dT/dx=0 is n₁ sin θ₁=n₂ sin θ₂. The second derivative is positive, so it is a minimum.

**6.** sin θ_c=1.333/1.52=0.877, θ_c=61.3°.

**7.** κ=(2π/λ₀)√(n₁²sin²θ−1)=(2π/500 nm)√(2.25×0.5−1)=(2π/500 nm)×0.354=4.44×10⁻³ nm⁻¹, so 1/κ≈225 nm, less than half a wavelength.

**8.** Adding n₂ times the first equation to the second: n₁−n₁r=n₂+n₂r, so r=(n₁−n₂)/(n₁+n₂) and t=1+r=2n₁/(n₁+n₂). Then R+T=[(n₁−n₂)²+4n₁n₂]/(n₁+n₂)²=(n₁+n₂)²/(n₁+n₂)²=1.

**9.** Water: r=(1−1.333)/2.333=−0.143, R=0.020. Diamond: r=(1−2.42)/3.42=−0.415, R=0.172, one reason diamond looks brilliant.

**10.** r_p=0 means n₂cos θ₁=n₁cos θ₂. Multiplying it by n₁sin θ₁=n₂sin θ₂ gives sin θ₁cos θ₁=sin θ₂cos θ₂, so sin 2θ₁=sin 2θ₂; since θ₁≠θ₂ for n₁≠n₂, 2θ₁=180°−2θ₂ and θ₁+θ₂=90°. Then sin θ₂=cos θ₁, and Snell's law gives tan θ₁=n₂/n₁.

**11.** Δy=λL/d=650×10⁻⁹×2.0/(0.25×10⁻³)=5.2 mm.

**12.** The reflected amplitude is −r+(1−r²)r e^(iδ)[1+r²e^(iδ)+r⁴e^(2iδ)+⋯]=−r+(1−r²)r e^(iδ)/(1−r²e^(iδ))=−r(1−e^(iδ))/(1−r²e^(iδ)). Its square modulus is r²·2(1−cos δ)/(1−2r²cos δ+r⁴)=4r²sin²(δ/2)/[(1−r²)²+4r²sin²(δ/2)], which is F sin²(δ/2)/(1+F sin²(δ/2)). The maximum, at sin²(δ/2)=1, is F/(1+F)=4r²/(1+r²)²=0.148 for r=0.2.

**13.** Bright reflection needs 2nd=(m+1/2)λ₀; the smallest is m=0, d=λ₀/(4n)=550/(4×1.33)=103 nm.

**14.** θ₁=1.22×2.2×10⁻⁶/10=2.7×10⁻⁷ rad=0.055 arcsecond, the same as Hubble in visible light, which is why ground telescopes need adaptive optics to reach it.

**15.** (700/400)⁴=1.75⁴=9.4.

**16.** The front-surface arrow has a half-turn and the back-surface arrow does not. When the film is so thin that the extra path adds almost no rotation, the two arrows of nearly equal length point in opposite directions and cancel, so the probability of reflection is close to zero for every color.
