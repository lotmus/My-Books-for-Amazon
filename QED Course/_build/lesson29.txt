TAGLINE: The energy-momentum tensors of the scalar, Dirac, and electromagnetic fields, angular momentum and boost charges, and the exchange of momentum between field and matter

# How to use this lesson

Lesson 27 proved Noether's theorem and Lesson 28 organized the symmetries of a relativistic theory. This lesson computes. For each of the three fields of QED it works out the conserved currents explicitly, starting with the energy-momentum tensor that comes from translation invariance, and checks the results against quantities already met: the energy density of the scalar field from Lesson 25, the Poynting vector of Lesson 7, and the Dirac current of Lesson 23.

Three points deserve attention. The canonical energy-momentum tensor of the electromagnetic field is neither symmetric nor gauge invariant, and the fix, an application of the ambiguity noted in Lesson 27, Section 6.1, is instructive. The Dirac field carries angular momentum in two parts, orbital and spin, and only the sum is conserved. And when the fields interact, the energy-momentum tensor of each is no longer conserved separately; the exchange of energy and momentum between the electromagnetic field and the charges is exactly the Lorentz force. Expect seven to nine focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Write the canonical energy-momentum tensor for any field theory and derive the energy and momentum densities.

- Compute T^(μν) for the scalar field and verify that plane waves carry momentum p = Ek/ω per unit energy.

- Compute T^(μν) for the Dirac field and show that on shell it gives the energy and momentum operators of the single-particle theory.

- Derive the canonical electromagnetic tensor, show it is not gauge invariant, and construct the symmetric gauge-invariant tensor.

- Show that T^(00) = (1/2)(E² + B²) and T^(0i) = (E × B)^i for the electromagnetic field.

- Derive the conserved angular momentum and boost charges from a symmetric energy-momentum tensor.

- Explain the spin contribution to the angular momentum of the Dirac field.

- Show that ∂_μT^(μν) = −F^(νλ)j_λ for the electromagnetic tensor and identify the Lorentz force density.

# 1 The energy-momentum tensor

## 1.1 The canonical tensor

Lesson 27, Worked example 3, showed that invariance under the four spacetime translations gives four conserved currents. For a set of fields φ_a they are

$$ T^μ_ν = Σ_a [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ_a)] ∂_νφ_a − δ^μ_ν ℒ,    ∂_μT^μ_ν = 0

and raising the second index with the metric gives T^(μν). The conserved charges are the components of the four-momentum.

$$ P^ν = ∫ d³x T^(0ν),    P⁰ = H = ∫ d³x T^(00),    P^i = ∫ d³x T^(0i)

The component T^(00) is the energy density, T^(0i) is the density of the i-th component of momentum, and T^(i0) is the flux of energy in direction i. Conservation of energy is ∂_tT^(00) + ∂ᵢT^(i0) = 0, the continuity equation for energy, and conservation of the i-th component of momentum is ∂_tT^(0i) + ∂ⱼT^(ji) = 0. The spatial components T^(ij) are the momentum flux, which is a pressure when i = j.

## 1.2 Why the tensor matters

The energy-momentum tensor is the source of gravity in general relativity, and this course does not need it for that. It is needed because it defines the local densities of energy and momentum from which the total charges are built, and because it determines how the energy and momentum of the fields are exchanged when they interact. The charge P^ν is unambiguous even when the local tensor is not, as Section 4 will show.

# 2 The scalar field

## 2.1 The tensor

For ℒ = (1/2)∂_αφ∂^αφ − (1/2)m²φ², ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ, and

$$ T^(μν) = ∂^μφ ∂^νφ − g^(μν) ℒ

which is symmetric in μ and ν. The energy density is T^(00) = φ̇² − ℒ = (1/2)φ̇² + (1/2)(∇φ)² + (1/2)m²φ² = ℋ, in agreement with Lesson 25, Section 3.2. The momentum density is T^(0i) = ∂⁰φ∂^iφ = −φ̇∂ᵢφ, so the total momentum is P^i = −∫d³x φ̇∂ᵢφ = −∫d³x π∂ᵢφ.

### Worked example 1

Compute the energy and momentum carried by the plane wave φ = A cos(k·x − ωt) with ω² = |k|² + m². With θ = k·x − ωt, φ̇ = Aω sin θ and ∂ᵢφ = −Ak_i sin θ, so T^(0i) = −φ̇∂ᵢφ = A²ωk_i sin²θ. Averaged over a cycle, sin²θ has mean 1/2 and ⟨T^(0i)⟩ = (1/2)A²ωk_i, while the average energy density was found in Lesson 25, Worked example 3, to be ⟨T^(00)⟩ = (1/2)A²ω². The ratio of momentum density to energy density is k_i/ω. Identifying E = ω and p = k, this is p/E, the velocity of a particle of energy E and momentum p (Lesson 3, Section 5.3). A wave of the free scalar field carries energy and momentum in exactly the proportion of a relativistic particle.

# 3 The Dirac field

## 3.1 The tensor

For ℒ = ψ̄(iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ, ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μψ) = iψ̄γ^μ, so

$$ T^(μν) = iψ̄γ^μ∂^νψ − g^(μν) ℒ

On solutions of the Dirac equation ℒ = 0, since ψ̄ multiplies (iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ = 0, so on shell T^(μν) = iψ̄γ^μ∂^νψ. The tensor is not manifestly symmetric; a symmetric form differing from it by a term of the type discussed in Section 4 exists and is used when the coupling to gravity is needed, but for the conserved charges the canonical form suffices.

## 3.2 Energy and momentum

The energy density is T^(00) = iψ̄γ⁰∂⁰ψ = iψ†∂_tψ = ψ†Ĥ_Dψ, where the Dirac equation i∂_tψ = Ĥ_Dψ (Lesson 19, Section 1.1) has been used. This is the Hamiltonian density found in Lesson 25, Section 5.3. The momentum density is T^(0i) = iψ̄γ⁰∂^iψ = iψ†∂^iψ = ψ†(−i∂ᵢ)ψ, so

$$ P^i = ∫ d³x ψ† (−i∂ᵢ) ψ = ∫ d³x ψ† p̂^i ψ

the expectation value of the momentum operator of Lesson 10 in the state ψ. The field-theoretic momentum reduces, for a single-particle solution, to the quantum-mechanical one.

### Worked example 2

Check the plane wave ψ = u(p)e^(−ip·x) with u†u = 2E (Lesson 19, Section 7). The energy density is ψ†(i∂_tψ) = E u†u = 2E². The momentum density is ψ†(−i∂ᵢψ) = p^i u†u = 2Ep^i, and the probability density is ψ†ψ = 2E, so per particle the energy is E and the momentum is p^i, as expected. The other component T^(i0) = iψ̄γ^i∂⁰ψ = Eψ̄γ^iψ = E · 2p^i = 2Ep^i, using ūγ^μu = 2p^μ (Lesson 23, Section 4.2), so for a plane wave T^(0i) = T^(i0): the momentum density equals the energy flux, as it must for a particle of momentum p and energy E, whose energy flows at velocity p/E (Exercise 9).

# 4 The electromagnetic field

## 4.1 The canonical tensor

For ℒ = −(1/4)F_αβF^(αβ), ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μA_λ) = −F^(μλ) (Lesson 26, Section 4.1), and the canonical tensor is

$$ T_c^(μν) = −F^(μλ) ∂^νA_λ + (1/4) g^(μν) F_αβ F^(αβ)

This has two defects as an energy-momentum tensor. It is not symmetric in μ and ν, and it contains the potential A_λ explicitly, so it changes under a gauge transformation A_λ → A_λ + ∂_λχ: the local energy density would depend on the choice of gauge, which is unphysical for a local density (Exercise 14). The physical energy density of the electromagnetic field is the gauge-invariant quantity (1/2)(E² + B²) of Lesson 7, so the canonical tensor cannot be the full story.

## 4.2 The symmetric, gauge-invariant tensor

The ambiguity of Lesson 27, Section 6.1, allows a term ∂_λX^(μλ) with X^(μλ) antisymmetric in μ and λ to be added to a conserved current without changing its conservation or the charge. For each fixed ν, take X^(μλ) = F^(μλ)A^ν, which is antisymmetric in (μ, λ). In vacuum ∂_λF^(μλ) = 0, so ∂_λ(F^(μλ)A^ν) = F^(μλ)∂_λA^ν. Adding this to the canonical tensor and using F^ν_λ = ∂^νA_λ − ∂_λA^ν,

$$ T_Θ^(μν) = T_c^(μν) + F^(μλ) ∂_λA^ν = −F^(μλ) F^ν_λ + (1/4) g^(μν) F_αβ F^(αβ)

This tensor is symmetric, since F^(μλ)F^ν_λ is symmetric under μ ↔ ν, and gauge invariant, since it is built from F alone. It differs from the canonical tensor by a total derivative of the required form, so the conserved charges P^ν are unchanged up to surface terms. Its energy and momentum densities are the familiar ones.

$$ T_Θ^(00) = (1/2)(E² + B²),    T_Θ^(0i) = (E × B)^i

### Worked example 3

Verify T_Θ^(00). With F^(0i) = −Eᵢ and F^(0)_i = F^(0j)g_(ji) = −F^(0i) = Eᵢ, the sum F^(0λ)F^0_λ runs over spatial λ = i and equals (−Eᵢ)(Eᵢ) = −E², so −F^(0λ)F^0_λ = E². The invariant is F_αβF^(αβ) = 2(B² − E²) (Lesson 25, Section 6.2), so (1/4)g^(00)F² = (1/2)(B² − E²). Adding, T_Θ^(00) = E² + (1/2)(B² − E²) = (1/2)(E² + B²). For the momentum density, T_Θ^(0i) = −F^(0j)F^i_j since g^(0i) = 0, and with F^i_j = −F^(ij) = ε_(ijk)B_k, T_Θ^(0i) = Eⱼε_(ijk)B_k = (E × B)ᵢ, the Poynting vector of Lesson 7, Section 6.2.

## 4.3 Properties

The electromagnetic tensor is traceless, T_Θ^μ_μ = −F^(μλ)F_(μλ) + F_αβF^(αβ) = 0 (Exercise 8), a reflection of the masslessness of the photon. For a plane wave, E ⊥ B and |E| = |B| (Lesson 7, Section 5.2), so |E × B| = E² and (1/2)(E² + B²) = E²: the momentum density has the same magnitude as the energy density, |T^(0i)| = T^(00) (Exercise 15). This is the field version of E = |p| for a massless particle (Lesson 3, Section 5.4), and it shows that light carries momentum equal to its energy divided by c.

# 5 Angular momentum and boosts

## 5.1 The angular momentum current

Invariance under Lorentz transformations gives six more conserved quantities. For a symmetric energy-momentum tensor the currents are

$$ M^(μαβ) = x^α T^(μβ) − x^β T^(μα)

and their conservation follows from a short calculation. By the product rule, ∂_μ(x^αT^(μβ)) = δ^α_μT^(μβ) + x^α∂_μT^(μβ) = T^(αβ), using ∂_μT^(μβ) = 0, so ∂_μM^(μαβ) = T^(αβ) − T^(βα) = 0 whenever T is symmetric (Exercise 16). The conserved charges are J^(αβ) = ∫d³x M^(0αβ). The spatial components J^(ij) = ∫d³x(x^iT^(0j) − x^jT^(0i)) are the components of the angular momentum, the moment of the momentum density, and the components J^(0i) are, up to sign, the boost charges.

## 5.2 The boost charge

The charge K^i = ∫d³x(x^iT^(00) − tT^(0i)) is conserved when T^(0i) = T^(i0) (Exercise 10). Dividing by the total energy, X_cm^i = ∫d³x x^iT^(00)/H is the position of the center of energy, and the conservation of K^i states that dX_cm/dt = P/H: the center of energy of an isolated system moves uniformly with velocity P/H. This is the analogue for energy of the statement that the center of mass moves with constant velocity in Newtonian mechanics.

## 5.3 Spin of the Dirac field

For the Dirac field the canonical tensor of Section 3 is not symmetric, and the conserved angular momentum is not just the orbital part ∫x × (momentum density). A term coming from the transformation of the spinor under rotations, the spin, must be added. The result is

$$ J = ∫ d³x ψ† ( x × p̂ + Σ/2 ) ψ

the sum of the orbital angular momentum x × p̂ of Lesson 13 and the spin Σ/2 of Lesson 21, Section 3. Neither part alone commutes with the Dirac Hamiltonian, but their sum does. For a particle at rest the orbital part vanishes, and by Lesson 21, Worked example 3, Σ_zu₁ = u₁, so the angular momentum is 1/2 per particle, the spin of the electron (Exercise 11). That the spin one-half of Lesson 14 appears as an ingredient of the Noether charge of rotations is one more confirmation that spin is angular momentum.

# 6 Interacting fields: exchange of momentum

## 6.1 The divergence of the electromagnetic tensor

When the electromagnetic field is coupled to a current j^ν, the Maxwell equation is ∂_μF^(μν) = j^ν (Lesson 26, Section 4.2), and the energy-momentum tensor of the field alone is no longer conserved. Differentiating T_Θ^(μν) = −F^(μλ)F^ν_λ + (1/4)g^(μν)F², the first term gives −(∂_μF^(μλ))F^ν_λ = −j^λF^ν_λ, and the remaining terms cancel by the Bianchi identity of Lesson 26, Section 4.2, so

$$ ∂_μ T_Θ^(μν) = −F^(νλ) j_λ

The right side is the rate at which the field loses energy and momentum to the charges.

## 6.2 Poynting's theorem and the Lorentz force

For ν = 0, −F^(0λ)j_λ = −F^(0i)j_i = −(−Eᵢ)(−jⁱ) = −E·j, and the left side is ∂_tu + ∇·S with u = (1/2)(E² + B²) and S = E × B, so

$$ ∂_t u + ∇·S = −E·j

which is Poynting's theorem of Lesson 7, Section 6.2, in Heaviside–Lorentz units. For ν = i, −F^(iλ)j_λ = −(F^(i0)j₀ + F^(ij)j_j) = −(ρE + j × B)ᵢ, so the field loses momentum at a rate equal to the Lorentz force density on the charges. The total energy and momentum, of field plus charges, are conserved, and what the field loses the charges gain. The whole of the classical electrodynamics of a charged particle in a field, the Lorentz force law of Lesson 5, is contained in the conservation of the total energy-momentum tensor.

### Worked example 4

Confirm the ν = 0 case from the components of Section 4.2. The left side is ∂_μT_Θ^(μ0) = ∂_tT_Θ^(00) + ∂ᵢT_Θ^(i0) = ∂_tu + ∇·S, using the symmetry T_Θ^(i0) = T_Θ^(0i) = Sᵢ. The right side is −F^(0λ)j_λ. Only λ = i contributes, with F^(0i) = −Eᵢ and j_i = −jⁱ, giving −(−Eᵢ)(−jⁱ) = −E·j. The equality is exactly the Poynting theorem: the rate of change of field energy density plus the outward energy flux equals minus the rate at which the field does work on the charges.

# 7 The electric current

The U(1) symmetry of the Dirac field gives the current of Lesson 23. Multiplying by the charge −e of the electron in the convention of Lesson 43 gives the electromagnetic current and the electric charge.

$$ j_(EM)^μ = −e ψ̄γ^μψ,    Q = −e ∫ d³x ψ†ψ

For a single electron described by a normalized wave function, ∫d³x ψ†ψ = 1 and Q = −e (Exercise 13). After quantization the integral becomes the number operator for particles minus antiparticles (Lesson 35), so Q counts the net electric charge in units of −e. This is the current that sources the field in ∂_μF^(μν) = j^ν, and Lesson 30 studies the U(1) symmetry that produces it.

# 9 Summary

1. The canonical energy-momentum tensor is T^μ_ν = Σ_a[∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ_a)]∂_νφ_a − δ^μ_νℒ, with charges P^ν = ∫d³x T^(0ν).

2. For the scalar field T^(μν) = ∂^μφ∂^νφ − g^(μν)ℒ, with T^(00) = ℋ, and a plane wave carries momentum and energy in the ratio p/E.

3. For the Dirac field on shell T^(μν) = iψ̄γ^μ∂^νψ, with T^(00) = ψ†Ĥ_Dψ and P = ∫ψ†p̂ψ.

4. The canonical tensor of the electromagnetic field is neither symmetric nor gauge invariant; adding ∂_λ(F^(μλ)A^ν) gives the symmetric, gauge-invariant T_Θ^(μν) = −F^(μλ)F^ν_λ + (1/4)g^(μν)F².

5. T_Θ^(00) = (1/2)(E² + B²), T_Θ^(0i) = (E × B)^i, the tensor is traceless, and for a wave the momentum density equals the energy density.

6. For a symmetric tensor, M^(μαβ) = x^αT^(μβ) − x^βT^(μα) is conserved, giving the angular momentum and the boost charge.

7. The Dirac field's angular momentum is ∫ψ†(x × p̂ + Σ/2)ψ, with spin one-half arising from Σ/2.

8. With sources, ∂_μT_Θ^(μν) = −F^(νλ)j_λ: Poynting's theorem for ν = 0 and the Lorentz force density for ν = i, so the total energy-momentum of fields and matter is conserved.

# Solutions

**1.** With ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ, T^μ_ν = ∂^μφ∂_νφ − δ^μ_νℒ, and raising ν gives T^(μν) = ∂^μφ∂^νφ − g^(μν)ℒ. Then T^(00) = φ̇² − ℒ = φ̇² − (1/2)φ̇² + (1/2)(∇φ)² + (1/2)m²φ² = ℋ. For T^(0i) = ∂⁰φ∂^iφ = φ̇(−∂ᵢφ) = −φ̇∂ᵢφ.

**2.** With θ = k·x − ωt, φ̇ = Aω sin θ, ∂ᵢφ = −Ak_i sin θ. T^(0i) = −φ̇∂ᵢφ = A²ωk_i sin²θ, with cycle average (1/2)A²ωk_i. The average of T^(00) is (1/2)A²ω² (Lesson 25, Worked example 3). The ratio is k_i/ω, and since ω² = \|k\|² + m² the group velocity is dω/dk_i = k_i/ω, the same.

**3.** On a solution, (iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ = 0, so ℒ = ψ̄(iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ = 0. Then T^(00) = iψ̄γ⁰∂⁰ψ = iψ†∂_tψ, using ψ̄γ⁰ = ψ†, and the Dirac equation i∂_tψ = Ĥ_Dψ gives T^(00) = ψ†Ĥ_Dψ.

**4.** T^(0i) = iψ̄γ⁰∂^iψ = iψ†∂^iψ = −iψ†∂ᵢψ, since ∂^i = −∂ᵢ. For ψ = u(p)e^(−iEt+ip·x), −i∂ᵢψ = p^iψ, so T^(0i) = p^iψ†ψ = 2Ep^i (with u†u = 2E). The particle density is ψ†ψ = 2E, so the momentum per particle is 2Ep^i/2E = p^i.

**5.** ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μA_λ) = −F^(μλ), so T_c^(μν) = [∂ℒ/∂(∂_μA_λ)]∂^νA_λ − g^(μν)ℒ = −F^(μλ)∂^νA_λ + (1/4)g^(μν)F_αβF^(αβ), since ℒ = −(1/4)F_αβF^(αβ).

**6.** F^ν_λ = ∂^νA_λ − ∂_λA^ν, so −F^(μλ)F^ν_λ = −F^(μλ)∂^νA_λ + F^(μλ)∂_λA^ν. Adding (1/4)g^(μν)F², T_Θ^(μν) = T_c^(μν) + F^(μλ)∂_λA^ν. In vacuum ∂_λF^(μλ) = 0, so ∂_λ(F^(μλ)A^ν) = F^(μλ)∂_λA^ν. The tensor is symmetric because F^(μλ)F^ν_λ = F^(μλ)F^(νρ)g_(λρ) is unchanged under μ ↔ ν, as is g^(μν)F². It is gauge invariant because it is built from F only.

**7.** F^(0)_i = F^(0j)g_(ji) = −F^(0i) = Eᵢ, so F^(0λ)F^0_λ = Σᵢ(−Eᵢ)(Eᵢ) = −E², and −F^(0λ)F^0_λ = E². With (1/4)g^(00)F² = (1/4)·2(B² − E²) = (1/2)(B² − E²), T_Θ^(00) = (1/2)(E² + B²). For T_Θ^(0i) = −F^(0j)F^i_j, with F^i_j = F^(ik)g_(kj) = −F^(ij) = ε_(ijk)B_k, T_Θ^(0i) = −(−Eⱼ)ε_(ijk)B_k = ε_(ijk)EⱼB_k = (E × B)ᵢ.

**8.** T_Θ^μ_μ = g_(μν)T_Θ^(μν) = −F^(μλ)F_(μλ) + (1/4)·4·F_αβF^(αβ) = −F² + F² = 0. For the scalar, T^μ_μ = ∂_μφ∂^μφ − 4ℒ = ∂_μφ∂^μφ − 2∂_μφ∂^μφ + 2m²φ² = −∂_μφ∂^μφ + 2m²φ². For m = 0 this is −∂_μφ∂^μφ, which is nonzero for general configurations, so the canonical scalar tensor is not traceless even for a massless field.

**9.** From Solution 4, T^(0i) = 2Ep^i. For the other component, T^(i0) = iψ̄γ^i∂⁰ψ = iψ̄γ^i(−iE)ψ = Eψ̄γ^iψ = Eūγ^iu = E · 2p^i = 2Ep^i, using ūγ^μu = 2p^μ (Lesson 23, Section 4.2). So T^(0i) = T^(i0).

**10.** dK^i/dt = ∫d³x x^i∂_tT^(00) − ∫d³x T^(0i) − t∫d³x ∂_tT^(0i). From ∂_μT^(μ0) = 0, ∂_tT^(00) = −∂ⱼT^(j0), and integrating by parts (surface terms vanish), ∫x^i(−∂ⱼT^(j0)) = ∫T^(i0). From ∂_μT^(μi) = 0, ∫∂_tT^(0i) = −∫∂ⱼT^(ji) = 0. So dK^i/dt = ∫T^(i0) − ∫T^(0i) = 0 when T^(i0) = T^(0i). Since ∫x^iT^(00) = HX^i_cm, this says d(HX_cm)/dt = P, so the center of energy moves with velocity P/H.

**11.** For ψ = u₁e^(−imt) with u₁ = √(2m)(1, 0, 0, 0)ᵀ, the spinor is uniform in space, so p̂ψ = 0 and the orbital term x × p̂ contributes nothing. The spin term is ψ†(Σ_z/2)ψ = (1/2)ψ†ψ, since Σ_zu₁ = u₁ (Lesson 21, Worked example 3). Integrating, J_z = (1/2)∫ψ†ψ d³x, which is 1/2 for each particle.

**12.** For ν = 0, ∂_μT_Θ^(μ0) = ∂_tT_Θ^(00) + ∂ᵢT_Θ^(i0) = ∂_tu + ∇·S, using T_Θ^(i0) = T_Θ^(0i) = Sᵢ. The right side is −F^(0λ)j_λ = −F^(0i)j_i = −(−Eᵢ)(−jⁱ) = −E·j, since j_i = −jⁱ. So ∂_tu + ∇·S = −E·j. For ν = i, −F^(iλ)j_λ = −(F^(i0)j₀ + F^(ij)j_j) with F^(i0) = Eᵢ, j₀ = ρ, F^(ij) = −ε_(ijk)B_k, and j_j = −j^j, giving −(Eᵢρ + ε_(ijk)B_kj^j) = −(ρE + j × B)ᵢ.

**13.** Q = −e∫d³x ψ†ψ = −e · 1 = −e for a normalized wave function. A plane wave normalized as ψ†ψ = 2E has ∫d³xψ†ψ = 2EV over a box of volume V, so to describe one particle it is rescaled by 1/√(2EV), which makes ∫ψ†ψ d³x = 1.

**14.** Under A_λ → A_λ + ∂_λχ, F^(μλ) is unchanged, so only ∂^νA_λ changes, by ∂^ν∂_λχ, and T_c^(μν) changes by −F^(μλ)∂^ν∂_λχ. In vacuum ∂_λF^(μλ) = 0, so this equals −∂_λ(F^(μλ)∂^νχ), a total derivative of a quantity antisymmetric in (μ, λ). The change in P^ν = ∫d³x T_c^(0ν) is then −∫d³x ∂_λ(F^(0λ)∂^νχ) = −∫d³x ∂ᵢ(F^(0i)∂^νχ), a surface integral that vanishes for fields decaying at infinity. The local density depends on the gauge, but the total momentum does not.

**15.** For a plane wave E ⊥ B and \|E\| = \|B\|, so \|S\| = \|E × B\| = \|E\|\|B\| = E². The energy density is (1/2)(E² + B²) = E². Hence \|T^(0i)\| = T^(00). For a photon of energy E and momentum p this is the statement E = \|p\|, the relation for a massless particle in Lesson 3, Section 5.4.

**16.** By the product rule, ∂_μ(x^αT^(μβ)) = δ^α_μT^(μβ) + x^α∂_μT^(μβ) = T^(αβ), using ∂_μT^(μβ) = 0. So ∂_μM^(μαβ) = T^(αβ) − T^(βα) = 0 because T^(αβ) = ∂^αφ∂^βφ − g^(αβ)ℒ is symmetric. The charge J^(ij) = ∫d³x(x^iT^(0j) − x^jT^(0i)) = −∫d³x π(x^i∂ⱼ − x^j∂ᵢ)φ, using T^(0j) = −π∂ⱼφ. The angular momentum vector is J^k = (1/2)ε_(kij)J^(ij) = −∫d³x π(x × ∇φ)_k, the moment x × (−π∇φ) of the momentum density T^(0i) = −π∂ᵢφ of Section 2.1, which is what the angular momentum of a distribution of momentum should be.
