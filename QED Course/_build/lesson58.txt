# How to use this lesson

Every calculation in Part VIII so far has ended with a spin-summed |ℳ|² and a promise: combine it with dσ/dΩ=|ℳ|²/(64π²s) to get a real prediction, a formula first quoted in Lesson 53 and reused since, always with its derivation deferred to this lesson. The gap between Lesson 50's general phase-space integral and that compact result is entirely mechanical, but the mechanics matter: integrating away one final-state momentum with a delta function, then handling the second delta function by treating it as a constraint on a single radial variable, a trick worth having in hand for any two-body process this course meets later. Expect four to six hours.

## Learning objectives

- Recall Lesson 50's general cross-section formula and identify which piece of it remains unevaluated.

- Use the three-momentum delta function to remove one final-state integral entirely, fixing the second particle's momentum to minus the first's in the center-of-momentum frame.

- Apply the rule for a delta function of a function, δ(f(p))=δ(p−p₀)/|f′(p₀)|, to the remaining energy constraint, and evaluate the resulting Jacobian.

- Derive the flux factor's center-of-momentum form, F=4p_i√s, from the general definition of Lesson 50.

- Assemble the general two-body result dσ/dΩ=(p_f/p_i)|ℳ|²/(64π²s), and its elastic-scattering special case.

- Reproduce the derivation, start to finish, from memory.

# 1 What remains to be done

## 1.1 The starting point

Lesson 50, Section 3.4, gave

$$ dσ = (1/F) |ℳ|² (2π)⁴δ⁴(p₁+p₂−p₃−p₄) d³p₃/((2π)³2E₃) d³p₄/((2π)³2E₄)

for a 2→2 process, leaving both the flux factor F and the six-dimensional phase-space integral (three components each for p₃ and p₄) unevaluated in any specific frame.

## 1.2 Choosing the center-of-momentum frame

Working in the frame where p₁+p₂=0 turns the delta function's spatial part into a statement purely about p₃ and p₄, and lets the flux factor take the simple form found in Section 4; every quantity in the final result is built from Lorentz scalars or from p_i, p_f (themselves fixed once s and the masses are given), so the formula, once derived here, applies without modification to a measurement made in any frame, converted back through the invariants alone.

### Worked example 1

State why deriving this formula in one particular frame does not limit its usefulness to that frame. dσ itself, and |ℳ|², are physical, frame-independent quantities once expressed through Mandelstam variables (Lesson 52); doing the phase-space integral in the frame where the algebra is easiest and then writing the answer in terms of s, t, and the masses produces a formula valid everywhere, the same strategy already used for the amplitudes of Lessons 53 through 57.

# 2 Removing one integral

## 2.1 The three-momentum delta function

Splitting (2π)⁴δ⁴(p₁+p₂−p₃−p₄) into its energy and momentum parts, (2π)δ(E₁+E₂−E₃−E₄)·(2π)³δ³(p₁+p₂−p₃−p₄), the spatial delta function collapses the d³p₄ integral entirely:

$$ ∫ d³p₄/(2π)³ (2π)³δ³(p₁+p₂−p₃−p₄) f(p₄) = f(p₁+p₂−p₃)

leaving p₄ fixed, at every point in the remaining integral, to p₁+p₂−p₃, which equals −p₃ in the center-of-momentum frame.

## 2.2 What is left

$$ dσ = (1/F) |ℳ|² (1/(2π)²) δ(E₁+E₂−E₃−E₄) d³p₃/(4E₃E₄)

with E₄ now understood as √(|p₃|²+m₄²), a function of p₃ alone.

### Worked example 2

Explain why only one delta function remains after Section 2.1's step, when the original had four components. The spatial part, three components, was fully used to eliminate the three-dimensional p₄ integral; the fourth component, the energy constraint, involves both E₃ and E₄, and since E₄ depends on p₃ once p₄=−p₃ is substituted, this remaining condition constrains the magnitude of p₃, not an independent variable.

# 3 A delta function of a function

## 3.1 The general rule

For a function f with an isolated zero at p=p₀, δ(f(p))=δ(p−p₀)/|f′(p₀)|, a standard property of the delta function under a change of variable (Exercise 5 derives it from the defining property ∫δ(x)g(x)dx=g(0)).

## 3.2 Applying it here

Writing d³p₃=p²dp dΩ (p≡|p₃|) and treating the energy-conserving delta function as a function of p alone,

$$ f(p) = E₁+E₂ − E₃(p) − E₄(p) = √s − √(p²+m₃²) − √(p²+m₄²)

$$ f′(p) = −p/E₃ − p/E₄ = −p(E₃+E₄)/(E₃E₄)

At the root p=p_f, energy conservation gives E₃+E₄=√s, so f′(p_f)=−p_f√s/(E₃E₄), and

$$ ∫ p² dp δ(f(p)) = p_f² / |f′(p_f)| = p_f E₃E₄/√s

### Worked example 3

State, in one sentence, why p_f, not p_i, appears in this result. The radial integral runs over the magnitude of the outgoing momentum p₃, so the delta function's root is by definition the value that magnitude takes once energy is balanced, p_f; p_i enters only later, through the flux factor built from the incoming particles.

# 4 The flux factor here

Lesson 50, Section 3.2, gave F=4E₁E₂|v₁−v₂|; in the center-of-momentum frame, with the incoming particles' three-momenta equal and opposite in magnitude p_i, v₁=p_i/E₁ and v₂=p_i/E₂ point oppositely, so |v₁−v₂|=p_i(E₁+E₂)/(E₁E₂)=p_i√s/(E₁E₂), giving

$$ F = 4 E₁E₂ · p_i√s/(E₁E₂) = 4 p_i √s

a clean cancellation leaving no trace of the individual energies E₁, E₂ — only the incoming momentum magnitude and the total energy survive.

### Worked example 4

Compare this cancellation to a similar one seen earlier in the course. Lesson 43, Section 2.1, found the coupling constant's dimension by a comparable process, terms that individually depended on the specific field content cancelling to leave a bare, universal number; here, the two particles' separate energies drop out of the flux factor entirely, leaving a formula built only from the collision's overall scale, s, and the shared momentum magnitude p_i.

# 5 Assembling the result

## 5.1 The general formula

Combining Sections 2.2, 3.2, and 4,

$$ dσ = (1/(4p_i√s)) |ℳ|² (1/(2π)²) (1/4E₃E₄) (p_fE₃E₄/√s) dΩ = |ℳ|² p_f / (16π² · 4 p_i s) dΩ

$$ dσ/dΩ = (p_f/p_i) |ℳ|² / (64 π² s)

the general two-body formula, valid for any final-state masses.

## 5.2 The elastic case

For elastic scattering, m₃=m₁ and m₄=m₂, Lesson 52, Section 4.2, showed p_f=p_i, so the ratio p_f/p_i equals one and

$$ dσ/dΩ = |ℳ|² / (64 π² s)

the formula quoted without proof since Lesson 53.

### Worked example 5

State which of this course's calculations needed the general form of Section 5.1 rather than the elastic special case of Section 5.2. Every process computed in Lessons 53 through 57 involves the same particle species before and after (electron-muon, electron-electron, and the photon pairs of annihilation, Compton scattering, and pair production, each an elastic-type configuration in the relevant sense), so Section 5.2's simpler form applies throughout; Lesson 59's decay-rate calculations and any future process changing particle species partway through would call on Section 5.1 instead.

# 7 Summary

1. Lesson 50's cross-section formula, restricted to the center-of-momentum frame, has its p₄ integral removed by the spatial delta function, leaving a single energy constraint on p₃'s magnitude.

2. Writing that constraint as δ(f(p)) and using the rule for a delta function of a function fixes the radial integral to a value built from p_f and the outgoing energies.

3. The flux factor reduces to F=4p_i√s in this frame, with the individual particle energies cancelling out.

4. The complete formula is dσ/dΩ=(p_f/p_i)|ℳ|²/(64π²s), reducing to |ℳ|²/(64π²s) for elastic scattering.

5. Every calculation in Lessons 53–57 used this elastic form, now finally derived rather than assumed.

# Solutions

**1.** dσ=(1/F)|ℳ|²(2π)⁴δ⁴(ΣP_f−ΣP_i)dΠ (Lesson 50, Section 3.4): |ℳ|² carries the process-specific physics; 1/F removes the beam-density dependence; the delta function enforces overall energy-momentum balance; dΠ sums over the kinematically allowed final states.

**2.** ∫d³p₄/(2π)³(2π)³δ³(p₁+p₂−p₃−p₄)f(p₄): the (2π)³ factors cancel, and the delta function, by its defining property, picks out the value of f at the point where its argument vanishes, p₄=p₁+p₂−p₃, giving f(p₁+p₂−p₃) with the integral removed entirely.

**3.** In the center-of-momentum frame, p₁+p₂=0 by definition (the frame in which the two incoming three-momenta are equal and opposite); substituting into Exercise 2's result, p₄=0−p₃=−p₃.

**4.** d³p₃=p²dp dΩ, with p≡|p₃| and dΩ the solid-angle element; the remaining energy delta function, once p₄=−p₃ is substituted so that E₄ depends only on p, constrains p itself, leaving dΩ as the only free integration left over once the radial integral is done.

**5.** Change variables in ∫δ(f(p))g(p)dp to x=f(p), dx=f′(p)dp, near the root p₀ where f(p₀)=0: the integral becomes ∫δ(x)g(p(x))dx/|f′(p(x))| (the absolute value from possibly reversing the direction of integration if f′<0), and by the defining property this equals g(p₀)/|f′(p₀)|; comparing with ∫δ(p−p₀)g(p)dp/|f′(p₀)|=g(p₀)/|f′(p₀)| shows δ(f(p))=δ(p−p₀)/|f′(p₀)|.

**6.** f(p)=√s−(p²+m₃²)^(1/2)−(p²+m₄²)^(1/2); differentiating each square root term using the chain rule, d/dp(p²+m²)^(1/2)=p/(p²+m²)^(1/2)=p/E, gives f′(p)=0−p/E₃−p/E₄=−p/E₃−p/E₄.

**7.** f′(p_f)=−p_f/E₃−p_f/E₄=−p_f(1/E₃+1/E₄)=−p_f(E₃+E₄)/(E₃E₄); substituting E₃+E₄=√s (energy conservation, evaluated at the physical root) gives f′(p_f)=−p_f√s/(E₃E₄).

**8.** ∫p²dp δ(f(p))=p_f²/|f′(p_f)| (Exercise 5, evaluated at p₀=p_f, using Exercise 4's p² weight); substituting |f′(p_f)|=p_f√s/(E₃E₄) (Exercise 7) gives p_f²/(p_f√s/(E₃E₄))=p_f²(E₃E₄)/(p_f√s)=p_fE₃E₄/√s.

**9.** F=4E₁E₂|v₁−v₂| (Lesson 50, Section 3.2). In the center-of-momentum frame, v₁=p_i/E₁ and v₂=−p_i/E₂ (equal magnitudes, opposite directions, using the shared momentum magnitude p_i since the two incoming three-momenta are equal and opposite).

**10.** v₁−v₂=p_i/E₁−(−p_i/E₂)=p_i(1/E₁+1/E₂)=p_i(E₁+E₂)/(E₁E₂); using E₁+E₂=√s, |v₁−v₂|=p_i√s/(E₁E₂).

**11.** F=4E₁E₂·[p_i√s/(E₁E₂)]=4p_i√s, the E₁E₂ factors in the numerator and denominator cancelling exactly, leaving no dependence on the individual particle energies.

**12.** dσ=(1/F)|ℳ|²(1/(2π)²)∫p²dp δ(f(p))(1/4E₃E₄)dΩ (Section 2.2, with the radial integral now made explicit). Substituting Exercise 8's result and F=4p_i√s: dσ/dΩ=(1/(4p_i√s))|ℳ|²(1/(2π)²)(p_fE₃E₄/√s)(1/(4E₃E₄)). The E₃E₄ in the numerator (from the radial integral) cancels the E₃E₄ in the denominator (from the phase-space measure), and the two factors of √s combine to give an overall s in the denominator, leaving dσ/dΩ=(p_f/p_i)|ℳ|²/(64π²s), using (2π)²×4×4=64π².

**13.** With equal masses before and after (m₃=m₁, m₄=m₂), the equation determining p_f, √(p_f²+m₁²)+√(p_f²+m₂²)=√s, is identical to the equation already solved for p_i in setting up the initial state, so p_f=p_i (Lesson 52, Section 4.2); if the final-state masses differed, this would be a different equation with, in general, a different root.

**14.** Every process in Lessons 53–57 keeps the same particle content (or the same total mass configuration) on both sides of the reaction in the sense relevant to this formula's derivation, so p_f=p_i throughout, and the simpler Section 5.2 formula applies without needing the more general ratio.

**15.** For unequal masses, the factor p_f/p_i, generally not equal to one, would appear, capturing the physical fact that a heavier or lighter final state carries away a different amount of momentum for the same total energy √s than the initial state supplied; Section 5.2 would simply miss this factor, giving an incorrect normalization if applied where the masses genuinely differ.

**16.** With three particles in the final state, the phase-space integral has nine momentum components rather than six; only four can be removed by the full four-dimensional delta function (fixing, for instance, one particle's momentum entirely in terms of the other two), leaving a five-dimensional integral with no single remaining delta function to collapse it to a point, unlike the two-body case where one delta function was enough to fix the entire radial variable. The radial-integral step of Section 3 would therefore need the most substantial revision, since there is no longer a single unknown magnitude for a delta function to determine uniquely; the flux factor of Section 4, built only from the two initial particles, is unaffected by how many particles occupy the final state.
