# PART ONE: STONE


## 1. Four Minutes

The window was four minutes and eleven seconds long. I know because I timed it, and I timed it because I am a man who times things. That is the whole of my character, and if you take only one fact from this account, take that one. Everything else I did followed from it.

The clinic's night technician had gone to move his car. He did this every Thursday at 11:40, because the garage charged by the hour and he had once been told by a woman at the desk that the first hour was free if you came back before midnight. He was not a stupid man. He was a man with a small, consistent economy, and I had been watching it for five months.

The second camera in the lab had a blind arc of its own. The facilities people called it a known limitation. I called it a door.

I will say what I did in the order I did it, and I will say it plainly, because plainness is a kind of honesty and I would like to be credited with some.

There were nine embryos in the incubator that week, from four couples. Seven were exactly what the paperwork said they were. For the other two, I had written the paperwork myself, and the lines on it were true in the way a good lie is true: every individual word was accurate, and the sentence they made was not. Officially I was correcting a lethal mutation, one of those errors in a single place that would take a child at six or at nine and leave a family with a photograph and a very clean house. That part was real. I did that part well. I would do it again.

A sensible reader distrusts any sentence that begins with the word *officially*. Officially, I was correcting errors. Unofficially, I kept a schedule, and the schedule was not measured in embryos or in years. It ran a hundred of them.

The second part is the part you have come for.

I will not set it down in a way that anyone could follow, and I want that understood now, at the start, in case you were hoping. It is not modesty. A man who writes the recipe for something like this has not thought about the person who reads it. What I will say is that it was fewer steps than people imagine and more than the law would forgive. It took a steady hand and a pair of eyes that never once looked up at the clock, because I had set the clock behind me, where I could hear it and not see it.

At three minutes and a little, the embryo that would become the boy went quiet in the dish. They do not do anything, of course. They are a small clear arrangement of cells that look like nothing at all. But I had spent two decades looking at them, and I had learned to feel when a thing had been done to them that could not be undone, the same way you feel a door close in a house you thought was empty.

I had meant it to be a clean moment. I had thought there would be a sort of grandeur. What I remember is that the refrigerator in the corner kicked on, and I thought, absurdly, *they ought to have fixed that*.

At three minutes forty I was back at my bench, a man with a pen and a chart, reading a number I had read a hundred times.

At four minutes and eleven seconds the technician came back through the door, stamping a little because the evenings were turning cold, and said, "Doctor. Burning the midnight oil?"

"Only the ten-forty-five," I said.

He laughed. I remember that he laughed. Everybody who has ever been wronged by a person like me has a moment like that, a small warm sound that is the last thing before it begins.

I signed out at 12:06. I drove home slowly. I stopped at a red light on an empty road and waited for it to change, because I believed then, and I believe now, that a man who has just done a thing like that must obey every other rule perfectly.

That is how it began. I have thought many times since about what I would say to the embryo, if it could hear me, and I have never come up with anything better than what the technician said.

*Burning the midnight oil.*

It is twelve years since, as I write this, and the boy has just told me what he can hear through my chest.

* * *

I have not said why, and a reader who has come this far is entitled to a reason.

There were two. One was respectable and one was not, and I have found that the respectable one is the one that makes you do it, and the other is the one that lets you sleep.

The respectable reason was a boy called Tomás.

It was the winter of 1995. I was forty-five. I had been at the clinic for eight years and had a reputation, which I had earned and did not enjoy, for being very good with difficult embryos. A couple came in from a town in the hills, a truck driver and his wife, who had lost a child already. They had a daughter of four with a heart that had never worked properly, and a son who had died at nine months of something with a long, quiet name, and they wanted, with a kind of dry terrible patience, to know whether the next one would be all right.

I told them what I could. I screened. I explained what a screen could and could not do. I said the usual things, in the usual order, and the woman nodded at each, with her hand on her husband's arm.

They had a boy. He was born at term. He was perfect. For eleven months he was perfect, a fat, solemn, black-haired child with a furious laugh, and they came to the clinic on his first birthday to bring me a cake.

He died on the fourteenth of March. It was the same condition, the one the screen had not caught, because it lived in a region of the genome that nobody had yet thought to look at. His mother called to tell me. She said it quite calmly. She said: "I wanted you to know. I didn't want you to hear it from someone else."

I said I was sorry. She said she knew. She said she was not calling to blame me. She said it a second time, so that I would believe it, and I did, and it did not help.

I put the telephone down. I went into the laboratory and shut the door and sat on the stool at my bench for four hours. I did not weep. I thought. I thought about regions of the genome, and about windows, and about the particular, intolerable asymmetry of a profession in which a thing can be known and not done.

That was the respectable reason. I went on being a very good doctor for another four years, and then I did the thing I had been unable to stop thinking about, which was to go and look.

The other reason I will tell you later. It was the fear of being ordinary. It came after, and it came from nowhere as far as I could tell, and it came with a clean cold clarity that I mistook, for a long time, for purpose.

* * *

I want to add one thing about Tomás, and then I will let him go.

I kept the cake tin. It was a round blue one with a picture of a robin. The mother had left it on my desk, and I had never had the heart to return it, and I could not bring myself to ask her what had become of the cake. It sat on a shelf in my office for seventeen years. It was among the things the clinic's cleaners found, when the rooms were cleared, and I am told it was put in a box with my coffee mugs and my reading glasses and sent to the home.

It is in the locker beside my bed. I cannot open it. I cannot ask anyone to.

I did not make the boy in the carrier for Tomás. I would like that understood. But I have wondered, in the long afternoons, whether a man who has watched a child die for want of a window might, in a certain mood, feel that he owed one.

I do not know if that is an excuse. I believe now that it is the thing I most want to be. It is certainly the thing I would least like to be judged by.

* * *

The transfer itself took eleven minutes, which was seven more than the part that mattered, and I mention it only because it was the part everyone else saw.

Connie Pruitt lay on the table with her knees draped in paper and her husband holding her hand as if it were a railing on a boat. Ray Pruitt drove a delivery truck for a bakery supplier. Connie worked the line in a school cafeteria. They had saved for four years and been turned down by two other clinics, and ours, whose program for couples of their means I had helped design for reasons I called charitable and had never examined, was the third door. They looked at me the way people look at a man who is about to hand them what they have prayed for, which is to say, without any suspicion at all.

I told them it was a good embryo. That was true. I told them it had been corrected for the condition they feared, a rare error that had taken Ray's brother at seven. That was also true. I told them nothing else, and I want to be plain about what that cost me, because the answer is that it cost me nothing. I felt nothing. I had expected to. A man is supposed to flinch.

Connie said, "Is it a boy or a girl?"

"We won't know for a while."

"I'd like a boy," Ray said, and then, quickly, to his wife, "I don't care. I'd like whatever it is."

"Of course," I said.

I withdrew the catheter, and the screen showed a small bright smear in the dark, and Connie wept, and Ray wept, and a nurse named Dolores, who had seen four hundred of these, wept, and I stood there in my gown and counted the exits.

* * *

He was born on the first morning of the new century, 1 January 2000, three weeks early, in a county hospital forty minutes from my clinic. I was not there. Doctors who have no business in a delivery room stay out of it. I read the discharge summary the following Thursday: seven pounds one ounce, Apgar nine and nine, no complications, a mother "in good spirits." I read it three times, and I did not know what I was looking for, and I found nothing.

At six weeks I did something I had told myself I would not do. I arranged a follow-up that was not required. A program like ours had a quarterly check for the first year, a courtesy for the families who had no pediatrician of their own; I made myself the physician on the day the Pruitts were scheduled and I told the front desk I wanted to see the corrected-condition cases personally. Nobody questioned this. Nobody does, if you say it with enough tiredness.

The baby was in a carrier on the examination table, a thin, wide-awake child with his father's chin and an alarming amount of dark hair, and he was crying, steadily, with the thin outraged note of the very young. Connie jiggled him. Ray stood by with the face of a man who had been up since four.

I leaned down and said hello to him. I do not know why. It is not what I do.

The baby stopped crying.

Not at me. He stopped as if a switch had been thrown, and his head turned, an inch and a half, toward the wall behind me, where the examination room backed onto a corridor. His eyes did not focus. They never do at six weeks. But the head stayed where it was, and he held perfectly still, and the room went quiet in the particular way a room does when nobody wants to be the one to break it.

Eleven seconds later, in the corridor, a cart rolled past, with a rattling wheel.

"Huh," Ray said. "Quiet all of a sudden."

"He does that," said Connie. "He hears everything. Mama's got a theory he's going to be a musician."

"It's perfectly normal," I said.

I have said many untrue things in my life. That one I said best.

I examined him. The paperwork recorded a healthy male infant, reflexes intact, length and weight at the fiftieth percentile. I pressed the stethoscope to his chest and felt, through the diaphragm, a very small heart going about its work, and for the first time since the night of the four minutes, I was afraid.

It was not fear of being caught. I want that understood. It was a plain animal fear, like discovering that the dog you left in the house has learned to open the door.

On the way out, Ray shook my hand. His palm was rough from the truck. He said, "Thank you, Doc. For everything."

I kept the handshake a good while longer than I needed to.


## 2. Test Time

The century turned over on a Saturday, and I spent it in the laboratory, which I do not recommend.

The clinic was empty. The staff had gone home to their families and their champagne, and the building made the small, ticking, cooling sounds of a large animal asleep. I sat at my bench and listened to the radio, which had been predicting the end of civilization for three weeks and now sounded faintly disappointed. At midnight, nothing failed. The lights stayed on. The incubators hummed. Somewhere a few miles away a firework went up, a single green one, and burst without a sound.

I did not celebrate. I made a cup of tea and, at ten past midnight, I wrote a figure on a pad. It was the number of hours since the technician had said *burning the midnight oil.* It came to six thousand, two hundred and eighty-two.

I do not know why I did it. I think I wanted to be able to say, at some later date, that I had known the moment.

On the second of January I telephoned the county hospital and asked, in the voice I use for such calls, whether a woman named Pruitt had been delivered of a child the previous day. The clerk said that patient information was confidential. I said that I was her physician. There was a pause, a rustle of paper, and the clerk said, in a voice that had decided to be helpful: "A boy. Seven pounds one. They had him at eleven past four on the first. The first baby of the year, can you believe it? There was a photographer."

I thanked her. I put down the telephone. I sat very still.

There had been a photographer.

* * *

I have said that the clinic's director was a competent and courteous woman. I should say something more, since she is going to matter. I will call her R., because she has asked me never to use her name, and because, more than any person in my life, she deserved to be protected from it.

She had come to the clinic in the year after I did. She was an administrator by training and a gardener by temperament, and she had an unusual gift, which was that she trusted everyone slightly less than they thought. She was fifty-two in the year 2000. She wore low heels, and carried a large handbag, and kept a bowl of mints on the corner of her desk, which she offered to everyone, including the man who came to read the meter. She had once, in a staff meeting, quoted a poem about bees. She was the only person in the building who ever asked me how I was and waited for the answer.

I never gave her one. I said I was well, and that the work went on. She would nod, and take a mint herself, and look at me over the top of her glasses with an expression I have thought about since. It was not suspicion. It was the look of a woman who has decided to let a thing be, out of kindness, and is waiting to be proved wrong.

In the winter of 2000 she came to my laboratory, unannounced, at six in the evening, with a small paper bag.

"I had a cake left over from the board," she said. "I thought you might not have eaten."

I had not. I took it. We stood by the bench, eating it from the bag with our fingers, and she looked round at the cabinet and the incubators and the old tiled floor and said, lightly, "You do keep a tidy shop, Frank."

"I try."

"Nobody tidy is ever quite as tidy as they look." She smiled, and took a second mint from her pocket, and went away.

I stood there with cake on my fingers and thought: *she knows nothing.* And I thought, a moment later, with a coldness that I did not enjoy: *she has made up her mind not to.*

I have always been grateful to her for it. I have always been ashamed of the gratitude.

* * *

I did not go back. I want that on the record, since much of what follows will not flatter me.

After the handshake I decided that a man who has frightened himself should stay away from the thing that frightened him. I returned to the clinic, reviewed forty cases that had nothing to do with the Pruitts, and wrote to the program office to say that the quarterly checks for corrected-condition families were adequately covered by the nursing staff. They agreed. Nobody enjoys a check-up program, least of all the people running one.

I kept one door open. If a family agreed to a test, a hearing check or a growth chart, something routine that a pediatrician would shrug at, I took the appointment myself. These were rare, and the parents consented in perfect good faith, and I told them nothing beyond what the form said. I never ordered the one test that would have told me everything. A man who never asks the question cannot be asked it.

What I kept was distance, and distance can be very informative.

The boy, whom the Pruitts called Danny, turned up in the world the way anyone does: in a hospital's yearly summary of its pediatric patients, in the county newsletter that printed a photograph of the spring school fair, in the anonymous column of the school district's reading-level tables, which are public and which no one reads. At four, Danny's file had been flagged by a kindergarten screener for "unusual listening behavior." At five, he had been moved out of the music room for what his teacher called "a distraction to others," meaning he flinched when the piano was a quarter tone flat. At six, a pediatric audiologist, bored on a Friday, ran an extra set of tests because the child was so unnervingly good at them. She wrote "likely measurement error" in the margin. She was wrong, and I knew it, and nobody else in the world did.

None of this troubled the family. I would like to make that clear to anyone who comes looking for a victim. Connie told a neighbor, in a conversation I will not say how I learned of, that Danny "had ears like a bat and the manners of a bishop." Ray built him a small shelf for his rocks. When the boy corrected the cashier at the bakery supplier's Christmas party, quietly and accurately, about a mistaken total of $41.60, Ray told the story for a year. The odd moments were just that, odd, and they were absorbed the way a family absorbs a child who is simply himself. I did not interfere, and nothing needed interfering with.

I should say that I have told this out of order. The memo that follows was written in the autumn of 2001, when Danny was not yet two, and everything I have just listed is what I learned over the years, not what moved me then. What troubled me then was a different arithmetic.

One embryo is an anecdote. I had a child with one insert, in one family, in one county, and I had no way of knowing whether what I saw was what I had built, or a fluke of the ordinary. A man who cares about the answer needs more than one.

I wrote the memo in the evening and sent it in the morning. The subject line read, *Request for protected bench time to refine embryo correction methods.* It ran to two pages. It said the corrections to date had been successful, which was true, and that the rate of rare errors, though low, suggested room for efficiency, which was a lie of the sort you tell in committees. The larger lie was in what it left out: the methods I meant to refine were for a scale I did not mention, and a century I did not mention either. I asked for four hours a week, after hours, on the old incubator, with the cameras left as they were.

The director's reply came in nine minutes. *Absolutely. Thrilled someone is thinking about methods. Take what you need. —R.*

I read it twice. It is a remarkable thing to be trusted. It is a remarkable thing to be trusted by someone who has stopped looking.

In the end I used the four hours for eleven years, though not always the same four, and not always the same incubator. The cameras, I will note, were never repaired.

I will tell you only this about the eleven years: Danny was not alone.

* * *

I should set down the first night of the bench time, because it was the only one that felt like anything.

It was a Tuesday in October, 2001, a little after eight. The staff had gone. The cleaners had been and left. I let myself into the laboratory with the key I had had for thirteen years, and turned on only the light over the bench, and stood for a moment in the half-dark with my coat still on.

The old incubator stood in the corner on its own stand, a squat cream machine the size of a washing machine with a glass door and a faded sticker that said **PROPERTY OF**— and then nothing, because the rest had peeled off. It had been replaced in 1996 by a newer model, which the clinic used. This one had been kept, by a kind of institutional inertia, as a spare. It did not appear on the equipment list. It did not appear on the service schedule. There was a small red light on the front that glowed to say it was on, and I had always thought it looked, in the dark, like a small patient eye.

I took off my coat. I hung it on the hook. I washed my hands, for a long time, in the old steel sink.

I will not say what I did. I have said I will not. I will say that it was quiet, and that my hands were steady, and that at some point, in the middle of it, I became aware that I was humming. It was a tune my mother used to sing, a nursery thing about a lamb that I had not remembered in fifty years. I stopped. I started again. I finished what I was doing, and put it back, and closed the door.

The red light glowed. The machine hummed, and went on humming.

It occurred to me that I was happy.

I want to be exact about this. It was not the happiness of a man who has done good, or even of one who has done well. It was a smaller, stranger, more private thing: the simple happiness of someone who has found the one room in the world in which he is entirely himself. I sat down on the stool and put my head in my hands and stayed there, with my coat on its hook and the light over the bench, for what must have been an hour.

Then I went home. I had been in the building for three hours and fourteen minutes. No one had seen me. The cameras in the corridor had looked at the empty floor, as they had been looking at it for years, with the patient indifference of machines whose recording tapes had been reused so often that they carried only a faint gray hiss.

* * *

There was one consented test, years later, in the spring of 2010, that I remember for another reason.

The Okafors had asked. It was the late third child, the girl, who was then six. Her mother had written on the form, in the neat hand of a teacher, *routine hearing check, please, she seems to be very sensitive to certain sounds,* and I had taken the appointment myself, because I was curious, and because a man who is always curious has no excuse.

I did it in the examination room at the end of the corridor, the narrow one with the high window. The child sat in the chair with her feet not touching the floor. She was small and neat, with beaded braids and an absorbed, grave face, and she held her mother's handbag in her lap as if it had been entrusted to her.

I put the headphones on her. I played the tones. She raised her hand at each, firmly, at the right moment, with a little decisive nod. At the end I removed the headphones and made a note.

"Is it good?" she said.

"It is very good."

"Is it too good?"

I looked at her. She was regarding me with the level attention of a person asking a plain question.

"What do you mean?"

"The lady at school said I hear too well. She said it is not always a good thing." She swung her feet. "Mama said she was being silly."

"Your mother is right."

"Yes." She considered. Then she said, in the same even voice, "Why are you sad?"

I have been asked many questions in my life, by committees and juries and by a woman with a pencil in her hair. I have never been so unprepared for one.

"I'm not," I said.

"You are. You breathe slow when you look at me." She frowned. "Mama says it is not polite to say."

"It's perfectly polite."

"Then I'm sorry you're sad." She slid down from the chair and picked up her mother's handbag with both hands. "I hope it gets better. It usually does."

She went out. I heard her small feet down the corridor, and her mother's voice, rising, saying *and what did the doctor say?* and the child's reply, which I did not catch, though I tried.

I sat in that room for some time. On the wall there was a poster of a smiling ear. I looked at it until it stopped looking like a joke.

I have thought since that I was wrong to call her a subject. She was a person who had noticed me. It is a rarer thing than most men think.


## 3. The Ledger

I have been asked, by the only reader I expect to have, to explain myself. I will do it in the order I would present a grant: aim, method, schedule, risk. I will leave out the method.

**Aim.** I wanted to replace us.

I am aware of how that sentence sounds. I have tried it in a dozen other ways. *Improve*, *advance*, *carry forward*. None of them survives a second reading. A species is not improved by being outnumbered. It is replaced, politely, over time, by people it has raised itself, who are kinder to it than it would have been to them. That was the plan. I have never been able to find a gentler way to describe it, and I did look.

I did not hate anyone. I want that understood, since it is the thing most readers get wrong. People who imagine a project of this kind imagine a man in a cold room with a grievance. I had no grievance. I had a clinic, a parking space, and a drawer full of children who would have died at seven. What I had was a spreadsheet of how often we fail, and I had read it every morning for twenty years.

**Schedule.** A century, in four stages. At the top of the page, in capitals, I had written one word as an instruction to myself, and it was not *quickly.* It was SLOWLY.

The first was the one I was living in: a small number of people, a few dozen, made quietly and given to ordinary families. Nothing about it could be allowed to look like anything. I called it *seeding*, which is what a gardener says to explain why the beds look empty.

The second was the long dull one. The first people would grow up, find partners, have children. The inserts would travel by the ordinary rules, half to each child, and some children would carry more than others, and for a generation or two nothing would be visible to anyone but me. I kept them in a single copy, so to speak, on purpose. A trait that arrives in everyone at once is a trait a government can find. A trait that arrives in one child in two is a family joke.

The third stage was the turn. After enough of them had met and married, by choice or by the pull I had designed into them, the odds would flip. Where the traits had been a coin toss, they would become the usual case. I will say only that this was the only stage that required nothing of me at all, which is why I loved it.

The fourth was arithmetic. If the third went as I expected, the rest was simply the passage of time. In a hundred years my grandchildren's grandchildren would be most of the people who lived, and the others would be, as my profession puts it, a minority population with excellent health outcomes. No one would be harmed. No one would be asked to leave.

You will have noticed that I used the word *grandchildren* as if I had any. I do not. I mean the word as it is used in banks.

**Risk.** There were three.

The first was detection. Everything I had done could be found by a competent person with the right machine, and the machine was getting cheaper. I made no secret of this to myself. It was the reason for the schedule: to be a quarter-century ahead of the machine, and to keep every step ordinary until it was too late to stop.

The second was the children themselves. I had not made them to be docile. I had made them to be quick, and a quick child grows up and asks questions. I thought of this as a design constraint, not a threat. I believed I could keep ahead of any one of them, since I had the paper.

The third risk I did not write down, and I record it now, late. It was that I might be wrong about myself.

* * *

I should explain the paper, because it matters later.

All of it lived in two places. The first was my head, where I trusted it. The second was a set of pages in my own hand, kept in a form that no one else could read quickly, in a place that I will not name in case I have the wrong sort of reader. I called them the index. They held what I had done, to whom, in what order, and what each thing was meant to become. Without the index, the children were a lineage with no map. With it, I could tell you in an afternoon which of them would find which, and I could stop it.

Most colleagues keep a backup. Most colleagues also have a department, and a mentor, and a spouse who will say *Frank, you are overdoing it.* I had none of those. I had a vault, which is a word I used because I liked the way it sounded, and I put the index in it, and I went on believing that a thing in a vault is a thing no one else has seen.

I want to be fair to the man I was. He believed this because it had always been true. The index had never been read by anyone but him. It had never been discovered, never lost, never been wrong. It is easy to take the record of a thing for the nature of it. I did, for eleven years, with the settled confidence of a man who has been right so often that being right has stopped feeling like an event.

I opened and locked it on the first Thursday of every month. I did it on the first Thursday before the boy opened my vault, though I did not yet know there was a boy who could, and I took my coat from the hook, and I went home, to a house where no one waited, and I slept very well.

* * *

I kept notes on the children the way a farmer keeps notes on weather: not because the notes change anything, but because a man who does not write it down begins to believe he remembers it.

There were never many. I will say that in my defense, if a defense is wanted. Over eleven years, in four hours a week, on one old incubator, I made fewer than sixty people. Some of those I never saw again after the transfer. Some I saw once, at a distance, in a park or a school corridor, and went home pleased and ashamed. The ones I tracked I tracked from outside, through the same public tables and school newsletters that had given me Danny Pruitt, and the notes that follow are what those sources gave me, and what I could not help noticing.

I give them as I wrote them, with the names changed in my head if not on the page.

* * *

**The Okafors.** A mother who taught secondary school, a father who repaired elevators. Two older children, then a late third, a girl. The girl read at three, which her parents put down to the older ones reading aloud, and did long division at five, which they put down to the school. At seven she reorganized the family's finances on the back of an envelope and was gently told that this was not a game. I admired the parents for that sentence. It was exactly wrong, and it was kind.

**The Brandts.** Both professors, at a small college, married to their work and to each other. They had waited until forty. The child was a boy who counted everything: stairs, bricks, the seconds between thunder and light. His mother wrote in a parents' newsletter that her son "liked to know how many." I read that sentence in a dentist's waiting room and had to put the magazine down.

**The Lindqvists.** A cardiologist and a violinist. Their daughter tuned the family piano by ear at six and corrected the violin teacher at eight. The violinist took it hard. A mother whose gift is her whole life does not enjoy being outplayed by a child with no practice, and for a year there was a coolness in that house that I could chart from outside, from the gaps in the child's lesson schedule. The coolness ended on its own. I do not know how. I am told that families have ways.

**The Duvals.** A bakery owner and a nurse who worked nights. I mention them because they were the Pruitts' kind: people who had not been to university and who had never needed to. Their son, who I will call Marc, was quick in a way that frightened him. At nine he was moved to a class two years ahead and cried every morning for a month, not from the work, which he did in minutes, but from what the other children said. His father, who could not follow a word of the textbook, sat with him at the kitchen table every night for a winter and read the problems aloud in a flat, patient voice, as if the numbers were a recipe. Marc did not need the help. He needed the sound of his father at the table. I put that in my notes without comment, as I put everything.

* * *

I should say something about the friction, since I promised myself I would be honest about it.

The homes with the least education had the most trouble, and the trouble was not what you would expect. The parents did not resent the children. The children did not despise the parents. What happened was smaller and worse. A mother would ask a question to which her son knew the whole answer and the whole history of the answer, and he would see her face and give her three words. A father would be proud and would not know of what. The children learned very early to translate themselves downward, and they did it so gracefully that no one noticed it was costing them anything.

The educated families had their own trouble, which was that nothing happened. There was never a gap to close. A child who has been understood all her life is a child who has never had to be brave about being misunderstood, and I suspected, without proof, that these were the ones who would find it hardest later.

I recorded all of it. I did not interfere. A different man might have taken the notes as a warning.

* * *

There were four I watched more closely, because they were interesting to me, and I will list them in the order I noticed them.

**A quiet boy** in a small town, the son of two schoolteachers, who at twelve took apart a radio and put it together as something better. I thought: *a physicist, if anyone is.* He had a habit of being very still when he was working, and of apologizing, when he was interrupted, to the object. He was a kind child. I wrote that down once and crossed it out, as irrelevant.

**Two girls** who lived on the same street and were not related and who, as far as I could tell from outside, had been inseparable from the age of five. I noted nothing about them except that they were happy. I did not then think of them as a pair. It would be many years before I understood that I had no category for what they were to each other, and that this was my failing and not theirs.

**A boy named Julian,** whom I will not describe in detail. He was the easiest child to like I have ever seen. Teachers liked him. Other children liked him. He was funny in the way that makes adults feel clever for noticing. He did not seem to need anything, which is a quality we admire in children and rarely examine. I wrote: *an unusually well-adjusted subject, high social intelligence, no concerns.* That is the sentence in all my notes that I would most like to take back.

I want to be careful about this, because I have been tempted since to tell a different story. I did not make Julian what he became. I have checked, with the only tools I have left, and the answer is that nothing I did could have produced it, because what he turned into is a thing that happens to a small fraction of all children at random, in every place and age, and that I had not designed and could not have prevented. It was bad luck. I want that written down, in my own hand, in case someone later wishes it to have been me.

* * *

That was the cohort. A few dozen people I mostly never met, in homes I mostly never entered, each carrying a little less or a little more of what I had put in them, each one growing up inside the ordinary difficulty of being a child, which I had not prevented and could not have.

I used to read the notes at night, in my kitchen, like a man reading letters. I told myself that I was auditing them. I believe now that I was keeping them company.

* * *

I said that I would not name the place where the index was kept. I have changed my mind, partly because the reader I expect is not the wrong sort, and partly because it has a part to play. It was a cabinet.

It stood against the east wall of my laboratory, a tall gray steel thing the width of a door, with two shelves, a drawer at the bottom, and a brass dial the size of a saucer set into the front. The clinic had bought it in the nineteen-eighties for the storage of controlled substances and then forgotten what it was for. When I took over the laboratory, I found it empty and unlocked. I put a hand on the dial and turned it a few clicks, and it ran with a pleasing, well-oiled resistance, like a good watch. I thought: *this will do.*

I chose the combination myself. It was three numbers, none of them a date.

What lived in it I have already told you. A bundle of pages, bound with a green ribbon, in my own hand, in a script that no one else could have followed quickly. A ledger, the true one, with the names. A second ledger of the cover story. Nothing else. I was disciplined about that. I had read somewhere that a safe with one thing in it is a safe that is looked at once, and a safe with a hundred things in it is a safe that is forgotten, and I chose, as a matter of principle, the first.

I opened it on the first Thursday of every month. I stood with my back to the door, facing the wall, and turned the dial in the way that muscles remember. I took out the pages and read them through, once, from the first to the last. I did this to check that nothing had changed. It was a ritual of the sort that only a lonely man invents, and I performed it with the same attention that other men give to a church.

It was a comfort. I will say that. I do not think a man can understand how deep a comfort until he has had one, and the shape of it was a small gray door in a plain room and a dial that turned, and the sense that behind it, perfectly preserved, lay all of the reasons for my life.

I never doubted the cabinet. It did not occur to me that it could be doubted. A lock that has held for eleven years has the authority of a law.

The wall behind it was an ordinary wall. I mention it because I never thought to. It was one of those partitions of lath and plaster that the building's first owners had put up in a hurry, and it divided my laboratory from the last of the examination rooms, a narrow room with a high window and a plastic chair and, on the wall, a poster of a smiling ear.

I had been in that room perhaps twice. I had never listened to it.


## 4. The Second Listener

The second one I saw by accident, which is how most of the important things in my life have been seen.

I had gone to a county aquatic center to watch a different child, a boy with a modest expression of the cognitive package, who swam a mediocre backstroke and read at a college level. He was fine. He was exactly what I had intended, and he bored me. I had a program, a cup of lukewarm coffee, and a seat in the upper rows, where the chlorine made my eyes water and the noise of two hundred parents rose off the water in a great flat roar.

The girl was in lane six. She was eight, thin, with a swim cap printed with cartoon whales, and she was not swimming. It was a warm-up, and the other children were doing what children do at warm-ups, which is everything except warm up. She stood on the edge of the pool with her eyes shut and her head tilted, as if she were listening to a distant radio.

Then she dived. Eyes closed. She went in without a splash and swam the length of the lane, and I assumed, as any reasonable man would, that she would hit the wall. She did not. She turned a hand's width short of it, neatly, with her eyes still shut, and swam back.

A coach saw it, laughed, and called something. She opened her eyes and shrugged.

I did the arithmetic in my seat. The roar in the room had no clean echoes in it, no single surface for a sound to bounce from. The wall was smooth tile and a few feet from the edge of the lane, and a ceiling full of baffles was doing everything possible to prevent it from being useful. A child with ordinary hearing could not have found that wall. A child with the circuits I had built could, because they were not made to hear a wall. They were made to hear where sound has been.

I want to be accurate about what I felt. I did not feel triumph. A man who has been told for eleven years that his life's secret work is a delusion would have felt triumph, and I did not, because it was not a delusion, and the only thing that is worse than being told you are wrong is being proved right at the edge of a swimming pool with nobody to tell.

Her parents were in the second row, a woman in a linen jacket and a man with a laptop open on his knee. A professor and an engineer, I would learn. They were reading together, not watching, and when the girl got out, dripping, and ran up to them to describe something, the mother put one finger on the page to hold her place and said, "Tell me in a sentence, sweetheart. Then I'll listen."

She did. I could see it from forty feet away. It was a loving, fluent, efficient exchange between three people who had the vocabulary to say what they meant, and she was, I saw, fine. She was entirely fine. There would be no friction in that house, no flat piano notes or fumbled explanations. She had been placed in the one kind of home that could absorb her.

I envied the Pruitts, in a way, for what they had. I envied the professor and the engineer for how easy it would be.

I stayed to the end of the meet. The mediocre swimmer came in fifth. I left without my coffee.

On the drive home I realized I was driving with the radio off, in a silence that was a little less empty than it should have been, and I thought: *there are at least two of them now, and neither knows the other exists.*

That was a Tuesday. I remember because the following Monday my left hand began to shake, and I put it down to coffee.

* * *

I called her B. in the notes. Danny was A. It was neat, and it spared me from using names, which have a way of attaching themselves to things.

I did not follow her. I want that understood, because a man in my position might be expected to. I had given up following the first month. What I did was subscribe, in the name of the clinic, to the quarterly bulletin of the school district, and to the county library's newsletter, and to a small magazine for parents that listed prizes. There is a great deal to be learned from prize lists. They are the only public record in the country of what children can do.

B. appeared in them four times in two years. She won a prize at a regional science fair for a poster entitled *How Do Animals Tell Where They Are?* She took second at a school spelling bee, deliberately, I suspected, having lost the final word on a vowel that she must have known. She played the oboe. She took a ribbon for a project involving marbles and the speed of sound in different floors. Her photograph appeared in a library newsletter next to a story about a bake sale, a thin girl in a green sweater with a ribbon on her chest and her head turned slightly to the left, listening to something that the photographer could not hear.

I had her cut out and put in the file.

* * *

There are two of them now, I wrote that autumn, in the notebook that was not yet a notebook, in a column of figures on a page of the clinic's own pad. *Two with the second package. Both in the better families. Neither aware. A. is quiet. B. is not. Both are well.*

It was the first time I had written the word *well* in that column.

I had made, in all, two. I should explain why I stopped there. It was not for lack of means. I had, at that time, the bench and the hours, and the method had steadied to the point where I could have done it in my sleep. It was a matter of caution, or what I called caution. The second package was the most conspicuous thing I had done, the one most likely to be noticed by someone with a good microphone and a suspicious nature. A child with the first package alone could pass for a gifted child with a good memory. A child with the second could not pass for anything. She could only be hidden.

So I decided, in the spring of 2008, that two were enough. That was the arithmetic I used. I told myself that the work would be proved by two cases or by none, and that the century could wait for a better method, and a better decade.

I now think I stopped because I was afraid of how much I liked them.

* * *

The tremor was a small thing, and I treated it as one. Small things, I have found, are the ones that kill you, because you are busy with the large ones.

For three years it came and went. A cup rattled in its saucer. A signature wandered off the line. My neurologist, a tidy woman with an unfashionable fondness for the phrase "let's keep an eye on that," ordered scans, and the scans showed what scans show, which is that the brain is a soft machine with an unreliable power supply. In March of 2011 I had what she called a transient episode. For twenty minutes the left side of my face refused to take instructions, and the word for a pen deserted me. I sat in the parking lot of the clinic with my hands in my lap and said "pen" aloud, over and over, until it returned. It came back with a limp, as if it had been somewhere unpleasant.

I told no one. I did not tell her, either. I said I had been tired.

It was in that month, while I was relearning to tie my shoes without looking at them, that the manuscript arrived.

* * *

It came by email from the editorial office of a genomics journal, which had my name on a list of reviewers for reasons of long standing and very little merit. Reviewers are asked to read in confidence, to say nothing, and to answer within three weeks. It is the one corner of science in which a man can know something no one else knows, legitimately, and be thanked for keeping quiet.

The authors were a team in Singapore. It was a population study, one of the dull large ones that governments fund so that someone can say they did: four thousand volunteers, sequenced deeply, for reasons having to do with heart disease. The abstract ran to six lines.

I read it standing up in my kitchen.

Line five said: *In 11 participants, we identified a short unannotated region of unclear origin, not matched to the reference.* Line six said: *We regard this as a likely artifact and plan to repeat the analysis.*

It was not an artifact. I knew it as one knows a voice in a crowd. Eleven participants, and none of them mine. The fragment was one of ours, or something very like it, and it was sitting in a stranger's blood on the other side of the world.

I want to be careful about how I explain what I understood in that kitchen, because I understood several things at once, and not in order.

The first was that I was not the only person who had ever done this. I had assumed, with the vanity of a man who works alone, that I was. But nobody makes an artifact that looks like that unless somebody has made the thing itself, and if somebody else had, there was a world in which the work was already moving, unwatched, with all my controls missing.

The second was that the manuscript had landed on my desk by an accident of mailing lists, and that I was, for three weeks, the only person alive who understood what it said. The authors did not know. The editor did not know. In a year they would repeat the analysis, and in two the fragment would be annotated, and in three a graduate student would ask a question in a corridor and the answer would be a surname I would recognize.

The third was that I could not read the last paragraph.

I had noticed it in the second sentence: the print slid a little at the left of the page. I tilted my head. It slid more. I moved to the window and held the page to the light, and the words on the left swam away as if I were reading through water.

I put the paper down on the counter. I put my hand flat on it. The hand shook, and I watched it shake, and I understood that I had perhaps a year of reliable eyes and a decade of unreliable ones, and that there was an eleven-year-old, somewhere in a city I could drive to, who would soon be able to find all of this himself.

* * *

I wrote the review that night. A reviewer may recommend that a paper be accepted, revised, or rejected, and I knew all three were in my power. Acceptance would put the fragment in print. Revision would invite the authors to look harder. I chose rejection, on the grounds of *insufficient validation of the unannotated region and inadequate controls*, which are the words you use when you mean nothing at all and wish to be believed.

It was a lie of exactly the size I had been telling for years. I noticed that. I noticed also that it would buy me perhaps a year, because a rejected paper does not vanish. It goes to another journal, and another reviewer, who is not me.

I thought: *I should run.*

I thought: *I should burn it all, and be the sort of man who does.*

And then, because I am not a good man but I am a methodical one, I took out a notebook with my right hand, and I began to write down everything I could remember, in order, before it left me.

That was the first night of the account you are reading.


## 5. The Apartment

I want to set down one thing about the picnics before I go further, because it is the only truthful thing I have to say about them.

I never saw the boy's face at a picnic. I saw his back, his shoulder, the angle of a head, a yellow sleeve at the edge of a crowd. In eleven years I had made it a point never to stand where he might turn and meet my eyes. It was not shyness. It was a kind of superstition, of the sort that attaches to men who work alone. I believed that if I looked him in the face while he looked at me, something would be said that could not be unsaid.

I was not wrong about that. I was only wrong about the date.

* * *

The clinic held a picnic every June for the families it had helped. It was the director's idea, and a good one, in the sense that it produced photographs for the annual report. I attended three times in eleven years, always for forty minutes, always in a short-sleeved shirt that I disliked, and always with the feeling of a man at a funeral for someone he had not been told was dead.

In June of 2010 I went because the Pruitts had RSVPed.

I had not planned to speak to them. I had planned to stand near the lemonade, hold a paper cup, and look at a boy of ten from a distance of sixty yards, which was the distance at which I had been looking at him for most of his life. But Connie Pruitt found me within ten minutes. She had a paper plate with a hot dog on it and a smile that arrived before she did.

"Dr. Stone! Ray, it's Dr. Stone."

Ray came over from the grill with a spatula in his hand and shook mine with the other, and I had the old impression that his palm was rougher than my whole life.

"I'll tell you something," Connie said, before I had finished saying hello. "You gave us a funny one."

I put down my cup. "Funny."

"Oh, in a good way. He told his teacher last month her fire alarm was off key. And she says, *Danny, it's a fire alarm.* And he says, *Yes, but it was a flat B.* And she checked, and the maintenance man said it was a flat B." She laughed. "The whole school's talking about it. The principal asked if we'd had his ears tested."

"And did you?"

"We said he'd been tested. When he was little, the lady at the hospital. She said he heard too well." She waved the hot dog. "I said, is there such a thing? She said not usually."

I looked past her shoulder. The boy was sitting alone on a picnic table at the edge of the grass, a thin child in a yellow T-shirt with a book on his knee, not reading. He was looking at nothing in particular. He was angled very slightly away from the loudest part of the park, and his head was turned a few degrees toward the trees.

"He says you have a nervous heart," Connie said.

I looked at her.

"I told him not to say that kind of thing to people. But he says he can hear it from over there. Isn't that a hoot?" She laughed again, kindly. "I told him, honey, it's the heat. People get nervous in the heat."

"It is warm," I said.

"We don't mind the funny stuff," Ray said. He said it quietly, so that his wife would not hear it as a correction. "He's a good kid. He's just a lot, sometimes. I don't always know what he's saying." He rubbed the back of his neck with the spatula hand. "He's real patient with me about it."

That was the sentence I carried home. *He's real patient with me about it.*

I stood in my kitchen that night with the sentence in front of me like a bill. I had done a great many things to Ray Pruitt without his knowledge, and the greater part of them were in the boy's blood, where nobody could see them. I had expected, over the years, to feel the weight of it. I had expected the reckoning to arrive in some form: an accusation, a lawsuit, an unexpected phone call. What I had not expected was that the first cost would be an ordinary man's gratitude, and that I would have to stand there and take it, and that he would thank me.

I waited that night for the punishment. It did not come. I have since come to think that this is the worst kind.

* * *

The hearing check in March, which I did not attend, left a note.

It was on my desk when I came back from a meeting, a half sheet of the clinic's pale blue paper, in the round hand of the nurse who had been covering. I read such notes as I read the weather, for what they could not tell me. This one said:

*Danny P. (age 11). Hearing normal for age. Parent present. Bright, polite. Waited 40 min in Exam 3 (apologies, ran behind). Asked a lot of questions about the building. Did the doctor keep sweets in the gray cabinet? I said I didn't know. Mother apologized for him. Charming child.*

I read it standing at the desk, with my coat still on. I smiled. I remember that I smiled. I thought: *a boy of eleven asked about sweets.*

I put it in the file. I filed it, in the correct drawer, under the correct letter, with the correct date. It is there still, I suppose, if the file survived. I think of it as the clearest piece of evidence in the history of the world that a man can have the answer in his hand and be unable to read it.

* * *

The next ten weeks were the quietest of my life. I understand now that the boy was working.

He did not write. He did not call. I saw him once, in June, through a window of the clinic, a thin figure in a yellow shirt sitting with his mother in the waiting room, reading a thick book about bridges. Our eyes did not meet. He turned a page. He had a way of turning pages that I had never noticed before, a deliberate, pressing motion, as of a person pressing a flower.

In July, on a hot Tuesday, I received a package at the clinic with no return address. It contained a single object: a small, plain, brown paper bag of the sort that holds a sandwich. Inside the bag there was a boiled sweet in a twist of cellophane, a barley sugar, slightly sticky, and no note.

I ate it. I do not know why. I sat at my desk and unwrapped it and put it in my mouth, and it tasted exactly as such things do, of burned sugar and childhood, and I sat there with my eyes closed and a warm unpleasant feeling behind them that I put down to the heat.

It did not occur to me to ask who had sent it. It did not occur to me, either, to ask why a boy should send sweets to a man who had none.

* * *

The boy was eleven when it ended.

I will tell the next part in the order it happened, since I know the order, and since I have come to distrust myself when I try to improve on it.

I had made my last Thursday visit to the vault in September. I know that because I have the dates. I had checked the index, as I did each month, and found nothing wrong, and I closed the cabinet. In October I wrote to the Pruitts' address, in the name of the clinic, offering a routine hearing check. They agreed in two days. Connie wrote back that Danny "would love to see you again." I read that line standing up, in my coat, with the envelope still in my hand.

The appointment was set for a Tuesday. On the Monday evening I received a message that had not come through any of the usual channels. It was a single sheet of paper in an envelope without a stamp, left in the mailbox of my house. It said, in a child's neat, upright hand:

*Come to the apartment on Tuesday at two. Not the clinic. My parents will be at work. Please take the stairs.*

I should have stopped there. I did not. I wish I could tell you that I did not know what the note meant. I knew precisely what it meant, in the way you know a sound in your own house at night, and I thought: *I will go, and I will be calm, and I will find out how much he knows.*

* * *

By the autumn of 2011 I had begun to sleep badly. The doctors had a word for the cause, a long and sensible one, and I took the tablets and slept badly anyway.

I woke at four on most mornings. I made tea, and stood at the kitchen window while it cooled, and looked at the dark garden. There was a fox that crossed the lawn at ten past four, with a trotting, insolent gait. I took an absurd comfort in it. I think it was the only creature in my life that arrived on time and wanted nothing.

I did not tell myself what I was waiting for. I told myself it was the weather.

The note had come on a Monday. I have said that I should have stopped there. What I did not say was that I read it standing in the hall, in my coat, and the first thing I felt was not fear. It was something much closer to relief, the plain physical relief of a man who has carried a case for eleven years and has at last been told where to put it down.

I did not sleep that night. At four I stood at the window and the fox did not come.

* * *

The building was an old brick block of six floors on a street I knew by sight. The lobby had black and white tiles and a ceiling with plaster rosettes. It smelled of floor polish and of somebody's soup. A doorman sat behind a small desk with a newspaper, a heavyset man in a navy blazer with a name tag that said **WALT**, and he looked up as I came in with the weary courtesy of one who has seen every kind of visitor and prefers none.

"Help you?"

"I'm visiting the Pruitts. Four C. Or four D. I'm sorry, I've forgotten."

He glanced at a ledger. "Four C is Aldana. Pruitt is two B." He made a mark. "Name?"

I gave it. He wrote it down, in a square, laborious hand, and underneath it, in smaller figures, the time: **1:52.**

"Second floor," he said. "Stairs on your left. Elevator's slow."

"I'll take the stairs."

He looked at me. It was the lightest of glances, up and down, taking in the coat and the limp and the expression of a man preparing to be sensible. "You're the first visitor for the Pruitts since Easter," he said. "Usually nobody comes." He shrugged and returned to his paper. "The kid's popular with the old ladies, though. Soup."

I went up the stairs, one flight, with my hand on the rail.

* * *

His parents were at work, which I had also checked, from the clinic's records, which was one of the things I should not have been able to do.

The boy opened the door before I knocked.

He was eleven that autumn. I want to say that he looked like any child of that age, and he did. He had a gray sweater with a hole at one cuff and he held a book he was not reading, and he stood to one side of the doorway at an angle that I understood later was geometry. He was keeping me where the room would tell him what I did.

"You took the stairs," he said.

I had not said a word. "I like stairs."

"Your pulse is a hundred and eight."

I laughed. It was a poor laugh, and the left side of my mouth did not join in. The doctors had a name for what that side was doing, and a schedule for it, and I had thrown the schedule away. "It's a hundred and eight because I'm sixty-one."

"No," he said. "That's the reason for the limp."

I sat down. The chair was a cheap one and it faced the window, which I realize now was also arranged. He took the other chair, the one that faced me, and put the book on his knee as if the book would say something.

The apartment was clean in the way of a home where two people work and the third is careful. A shelf of rocks. A calendar from the bakery supplier. On the refrigerator, a drawing by a younger child of a dog with too many legs, and beside it, in a different hand, a diagram of what I took to be the acoustics of the room, with arrows.

I had come with a speech. I had practiced it in the car: that the Singapore matter was a question of months, that a competent reviewer would find the fragment in a week, that there were people who would put a man in prison for what I had done and put what I had made somewhere worse. It was a good speech. I had written it the way I write everything, with the conclusion first.

"Don't," the boy said, before I got to the second sentence. "You'll do better if you let me say it."

"Say what?"

"What you did. I've read it. All of it."

I want to be exact here, because later I would be asked, by people with power, how long I had known. The answer is that I knew in that moment. Not before. I had locked the indexed notes in a place I considered a vault, and in nine seconds a child told me, without raising his voice, that the vault had been a courtesy.

"Where did you find it?"

He did not answer. He tilted his head very slightly toward the wall behind me, listening to something I could not hear, and then back.

"Mrs. Aldana in 4C is going to knock in about forty seconds to ask whether I want soup," he said. "I'd like it if you were already gone."

"I came to talk."

"I know. I have things to say first." He looked at me with the clear, level look of a child who has been told all his life that he is not to be rude. "I've read the Singapore paper too."

My mouth was dry. "That was confidential."

"You kept a copy in the vault. With your notes on what to say." He said it without reproach, the way one reports a misfiled form. "You write everything down. It's the thing I like best about you."

I will not describe what I felt. I will say that I had told myself for eleven years that a locked room was a private room, and that the only person who had ever shared my handwriting was a boy of eleven in a gray sweater.

"How long," I said.

"Since spring. I found where it was in March and I worked out the rest by summer." He turned the book on his knee. "I've been reading about you for a while."

"Why?"

He thought about it, with the care of a child who does not wish to say something unkind.

"To see what you were like."

* * *

Mrs. Aldana knocked in forty-one seconds.

I do not think he intended it as a joke.


## 6. The Bargain

We went through to the kitchen, because the woman in 4C had decided that a visitor should be fed.

She was a small, brisk widow with a cardigan the color of oatmeal, and she stood at the door with a covered dish and the expression of someone who had heard every word through the wall and had resolved not to have. "I made too much," she said. "You must be the doctor. Danny's told me all about you." She looked at me, then at the boy, then at the dish. "I'll leave it. Don't let it go cold."

When she had gone the boy lifted the lid, smelled it with his eyes closed, and said, "Lentils. She puts too much cumin in. I never say."

"You're a very polite child."

"I'm an average child with unusual hearing," he said. "It's the same thing." He fetched two bowls, as if he had done it all his life, and sat down across from me with the book beside his spoon. "I'm going to say what I want. Then you can say what you want. Then we'll see if there's a place where they touch."

I want to be honest about what I felt, since it was not what I had prepared for. I felt, to my surprise, the thing I feel in front of a good student.

* * *

He had four terms. He gave them in order, the way I do, with the least important first.

"One. You don't come to this building again. If you need to see me, you write, the way I did."

"Agreed."

"Two. You don't contact my parents. No more follow-ups. No letters from the clinic. If they ask you something, you answer it, and you don't start anything."

"That is already my practice."

"It's been your practice because you were afraid. I'd like it to be your practice because you promised." He ate a spoonful and waited for me to say I would. I said I would.

"Three. You don't make more of us without telling me."

I put my spoon down.

"Not stop," he said, before I could speak. "I know you won't stop. I read what you were planning. I know about the century." He said the word without any weight at all, as if he were reading a bus timetable. "I'm not going to ask you to stop something you've decided to do for a century. I'm asking you to tell me. Who. When. Which family. I'd like to know them before they know themselves."

"Why?"

"Because somebody should."

I will give you my answer to that, and then I will tell you what I should have said. My answer was a silence, long enough that the lentils cooled. What I should have said was: *Because you are right.*

"Four," the boy said. He took a sip of water. "You write it all down. Everything you remember. Not the index. I have the index."

My hands did something at my sides, and he saw it.

"I have four copies," he said gently. "Don't ask where. They are in different places, and none of them is in this building, and none of them is anywhere you can find. I don't want you to feel I took it. It's more like I borrowed it. If you ever need to know what's in it, you can ask me."

"And the fourth term?"

"Write it down. The part that isn't in the index. Why." He looked at me with that clear, level gaze. "I read the pages and the pages tell me what. They don't tell me why. I don't think you've told anyone. I think you'd like to."

* * *

I said yes to all four.

I have told you that the boy could hear a pulse. I have not yet told you what he did with that, and I should, because it is the part I am least proud of and the part that taught me the most.

When I said *agreed* to the third term, he stopped with the spoon halfway to his mouth.

"No," he said. "You don't."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You don't mean it. Your heart went up by six." He put the spoon down. He did not look angry. He looked, if anything, patient, as one looks at an old dog that will not stay out of the road. "It's all right. I thought you might not."

I opened my mouth. I had a defense ready. It was a good one. I had written it in the car.

"I'm eleven," the boy said. "I can't stop you. I know that. If I tell someone, they'll take you away, and then they'll take us away, and then there won't be anything left to protect. So I can't tell. I've thought about it for months, and the answer's always the same." He paused. "But I wanted to know whether you'd say it anyway. And you did. So now I know."

"Know what?"

"That I'm going to have to watch."

I do not remember the walk to my car. I remember that the stairs seemed longer going down than coming up, and that on the second landing I stopped and stood with my hand on the rail, because my legs had grown unreliable in a way that had nothing to do with my health.

I had come to give a speech to a child, and a child had given a speech to me. I had been judged by the only jury in the world with the qualifications, and the verdict was not guilty, and the sentence was that I would be observed.

* * *

Now I have to tell you what I did next, and I will do it in the order it happened, with no excuse.

I went back to the clinic that evening, let myself into the lab with the key that was mine, and sat down at my bench.

It was the second week of November. There were three embryos in the old incubator that I had been keeping in reserve for the spring, for a couple from the other side of the county who had waited seven years. They were ordinary. I had not touched them. They were what they were, and I could have returned them to the couple in April, with a small apology about the delay, and no one would ever have known the difference.

I want to tell you about the hour I sat looking at those three.

I told myself the following. That a promise made under duress is not a promise. That a child of eleven was not a regulatory authority. That I had a plan of a century and a body that, as of the previous March, was beginning to break its contracts. That if I waited, I might not be able to. That the boy would watch, but watching is not stopping, and I would at least know exactly what he could see.

I told myself that this was the last batch, the last improvement, the final adjustment, and that it was for the sake of the work.

That last sentence is the one I would like to strike out. I have tried. It remains.

I did what I did in the usual four minutes and a little, with the camera, as I have said, unrepaired. I did not tell the boy. I wrote nothing down. I returned the embryos to the incubator, signed out at 11:52, and drove home with the radio off.

I slept very badly that night. The next morning, over coffee, I noticed that I could not read the headline of the newspaper on the left side of the page. I read it with my head turned. It said something about a bridge. I put the paper down, and I took my pen in my right hand, and for the first time in weeks I wrote, on a fresh page of the notebook, the answer to the boy's fourth term.

It began: *The reason I did this was not that I thought I was good. It was that I was afraid of being ordinary.*

I do not know if it is true. It is the first thing I have ever written that I could not edit afterward.

* * *

It was three weeks before I understood how he had done it.

I had gone through the possibilities in order, as I go through everything. A copied key: no, the cabinet had a combination lock, an old brass one with a dial and a spindle, and there was no key to copy. A confederate: no, I had no confederate, and neither, so far as I could tell, had he. A flaw in the cabinet itself: I had the locksmith up, on a pretext, and he spent twenty minutes on his knees and went away saying that it was *a very honest lock.*

I sat in the lab, one evening in December, with the lights off and my hands flat on the bench, and made myself go back through the year.

In March I had had a consented test. It was one of the rare ones, a hearing check for a family who had asked. The family was the Pruitts. The boy had come in with his mother, on a Wednesday morning, to the examination room at the end of the corridor, and I had been out of the building, at a meeting, and had left the check to a nurse. It had not seemed important. It had been the boy's own request, relayed by his mother, with the note that *Danny would like to see where the doctor works.*

I thought of the plan of the building. I took out a sheet of paper and drew it, in pencil, from memory, with the neat labels I use for everything.

The lab was at the north end of the corridor. The cabinet stood against its east wall. The east wall was the west wall of Examination Room 3, where the boy had waited for his appointment, for forty minutes, because the nurse was late, with his head against the plaster and a book on his knee.

I put down the pencil.

That Wednesday, at a quarter to eleven, I had come back from the meeting early. I remembered it now with total clarity: the empty corridor, the smell of the floor polish, my own footsteps. I had gone into the lab to check a file. I had opened the cabinet. I had stood, as I always did, with my back to the door, and turned the dial: left to forty-one, right to seven, left to twenty-two, and the handle.

It is a lock with a particular voice. Each number drops a tumbler into its slot with a click, thin and distinct, in a rhythm you can hear if you hold your breath. I had never held mine. I had turned that dial at least a hundred and thirty times in eleven years, in a room with thick walls, and it had not once occurred to me that the walls were only thick to ears like mine.

He had been eleven. He had been listening to a plaster wall for forty minutes because he had nothing else to do. And on the other side of it, at a quarter to eleven, a man he hardly knew had opened a cabinet.

He had counted the clicks. As other children count stairs.

In June there had been a second visit, a growth check that the nurse had insisted upon, and she had been called away for ten minutes to fetch a form. The lab door, which I locked at night and left on the latch by day, had been ajar. Ten minutes is long enough to turn three numbers and to photograph forty pages with a telephone. I had not thought of telephones. I was sixty-one years old, and I had designed a child to hear a pin drop in the next building, and I had never once thought of the telephone in his pocket.

* * *

The first letter came in the first week of January.

It came without a stamp, as the note had, in a plain white envelope, put through the slot of my front door at some hour when I was asleep. I do not know how he got it there. I did not ask. I held it for some time before I opened it.

It was a single sheet. It said, in the neat upright hand:

*Dear Dr. Stone,*

*You asked me how I got in. I think you will have worked it out by now. I am sorry about the wall. I did not mean to listen. It was the only thing in the room.*

*I have been thinking about the first term. I would like to change it. You may come to the building if you need to, so long as you tell me first. I think it is better if we know where the other one is.*

*I hope your eyes are better.*

*Danny*

I read it at the kitchen table, in my coat, for a long time. The handwriting was beautiful. It was the handwriting of a boy who has been told to take his time.

There was a postscript, in smaller letters, squeezed at the bottom edge of the page, as if added at the last moment.

*P.S. Mrs. Aldana says you looked thin. She is sending soup.*

* * *

I wrote back that evening. It took me four drafts, which is three more than I have ever needed for a letter. I said that I was grateful. I said that the first term could stand as it was. I said that I would tell him if I made any more of them, and then I looked at that sentence for a long while, and crossed out *if* and wrote *when,* and then crossed out *when,* and left it blank, with a small pencilled line where the word should have been.

I do not know why I did not tell the truth. I had told it, in the end, to the notebook. It was easier to tell a notebook. A notebook does not have a pulse.

I put the letter in an envelope. I stamped it, which seemed absurd, and I walked it to the mailbox at the end of the road in the dark. It was very cold. The streetlamps made small yellow rooms in the frost. At the mailbox I stood a moment with my hand on the slot, and had the odd conviction that I was being listened to, from some great distance, by someone patient and young, who had not yet decided what to make of me.

I let go. The letter dropped. I heard it land on the others with a soft dry sound, and I turned for home, and I thought: *he has a better ear than I made him.*

It was the first time I had ever thought of it as an accident.


## 7. The Stroke

It happened on a Thursday, which I mention only because I have mentioned the Thursdays before, and I should like the record to show that I noticed the irony and did not enjoy it.

It was the second week of April. The windows of the lab were open an inch, for the first time since October, and the air that came in smelled of wet earth and bus exhaust. I had arrived at six, as I always did, and put the kettle on, and sat down at my bench with the three-by-five cards for the next batch spread in front of me like a patient hand of solitaire.

I should say what else was on the desk, since it is part of the picture.

On the left was a folded sheet of paper from the front office, which had come up with the morning mail. It was a routine confirmation, in the dull language of such forms, that a patient of the clinic had returned a positive result. The patient was a woman in her late thirties, from the other side of the county. She and her husband had waited seven years. In November I had taken three embryos out of an incubator and done something to them that I had promised a child I would not do, and in March I had placed one of them in her, with a steady hand and a firm voice, and told her, with a smile, that it was a good embryo.

It had held. I looked at the sheet for a long time. I did not feel guilt. I want to be honest: I felt something I can only describe as tenderness, of the kind a man feels for a thing he has made and has not yet met.

I folded the sheet in half. I put it under the stapler.

I do not know what became of the child. I know only that there was one.

* * *

The kettle had begun to whistle. I had the cards in my right hand. They were the white ones with the green line, and I had written on them in the small, upright script I have always used for things I do not wish to be read.

Then the floor came up.

It was not a fall. It was more as if the building had been tilted a few degrees by a person who wished to see what would roll. The cards left my hand. I watched them go, one by one, in no hurry, as if they too were considering the matter. I remember thinking, with great clarity, that I should call someone. I remember reaching for the word for the telephone. It was not there. In its place was a quiet, smooth gap, like the place in a mouth where a tooth has been.

*Pen,* I said, or tried to say. *Pen. Phone. Pen.*

The sound that came out was not a word. It was breath, in a shape. I lay on my side on the cold floor with the kettle screaming and my cheek against a tile. I could see under the bench. There was a paperclip there, and a single glove, and a small piece of green card.

I thought: *so this is how it is done.*

I thought, with a strange and tidy irony, that I had always known what it was to close a window. I had had my own, once, four minutes and eleven seconds of it. I had timed it. I had been a man who timed things. And now my own was closing, with the cameras unrepaired, on a floor, with no one to time it for me.

I counted. I had lost the numbers. I counted anyway, in a language of my own, and lost it.

* * *

The technician found me at six-forty. He was not the same technician; the Thursday man had left some years before. But he was a man of the same type, with a small, consistent economy of his own. He knelt beside me and said my name, and for the first time in thirteen years I wanted nothing more than to answer him.

I am told the rest. I am told that the paramedics were quick, and that I was in the hospital within the hour, and that the doctors said there was a window of treatment and that it was closing. They said it to the air above me. They said it in the voice that people use to speak over a patient who may or may not be listening. I was listening. I could not tell them that I had had my own window once, and that I had always known what it was.

I remember the corridor. The ceiling lights went by, one, two, three, in a long white strobe. I remember the smell of antiseptic. I remember a young doctor's face, close to mine, with a small cut on the chin from a morning shave, saying: *Dr. Stone, can you squeeze my hand?* I did what I could. I do not know which hand it was.

I remember a nurse's voice, a woman's, kind and tired, who said to someone I could not see: *He keeps trying to say something.*

* * *

I have been told what the stroke took, and I will set it down plainly, because it is the reason this account exists.

The index was not in the vault. It never had been, in the sense that mattered. The vault held the pages. The meaning of the pages was in my head, in a language only I had learned, and what the stroke took was the grammar.

When I looked at my own handwriting, afterward, I could read the letters and not the sentences. I could say, *that is an A, and that is a number, and that is the name of a family,* and I could not say what any of it was for. It was as if a man had been left with every word of a great poem and the ability to read only its punctuation. I sat in a hospital chair, on the fourth day, with the pages on my knee, which someone had brought from the lab in a plastic bag, and I turned them, one after another, and felt nothing at all.

That was the most frightening hour of my life. I wish I could say that it was because of the plan, the century, the children. It was not. It was because I knew, with perfect clarity, that I did not mind.

I had spent forty years being a person who could tell you at any moment what he had done and why. In the space of an afternoon I had become someone who could not. And the man I had been, who would have been appalled, was no longer present to be appalled. There was only a calm old man in a hospital gown, holding a handful of paper, and the calm was the worst of it.

I could read, however, the notebook. It was written in plain words. I had been writing it for a year, in the evenings, with my right hand, in the voice of a man explaining himself to a stranger. It was, I understood, in the bed with the rail up, the only complete account of anything that remained.

I could not say so. I could only listen to the machines.

* * *

The clinic's director came on the eighth day.

She stood at the foot of the bed in a good coat, with a bunch of flowers wrapped in paper, and the expression of a person who has been told to say something kind. She was a competent, courteous, harassed woman whom I had known for twenty-four years and had never once told the truth. She said that everyone at the clinic sent their love. She said the program was in good hands. She said that Dr. Okonjo, who had taken over my cases, was *a very safe pair of hands,* and I thought, from the way she said it, that she meant it as a reproach.

She said she had been to my lab.

I looked at her. I could not speak, but I could look. I had always been told that I had a good look.

"I found the cabinet," she said. She was fiddling with the paper on the flowers. "I didn't open it. It was locked, and I didn't want to presume. But I thought I should tell you. I've asked the facilities people to leave it alone, until—" She stopped. "Until you're better."

I held her eyes.

"And the incubator," she said. "The old one. I've asked them to... well. I'm sure there's nothing in it." She laughed, lightly, as people do at a joke that has not been made. "Four hours a week. You're the only person I've ever known who works *less* than he's allowed."

She put the flowers on the table. She touched my hand, once, and left.

I did not know whether she knew. I have wondered since, many times. I do not think it matters. A woman who has decided not to look is as good as blind, and I had made her so.

* * *

I was moved on the fifteenth day to a place with a view of a parking lot.

It was a rehabilitation unit, and the staff were brisk and cheerful, and they gave me a board with the alphabet on it and told me to point. I pointed. I spelled out, with my right hand, very slowly, the word *notebook.*

The nurse, a woman in her fifties with a silver cross at her throat, looked at me with real kindness. "A notebook? Of course, Dr. Stone. I'll see to it."

She did. It was in my locker the next morning, in the black cover, with the tape on the spine, and I took it into my hands and held it. It was warm from the radiator. I opened it to the last page I had written, and read the last sentence, and could not remember writing it.

It said: *There are things I should tell you, and I will, in order.*

I have not told them in order. I have told them as they came. But I have told them, as far as I am able, and when I could not, I have left the line blank.

* * *

This is the end of the part that is mine. I have composed these last pages on the board with the alphabet on it, a letter at a time, over a good many months, with a patient aide writing down what I pointed to. They are therefore short.

I am told that the one who reads this will be younger than I was when I began it, and that he will have my ears, or better. I am told that he will do with it what he chooses. ## 8. The Notebook

You have just read Stone's account. I should say that I have not changed a word of it.

I have been tempted. There are places where I could have improved him: made him kinder, or crueler, or simply less in love with the sound of his own sentences. There are places where I know he is wrong, and I could have said so in a footnote. I have not. A man's account of himself is a piece of evidence, and the only honest thing to do with evidence is to hand it over whole. What follows is my own account, which he would have corrected, and which I have tried to keep as plain as his.

I was sixteen when I found it, and I want to say at once that I was not looking for it.

* * *

For four years I had known that Stone was in a care home forty minutes from our apartment. My parents knew it too, in the way they knew about a distant cousin's operation: with a kind sentence and no follow-up. *Poor Dr. Stone. He was so good to us.* My mother sent him a card each Christmas, signed by all three of us. I signed mine with a period after my name, which I thought was funny.

I had kept the terms of the bargain. I had not gone near him. I had written no letters. When the stroke came, in the spring of 2012, I learned about it from the clinic's newsletter, which printed a paragraph of regret under a photograph of him in a bow tie at a picnic, looking, as always, like a man who had lost a bet. I read it twice. I did not feel what I had expected to feel. I felt, I am ashamed to say, a kind of weightlessness, like a boy whose teacher has been called out of the room.

It lasted about a week. Then I began, slowly and with no plan, to feel afraid.

Stone had been the only other person who knew. For a year I had been the watcher and he the watched, and I had told myself that this was the order of things, and that it would last. Now there was no one to watch. There was a man in a bed who could not speak, and a world that did not know there was anything to speak about. And I was eleven, then twelve, then thirteen, and a person, I understood, who held in his head the only complete account of what had been done to fifty-eight children, and who had told no one.

I carried it for four years. I do not think I have ever been so lonely.

* * *

I went in the spring of 2016, because my mother's Christmas card had come back marked *undeliverable,* and I thought, with the logic of an adolescent, that someone had better find out.

The care home had been moved. I learned it from a woman on the telephone, a patient, tired voice that gave me the new address without asking who I was. It was an old brick building at the end of a long drive, with a parking lot, a flagpole, and a sign that said **WILLOW GROVE: A COMMUNITY OF CARE.** It smelled of boiled carrots and floor wax. In the entrance hall there was a corkboard with a notice about a bingo night and another about a visiting petting zoo.

The aide at the desk was a young man with a swallow tattooed on the inside of his wrist, in the soft blue of a drawing done by someone who loved birds. He said Dr. Stone had not had visitors in two years, and was I family?

"A family friend," I said.

He believed me. Adults believe boys who are polite.

He took me down a corridor with handrails on both sides. Through the open doors I could hear a dozen small private sounds: a television, a cough, the slow wheeze of someone sleeping in a chair. At the end was a day room with big windows and a view of a lawn. A row of old people sat in armchairs facing the glass, like an audience at a very quiet play.

* * *

Stone was in the third chair from the left.

He was smaller than I remembered, and his left hand lay in his lap like something he had been asked to hold. His hair had gone white, and someone had cut it too short. He wore a cardigan with the elbows worn through, and a pair of soft gray trousers, and his feet, in slippers, did not quite reach the floor.

He looked at me with an attention that I could not read. It was not recognition of a face. It was recognition of a sound. His mouth made a shape that was nearly a word and was not.

I pulled up a plastic chair and sat down beside him. I told him who I was. I told him the date. I said, in a normal voice, as one speaks to a person who may or may not be listening, that I had read everything, and that I had not told anyone, and that I was keeping my part of the bargain.

At the word *bargain* his good eye moved, a little, toward the locker beside the bed.

I looked at it. I looked back at him.

"Is it in there?" I said.

He did not answer. But the eye went again, a small bright flick, like a bird's, to the locker and back. It was the most deliberate thing he had done, the aide told me later, in three years.

* * *

The notebook was in the bottom drawer, under a folded cardigan.

It had a black cover with soft corners. The spine had been mended with tape. The aide, who had followed me in with a tray of tea, said the doctor liked to hold it: that he would not be parted from it, that they let him keep it by the bed, and that he sometimes sat for an hour with his hand flat on the cover, the way you rest a hand on a sleeping dog.

I opened it standing up.

The first line was in small, upright, impeccable handwriting, in black ink.

*The window was four minutes and eleven seconds long.*

I sat down. I read for two hours in a plastic chair, with the tea going cold on the tray and the light moving across the floor. I read the whole thing. I read the pages about the four minutes, and the picnic, and a man in a gray sweater who had said *I've read it. All of it.* I read the part where I was eleven, and a stranger, and frightening. I did not recognize myself and I recognized every word. It is a strange thing, to be described by someone who did not like you and did not stop looking.

Near the end of the notebook was a page that began with a sentence I had never expected to read.

*The reason I did this was not that I thought I was good. It was that I was afraid of being ordinary.*

I stopped. I read it again. I put my hand flat on the page, as he did. And I became aware, slowly, that he was watching me.

He had turned his head. He was looking at me with his one good eye, with a steady, clear, and not unkind attention. His mouth was working.

I leaned in. I put my ear close to his lips, as I had learned to do at a hundred bedsides, and I heard it. It was not a word. It was breath given a shape, a rounded open shape, the one a mouth makes before it says *oh.*

"Yes," I said. "That's the one."

I do not know why I said it. I am told by people who know about these things that a man in his state may respond to the *tone* of a sentence, and that I should not read too much into it. I am sure they are right. I read a great deal into it anyway.

* * *

I did not take the notebook. I want to be clear. It was his, and it was not mine to carry off in a bag. I took a hundred and twelve photographs of it with my telephone, standing in the corridor, page by page, and I put it back in the drawer under the cardigan, and I closed the drawer, and I said goodbye to him.

He raised his good hand. It did not wave. It lifted a few inches off the blanket and let itself drop again, which I took to be the same thing.

I took the bus home. It was nearly dark. I sat by the window with the telephone on my knee and watched the streetlamps come on, one by one, in a long yellow row, and at each of them I heard a faint electrical hum, and I thought: *I am the only living person who can read it all.*

It was not a pleasant thought. It was heavy and precise, like a key.

* * *

That night, at dinner, my mother asked if I wanted more potatoes.

My father was telling a story about a customer whose truck had run out of gas twice on the same bridge. He told it well. He had a way of building to the punchline by slowing down, as if he were backing a truck into a bay. My mother laughed at the places she was meant to. The lamp threw a warm circle on the table, and the kitchen smelled of onions.

I had a sentence ready. I had had it ready for weeks. It was four words long.

I opened my mouth.

"Yes, please, Mom," I said.

She heaped my plate. My father reached the punchline. We all laughed.

I tried again the following week, over a pot roast. I tried the week after, in the truck, on the way to a hardware shop, with my father's hands steady on the wheel and the radio low. I tried in the middle of a thunderstorm, in a doorway, when my mother had stopped to find her keys. Each time I had the four words. Each time something else came out: a question about the weather, a remark about the soup, a joke.

It was not cowardice, or not only. I have thought about it a great deal. It was that they were happy. I had never in my life seen two people so entirely at home in the world as my parents in their kitchen at seven o'clock, and I understood that what I carried would not be given to them as a gift. It would be a sentence passed. And I could not make myself be the judge.

I would try, I told myself, again next week.

I tried, in the end, for twenty years.


## 9. Odd Moments

An unusual child in an ordinary family is mostly a matter of small adjustments, and I made hundreds.

I learned to look at people when they spoke, not at the sound. I learned to wait a full second before answering, because the answer arrived a second earlier than the question. I learned that a person who says *I'm fine* with a particular dryness in the voice is not fine, and that the best response is to hand them something warm and ask nothing. I learned to walk into a room as if I did not already know where everyone was standing.

None of it was hard. That is the thing people never believe. They imagine a child like me as a prisoner, forever on guard, and they weep a little in advance. But no one is on guard for years. One makes a few rules, and the rules become habits, and the habits turn into a face. By the time I was fourteen, I did not notice I was doing it, any more than a man notices he is walking on the left of the sidewalk.

What I noticed instead were the odd moments, when the face slipped, and what happened next.

* * *

The first was the piano.

It was in the front room of the apartment, an upright that my father had been given by a customer in lieu of a payment, and that no one in the family could play. It stood against the wall with its lid shut and a bakery calendar on top. It was flat. Not badly, only by a hair, in the middle octave, where the felts had gone. For years I had been unable to hear a hymn on the radio without my teeth wanting to stand on end.

One evening in the autumn of 2012, when I was twelve, a neighbor's daughter came to tea. She was a small, serious girl, a year younger, with a braid and a violin case. She sat at the piano to be polite, and played a few notes, and then a few more, and I said, before I could stop myself: "It's flat."

"What?"

"The A. And the one below. It's about a fifth of a semitone."

She stared. My mother, in the doorway with the teapot, stared too.

"How do you know that?" said the girl.

I had two sentences ready. I had practiced them. *I read it somewhere.* *My uncle tunes pianos.* I opened my mouth, and the girl looked at me with such open, uncomplicated interest that I forgot them both.

"I just hear it," I said.

My mother set down the teapot. "Danny's got good ears," she said, quite steadily. "He always has."

The girl accepted this at once. Children do. She said that her teacher had told her the same about her, and that she could tell if a string was off by the sound of the pluck. She and my mother then had a long and animated conversation about teachers, while I stood by the shelf and looked at my shoes.

That night my mother came into my room. She sat on the end of the bed, as she did when she had something to say, and smoothed the quilt with her hand.

"You don't have to be careful with me, you know," she said.

"I'm not."

"You are. You do this thing with your face." She did it, a small neat smile with the eyes held still. It was an accurate imitation. "Like you're holding a tray."

I said nothing.

"I don't need to know everything. I don't want to be a nuisance." She patted the quilt. "But I want you to know that I'd rather you were yourself in the front room than careful in the kitchen." She stood up. "That's all. Goodnight, love."

I lay awake for a long time. It was, I think, the most intelligent thing anyone said to me that year.

* * *

The school wanted to skip me three grades.

My parents went to the meeting in their good clothes. They sat in two small plastic chairs, on the other side of a large desk, and the principal and the guidance counselor and a woman from the district office explained, in the careful, hopeful voice of professionals who have said it before, that I was *performing at a level well beyond my cohort.* They said *accelerated pathway.* They said *a unique opportunity.* They had a folder.

My father, who had left school at sixteen, listened to all of it with his hands on his knees. When they finished, he cleared his throat.

"He's twelve," he said.

"Yes, but—"

"I know he's quick. I'm not saying he isn't." He looked at the folder, then at the principal. "But he's a normal kid who happens to be quick. We'd rather he had friends."

The principal said that was very reasonable.

"He's got one," my father said. "A boy named Teo. They build things in the shed. I'd rather he kept that than a gold star." He glanced at my mother, who nodded. "If he wants to go up a grade, he can go up one. If he wants to read what he likes in the library, he can do that. But he's going to stay with his own age."

It was, as it turned out, the wisest thing anyone said about me in thirty years.

Teo was a stocky, cheerful boy with a gap in his teeth and a talent for taking things apart. He lived two floors up. I had known him since we were seven, and he had never once asked me a question I could not answer. We spent the summers in his father's shed, with a soldering iron and a stack of broken radios, making things that did not work. Teo was patient with my hands. I was patient with his arithmetic. We understood each other well enough to be silent for an hour at a stretch.

He noticed everything, of course. He is the sort of person who does. But he noticed it as a friend, which is to say, by not saying so. When I stopped in the middle of a sentence because a door had opened in the next building, he simply waited. When I called out *someone's coming* a minute before the doorbell, he got up and let them in. When I once, in a bad moment, told him I could hear his father's heart through the wall and that it sounded tired, he put down the wire and said: "Yeah. He's been working nights."

It was the whole of our conversation on the subject. I have never forgotten it.

* * *

I got it wrong exactly once, and I was thirteen. The history teacher was a mild, round, balding man called Mr. Hale, who wore the same brown cardigan every day and said *right* at the start of every sentence, as if he were bracing the ground. On a Friday in March he stood at the front of the class and announced that he had lost our essays.

"Right," said Mr. Hale. "I'm afraid the essays have gone missing. I've looked everywhere. I'm so sorry."

His pulse went through the roof.

I want to be fair to myself. It was a hundred and forty. It was a pulse like a drum solo, and it went with a small dry swallow and a quarter-second hesitation before *missing.* Every instinct I had said *liar.* I had been listening to my father tell customers the truck was *ten minutes out* for thirteen years. I knew a lie when I heard one.

"You didn't lose them," I said.

The room went quiet.

"You're lying." I should have stopped. I did not stop. I was thirteen and right. "Your heart's doing about a hundred and forty. You've got them. You've hidden them. Why would you hide them?"

I will never forget his face. It did not go angry. It went *white,* and then, very slowly, a kind of gray, as though a light were being turned down behind it. Thirty children looked at him. He opened his mouth and nothing came out. Then he said, in a small voice, "Daniel, would you step outside, please?"

In the hall he said nothing for a minute. He was holding his glasses. He said, at last, with great gentleness, "I was going to tell you on Monday. I was going to tell the whole class."

"Tell us what?"

"I sent your essay to the state competition." He cleaned the glasses on his cardigan and did not look at me. "I sent in six. I wanted it to be a surprise. I told the principal I'd lose them, to see if anyone would find out. It was a silly joke." He gave a short, embarrassed laugh. "I was so nervous. I've never done anything like that in twenty-two years of teaching. I could hardly stand."

I had heard a man's heart racing, and I had taken it for guilt. It had been joy, or the nearest thing to it that a shy man can bear: the pounding of someone who has done a kind thing in secret and cannot hold it in.

Mr. Hale was entirely kind about it. He said it was a very understandable mistake. He said he'd have done the same. I went back into the room and apologized to the class, and thirty children stared at me, and a girl in the back row mouthed the word *freak,* and Teo, beside me, leaned over and said in a low voice, "In my experience, when people lie, they don't blush that well."

I won the state competition. I did not enjoy it.

I learned something, and I have had to relearn it every year. A pulse tells you *that.* It does not tell you *why.* A heart that is racing is a heart that is racing. All the rest is something you are adding yourself.

* * *

At fourteen, the school nurse tested my hearing.

It was routine. She was a plump and kindly woman with a clipboard, and she took me into a small room with a booth and a pair of enormous headphones and told me to raise my hand each time I heard a tone. I sat in the booth. I looked at the wall. The tones came, high and low, soft and softer, a long climbing run of them, from a machine whose wiring I could hear humming behind the plaster.

I heard all of them. I heard tones that the machine was not producing, harmonics of its own heat. I heard the nurse breathing on the other side of the glass. And somewhere around the fourth frequency, I made a decision.

I let two go by.

I raised my hand late, at the next, and missed one in the middle, and then I raised it at the right moment for the rest. I did it so lightly that I wasn't sure myself. When I came out, the nurse looked at the chart for a moment, then at me, and said: "Perfectly healthy. A little dip around four thousand, but nothing to worry about."

"Thank you."

"You'd be surprised how many boys your age have it. All those earbuds." She smiled and tore off the sheet. "Keep the music down."

I said that I would.

I walked home that afternoon with a paper folded in my pocket, and a feeling that I could not at first identify. It was not guilt. It was something more like loss. I had, I realized, deliberately made myself a little less than I was, for the first time, in order to be easier to describe. I had given the world a smaller number. And the world had thanked me for it.

I wrote it down, that night, in a notebook. I did not know then that I would one day have another notebook with a black cover, and that it would begin the same way.

* * *

My mother got her diploma in the spring of 2014, at the age of forty-two, at a kitchen table, with a pencil behind her ear.

It had been her idea. She had left school at sixteen to help in her aunt's bakery, and had spent twenty-six years working the line in the school cafeteria, serving macaroni to children who were, by the end of each year, better educated than she was. She never said it bothered her. One evening in October she put down her fork and said, to the table in general, "I'd like to do the equivalency."

My father looked at her. "The what?"

"The high school one. I've looked it up. It's seven weeks of classes at the community center, and then there's a test." She picked up the fork again. "I'm not saying I'll pass. I'm saying I'd like to try."

I was fourteen. I said I'd help.

Reader, she was *magnificent.* She was also, for the first three weeks, a person in a state of low-grade panic, because the algebra book began with the sentence *Let x be an unknown quantity,* and she had never in her life let anything be unknown. I sat across from her with a pencil and a plate of cookies and realized that I had no idea how to teach anything to someone I loved.

On the fourth Tuesday I took away the book.

"Mom," I said. "How do you make a cake for forty if the recipe's for twelve?"

She looked at me as though I had asked her to explain the color blue. "You multiply by three and a third. You don't, you do it by eye. You do the eggs in a bunch and then you fudge the baking powder."

"What's the baking powder?"

"A teaspoon for twelve. So for forty, three and a half." She narrowed her eyes. "Oh. *Oh.*"

"That's algebra."

"That's *ratios.*" She put down her cookie with dignity. "Why didn't you say?"

She did the rest as recipes. She wrote *x* in the margins as *the egg, or whatever it is I haven't got yet,* and she solved for it as she would have solved for a missing ingredient: by tasting, adjusting, and squinting at the oven. She got a ninety-one on the math. She got a ninety-four on the reading, and wrote a one-page essay on a poem about a red wheelbarrow that made the examiner, a retired teacher, call the community center to say she had never seen anyone *look* at a poem like that.

My father drove her to the ceremony in the truck. He wore his good jacket. He stood at the back of a fluorescent-lit hall with sixty-one strangers and their families and cried so hard that a woman offered him a pack of tissues, and he said, "No thanks, I've got my own," and took out a handkerchief the size of a flag.

The certificate hangs in the kitchen, next to the bakery calendar. It has a gold seal. My mother dusts it on Fridays.

She never once mentioned that I had helped. That was the thing I loved most. She took what I had given her, as she took everything, with both hands and no commentary, and made it hers. It was a lesson in teaching that I did not understand for twenty years.

* * *

The neighbor in 4C died the winter I turned fifteen.

Mrs. Aldana had been a widow for twenty years, a small brisk woman in an oatmeal cardigan who brought us soup whenever one of us was ill, and sometimes when none of us was, in a covered dish, with the lid on crooked. She had known me all my life. She had never asked me anything about it. She had, I think, decided years before that there were things a neighbor did not need to hear, and that the wall between our apartments was thin for a reason.

She died on a Tuesday in January, quietly, in her chair. I heard it, from my room. I heard her breath change, and the long pause that followed, and then the clock on her mantel going on.

I did not call for help. I want to put that down plainly. I sat on my bed for ninety seconds with my hand over my mouth, counting. It was already over, and I knew it. It was not the dying that held me, but the clock, which went on, with its small dry tick, as though nothing had happened.

Then I got up and knocked on her door, and when no one answered, I went and told my mother.

At the funeral I carried a casserole. I stood at the back of a small chapel with sixteen other people and listened to the minister say the usual things. I heard a man in the third row say, to the man beside him, in an undertone, that she'd left the apartment to a niece in another state. I heard an argument begin, softly, over a bequest. I heard who was lying about how much they had loved her, and who was not, and I knew, as I knew my own name, that the only one in that room who was truly grieving was the oldest woman there, in a plain dark hat, who did not weep at all.

I said nothing to anyone. I have carried that for twenty years. It is not a heavy thing. It is a small, clear weight, like a coin in a pocket.

* * *

My father broke a rib on a loading dock the following spring.

It was a stupid accident. A pallet shifted, a strap gave way, and he went down on his side with sixty pounds of flour across his chest. He drove himself to the hospital, which was typical, and my mother met him there, and I took the bus.

I sat with him in the emergency room, in one of those curtained cubicles, on a plastic chair that creaked. He was propped up on a gurney with a thin blanket and an oxygen line, looking at the ceiling. The doctors had said it was only a rib, and he was holding himself with the stillness of a man trying not to laugh. My mother had gone for coffee.

After a while he said, still looking at the ceiling: "You can tell me anything, you know. I'm not as dumb as I look."

"You're not dumb at all, Dad."

"No. But I'm slow." He turned his head. His face was gray with the pain and the effort of being cheerful. "You've never made me feel it. In all these years. I want to say thank you for that."

I said, "It's nothing."

It was not nothing. It was nearly everything, and I could not say so, and I have learned since that this is the cost of what I am: that I can hear the whole sentence in my father's voice, the years in it, the patient shame, the fierce unspoken pride, and cannot say its answer without telling him what I am.

He closed his eyes. "Good kid," he said, to himself. He was asleep within the minute.

I sat there with my hand beside his on the rail and listened to the breath go in and out of him, steady and slow, and I thought of the sentence I had been trying to say for months, and the four words in it. They were still there. They did not move.

I decided that I would be ordinary for as long as I could.

I have kept that decision. I am not sure whether it was a vow or a mistake. I know only that it was made out of love, and that love does not always make the wisest decisions, but it makes the ones that last.


## 10. The Search

A list is not a conspiracy, though it can look like one.

By the time I was eighteen I had Stone's notebook in my head, four copies of an index he did not know I had in four places he would not have guessed, and a spreadsheet. I kept the spreadsheet on a small drive inside a hollow rock on my shelf. The rock had been a present when I was nine, from my father, who had found it in a creek and thought it looked like a loaf of bread. It was an excellent hiding place. Nobody in the history of my family had ever picked it up.

There were fifty-eight names. Some were whole, with an address and a school. Some were partial, a first name and a county. A few were nothing but a date of birth in the spring of one particular year, and a note in Stone's hand: *family relocated.* I had to build most of it from the outside, because the notebook was an account, not a registry. Stone had told me *why* he did what he did, and what it felt like, and what the technician's laugh sounded like at eleven minutes past midnight. He had not told me where anyone lived.

So I did what he had done. I watched from far away.

* * *

I used the public things. A school district publishes its enrollment tables, and a county its birth notices, and a newspaper its columns about the Fourth of July picnics. A church prints its baptisms. A small-town paper will tell you, if you ask it nicely, that Mrs. So-and-so's grandson has taken first prize in the regional spelling contest and is thought to be *something of a prodigy.* I became a connoisseur of that word.

I used the clinic. There was a program, I learned, a small one, which had offered help to families of modest means over the course of eleven years. It had a name, a logo with a green leaf, and a yearly newsletter. The back issues were in a university library. I read them in a carrel, in a building where nobody knew me, and made notes on the backs of grocery receipts. I read fifty-eight names in the photograph captions, with last names, under a banner that said **OUR FAMILY GROWS.**

And I used the ear.

I have said that people with the package reveal themselves in a hundred small ways, if one knows how to listen. A pause before a loud noise. A glance at a door before it opens. A way of leaving a room, a half-second before an argument begins in the next one. Children do it more than adults, because adults have learned to cover it. I sat in a good many parks. I stood at the edge of a good many playgrounds, a tall kid of eighteen with a notebook and an apologetic face, listening. I was twice asked by a parent whether I was lost. I said that I was waiting for a friend.

I made one rule. I made it on the bus, the first day, and wrote it on the inside cover of the notebook in block letters, so I could not fool myself.

**NO ONE IS APPROACHED BY SURPRISE.**

If I could not find a gentle way to say it, I would not say it. If the person was too young, I would wait. If they were happy, I would let them be. I had been told, once, by a man in a chair, that he had never asked the question because a man who never asks cannot be asked. I would not become the man who asks.

* * *

The first family I found was the Duvals.

I knew them from Stone's notes. The baker and the nurse who worked nights. The son who had been moved up two grades and cried every morning for a month, whose father had sat with him at the kitchen table, all one winter, reading the problems aloud in a flat patient voice, as though the numbers were a recipe.

They lived in a mid-sized town on a river, in a brick house with a red door. The bakery was on the main street, under an awning with a cartoon wheat sheaf. It was called *Duval & Son.* I stood across the road on a Tuesday morning in March of 2018 and listened to it.

Through the glass I could see a man behind the counter, in a white apron, with a face I had never seen and knew at once. He was forty-nine, perhaps, and broad, with flour on his forearms. He was putting a loaf into a paper bag for a customer. I heard the tissue rustle. I heard his voice, low and slow, saying *and a good morning to you.*

Behind him, in the open door of the kitchen, a young man stood with his back to the street. He was eighteen, like me. He had a tray in his hands. He stopped.

He turned his head, a few degrees, toward the road.

He could not have seen me. I was across a wide street, behind a bus shelter, under a tree. But he stood quite still, with the tray balanced, and his head tilted. I knew what he was doing. He was listening to a car three streets away that had begun, very faintly, to rattle.

His father said, over his shoulder, in the same low voice: "Marc. The oven."

Marc put the tray down.

I watched him for forty minutes. I watched him carry flour and slice bread and make change. I watched him turn, twice more, to a sound no one else had noticed. I watched his father look at him, each time, with a small, attentive, unfussed expression, and then look away. It was the look of a father who had decided, once, at a kitchen table, years ago, that there were things he did not need to be told.

I did not go in. I did not buy a loaf. I wrote *M.D.* on the sheet, and a small star beside it, and put the sheet away.

On the bus home I thought: *he's all right.* I had expected to feel satisfaction. I felt a strange, sharp envy. He had a father who had read to him. He had never had to be told anything.

* * *

By the winter I had found nineteen, and approached none.

I should say what that cost. It was not a matter of loneliness, though I was lonely. It was a matter of knowledge. A person who has found nineteen strangers whom he knows more about than they know about themselves is not an observer. He is something else. I felt it first as a kind of weight, then as a kind of vertigo, and in the end as a low steady shame, like the sound of a refrigerator in a quiet house. I knew that a girl of fifteen in a town in the north was going to be offered a place at a music school, and that she would have to choose between it and a different life, and that her choosing would turn on whether anyone told her what she was. I knew that a boy of twelve had stopped talking at school. I knew his mother's name. I was not permitted, by my own rule, to call her up and say *he can hear the fluorescent lights.*

I went to see her anyway. I did not speak. I sat across the street in a car I had borrowed from a friend, and listened to her through an open window, and heard her say, very gently, to her son, *it's all right, sweetheart, you don't have to say anything.* He did not. But he leaned his head, a little, against her arm.

I wrote his initials on the sheet, and a question mark.

There were four question marks by the end of the second year. They were the ones that frightened me. A boy who had stopped talking. A girl, eighteen, who had been admitted to a hospital with a diagnosis that did not fit, and who told a nurse, quite calmly, that she could hear the building. A young man who had left home at seventeen and not been seen since, though his mother wrote to him every week, and his sister wrote, and nobody knew where to send the letters. And a woman of eighteen, a first-year university student with a blameless record, who had walked out of an exam and gone to live in a field.

I did not put stars beside them. I put the marks, and drew a box around each.

I told myself that I would find a way. I told myself that it was a matter of time, and care, and the right words. I have found, since, that this is what the young tell themselves about everything.

* * *

The last one I found by accident.

It was a Saturday in October of 2018, in a park in a town I had gone to for a different reason. I had been looking for a family named Okafor, the ones with a late third child, a girl who read at three and reorganized the household accounts at seven. I had found them. She was fourteen now, in a school uniform, and she walked down the steps of a library with a stack of books, humming a tune I did not recognize. She was fine. She was more than fine. I put a star beside her name.

I walked back through the park with my notebook under my arm. It was a dull, mild afternoon, and the leaves were turning. There was a playground at the far end, with a slide and a merry-go-round, and a small fenced sandpit. I had passed a hundred like it.

A child was sitting in the sandpit, alone.

She was about five. She wore a yellow raincoat, which was too big, with the sleeves rolled twice at the wrist. She was not digging. She was not playing. She was sitting with her legs crossed, in the middle of the sand, quite upright, with her head tilted slightly to the left, and her eyes closed.

On a bench by the fence her mother sat reading a paperback. She had the loose, relaxed posture of a woman who has been told that her child is *dreamy.*

I stood at the gate, and I listened.

I do not know how to explain what I heard. It was not a sound. It was the absence of one. The child in the sandpit was listening to the park, to its whole width and depth: the traffic, and the crows, and a distant lawnmower, and a man calling a dog, and the rustle of the wind in the plane trees. She was listening with a patient, absorbed, unhurried attention, as one listens to music. And I understood, as I stood there with my hand on the cold wire, that she had never been told to stop.

I checked the sheet. I looked at every name. She was not on it.

I looked at the date on the notebook in my bag. I did the arithmetic on my fingers, as a child does. A five-year-old in the autumn of 2018 had been born in the early months of 2013. She had been conceived, if she had been conceived through the clinic, in the spring of 2012.

I thought of a Thursday in November of 2011, and a man sitting at his bench with three embryos in an incubator, and a couple who had waited seven years.

I thought: *the last batch.*

Her mother turned a page. The child opened her eyes, and looked straight at the gate.

I did not move. She regarded me for a long moment with perfect, grave attention, and then, quite slowly, she lifted one small hand in the yellow sleeve, and waved.

I waved back.

Then I went away, as quickly as I could without looking as if I were running, and I sat on a wall at the edge of the park for an hour with my head in my hands. I had broken my rule. I had not approached her. But she had approached me.

I wrote *last batch* on the sheet. I put a star beside it, and a second, and I did not know what they meant.


## 11. The Phonetician

I found her in a basement, which is where linguists are kept.

It was the autumn of 2019. I was nineteen, in my second year of a degree that combined biology and chemistry in roughly equal parts of bewilderment, and I had a list. Beside one name, in my own small handwriting, was a note: *Phonetics? Sound lab? Hears first.* I had spent eight months working out what that meant. It had meant a good many hours in libraries, reading the acknowledgments sections of papers on speech perception, until one name appeared three times in a row. A student, a second-year, in a laboratory at a state university four hours from my parents' flat.

I took the bus. I told my mother I was visiting a friend.

The laboratory was in the sub-basement of a building that smelled of paint and dust. It was three rooms and a sound booth. In the corridor outside there was a corkboard with a notice about a bake sale and another about a lecture on the acoustics of whispered Danish. The door to the booth was ajar. I stopped well short of it. I stood against the cinderblock and held my breath.

A young woman was in the booth, with her eyes shut, speaking one word, over and over, while a machine drew a picture of it.

The word was *better.*

She said it with a flat *t,* a tapped *t,* a glottal one, a ghost of one. She said it as though it were a rare coin, turning it to see the other side. I stood in the corridor for what must have been five minutes. I had never in my life heard anyone listen to her own voice with such precision. It was like watching someone read a map by touch.

About the fortieth repetition she opened her eyes, and looked directly at the door.

* * *

She came out. She was nineteen, small, with a pencil in her hair and a lanyard with a name on it that I have promised not to repeat. I will call her Mira.

"You stopped breathing when I did the glottal one," she said.

"I'm sorry."

"No." She looked at me for a long moment, with her head tilted. "Nobody ever hears that. I've been listening to people hear nothing for nineteen years." She held out her hand. It was cool and slightly inky. "I'm Mira."

I said my name. I said it as I had said it to no one else, with the surname, in full. She repeated it softly, as a person tries on a coat.

"You're not a student here."

"No."

"You came to find me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I had a speech. I had written it on the bus. It was good, and it was careful, and it began with the sentence *I think you may have an unusual sense of hearing.* I looked at her, at the pencil and the lanyard and the gray, appraising eyes, and I put the speech away.

"Because you can hear that the refrigerator in the next room is flat," I said.

She did not answer for a moment. Then, very slowly, she smiled.

"It's a B flat," she said. "Nobody believes me. It's slightly under."

"A quarter tone."

"A *bit* less than a quarter tone." She tucked the pencil more firmly into her hair. "Come on. The building closes at nine. I think we should be somewhere that no one is listening."

* * *

We talked for six hours, in a stairwell, because the building closed and the security guard, a kind and weary man, let us stay on the condition that we did not touch anything.

It was concrete and cold and smelled of bleach. We sat on the third step with our backs against the wall. Above us a light buzzed. Below us a pipe knocked, once every forty seconds, and Mira said, "Seventeen minutes past the hour it'll do two," and it did.

We tested each other, without discussing it. It was done in small ways. She dropped a pencil on the landing, and I said where it had landed before it stopped rolling. I tapped the rail with my ring, three times, and she told me, with her eyes closed, how far away the end of the corridor was. We played a game with sounds. She made a small click with her tongue, and I turned my head to the exact spot on the far wall where it had returned. She laughed aloud.

"You can *see* it," she said. "I mean the room. You can see the room."

"It isn't seeing."

"No." She went still. "It's something better."

We stopped talking. We sat side by side on a stair, in the cold, with the pipe knocking, and for the first time in my life I was not translating. I do not know how to explain what that is. A person who has had to put every thought through a filter for nineteen years does not notice the filter, as the sea does not notice the water. When it is removed, one feels the lack of it like a missing tooth. I said a sentence, and she understood it. She said one, and I did. We said another. We went on like that for an hour.

At eleven, I told her.

I told her all of it. I told her slowly, in short sentences, without any of the words that make a thing smaller. I told her about the clinic and the four minutes, and the man with the paper cup, and the notebook with the black cover. I told her what was in her, and what was in me, and how we had the same inserts in different places. I told her what Stone had planned for a century. I said it as flatly as I could, as one reads a diagnosis aloud.

She did not interrupt. Once, when I said *one hundred years,* her hand closed on the edge of the step.

When I had finished she was silent for so long that I could hear the pipe knock twice.

"Was he kind?" she said.

It was not the question I had expected.

"I don't know," I said. "I think he thought he was."

"That's not what I asked." She looked at the floor between her feet. "I mean, when he did it to us. Was there any kindness in the doing? Did he *look* at us?"

I thought about the notebook. I thought about the first page, and the technician's laugh.

"He looked," I said. "I think he looked a great deal. I don't think he ever saw."

She nodded slowly. She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, and I realized she was not crying but laughing, in a silent and horrified way, with her shoulders shaking.

"It's very odd," she said, "to be told you've been designed. I think I feel *relieved.* Isn't that dreadful? I've spent my whole life thinking I was a mistake. It never occurred to me I was a plan."

I said that I did not think she was either.

"No," said Mira. "I think I'm only a person." She wiped her face. "But I would like to meet the plan's other pieces." She turned and looked at me, direct and clear. "I've got one question."

"Ask."

"Who else?"

* * *

The following month she introduced me to Jonas.

We met in a university cafeteria at two in the afternoon, at a table by a window. He was tall and tired, with a long, intelligent face and a habit of tapping a pen against his knee in a rhythm that I counted without thinking. It was a bar of seven. It never varied. He stood up when I approached, which none of the other students did, and shook my hand as though I might be a surveyor.

He was an engineering student, in his third year, and had been Mira's friend since a first-year class on waves. They had a way of finishing each other's sentences that was not intimate so much as practical, like two people who share a toolbox. He had a dry, sparing way of speaking, and when he did, it was usually to correct someone, kindly.

He was not like us. I knew that within a minute. He had the other package, the quieter one: the speed of thought, the extended window, the long steady attention. He did not hear a wall. He solved a problem the way a stream solves a hill, patiently and from the bottom, and he had the faint, wary look of the cleverest person in any room, who has learned to apologize for it.

We told him over coffee. Mira did most of the telling, in her clear, steady voice, with her hands flat on the table. I added what she left out. Jonas listened to all of it without a word, the pen quiet in his hand. When she finished he looked at the window for a long time. A pigeon walked along the ledge.

"Does this mean," he said at last, "that she's right about the refrigerator?"

Mira said, "I *told* you it was humming in B flat."

"You did." He turned back to us. "I thought it was a figure of speech."

Then he laughed. It was a short, startled sound, like a man dropping a plate. He put his hand over his eyes and went on laughing until his shoulders shook, and then, quite suddenly, he stopped, and his face changed. He lowered his hand.

"It would explain a great deal," he said, quietly. "I've always thought I was a bit... *ahead,* somehow. Not clever. Ahead. Like a man who is always standing where the rest of the line is going to be." He looked at the table. "I thought it was loneliness."

Mira took his hand. It was an automatic gesture and she did not seem to notice it. I looked politely at the window.

* * *

They decided that evening, with no argument I could detect, that they would not be tested, would not be told more than they needed, and would stay exactly as they were.

I had expected a debate. I had a list of reasons, in a pocket, on a card. What I got instead was a short silence and a nod from each. It was Jonas who spoke first.

"I think we'd rather not know the details," he said. "I'd like to know what we are. I don't think I'd like to know *how.*"

"There's a notebook," I said. "I have it."

"Keep it." He smiled. "You're the one who can read it."

"And the others?" said Mira. "The list."

"I'll tell you who I've met. I won't give names."

"Good." She set down her cup. "I don't think anyone should be approached by surprise. I think we should *wait,* and let each of them come to it."

I said it was the rule I had made myself.

"It's a good rule," said Mira. "Don't let it become a habit."

I walked out into the evening, on a campus I did not know, past a fountain turned off for the season. I listened to the dusk coming on: the traffic on a distant road, the chime of a bell, the voices of students crossing a lawn. And under it all, a faint and steady sound, as close and as quiet as my own pulse.

I thought: *the first of the quiet ones.*

I was right. There were thirty more. But Mira and Jonas were the first, and they were the ones who stayed.


## 12. Under Cover

The laureate-to-be was a shy man in a gray cardigan who apologized to chairs.

I met him in the spring of 2022, in a hallway outside a lecture room, after a talk on something I did not understand. He was twenty-two, a graduate student with a stoop and a battered folder, and he was standing against the wall with the look of a man waiting for a draft to pass. I had spent three weeks finding him. His name was in the third column of a list in a drawer, with a pencil mark beside it that meant *probably.* I had come to the talk because he was giving a small, nervous question at the end of it, and I wanted to hear how he said it.

He said it beautifully. It was a question about a missing term in an equation. It was eleven words long, and the speaker, a distinguished old man with a bow tie, paused and took off his glasses.

When the room emptied I said, "Elias."

He stopped. He had been about to leave, and he stopped as a man does when a name he has not heard in a while is spoken in a voice that expects him to know it. He turned. I said the rest. I said it as I had said it to Mira, slowly, in short sentences, and without any of the words that people use to make a thing sound smaller than it is.

He heard it with his whole body, the way people hear a diagnosis. He did not move. I listened to his pulse climb, and level, and climb again. At the end he asked, in a faint voice, if he could sit down.

We sat on a bench in the hall. A janitor went by with a cart and gave us a glance of professional neutrality.

"I'd rather not have known," said Elias, after a while.

"I know."

"I think I'd have been happier." He looked at his hands. They were long and unsteady, with ink on one finger. "I've always felt that I was a bit off. Like a piece from another set. I thought it was because I was dull."

"You're not dull."

"No. Apparently not." He gave a short, painful laugh. "But I would like the pages. The ones about me. If you have them." He looked up. "I want to see what they say I can do."

* * *

Then there was Julian.

I met him at a debate, the same spring, in a hall with bad acoustics and a banner that said *Open Forum.* He was twenty-two as well, and he was the best speaker I had ever heard.

It was nothing he did. He did not use gestures, or raise his voice, or stride about. He stood quite still at the lectern, in a good gray suit, with one hand lightly on the wood, and he spoke in a clear, unhurried voice about the dignity of ordinary work. He told a story about his grandfather's hands. He paused, and the room paused with him. He read the audience as one reads a book he has written: not as a performance, but as a thing that was already his.

When he looked at me across the hall, I felt the particular warmth of being understood. It was a physical sensation, like stepping into a sunlit room. I did what I always do. I listened to his pulse.

There was nothing in it. It was steady, sixty-two beats to the minute, the way a metronome is steady. There was no tremor under the confidence, and no hurry under the calm. The words were warm and the heart was cool, and the two had no quarrel with each other.

I thought: *no. Not you.*

I said nothing. We shook hands afterward, in the crush at the door. He said, "I think we'd get on," and I said, "I think so too," and neither of us was lying.

That is how I came to keep a secret from the person with the most reason to be told. I want to be honest about my motives. I did not suspect him of anything. I had no evidence, and I do not trust a suspicion with no evidence. It was simply that I had heard something I did not recognize, and I had learned, from Stone's notebook, from a thousand hours in a hospital, and from my own life, that when I do not recognize a sound I should leave it alone until I do.

I left it alone for six years. In that time he found me.

* * *

Over the next four years, I found the rest of the quiet ones.

There was a florist in a small city in the north, a stout, cheerful woman of twenty-nine who raised tulips and talked to them. When I told her, she said, "Oh, *that,*" with the air of a person who has been told that her house is on a fault line. She said she'd always known. She said, don't make us visible. She said, if I ever needed a bunch of anything, it was on the house.

There was a surveyor, a quiet, sunburned man of thirty-one who measured land for a county, and who had, since boyhood, been able to tell at a glance whether a fence was a quarter inch out. There was a violinist with a broad, gentle face who played in a regional orchestra, and who was embarrassed to say she had been hearing the conductor's mistakes for years. There was a man who built a boat. He had begun it in his garage at nineteen and, at thirty, was still building it. He said it was not finished and it never would be, and that this, in his view, was what a boat was for.

There were two women who lived on the same street, and who had lived on it all their lives. They had met at five. They had been inseparable at ten, and at fifteen had been sent to different schools, and had spent a miserable year apart before the families, with some embarrassment, gave in. They shared a house now, a narrow brick one, with a lemon tree. I came to the door with a speech. I got as far as "I think you're both—" and one of them, a small, red-haired woman with a pencil behind her ear, said: "We know."

"I was going to say *like me.*"

"Oh." She laughed. "Well, that too."

They listened to all of it. They were quiet for a long while. Then the other one, tall and calm, said that they had talked about children, once, a long time ago, and had decided, without any great drama, that they would be the best aunts anyone had ever had. She said it without bitterness. She said that whatever line there was, it did not end with them so much as bend. And the red-haired one said: "Don't make it sad. It isn't sad. It's a good house."

I said it was.

"Come to dinner," said the red-haired one. "We make a very good pie."

* * *

I graduated in the spring of 2026, in a large hall with a vaulted ceiling and the smell of cut flowers.

I had my degree. I had a long black gown and a hood in the colors of my school, which my mother said was the nicest shade of green she had ever seen. My parents had driven four hours. My father wore his good jacket, and had cleaned his fingernails, and sat for two hours with his hands on his knees, looking at the stage with the stern happiness of someone carrying a piano up a flight of stairs. My mother cried from the first name to the last.

When my own name was called, I walked across the stage, and I heard it all: the shuffle of the audience, the creak of the stage, the man at the microphone clearing his throat before the next name. And over it, in the third row from the back, a woman I knew, saying under her breath, in a voice that nobody else could possibly have heard: *That's my boy.*

I took my diploma. I shook hands with the dean. I walked back to my seat, with my eyes down, and I did not look at her, because if I had I would have lost my composure in front of eight hundred people.

* * *

That afternoon, in a small restaurant near the campus, with a cloth on the table and a small vase with one carnation, I tried again.

It was the twentieth time. I had counted. I had tried at a birthday and at a funeral and once, ridiculously, at a hospital. I had tried at the kitchen table, and in the truck, and over the phone. I had a sentence, which had never changed, and it was four words long.

My father was telling a story about a customer whose delivery had gone to the wrong building. My mother was cutting a piece of fish into very small squares. I put down my fork.

"Mom," I said. "Dad. I've got something I need to tell you."

My mother stopped cutting. My father stopped talking. They both looked at me, with the same attentive, slightly frightened face, and I understood that they had been waiting for this since I was sixteen, and that they had always known there was something, and had never asked.

"Wait," said my mother.

She opened her handbag. She took out a small square box, wrapped in tissue paper, and set it down in front of me. "Before you say anything," she said. "I want you to have this. I was going to wait until the wedding, but I don't think you're going to have one, you're far too busy. So I'm giving it to you now."

I unwrapped it. Inside was a pair of baby shoes, tiny, white, with the leather worn pale at the toes. Tucked into one of them was a hospital bracelet. It was a plastic band, printed in a smudged blue, and it said: **PRUITT, BABY BOY. 1/1/2000. 7 LB 1 OZ.**

"They gave us that in the hospital," said my mother. "I kept it in a drawer. I never knew why." She put her hand over mine. It was warm and dry, and the nails were short, and there was a small scar on the thumb from a kitchen knife in 1994. "I think I kept it for today."

My father cleared his throat. "We're proud of you, son. That's all. Whatever it is, we're proud."

I looked at the shoes.

"What did you want to tell us?" said my mother.

I had the four words. They were there. They had been there for ten years. I could feel their weight in my mouth, and their shape, and the particular place in my chest from which they would come.

"I love you both," I said.

My mother smiled, and wept, and said we knew that. My father blew his nose.

I put the bracelet in my pocket. It is still there.

* * *

The club, if it was a club, began that autumn.

It did not begin with a vow. It began with a promise to answer the telephone. That was the only rule, and it was the only one we ever needed: if one of us called at three in the morning, the other answered. The florist said it first, half laughing, over a long call about nothing, and it was repeated, by the others, one after another, until it was a thing that everyone knew.

We met, that first year, in a rented room above a bakery in a town I will not name. There were fourteen of us. We sat on mismatched chairs, with paper cups, under a ceiling stained brown at one corner, and the florist had brought tulips, which she distributed without ceremony. I remember the smell of yeast. I remember Elias at the back, perched on the edge of a stool like a man ready to leave.

We argued. I want to be clear that it was not a quarrel, though it was nearly one, and that it was the most important conversation I have ever been part of. We argued about what the gifted owe the rest.

"Nothing," said the man who built the boat. He said it mildly, from a corner, with his arms folded. "We owe nothing. We didn't ask. If I owe anything, it's to be left alone."

"We owe *everything,*" said Elias. He had stood up. He was holding his folder to his chest like a shield, and his voice, for once, was not apologetic. "We could change the world. That is not a boast. It's arithmetic. Every year, people die of things that someone like me could fix, and we sit in garages and floral shops and *hide.*"

"I like my floral shop," said the florist.

"I know. I'm sorry. But—"

"And what," said Mira, quietly, "do we give them? Ourselves?" She had come from another city, with Jonas, in a borrowed car, and she sat near the front with her hands in her lap. "You can give a gift and it's a gift. But what we have isn't a thing you can pass across a table. It's a life. I'm not sure it's a kind one."

"Then we trade," said someone.

"For what?"

"For protection."

"From whom?"

It went on for three hours. I said very little. I listened, which was what I was for. I listened to the people I had found, one by one, and I heard under each of their voices a different hunger: for ease, for purpose, for the plain relief of being in a room where no one had to translate. I heard the surveyor say that he would give anything to stop being the one who noticed. I heard the violinist say that she would not give up the one thing that made her music. I heard the two women in the corner, the red-haired and the tall, hold hands and say nothing, which was also an answer.

At the end, Mira stood and said, with a kind of dry finality: "I think we've found the question. I don't think we're going to find the answer. I propose we keep the question and answer the phone."

It was carried without a vote.

I wrote down only one thing that night, in the notebook with the black cover. I wrote the phrase that Elias had used, and underlined it twice, and put a question mark beside it. It was the phrase the whole room had been circling.

*Change the world for the better.*

I did not know yet whether it was a promise or a warning. I know now that it was both.


## 13. Munich

They moved in the spring of 2027, on a Tuesday in April, to an apartment in Munich with a balcony facing a courtyard.

I will say at once that I did not want them to go. I said the opposite. I said that it was a wonderful opportunity, and that Germany was a serious country, and that Mira's German, which she had acquired in nineteen months of evening classes and a good deal of eavesdropping, was already better than mine. All of that was true. But I had found them, eight years earlier, in a basement, and they were the first of us that I had been able to tell the truth to, and I had a childish sense that if they went away I would have to start again.

Mira had been offered a post at a phonetics institute at a university (invented). It was the sort of position that a linguist hears about once, and at the end of a conversation she had no intention of having. A professor in Munich had read a paper of hers on vowel perception and written to say that she could not be allowed to waste it in a basement. The salary was modest. The labs were not. The institute had a soundproofed chamber that Mira described, on the telephone, in the voice of a woman describing a lover.

Jonas had a position at an engineering firm (invented), which designed bridges and tunnels and was run by a family who had been doing it for four generations. He had applied for the job in English, and been interviewed in German, and been taken on, he said, because he was the only candidate who could explain why a certain beam would fail. He did not tell me that he had known the answer at once.

"I had it on the second line of the drawing," he said. "I thought: that can't be right. And then I thought: oh."

"Did you tell them?"

"I said it looked interesting." He smiled his tired, crooked smile. "It seemed rude to be quicker than the chief engineer."

* * *

I helped them carry boxes.

The building was on a street of tall pale houses with green shutters, in a quarter where the trams ran under the trees. The stairs were stone, and every footfall came back to me half a second later from an unexpected direction. I stood on the second landing with a crate of kitchen things and listened. A radio. A tap. Someone practicing scales on an upright piano, badly, with great determination.

"It's like living inside a cello," I said.

"I know." Mira was behind me with a lamp under each arm. "I've never been so happy."

The flat was on the third floor. It had high ceilings and a parquet floor, and the previous tenants had left behind a grandfather clock that neither of them knew what to do with. There was a long hallway, a kitchen with a yellow stove, two bedrooms, a room for work, and the balcony. I went out onto it and stood at the rail. Below me was a courtyard with a chestnut tree and a row of bicycles, and on the far side, at an angle, a pair of tall windows with lace curtains. Somewhere behind the curtains, very faintly, a clock was ticking.

I turned my head. I listened. I counted.

"Cuckoo," I said.

"What?"

"There's a cuckoo clock in that building. Second floor. It runs about forty seconds slow."

Mira stood beside me, with her head on one side, and said, in a low voice: "Oh, I *love* this city."

* * *

The landlady came up at four. She was a small and formidable widow in a cardigan, with hair like spun steel, and she inspected the apartment with the air of a customs officer. She asked Jonas, in a German so rapid and so Bavarian that I caught perhaps one word in five, whether the radiators had been bled. She asked Mira whether she was a professor. She asked me whether I was the brother.

"A friend," I said.

"From the United States?"

"Yes."

"Hm." She looked at me for a long moment, and I had the impression of being measured for something. "You listen like a musician," she said, in careful English. "Are you a musician?"

"A doctor."

"Hm," she said, again, and there was a world in it. She gave Mira a key, and Jonas another, and left. We heard her descend the stairs, one flight, two, and a door shut with a particular care.

"She knows," said Jonas, quietly.

"She does not know," said Mira.

"She *suspects.* She has a very good ear."

"She has a very good *nose.* She's a landlady." Mira put the key on the hook by the door. "It's what they do."

* * *

There was one thing I had not expected, and that was how little the place cared.

I had imagined, in my American way, that a city with a thousand years of history would somehow notice three people with unusual hearing. It did not. Munich went about its business, on its trams and bicycles, with a great deal of paper and a mild air of disapproval, and among its six hundred thousand voices ours were merely three. We bought bread. We got lost. We argued with a woman at an office about a form. On the third day, Mira was told by a clerk with a blue stamp that her name was spelled wrongly, and she replied in perfect German, with a perfect regional accent, that it was spelled as she had been born with it. The clerk stared at her. Then he said, with a flicker of respect: *Aber Sie sind keine Amerikanerin.*

*But you are not an American.*

Mira said she was from a very small place.

* * *

She told me on the balcony.

It was the last evening of my stay. The boxes were in rooms, the furniture in places, the wall in the hallway hung with the three framed prints that Jonas had refused, until then, to explain. Down in the courtyard a woman was calling a child in for supper, in a long sweet descending phrase that I will hear in my sleep. The light was low and gold.

"I'm pregnant," she said.

I put down my glass. "Mira."

"Three weeks." She laid her hand flat on her stomach. She was smiling, but her mouth was uncertain at the corners. "Or four. I haven't been to anyone. I know it's too early to say. I wanted to tell you before I told my mother."

I did not trust my voice. I held out my arms and she stepped into them, and we stood like that for a long time, with the city going on around us, and I listened to the sound of two hearts, hers and, a long way beneath it, a very small third, that I could not hear and did not need to.

When she stepped back she said, steadily: "We've decided no tests."

"I know."

"Jonas wants it. I want it. We've talked it through. If it's a girl who can hear a wall, that's a girl who can hear a wall. If it's a boy who can't, he'll have a mother who can hear for him." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, and laughed. "It's the strangest thing. I thought I'd be more frightened. And I'm not. I'm only so *curious.*"

I said that it sounded reasonable. It sounded like the most reasonable thing I had ever heard.

* * *

That summer, a paper appeared in a physics journal under a name I knew.

It was Elias's. It was eleven pages, and three of them were equations. It had been submitted in March and refereed in a two weeks, by three reviewers who had each written, in the polite and slightly stunned language of their profession, that they could find nothing wrong with it. The world went mad for a week.

I do not mean the whole world. I mean the small, well-lit part of it that reads such things. For seven days there were articles and podcasts and breathless threads. A professor in Zurich called it *the most elegant thing I have seen since my student days.* A man at a conference stood up in the middle of someone else's talk and applauded. The newspapers ran a photograph, taken for a university newsletter, of a shy young man in a gray cardigan, looking at the camera as one looks at a bus that has failed to stop.

By August he had given three interviews. In every one of them he said the same thing, slightly differently: that he had been lucky, that it was the right problem at the right time, and that he hoped people would not make too much of it.

I read the interviews in Munich, at the kitchen table, in the yellow light, with Mira peeling an orange across from me. She read them after I had. When she had finished the third, she looked up and said, in a thoughtful tone:

"He says *lucky* eleven times."

"I counted nine."

"There are two in the headlines." She put the tablet down. "It's the same word each time, and it's said in the same place. He's practicing."

"For what?"

"For when he'll need it."

I looked at the window. A pigeon had settled on the sill. I did not say that I had been thinking the same thing for a week.

* * *

The following month I received a card from Julian.

It came in a cream envelope, forwarded from my old address, with a handwritten note. By then he was twenty-seven, and was in his second term in the state legislature, and had a name that people said without being asked.

*Congratulations to your friend,* it said. *Interesting how some of us are lucky. J.*

I read it twice. I read it four times, standing at the kitchen counter, with the radio on. I put it down. I picked it up. I held the card against the light, as if the paper might yield something the writing did not.

It said nothing, and that was the point. A man who knew only that two young scientists were fortunate would not have written that note. A man who did not know would not have thought to underline the word *some.* The card was not a threat. It was an invitation to understand that he had been paying attention.

I put it in the drawer where I kept the rock, and I did not tell Mira.

I have thought many times since that this was my first real mistake. I was not wrong to protect her. But I should have known that a secret kept from one's friends is a secret that begins to work for someone else.


## 14. The Prize

Julian found out in 2028, and I will say how, since it is the one thing in this account that I cannot blame on anyone else.

He did not hear it from a friend. He did not find a file or pick a lock. He worked it out, the way a good student works out the answer to a problem he has not been shown, from the shape of what was left on the page. And he did it at dinner, in a quiet restaurant in a state capital, over a plate of fish that neither of us touched.

I had gone because he had asked. He was twenty-eight, a state representative, and a rising name. His invitation had come on heavy cream paper, in his own hand: *I'd like to see you. No agenda. J.* I had taken that for what it was, which is to say a warning, and I had gone anyway, because a man who is afraid of a dinner is a man who has already lost it.

The restaurant had white tablecloths and a hush. He had reserved a table at the back, away from the kitchen. I noticed that. Of the six tables with a better view, he had chosen the one where the room's sound pooled, and arrived at the same moment I did, as though the two of us had been arranged by a single hand.

"You look tired," he said, rising. "Sit. I've ordered the fish. They do it very simply."

I sat. I listened to his pulse. It was exactly as it had been in 2022, a steady sixty-two, as clean as a line drawn with a ruler.

* * *

He began with small things. He asked after my parents. He remembered their names, and their jobs, and the street they lived on. He said that my father sounded like a decent man, and that it was a shame there were not more of them. He asked about my work. He asked what I thought of the weather in the city where I lived, and whether I had ever thought of moving, and I said that I had not.

He asked whether I had ever been tested.

I said, "For what?"

"For anything." He turned his glass a quarter turn. "Hearing, say. I only ask because I've been reading about auditory processing, and I thought, there's a man I know whose ears are extraordinary. I've watched you, you know. You always turn your head a moment before the waiter reaches the table. You've never once been surprised by a door."

I said that I had a good sense of timing.

"I'm sure." He smiled. It was a beautiful smile, with exactly the right quantity of self-mockery in it. "I've got a good sense of timing too. It's why I'm in politics."

The fish arrived. He did not look at it.

"I did some reading about the clinic that made us," he said.

There are a few sentences in my life that I have heard with my whole body, in the way one hears a tire go flat at speed. That was one. I did not move. I did not blink. I had spent seventeen years training myself not to react, and I think the training held. But he had said *made,* and *us,* and he had said both of them lightly, as one mentions the name of a school.

"Which clinic?" I said.

"Oh, you know." He waved a hand. "The one with the June picnics. I went to two or three as a child. There was a man who stood by the lemonade, always alone, with a paper cup. I used to think he looked like someone who'd lost a bet." He glanced at me. "Dr. Stone. Isn't that right?"

I said nothing. The silence in the room was a very particular silence, the kind that is composed of forty small noises being politely held back.

"I didn't know what he was, of course. I was nine. But I remember him looking at me. Not the way adults look at children. The way a man looks at a thing he's put in the oven." He laughed, quietly, and the laugh was perfect. "I worked it out slowly. A few years ago. There was a thing about the way I was always ahead of the other boys, in a way I couldn't account for. And the way I never *minded* things. I thought it was character. I was rather pleased with myself." He lifted his glass. "And then I came across a mention in a trade journal of a program for children of couples with a certain condition. It had a very short, very dull description. But the name of the physician was there, and I thought: *I have met that man.*"

"That isn't evidence," I said.

"No. But you're here." He drank. "You came, when I asked. You didn't make an excuse. You never asked why I wanted to see you, which is the first thing anyone asks. And you've been sitting there for twenty minutes, listening to my heart."

I looked at him. For the first time in all the years I had known him, I heard something behind the clock. It was not fear. It was a kind of interest, mild and unhurried, like a man looking at a map he intends to use.

* * *

"I would like to be part of what you are building," he said, at the end of the evening.

"I'm not building anything."

"Of course you are." He put his napkin on the table. "You're building a world where people like us are not afraid. I want the same thing. I'm simply better placed to do it."

I said no.

I said it quietly, and politely, and with a clear head. I said that there was nothing to be part of, that there was no organization, that the people he was thinking of were scattered across the world and would not welcome his attention. I said that I would ask him to let it lie.

He listened to all of it, with an expression of courteous regret. Then he stood and held out his hand.

"I understand," he said. "I'd have thought less of you if you'd said yes."

I took his hand. It was warm and dry. His pulse, against my palm, did not alter by so much as a beat.

I walked out into the street and stood under a lamp for a long time. A car went by. The city hummed, and the hum came back to me from the buildings, a quarter of a second late, in layers. I thought: *he has taken my no and put it in a drawer. It has not changed his mind. It has only told him how to speak to me.*

That was the evening on which I understood that I was no longer the only person in the world with a secret. I had become, rather, a man who has been told what a secret is worth.

* * *

The prize came the following year, in October.

I should explain that Elias did not want it. He had never wanted anything, as far as I could tell, but a clean blackboard and a long afternoon. The breakthrough that had made him famous was eleven pages long, and three of them were equations, and the committee in Stockholm described it, in the citation, as *a simplification of a kind that occurs perhaps once in a generation.* He had come to it, he told me, by getting up one morning with a headache and noticing that a symbol in a standard expression was redundant.

"It was just there," he said. "Anyone could have seen it. I only happened to be looking."

I did not say *anyone could not have.* I did not say that I had watched him work and that the speed of his attention, in a room, was the speed of a different sort of animal. I said that I was happy for him, and I was, and I meant it more than I could say.

He called me at ten past five in the morning on the day. He had been told by a woman with a Swedish accent, on a bad line, and he was sitting on the edge of his bed in his pajamas, with his glasses in his hand.

"They've said I've won," he said. "I think they've made a mistake."

"They haven't."

"I'm twenty-nine."

"Yes."

"It isn't *done.*" His voice cracked on the last word. "People are meant to wait. They're meant to be sixty."

"You were lucky," I said. "I've heard it from several people."

There was a pause. Then he began, very softly, to laugh.

* * *

Stockholm was cold and quiet in December. The ceremony was held in a large concert hall with a blue ceiling and a great many white flowers. I sat in a borrowed suit, three rows from the back, between a retired professor of chemistry and a young woman from a newspaper who took notes in shorthand.

I listened to Elias accept the medal. He walked across the stage like a man crossing a frozen lake. He bowed, rather too deeply, to the king, and was gently put right by a courtier. He stood with his hands at his sides and the light on his face, and the hall, which held perhaps fifteen hundred people, went completely silent.

I could hear every one of them. I could hear the cough that someone suppressed in the balcony and the rustle of a program and a child, three rows in front of me, who said in a whisper: *Is that him?* I could hear the great, collective, slightly foolish tenderness of a room that has agreed to be moved.

Elias said, in his small and exact voice, that he had been lucky. He said it so quietly that the microphones barely caught it. He thanked his teachers. He thanked his mother, who was sitting in the front row in a green dress, and who put her hand over her mouth.

In the hush afterward I heard a man in the second row whisper to the person beside him. He said it in German. I have a poor ear for German, but I have an excellent ear for questions.

*Wie ist das möglich, in seinem Alter?*

*How is that possible, at his age?*

The person beside him did not answer. But I heard the question go, quietly, from mouth to mouth, along the row and across the aisle, like a draft through a house. I heard it in a dozen languages. I heard it among the journalists, and the physicists, and the delegation from a ministry, and the wives. No one meant anything by it. It was only the sort of thing that people say.

I was, I think, the only person in the hall who was afraid of it.

* * *

Julian took his seat in the Senate in the spring of 2030.

It happened without any election. The man who had held the seat for twenty-two years died in his sleep, in February, at the age of eighty-one, and the governor, who had been Julian's patron for a decade, appointed him to fill the vacancy. He was thirty years old. He had turned thirty on the first of January. It was, as the newspapers all noted, the youngest age the Constitution allows.

I watched the swearing-in on a screen, in a hospital cafeteria, over a bowl of soup. A tall man in a good coat put his hand on a book, and said the words, and turned to the cameras. He did not look at the cameras. He looked, I could swear, through them, with an expression of such warm and perfect composure that a woman at the next table said: "Oh, he's lovely."

He made a short speech. He said that he believed in the dignity of every ordinary person. The room applauded. I heard the applause go on a half-second longer than it should. And I understood that I had been dealt, without my consent, into a game whose rules I did not know, and that the other player had already read the book.

He spent his first year in the Senate passing bills with titles that were very hard to vote against.

There was the **Kitchen Table Fairness Act,** which established that kitchen tables should be fair, and which nobody could find a way to oppose, and which, on close reading, directed the Department of Commerce to *study the concept.* There was the **Ordinary Families Dignity Act,** which renamed a post office. There was a resolution recognizing the first week of October as **National Listening Week,** which passed ninety-eight to two. The two, it turned out, had both asked whether anyone had checked the spelling of *listening,* and had been described in the newspapers as *obstructionist.*

I watched him work. He did not do it as other politicians do, with tiring bombast. He did it with a kind of leisurely precision, like a man tuning a piano. A colleague who disagreed with him would find, within the week, that Julian had not only listened but remembered, and had sent a handwritten note about the colleague's daughter's recital. By the end of the year, he had a reputation as the one man in the building who never raised his voice and never lost a vote. Journalists called him *the quiet one.* He did nothing to discourage it.

On the day itself, I finished my soup. I paid at the register. I walked back to the ward, where there was a man waiting for a scan, and a woman who had lost the use of her left hand that morning, and a great deal that I could do something about.

I did it, as I always did. It was the one thing I was sure of.


## 15. The Child

Their daughter was born in December of 2027, in a hospital by the river, at eleven minutes past four in the afternoon.

I was not there. I was on a night shift in a ward three thousand miles away, with a cold cup of coffee and a patient who would not stop humming. Mira called me at ten past eleven, my time, in a whisper, and said: "Seven pounds. She has all her fingers. She's looking at me as if I owed her an explanation."

"What have you called her?"

"Lena." She laughed, softly, so as not to wake someone. "After Jonas's grandmother. It's the only name on either side of the family that nobody has a grievance about."

I asked whether she had listened. It was an absurd question, and Mira knew what I meant. I meant: *has she turned her head?*

"I haven't tried yet," said Mira. "I'm too tired. And I think it's rude. She's been alive for seven hours. I'd like her to have one day when nobody is listening to her."

I said that was reasonable.

"But I will tell you," she said, "that the midwife dropped a tray in the corridor, and Lena didn't flinch. She opened her eyes about a second *before* it fell." She was silent a moment. "I haven't told Jonas."

* * *

I went in the summer of 2030, when Lena was two and a half.

It was the first time I had been in Europe since the move, and I had forgotten how the light behaves there in July. It does not arrive so much as extend, hour after hour, past nine in the evening, with a long gold patience, as if it had nowhere better to be. Jonas met me at the station in a linen shirt that did not suit him. He had a way of carrying my bag as if it were a rare instrument. We took the tram, and he told me about a bridge he was working on, with the detailed and affectionate pessimism of a man who loved a thing that might fall down.

The flat was on the third floor, over a courtyard. The stairs were stone, and they carried every footfall to every wall. I stood on the second landing and listened. A radio, a floor above, playing something with a cello in it. A tap. A woman's voice, lightly arguing, in Turkish. A clock.

"It's like being inside an instrument," I said.

"She says that too," said Jonas. "She says she can't sleep for the building tuning itself." He put his key in the lock. "I tell her it's a very old building. It has opinions."

Mira opened the door, with her hair in a scarf and her sleeves rolled up, and flung her arms round my neck. She smelled of flour and of someone else's shampoo. "You're thin," she said. "You're eating on the run. Come and see her. She's on the balcony."

* * *

Lena was on the balcony floor in a red sweater, listening to the courtyard.

I stood in the doorway. Mira said, quietly, "Wait."

The child was perhaps three feet from me. She was sitting cross-legged on a rug, with her back to us, and a wooden duck in front of her that she was ignoring. She had her head tilted, exactly as her mother's did. After a moment she said, in a clear, small, precise German:

"Die Uhr im anderen Haus geht gleich."

Mira translated, though I did not need it. *The clock in the other house is about to go.*

We stood there, the three of us, and I counted. It was nineteen seconds. Then, from the next building, through two walls and a courtyard, a cuckoo clock announced the hour. Six. It cuckooed six times, in a thin tinny note, with a pause between the third and the fourth where the mechanism hesitated.

Lena nodded, once, as if the clock had behaved as expected.

"She's been doing this since she was one," Mira whispered. She had tears in her eyes, and she was laughing. "Jonas thinks it's the refrigerator."

"I do not," said Jonas, mildly, behind me. "I think it's a very good ear. I have never said otherwise."

"You said it was the refrigerator."

"I said the refrigerator was a possibility."

I lowered myself to the floor, beside the child. I did it slowly, as one approaches a cat. She looked up at me, and I saw her face for the first time: a solemn, round, pale face, with her mother's chin and her father's forehead and a pair of wide gray eyes that had the grave, appraising quality of someone who has seen a great many things and has decided to reserve judgment.

She said something to me. It was a composite of German and English and something else, a sort of private grammar, and I understood perhaps a third of it. But I understood the intonation. It was a greeting. It was the greeting of one person to another who has come a long way.

I answered her in the same.

* * *

We had supper on the balcony. Mira had made a soup with dumplings in it, and a bread with seeds, and there was beer in tall glasses. The light went on and on. Down in the courtyard a boy of about ten was kicking a ball against a wall, and every time it struck the brick I felt it in my teeth.

Mira's parents had been staying with them for a week. They sat at the end of the table, a tall silver-haired woman in a linen jacket and a stooped, quiet man with a laptop that he had, for the occasion, closed. They were the professor and the engineer, whom I knew from Stone's notebook as two figures in a second row at a swimming meet, a laptop open on a knee. They had, I learned that evening, retired the previous year, and had come to Munich to be grandparents in the way that people do when they have time.

"She's extraordinary," said Mira's mother, to me, across the table. She said it with the dry, impartial air of an examiner. "I mean it. I've taught for thirty-five years. I've never seen a child so attentive. She listens to the whole room."

"She gets it from her mother," said Jonas.

"Oh, no." Mira's mother smiled at her daughter, and it was a smile of a particular kind: affectionate, puzzled, and a little proud. "Mira was never like this. Mira talked. She was a talker. She was a very chatty child. But she did have a thing about sounds." She turned her glass. "She used to say the dishwasher was *flat.* We didn't know what she meant."

There was a pause at the table. I watched Mira's hand go still on her fork.

"It was flat," said Mira.

"I'm sure it was, dear."

Mira's father, who had said nothing until then, lifted his eyes from his plate and looked, for a long, quiet moment, at his daughter. He had a lined and kindly face, like a map that has been folded many times. Then he said, to no one in particular, in a gentle voice:

"We have always been very glad of her."

It was all he said. But I heard something in the way he said it, a deliberateness, a kind of sealed courtesy, as though a door had been closed gently in a room where he did not wish to be overheard. I do not know what they knew. I have never asked. I think perhaps a parent knows more than she says and says less than she knows, and that this, too, is a form of love.

* * *

Late in the evening, when the others had gone in, I sat with Lena on the balcony floor.

She had been put in a nightgown and told to go to bed, and had come out again, as children do, with the air of a diplomat. She sat in my lap and looked at the courtyard. It was nearly dark. The ball had stopped. A dog barked, three streets away, and she turned her head to it, a few degrees, and then turned it back.

"Hörst du das?" she said. *Do you hear that?*

"Yes."

"Alle?" *Everyone?*

I thought about it. It was a very large question, and she had asked it in the way that very small people do, without any idea of its size.

"No," I said. "Not everyone."

"Du?"

"Me. And your mama. And you."

She nodded gravely. "Wir drei." *The three of us.*

"Yes."

She put her head against my chest, as children do, and I felt her go still, and I knew that she was listening to my heart. It was beating rather hard. I did not tell her.

It struck me that this was the first person I had ever met who had been born into what I was and who had never once been told she was alone. She had a mother who could hear, and a father who loved her, and a pair of grandparents who said *we have always been very glad.* She had a cuckoo clock in the next house. She was perfectly all right.

I had spent ten years wondering what I would do with the thing that Stone had left. I had made lists. I had written *for* and *against.* I had weighed the dying child on the other side of the world against the child who had not asked.

And here was one child, on one balcony, who was not an argument at all.

That was what changed my mind. No argument did. No one persuaded me. It was a little girl in a red sweater, with a wooden duck, who had a mother and a father and a cuckoo clock, and who had said, in a voice like a bell, *wir drei.*

She fell asleep against me, in the dark. I did not move for a long time. Somewhere below, in the courtyard, someone was singing under their breath, and I listened, as I always do, and I thought that I had finally been given an answer, and that it was not one I could have found by thinking.


## 16. Restart

Julian made the offer in the autumn of 2030, on the telephone, in the voice of a man ordering lunch.

He had been a senator for four months. I had seen him on the news, standing on the steps of a building in a good coat, saying that he would serve the people of his state with humility. I had heard the applause go on a half second too long. I had put the television off and gone for a walk.

He called on a Sunday. I did not give him my number. I never learned how he got it.

"Danny," he said. He was the only person outside my family who called me that, and he did it with a warmth that no one could have faulted. "I hope I'm not interrupting."

"You're not."

"I want to say something plainly, because I think we've both been rather careful with each other." A pause, perfectly judged. "I know what you've got. I don't mean the hearing. I mean the pages. I think you've got the whole of Stone's work in a drawer somewhere, and I think you've been trying to decide what to do with it."

I did not answer.

"I'd like to help you decide," said Julian.

I held the telephone away from my ear and looked at it, and then I put it back. His voice was exactly as it had been the first night, at the debate: unhurried, clear, without a flaw. His pulse, which I could hear in the small sounds his breath made against the receiver, was as steady as a clock.

"There's a foundation," he said. "It doesn't matter whose. It has a great deal of money and it would like to do some good. A laboratory, quiet and well run, with proper oversight and a proper ethics board. People like us, and people who'd like to be, and all of it done *kindly.* No secrets, no Stone. Just the work, finished the way it should have been."

"And you'd run it."

"I'd see that it was run well." He laughed, gently, at himself. "I wouldn't touch it. I'd simply make it possible."

I thought of a cold night in April 1999, and a man with a card in his hand, and a refrigerator coming on. I thought: *he is describing it as a favor.*

"No," I said.

"Think about it."

"I have. No."

"Of course." There was no change in his voice at all. "I only wanted you to know it was there. Come and see me in Washington, if you like. I'd enjoy that."

He hung up. I sat for a long time, with the telephone on my knee.

I understood two things. The first was that I would never be able to tell him no in a way that he could hear. The second was that I had been thinking about the offer, for years, in a different shape, in my own voice.

* * *

I had the copies. I had four of them, in four places, and I had known their locations since I was eleven. I had a laboratory I could have rented, a bank balance that would have embarrassed me, and thirty-one people across three continents who trusted me. I had a world that might need what Stone had planned, if it were done kindly, with consent, openly, by people who understood what it cost.

I made a list. I always make a list.

*For.* We are few, and we are fragile. A single bad winter, a single indiscreet scientist, a single hearing in a legislature could end us. A larger number would be safer. The senator is proof that no one is safe in any case. A world of people like Mira could solve in a decade what now takes a century. A child born with a gift is not harmed by being born.

*Against.* No child consents to being made. Stone thought he was kind. So did I, every time I thought it through. The families would have to be lied to, or told, and I did not know which was worse. It is irreversible, and I was not.

I put the list in a drawer. I took it out again. I added a line to the first column, and then crossed it out. It said: *I am not sure that I know what is best.* It was true, and it did not belong on either side.

* * *

I took it to Elias first.

He was in a small apartment in a college town, with a window onto a parking lot and a whiteboard that took up one wall. He had been a Nobel laureate for a year. He was thirty. He had taken the prize money and given most of it to a fund for graduate students, and had bought, with the rest, a very good chair.

He read the list in silence. Then he read it again.

"You've written *for* in a smaller hand," he said.

"Have I?"

"Yes. And you've given *against* more words." He looked up, and his face, which was usually apologetic, was alive with something I had not seen in it. "You want to be told to do it."

"I want to know what you think."

"I think you should." He said it quickly, and with a kind of ferocity, as if he had been holding it in for years. "I think we're sitting on the greatest opportunity in the history of the species, and we are using it to hide. Do you know what I could do with ten of me? A hundred? I've solved one problem in my life, and it was easy. There are a thousand harder ones. There's a child dying of something on the other side of the world this minute, and if there were enough of us—"

"There would be a child."

"Yes."

"Who didn't ask."

"No one asks," said Elias. "No one has ever asked. You didn't ask. I didn't ask. You think the people who are born ordinary, who are born into poverty, into war, you think they were *asked?*" He stood up. He was shaking. "We're not the ones who made the rule. We're only the first who can see it."

I said, "That's what Stone said."

He stopped.

It was the cruelest thing I have ever said to him. I knew it as I said it. I watched it land, and I watched his face change: the color go out of it, the anger go, and a sort of bewildered sadness come in its place, like a man who has lost the rest of a sentence.

"That's not fair," he said quietly.

"No."

"I'm not Stone."

"No." I looked at my hands. "But he'd have liked you."

He sat down. He did not speak for several minutes. When he did, it was in his old voice, small and exact.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I think I wanted it to be simple."

"So did I."

* * *

I took the list to Mira on a Sunday in the spring of 2031, when Lena was three and a half.

It was in the kitchen of the apartment in Munich. The windows were open to the courtyard, and a smell of cut grass came up from a garden somewhere below. Jonas was out with Lena, at a playground, and I could hear them, a quarter mile away, from the apartment, the high clear note of a child on a swing and a tall man's patient voice saying *noch einmal.*

Mira read the list standing up, with her pencil in her hair. When she had finished she put the sheet down on the table and said:

"You've written this as though it's your decision."

"Isn't it?"

"No." She took my hand. It was an odd, startling gesture. She was not a woman who touched people. "It's what you're going to do. It was never going to be what *we* decide. We're already here. We have children, or we will. You can't start it, and you can't stop it. All you can do is not to *add.*" She squeezed. "And you have. Good. You've decided not to. That is the right thing, and it's also the only thing that's yours."

"What if I'm wrong?"

"Then you're wrong." She smiled, a little crookedly. "Danny, you're the most careful man I know. You have thought about it for ten years. I think you've earned the right to be wrong once."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me like a cough.

"Now," said Mira, and she let go of my hand. "Stop deciding for all of us."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You do it constantly. You keep a list of people, and a list of reasons, and a list of what each of us ought to want." She said it without malice. "We love you for it. But you're not a god. You're a physician with a good ear, and some very bad habits."

I said that I would try.

"Good." She picked up the list again, looked at it for a moment, and folded it twice. "I'm keeping this. It's the best argument I've ever seen against something."

* * *

I took the copies to a place I will not name and locked them. I did not burn them. I am not that kind of person, and I am not sure it would have helped. I stood in the cold, in a room with a concrete floor, and put four envelopes into a box, and a lock on the box, and I thought of Stone's vault, and the word he liked, and the way he had believed that a thing in a vault was a thing no one else had seen.

It was a good lock. I did not believe in it any more than he had.

I went home, and I took out my own notebook, the one with the black cover, and turned to a clean page. I wrote, in the middle of it, one word.

*No.*

I looked at it for a long time. It did not look like a decision. It looked like something that had always been there, and that I had been walking round for years, the way one walks round a piece of furniture in the dark.

Then I put the pen away, and went to bed, and slept, for the first time in many weeks, without dreaming of a refrigerator.


## 17. The Foundation

The first I heard of it was a press release, and it had a leaf on it.

It was the spring of 2032. I was eating a bagel in the fourth-floor cafeteria of the university hospital, which is the only way anyone should read the news, and my phone buzzed with a link from Elias, who never sent links. There was no message. Just the link and a single character, which I took to be a question mark until I enlarged it and found it was a speck of dust on his screen.

The headline said **LEAF FOUNDATION ACQUIRES FORMER FERTILITY CLINIC FOR FAMILY HEALTH CAMPUS.**

I read it twice, with the bagel going cold in my other hand. The Leaf Foundation, it said, was *a new philanthropic organization dedicated to the flourishing of ordinary families.* It had bought the old Central Valley building, the whole of it, with its records, its equipment and its parking lot, for a price that the article described as *undisclosed* and the county assessor, in a smaller paragraph below, described as *eleven percent above market.* The foundation's chair was a retired judge I had never heard of. Its board included a hospital administrator, a bishop, and a woman who made frozen pizza. Its funding came from *a family trust.*

The logo was a small green leaf. It was an unremarkable leaf. It could have been the logo of a bank, or a tea, or a vegetarian restaurant.

I had seen it before. It had been in the corner of a newsletter in a university library carrel fourteen years earlier, under a banner that said **OUR FAMILY GROWS.**

I put the bagel down.

* * *

I should say how little I knew about Julian by then, and how much I thought I knew.

I knew he was good. I had said it to myself so many times that it had stopped being an observation and become a sort of furniture. I knew that he had a gift, and that the gift was people, and that behind it lay something I could not hear. I knew he had called me *Danny* on the telephone in 2030 and offered to pay for a laboratory. I knew I had said no.

What I did not know, and what I should have asked myself, was why a man who had been told no in a courteous voice would go on being courteous. I assumed it was manners. People who are very polite are mostly very patient, and people who are very patient are mostly waiting for something.

I called Elias. He picked up on the first ring, which meant he had been holding the phone.

"I'm not saying it's him," said Elias.

"You sent me a link."

"I sent you a link to a newspaper article about a charity."

"With a leaf on it."

"Lots of things have leaves." He breathed. "Danny. I've been a Nobel laureate for two years. I get forty requests a week from foundations who want me to put my name on something. Do you know how I tell which one is a trap?"

"How?"

"I don't. I put my name on all of them and then I feel ill." A pause. "This one hasn't asked."

That was the thing. It had not asked. Julian, who had known about Elias for years, who had sent him a handwritten note on the day of the Nobel, had not so much as added his name to a mailing list. It was the politeness of a man who has already decided.

* * *

Mira called three weeks later, and I knew from the first word that something had gone wrong.

She did not say hello. She said, "Where are you?" in the voice she used for dropped glassware, and I said I was in a parking lot outside a supermarket, and she said, "Good, sit down."

"I am sitting down."

"I've had a letter."

I should explain that Mira and I spoke every two weeks, on Sundays, at an hour that was convenient to nobody, and that we had done so for thirteen years without missing more than four. We spoke about Lena's vocabulary and Jonas's bridges and the way the radiator in the apartment knocked in B flat. We did not usually begin with *I've had a letter.*

"From whom?"

"From a senator." She said the word like a woman holding something wet. "A very warm letter. A very *intelligent* letter. It says he's delighted to learn that there is a thriving community of gifted and sensitive people in Europe, that he's long admired their discretion, and that he looks forward to a day when they may be of service. It says he is *a friend of Danny's.*"

I did not say anything.

"It says," said Mira, "that you and he have known each other since the spring of 2022."

The parking lot went very quiet. A cart rolled across the asphalt by itself and stopped at a curb.

"Danny."

"Yes."

"Is that true?"

I have thought about what I could have said. I could have said, *it's complicated.* I could have said, *I was going to tell you.* I could have said, in my best level physician's voice, that I had good reasons, which I did, and that I had kept them from her out of care, which was half true. Instead I said the thing that I had learned, at the age of eleven, from a man in a vault: that when you are caught, the useful thing is to be exact.

"Yes," I said. "I met him in 2022, at a debate. His pulse was wrong. I mean it was right. It was perfect. There was nothing under it. I didn't know what that meant, so I said nothing. In 2027 he sent me a card about Elias. I knew then. I knew he knew. I didn't tell you. In 2028 he took me to dinner and told me what he was. In 2030 he offered to fund a laboratory. I said no."

There was a long silence, and in it I could hear her breathing, and behind her breathing, as clear as a bell, the sound of Lena in the next room, practicing the recorder.

"Four years," said Mira.

"Ten, if you count the debate."

"I don't count the debate. I count the card. The card, Danny. You had a card from a man you thought was a danger to all of us, and you put it in a drawer with a *rock.*" Her voice broke, and she took it back. "I have a daughter who has to hold herself still in a room. I have a son. I have thirty-one friends who are scattered across three continents, and every one of them has been living for five years in a world where there was a man who knew, and nobody told them."

"I was protecting you."

"No." She said it very quietly. "You were deciding for us."

It was the thing she had said in her kitchen in 2031. It had been funny then. It was not funny now.

"I'm not going to shout," said Mira. "I'd like to. I'm going to put the phone down. I'm going to be angry for some time, and then I'm going to be less angry, and then I'll call you. It might be a year."

"Mira—"

"It'll be a year, Danny."

She hung up.

I sat in the car for forty minutes, and a woman with a shopping cart tapped on the window and asked if I was all right, and I said I was fine. I was not fine. I had heard every note in what Mira had said. I had heard the fury and the fear, and under both of them, and this was the part I could not forgive, I had heard that she was right.

* * *

It was a year and eleven days.

I will say that I learned a great deal about silence in that time, and that most of it was unwelcome. I learned that my ears, which could tell a lie from a pulse at forty feet, could not tell me what I was doing wrong. I learned that the quiet ones, who had always rung me on Sundays, called on other days, and spoke to one another first. I learned from the florist, in a long careful email with a photograph of tulips attached, that *Mira told us.* Not in anger, she said. In sequence. *She told us, one by one, in the order that you found us. You'll be pleased to know it took four days. She said you would want it done properly.*

I was not pleased. I read it at my desk with a cold coffee, and I had the odd sensation of reading my own obituary, written by someone who had liked me.

I wrote to the surveyor. He wrote back, in two lines, that he understood and was not angry, and that he'd had a feeling for some time that there was something I wasn't saying, because *you stopped breathing a certain way when anyone said the word senator.* I wrote to the violinist. She sent me a recording of herself playing a piece I did not recognize, with no message, and it was the kindest thing anyone did that year.

Mira called on a Sunday, a year and eleven days after, at the usual hour, as if nothing had happened. "I'm less angry," she said. "Not by much. Tell me everything, and this time do not edit it." I told her everything. It took two hours and forty minutes, and she did not interrupt, and at the end she said, "Good. Now I would like to be angry again for a week," and she was, and then she wasn't, and on the following Sunday she asked after my parents.

I did not write to Julian. I was waiting, I think, for the next move, the way a man waits who has been told there is going to be a knock at the door.

It came on the first Thursday of the following month, in a cream envelope with no stamp, put through the slot of my apartment door. The handwriting was one I knew.

*Dear Danny,*

*The old building is open to visitors on the first Thursday of the month. I thought you might like to see what we have done with it. I have kept one room exactly as it was.*

*J.*

I read it standing in the hall in my coat. And I did, God help me, exactly what Stone had done in 2011 when a note came through his door.

I went.


## 18. The Cabinet

They had put a smoothie bar where the waiting room used to be.

I want to say that I took this in calmly, but I stood in the doorway of the old Central Valley lobby with a laminated visitor's badge on a lanyard around my neck, and what I thought, with great clarity, was: *I hope nobody was ever told bad news here, for the smoothie's sake.* It was a very nice smoothie bar. It had reclaimed wood and a chalkboard that said **TODAY: MANGO + CALM.** A young man in a green apron was explaining to a woman with a stroller that the kale was local.

The lobby was the same lobby. That was the unsettling part. They had kept the high ceiling and the black and white tiles, and the radiator under the window that Stone had hated. They had painted over the gloom with a color the brochure called *Meadow* and hung, on the long wall where the donors' plaques used to be, a mural of a very large, very friendly tree. Under the tree, in a loose and cheerful script, were the words **EVERY CHILD DESERVES TO BE HEARD.**

I stood there for some time, reading it.

"Isn't it wonderful?" said the woman with the stroller. She had caught me looking. She was perhaps thirty, with flour on her sleeve and the beaming, slightly hunted look of the parents of babies. "My sister told me about it. They do the hearing screenings for free. For the kids, I mean. The whole family health thing. You just bring them in on a Thursday and they play them little sounds."

"Do they?"

"Eleven minutes." She jiggled the stroller. "He was so good. The nice lady said he's a *very sensitive listener.*" She said it as though it were a prize. "She said a lot of the little ones are. She gave me a leaflet."

She handed it to me. It was on glossy green paper, with a drawing of a child's ear wearing a small and slightly smug smile. It said **KNOW YOUR LITTLE LISTENER.** Underneath, in smaller type, it said *Free auditory wellness screening for children 0 to 12. Results confidential. A service of the Leaf Foundation.*

I read it again. I could feel the pulse in my own throat, going at about a hundred and ten, and I stood very still so that nobody could hear it.

*He's screening them,* I thought. *He's screening children for the one thing it would be impossible to ask them.*

* * *

The tour began at two.

I joined it because it was the only way to get past the smoothies. There were nine of us: two retired couples, a man in a suit who was either a donor or a journalist, a very young reporter from a local paper, and a family of four who had clearly come for the free cookies. The guide was a bright-eyed young woman in the green of the leaf, who said her name was Brianna and walked backward through the building the way they teach you to.

"The Leaf Family Health Campus," she said, "is built on one simple idea. Every family is unique. And every family deserves the very best start."

She took us through the old examination rooms, which had been turned into *wellness pods.* They had beanbags. In Room 1 a woman in a lab coat was reading a story to a toddler in a voice that was both soothing and clinical, and the toddler was wearing a pair of headphones the size of dinner plates. In Room 2, a girl of about six sat very straight with her hand raised, waiting to hear a tone. A technician watched her with a face that gave nothing away. The girl's father stood in the corner with his arms folded, nodding at each beep as if he were being asked to approve it.

"The results are completely private," said Brianna. "We only look for areas of *exceptional sensitivity.* Our goal is to make sure that every child's gift is nurtured."

"What happens to the ones who have it?" said the man in the suit.

"They're offered a place in one of our enrichment programs." She beamed. "It's all voluntary. And free. We're a nonprofit."

"Of course," said the man in the suit.

I did not look at anyone. I looked at the carpet. It was gray, and it was new, and it had been laid where the green linoleum had been, and under it, I was fairly sure, was the floor on which a woman named Dolores had once wept at the sight of a small bright smear on a screen.

We came, at last, to Exam Room 3.

The door was open. They had not changed it. That was the first thing I saw, and the second, and the one that made my legs go slightly: the same plastic chair, the same high window, and on the wall, quite undisturbed, the poster of the smiling ear. It had been framed. Underneath, a small brass plate said: *Original fixtures preserved in honor of the families who came before.*

"This is our heritage room," said Brianna. "We think it's important to remember where we started."

I looked at the ear. It looked, I thought, a good deal more sinister in a frame.

* * *

The end of the tour was not on the tour.

I had been told to follow the corridor to the very end, which was a thing I had done once before in my life, at the age of eleven, and I knew what was there before I turned the handle. The door was gray. It had a small brass sign. It said **THE ORIGINAL LABORATORY: PRIVATE.**

I did not knock. I think I was too angry. I opened the door, and went in, and shut it behind me.

It was exactly as I had read it. I have read Stone's descriptions so many times that I had begun to think of them as memories, and I stood on the old tiled floor, in the light of a single lamp over the bench, with a feeling that I can only compare to meeting, in the flesh, a person who has been described to you by someone who loved them. There was the stool. There was the old steel sink. There was the cream-colored incubator in its corner, on its own stand, with the faded sticker that said **PROPERTY OF**— and nothing, and the small red light on the front, glowing, steadily, like a patient eye.

It was on. Someone had kept it running.

And against the east wall, with its door standing open like a mouth, was the gray cabinet.

"I thought you'd want to see it," said Julian.

* * *

He was sitting on Stone's stool with his sleeves rolled, like a man in his own house.

He looked well. He was thirty-three. He had been a senator for three years, and had grown, in the way that handsome men do, into a sort of weathered, assured attractiveness that photographed beautifully and looked, in person, slightly tired. He had a mug of tea. He had made a second one and put it on the bench beside the other stool, and I understood that he had known exactly how long the tour would take, and what I would do on the way.

"You've done a good job with the smoothies," I said.

"Thank you. I can't take credit. A committee." He smiled. "Sit. You look like you want to hit something, and I'd rather you didn't do it to the equipment."

I did not sit. "You're screening children."

"I'm offering families a free service." He sipped. "Eleven minutes. Most of them love it."

"For one thing."

"For one thing." He said it so easily that I had to listen twice. "Yes. Of course. What else would I be looking for?" He put the mug down. "You've been looking for them for fifteen years, Danny. You've a spreadsheet in a rock. I've a *foundation.* The difference is that I have money, and that the people I find are glad to be found."

"They don't know what they are."

"Neither do the ones you find. You tell them. I offer them a place." He tilted his head. "Which of us, I wonder, does less harm?"

It was a good question. That was the dreadful thing about Julian. He asked good questions, and he asked them as though he were genuinely curious about the answer, and I was never sure that he was not.

* * *

He showed me the pages.

They lay on the bench, under a sheet of glass, in a neat row. I knew them at once. They were bound with a green ribbon that someone had untied and laid beside them, in a loop, like a hair from a dead man's head. They were in Stone's small, upright hand, black ink on yellowing paper, in the script that no one else could follow quickly.

"They were in the cabinet," said Julian. "It was locked. A very honest lock, apparently. The director had the whole thing moved to a basement in 2013 with a note that said *do not open until he's better.* Nobody did, and he wasn't. I had a man in." He said this with every appearance of sincerity. "I was moved." 

"Why are you showing me?"

"Because I can't read them."

He said it simply, like a man admitting that he cannot swim.

"I can read the letters," he said. "I can read the numbers. I can see that it's a ledger, and that these are names, and that there are columns for something that looks very like intent. But I can't read the *sentences.*" He touched the glass with one finger. "He wrote it in a grammar of his own. And he's no help. I've sat with him." He looked up. "Did you know that? I went to the home. I sat for an hour. He looked at me with such interest. He said nothing. I rather liked him."

"You went to see Stone."

"Twice. Don't worry. He didn't know who I was." He smiled. "It's remarkable how many people don't."

There was nothing to say to that. I stood in the lab with my hands at my sides, and I did the thing I have been trained to do from the age of eleven, which is nothing.

"I need a translator," said Julian. "I'll be plain. I think you're the only one alive. I think you learned it as a boy, in about a week, because you were eleven and brilliant and it was the only thing in the room."

"No."

"I'd pay you whatever you like."

"No."

"I'd put your name on it."

"No."

"All right." He leaned back. "Then I'll ask you for nothing at all. I'll ask you only to look at one line, and tell me whether I'm right that it's a name."

He turned the glass, with a small and delicate gesture, and slid a finger toward a page.

And I looked.

I did not mean to. I had spent twenty years teaching myself not to look at things, and I looked at that line the way a man looks at a house he used to live in, with his whole face. It was a family. It was a Pruitt, in fact, the first line of the first page: *A. Born 1/1/2000. Cog + Aud. Heterozygous. Family: R. & C. P.* I had read it at eleven. I had not read it in twenty-two years.

I looked at it for perhaps a second and a half.

"Thank you," said Julian, gently.

My throat went dry.

"I wasn't sure," he said. "I was almost sure. I've been watching you for eleven years, you know, and you're very good. You don't move. But there's a thing your breath does. You read faster than you breathe." He smiled, with a kind of rueful tenderness. "That tells me it can be read. That's all I wanted. I'm grateful."

I had walked into the room certain that I was the best listener in it. It had not occurred to me that a man might listen to a listener.

* * *

"It isn't a threat," said Julian.

We were standing by the door. I had my hand on the handle. I had not decided what to do with my other hand, and it hung there, shaking a little, like an animal that had not yet been told it could run.

"I'm a very patient man. I'd like you to know that. I've never done anything to anyone I could not explain to their face. I send your father a Christmas basket. He's a lovely man. He wrote me a card." He said it lightly. "He thinks of me as a friend of the family. I've not been near him. I'm very careful about what I touch."

"Stay away from them."

"I will." He looked at me, and for a moment there was a flicker I could not name, the nearest thing to feeling I had ever heard in him. "I don't have a very good record of wanting things, Danny. It's a thing I've noticed about myself. I want to be good at this. Someone has to be."

"At what?"

"At whatever comes next."

I went out. I shut the door behind me. I walked the length of the corridor between the wellness pods, past the toddler with the dinner-plate headphones and the man at the beanbag nodding at each beep, and out through the lobby with its great friendly tree.

In the parking lot I leaned against my car and was sick on the asphalt, neatly, with great economy. A man on his way into the smoothie bar looked away politely.

I still had the leaflet. I found it in my pocket, folded in four, with the small smug ear at the center. *KNOW YOUR LITTLE LISTENER.*

I looked at it for a long time. Then I called Elias.

"He's screening children," I said.

There was a very long silence on the line.

"I know," said Elias, in a small voice. "I've been trying to say it for three weeks."


## 19. More Than Enough

The genomes were out. I do not know when it happened, and there is no date on it. It happened the way a rumor does, through a hundred people each taking a little, until it was a fact that belonged to everyone and to no one.

There was no moment. There was a conference in which a speaker showed a slide of a sequence that did not match anything. There was a database in which a thousand strangers had put their DNA for the sake of finding cousins, and in which, if you looked in the right way, a certain stretch appeared more often than chance allowed. There were papers, then preprints, then blog posts about the papers, then a whole forum of amateurs who spent their evenings comparing notes. By 2032 you could find the outline of it in a library, if you knew where to stand.

Nobody connected it to a man. Nobody connected it to a clinic. The science was too strange, and the people who might have understood it were too busy being right about something else. It sat in the open, like a stone in a field, and the world walked around it and said, *what an odd shape.*

* * *

The carriers had children. They had them in Stuttgart and Ohio and Recife, in apartments and farmhouses, in the usual ways, with the usual accidents.

Some of the children could hear a wall. Some could hear nothing special. Some had a father's nose and a mother's temper and no one had told them anything else. I heard about them from mothers on telephones, from the long list that Mira and Jonas kept in a shoebox, and from the florist, who sent a photograph each spring of a different bunch of tulips and a different child, grinning, in front of them. I was careful to hear about them only as people. I did not keep a spreadsheet. I had learned that lesson.

Mira's second was a boy, born in the winter of 2031. They named him Anton. At the age of six months, to Mira's open delight and Jonas's discreet relief, he showed no sign whatever of hearing anything he should not. He liked the cuckoo clock. He liked his sister. He liked, with a single-minded passion, the sound of a wooden spoon on a pan. When Mira called to tell me, she was laughing so hard she could hardly speak.

"He's *normal,*" she said. "He's perfectly, gloriously normal. I cried for an hour. I'm a terrible mother."

"You're a very good one."

"I wanted him to be, you know." She stopped. "I wanted him to be ordinary. Is that terrible?"

"No."

"It isn't that I wanted to make him less. It's that I wanted him to be able to *stop,* if he wanted." She breathed. "Lena can't stop. She has to hold herself still in a room."

"I know."

"He doesn't. He gets to be five years old, and eleven, and forty, and nobody has to hold a thing."

I thought of my own childhood, and my mother's patient face, and the translation downward. I said, with all the care I had, "He's very lucky."

"They both are," said Mira, a little fiercely. "That's the point. That's what I keep forgetting. They both are."

* * *

My own work moved, in those years, to a laboratory on the fourth floor of a university hospital.

I had my degree by then. I had gone to a medical school with a long corridor and an unreliable coffee machine, and had learned there, as everyone does, a great quantity of things I would forget and a few I would not. I had done my residency in neurology, at night mostly, with my ears open. In the small hours of the morning in a ward, you hear everything: the monitors, the breath, the particular wet catch that a lung makes before it fails. I became good at it. The consultants said I had a gift for bedside diagnosis. I said I had a good stethoscope.

My first paper was about the early hours after a stroke. This was an unfashionable subject. The field wanted to know about prevention, or rehabilitation, or the grand question of what consciousness is. Nobody cared about the first three hours, which everyone treated and nobody understood.

I did it because of a man in a chair by a window. I told no one that. When asked, I said I was interested in the brain.

Our group was eleven people, in four countries, with a shared server and a standing Thursday call that began at an hour convenient to no one. There was a pharmacologist in Lyon with a dry laugh and a collection of ceramic owls. There was a cell biologist in Pune who answered every question by drawing it. There was a statistician in Edmonton whom I never saw, whose voice came through the speakers like a disapproving radio. And there was my supervisor, a woman called Dr. Helen Voss, who had been in the field for forty years and who said, at our first meeting, "Young man, I have never seen anyone listen so hard."

I said that I tried.

"No," she said. "I mean that you *listen.* You don't wait for your turn. It's rare." She stirred her tea. "I don't know what you are, but I'd like to find out."

I told her I was a neurologist.

"Yes," she said. "But what else?"

I will say it was hard not to tell her.

* * *

The idea was simple. After a stroke, a core of tissue dies at once, and around it lies a larger zone that is dying slowly. It has the blood it needs, in a thin trickle, but not enough to live on, and not enough to die by. If you could keep that zone alive for a few hours more, long enough for the blood to return, you might save a great deal. The question was how.

I will not describe the molecule. It is not mine to describe, and it is not interesting to anyone who has not spent years in a laboratory. I will say that it took us three years to find a version that did not kill the mice, another two to find one that did not kill the monkeys, and a long and very dull year of trials in people who had volunteered for a thing they could not have understood.

We got a result in 2033. It was small. It was a molecule that, given in the right hour, protected cells that would otherwise have died. We published it in the usual way, in a good journal that few people read. The world did not notice. It was one of a thousand such results that year, and it mattered to forty people, who read the abstract.

What nobody noticed, for nearly two years, was what happened to the patients we had enrolled late.

It was a nuisance. A trial of an acute drug is meant to start within hours, and every site had a few people who had arrived too late, or who had been given the drug by mistake on the wrong day, or who had been admitted with a stroke that turned out to be several months old. We were supposed to exclude them from the analysis. We did. And in the spring of 2034 the statistician in Edmonton, whose voice came through the speakers like a disapproving radio, said, in the flat tone she used for everything: "Your excluded patients are improving."

"They can't be," said Dr. Voss.

"I did the arithmetic," said Edmonton. "I did it twice. I'm not enthusiastic. I'm reporting."

We looked, and it was true. In the old injuries, the ones that were supposed to be finished, repeated courses of the same molecule did something that had nothing to do with saving tissue. They woke things. Circuits that had gone dark when the blood failed came up, slowly, like lights in a house after a power cut. A man who had not used his left hand in six years held a cup. A woman who had lost a word at fifty found it, at fifty-six, in the middle of a sentence about her cat.

We ran the late-recovery trial in 2035. It was long and very dull, and nobody believed it until the third site reported. By the autumn of 2036 it was approved, in a small way and with a great many conditions, for strokes more than a year old.

I do not want to make too much of this. It was a slow, modest, expensive result, arrived at by accident and mostly by other people. But I will say that on the day the third site reported, I went into the stairwell and stood against the wall with my hands over my face, and Dr. Voss, who had followed me out, said nothing at all for a full minute, and then said, quite gently: "I thought so. It's him, isn't it? The man in the chair."

I said that I did not know what she meant.

"Of course you don't," said Dr. Voss.



I did not go to the celebration. I went to the care home.

* * *

Stone was eighty-three. He was in the chair by the window, which was the third window he had had, in the third home. The staff had changed several times since 2016, but the nurses all knew him as *Dr. Frank,* and they all said he was no trouble. He did not try to leave, and he did not shout, and when anyone sang in the corridor he turned his head toward it with an expression of faint, wary approval.

I told him what the paper said. I did not know whether he understood it. I read him the abstract, in an even voice, as one reads to a child, and when I had finished I put the paper on the table and said: "I think it might help."

He looked at me. I had grown used to this look, over seventeen years, and I could not have said what was in it. It might have been intelligence. It might have been the long patience of a creature that waits at a window.

His right hand moved on the blanket. It was not a gesture. It was a hand deciding to be a hand.

I watched it for some time.

Then he made a sound. It was the same sound he had made in 2016, in the day room, when I put my hand on the page: breath given a shape, a rounded, open shape, the one a mouth makes before it says *oh.*

"Yes," I said again. "That's the one."

Mira, who knows about these things, says that people in his state sometimes respond to the *tone* of a sentence, and that I should not read too much into it. I am sure she is right. I read a great deal into it anyway.

* * *

There is a question that I put to myself in those years, and I will set it down, since I promised to set down everything.

The question was: *what if it works?*

I mean the cure. I mean the real one, the one still some years away, the one that would take a man who had not spoken in twenty years and give him back his voice. I mean what he would do with it.

I had thought about this at length. I had written lists, as I do, in a notebook with a black cover. *For:* he is a man, and it is not mine to withhold. *For:* mercy. *For:* the chance that, once his mind returned, he would see what I saw. *Against:* the index. *Against:* the century. *Against:* the fact that a man who believed what he had believed could believe it again.

But the index was gone. I knew that. I had seen him look at his own handwriting and fail to read it. He was eighty-three, and whatever the molecule gave him, it could not give him back a language that the stroke had taken. The danger I imagined, a rejuvenated Stone, striding out of a hospital with the plan in his head, was a danger I had invented. The man in the chair could not do arithmetic.

It was, I realized, a relief.

I had been afraid of the wrong thing. I had been afraid of his power, when the thing I should have feared was his regret.

I put the notebook away. I did not decide, that day, to give him anything. I decided only to go on, and to see whether the thing worked, and to ask him, when it did, what he would like.

In the corridor, a cart went by with a rattling wheel. I counted eleven seconds. I did not look round.


## 20. Four Question Marks

I had a rule, and Julian had a better one.

Mine was *no one is approached by surprise.* It had taken me fifteen years to build. It was careful and kind and it had kept me, so far as I could tell, from doing a single useful thing for four of the people on my list. His was simpler. His was *approach everyone, gently, with money.*

I went through the list that night, at the kitchen table, with a cup of tea going cold. There were four names with a box drawn round them, each in my own small handwriting, each with a question mark. I had not touched the sheet in years. I had told myself that it was a matter of time, and care, and the right words.

I called Elias and said I was going to find them. He said he would pay for the gas. I said it was four hundred miles. He said he was a Nobel laureate and could afford four hundred miles, and hung up before I could argue.

I took a month's leave. I put the notebook in the glove compartment and drove.

* * *

**Tobias** was the boy who had stopped talking.

He was twenty-eight now. I found him in a low brick building at the edge of a college town that had once been a hosiery factory and was now, according to the sign, **THE LEAF STUDIO: A SPACE FOR LISTENING.** The sign was green. I did not need to read it to know whose it was. I stood in the parking lot with my hands in my pockets and told myself that I would be calm.

It was a very good studio. I want to say that, because it would be much easier to tell you it was a prison. It was warm and quiet, with high windows and wooden floors and a smell of beeswax. It had been soundproofed by someone who knew what they were doing: the walls were thick and curved, the corners rounded, and the one clock on the wall had been fitted with a mechanism that did not tick. In the middle of the main room there were eleven people, aged from nine to fifty, sitting in a loose circle on cushions and doing absolutely nothing. A woman with a viola played one long note. They listened.

I counted. Eight of the eleven turned their heads, a few degrees, to a car that went by on the road three hundred yards away.

I had never been in a room full of us. I had never seen it. I had met them one at a time, in kitchens, in cafés, in basements, and I had come to think of what we were as a solitary condition, like a particular kind of loneliness. I stood at the back of that room, and I felt something give in my chest like a rib. They were so *at ease.* They sat as though the chairs were theirs.

Tobias sat at the end, with his knees up, a thin, dark young man in a gray hoodie. He had his father's chin and a very faint smile. He did not look at me. He did not need to. When I came in, I saw him go still, and I knew that he had heard me from the car.

A young woman at his elbow, a signing interpreter, glanced at me and then at him.

He signed something.

"He says," she said, "that you've been standing at the door for four minutes and you're breathing like a person who's afraid of dogs."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. "I am a bit afraid of dogs."

He signed again, and the interpreter's face did a complicated thing.

"He says, *everyone is. It's the barking.*"

* * *

We talked on a bench in the garden, where Tobias had a plot of beans.

He had begun speaking again at fifteen, he told me, through the interpreter, in a series of signs that were quick, dry and not unfunny. Not at school. At a music camp, where the cellos had tuned, and a boy next to him had said, in a loud cheerful voice, *that's flat,* and Tobias had said, *yes,* aloud, and had been so startled by it that he did not speak again for three weeks. The foundation had found him at eighteen. They had asked him nothing. That was what he wanted me to understand.

*They asked me nothing,* he signed. *Nobody had ever asked me nothing before.*

"What does that mean?"

*It means everyone before wanted me to say something. Teachers, doctors, my mother. Say it, sweetheart, tell us what's wrong. They had a room at the foundation for me, and a cello, and an interpreter, and they said, you can stay as long as you like, and you needn't ever say a word.* He looked at the beans. *I stayed. I said a word in March. It was "cabbage."*

"Do they know what you are?"

*They know what I am,* he signed. *Better than you, I think. You tell people what they are. They let me find out.* He gave a small shrug, almost apologetic. *I'm happy, Danny. I'm sorry. I know it would be easier if I weren't.*

I sat there with my hands on my knees. I had come to rescue someone, and the person in question was kindly, and glad, and growing beans.

* * *

**Imke** was the young woman who could hear the building.

She was thirty-four. She lived in a room at a private clinic forty miles north, which belonged to a company that belonged to a trust, and the receptionist, who was very sweet, said that Ms. Imke saw visitors on Wednesdays between two and four.

It was a pleasant room. It had a window seat. It had a cat, a fat white one called Rudolph, and a bookshelf full of paperbacks about the sea. She was small and fair and a little pale, in a blue cardigan, and she sat in the window with her feet tucked under her and watched me come in.

"You hear it too," she said.

I stopped in the doorway.

"The building." She smiled, rather shyly. "Nobody else does. They tell me it's a very quiet building. It isn't. It's a sort of choir." She tilted her head. "The pipes are in D. The elevator is a bit sharp. And under it all there's a long low note that I think is the foundations."

"I hear it," I said.

"I thought so. I could tell you were listening to it when you came up the stairs." She stroked the cat. "They put me in the hospital when I was eighteen, you know. I'd told a nurse that the ward was singing. They gave me a drug that made it stop, and I stopped being anyone for about a year." She said this quite lightly. "Then a woman came with a nice smile and said she'd heard about me, and that there was a doctor here who thought I might not need the drug."

"And do you?"

"I have a lower dose." She looked at me with grave, clear eyes. "I don't know if I'm ill. I think a bit. The things I hear aren't always in the building. Some are in me. But Dr. Okonjo says that I should not have to choose between hearing and being well, and that nobody should ever have taken me for someone who had to."

I sat down in the other chair. "Dr. Okonjo."

"He's lovely. He used to work somewhere else." She waved a hand. "Julian visits sometimes. Do you know him? He's the nicest man. He brought me the cat."

*He brought her the cat.* I want you to understand what I felt. It was not rage. It was something much more difficult, a sort of astonished, nauseated helplessness, like watching a man you have been told is a thief hand a coat to a stranger on a cold night.

"Do you want to leave?" I said.

"Not at all." She looked amazed. "Why would I?"

I could not tell her. I did not know. I sat in the sun with a cat on my knees and listened to a building sing, and I thought: *I have been looking for people to save, and I have found two who are better off than I am.*

* * *

**Theo** I found by his mother.

She was a woman of fifty-six, with a tired, kind face and a house in a town I will not name. She still wrote to him every week. She sent the letters care of a post office box that had stopped being valid in 2023, and then, for reasons she could not explain, had gone on writing, and keeping carbon copies in a drawer. She had a hundred and ninety-three of them. She gave me one, and she looked at me with a terrible, shining hope, and said: "If you see him. If you see him, will you tell him it's only that I miss him?"

He was working for the foundation as a groundskeeper. It took me six weeks to find him, and in the end I did it by following a lawnmower. He was thirty-three. He was sunburned, broad-shouldered, and clearly very good at it, and he stopped the machine when he saw me and wiped his hands on his jeans.

He knew me. I do not know how. I think they had been told.

"I'm not going to come with you," he said.

"I'm not asking."

"Good." He looked at the letter in my hand. "That's from my mom."

"Yes."

"I know what it says. It says she misses me." His face did something quick. "She always says that. She's right. I don't know what to do about it." He took the letter, very gently, and put it in his shirt pocket without opening it. "He gave me a job when nobody would. I was living in a car. He didn't ask where I'd been. He asked if I could drive a tractor."

"Could you?"

"No." He almost smiled. "I learned."

I stood there in the sun with the mower ticking as it cooled. "Will you read it?"

"Later," said Theo.

I have thought about that word a great deal. It is the word we all use for the thing we are not ready for, and it is not always a lie.

* * *

**Maren** was the woman who lived in a field, and she would not see me.

I found her in a county I will not name, in a place that was less a field than a long, tilted piece of hillside with some goats on it. She had built a house the size of a garden shed out of old pallets, and she had a stove, and a rain barrel, and a view. She was thirty-four. She had been an excellent student, a record of straight A's, until the day she walked out of an exam in the middle of a sentence and did not go back.

She was sitting on the step of the shed in a man's wool coat, peeling a turnip, when I came up the path. She watched me approach. She did not stand up.

"If you're from the foundation," she said, "I've said no twice."

"I'm not from the foundation."

"Then you're from the other lot." She gave a small, dry laugh. "The ones who think they know what's wrong with me. The man with the lovely manners came in a car like a hearse. He sat on that stump, there, and he said he could help. He had a *brochure.*"

"I don't have a brochure."

"No." She looked me up and down. "You've got a notebook in your pocket. I can hear it."

I took it out and held it in front of me, a little sheepishly, like a hat.

"I walked out of an exam," said Maren, "because I could hear forty-three pens, and every one of them was telling me what its owner was afraid of. I'd been hearing it for years. I don't know what I'm supposed to do with that. I came here because there's nothing in the field but wind and goats, and goats have nothing to be afraid of." She turned the turnip. "Everyone thinks the quiet is a problem to be fixed."

"I don't."

"No. I can hear that you don't." She looked at me for a long moment, and for the first time, something in her face eased. "You may sit on the stump. You may not tell me what I am. I may give you an egg."

I sat on the stump.

I did not tell her what she was. She did not ask. We watched the goats for an hour, and at the end she went into the shed and came out with a brown egg in a twist of newspaper and put it in my hand, as one would give a coin to a child. I thanked her. She said it was nothing. I drove away with the egg in the cup holder, and I did not break it for four hundred miles.

* * *

I drove home through a long gray rain.

I will say what I learned, since I promised to set down everything. It was this. I had spent fifteen years believing that the thing wrong with the four question marks was that they did not know. I had told myself that when I could find the right words, they would be saved by the telling. And I had found that two of them were happy, one was loyal, and one had chosen to live in a field, and that every one of them had been found by someone who did not tell them anything at all.

*They let me find out,* Tobias had said.

I had thought of my rule as kindness. I saw now that it was control, in a gentler coat. I had wanted to be the one to decide when each of them was ready. It was Stone's sin, in miniature, and I had worn it for fifteen years without noticing, because it fitted so well.

I pulled over at a rest stop, in the rain, and sat in the car for an hour with the engine off. A truck went by. I heard the driver humming. I listened to him until he was gone.

Then I took out my notebook, the one with the black cover, and turned to the inside cover, where I had written my rule in block letters at the age of eighteen. **NO ONE IS APPROACHED BY SURPRISE.**

I crossed it out. Underneath it, in my small, untidy hand, I wrote:

**ASK. THEN LISTEN. THEN LEAVE THE EGG.**

It does not scan. It is the best I have done.


## 21. The Leak

It came out in pieces, the way a flood does, and for a long time none of us could tell which piece was the water.

The first piece was the manuscript. I have said that Stone rejected it in 2011, on grounds he described as *insufficient validation*, and that I had always assumed it would reappear. It reappeared three times. Each time it had been improved a little, as a tooth is improved by the dentist, and each time a different set of reviewers had found, with a different set of sighs, that the unannotated region was probably an artifact. In the spring of 2035, a fourth journal, one that did not ask hard questions, printed it. It ran to eleven pages. Its authors, three men and a woman who had by then lost most of their hair, thanked their funders and their families and, at the very bottom, in a sentence that no one read for months, a *dedicated reviewer.*

The second piece was a clinic audit. A county, in a state I will not name, had reorganized its health services, and a junior accountant, whose name I never learned, was sent to a basement to reconcile the old logs. In a ledger of facility hours, between the cleaning schedule and the freezer maintenance, she found a column for after-hours use of an incubator. It was the same four hours, every week, for eleven years, signed by the same initials. She went to her supervisor. Her supervisor said that it was probably a research arrangement. She wrote a memo anyway, because she was the sort of person who writes memos. The memo went to the wrong office, then the right one.

The third piece was a journalist. She was a patient woman in her forties, with a cardigan and a laptop and a three-year-old that she was raising on her own. She had spent a decade writing about pharmaceutical prices, and nobody had ever asked her to write about anything else. She read the manuscript on a Tuesday, the memo on a Thursday, and on the Sunday afternoon, at a kitchen table, with a cold cup of coffee and a sleeping child in the next room, she spread the two sheets side by side and wrote a single sentence.

*Somewhere, an unknown number of people may have been born with deliberately altered genomes, and no one knows who they are.*

It appeared on a Monday. By Monday evening it had been translated into forty languages. By Tuesday, it was the only thing anyone wanted to talk about.

I noticed one more thing, a week later, in a footnote, and it was the footnote that made me put down my tea.

The editor-in-chief of the fourth journal, the one that did not ask hard questions, sat on the advisory board of the Leaf Foundation. So did the county's new health commissioner, who had been appointed in January, and who had opened the basement ledgers in February *as part of a routine transparency review.* Neither of them was a villain. Both had been sent, by someone, a very pleasant letter.

I called Elias. He listened to all of it without a word.

"Of course," he said at last. "He isn't waiting for the world to find out. He's *scheduling* it."

* * *

I heard it first from my mother.

She telephoned at seven in the morning, which she never did. She said, "Did you see the news?" in the tone she used for deaths and for lottery numbers. I said I had not. She told me about the sentence, and the numbers, and the experts, and a woman in a suit saying that it was *a matter of the gravest concern.*

"It's all over the television," she said. "It's the only thing on. Your father's watching it in his undershirt."

"What does he say?"

"He says it's a lot of fuss over smart people." I heard my father in the background, mildly: *Well, it is.* My mother lowered her voice. "I said, Ray, you can't say that. They're saying these people were *made*. In a laboratory. Like, I don't know, like a cake."

"Do they say who?"

"They don't know who. That's what's so awful." She paused, and I heard the kettle begin to sing. "Danny, you were always smart. Isn't that funny? Isn't it funny, with all this going on, that I'd think of you."

I kept my voice level. I think I managed it.

"It's a coincidence, Mom," I said. "Lots of people are smart."

"Oh, I know." She laughed. "That's what I told your father."

* * *

The world had a month of fear and a season of procedures.

Employers began to ask. They said they were *updating the onboarding packet.* The packet now had a voluntary section, which was voluntary in the way that a handshake is. A saliva swab, offered in a small plastic tube, for *wellness purposes.* A company in the Midwest made a notice of it. A bank in the South announced that it would hire only people who had *consented to the sequencing of non-medical variants.* Within three weeks, thirty others had followed.

Schools were next. A district in the Northwest sent a letter to parents asking about *family history of exceptional ability.* Another district printed a flyer with a cartoon of a magnifying glass and the words *Know Your Child.* A mother in a suburb I had once lived near was filmed outside a school holding a sign that said **WE ARE NOT AFRAID.** She looked afraid.

An insurer launched a new product with a cheerful name and a long list of exclusions. A television station polled its viewers on whether people with enhanced abilities should be required to say so, and the answer, in a majority, was yes. The following week it polled them on whether such people should be *forbidden* to say so, so as not to cause distress, and the answer, in a majority, was also yes. Nobody remarked on the contradiction. People hold two views as easily as they hold two arms.

A campaign aired an advertisement, black and white, set to a single slow piano note. It showed a child's silhouette in a window and a line of text: *Who is she really?*

I watched it in the staff lounge. The technician with the nose ring had put it on. When it finished she said, to the television: "That's a lot of money to spend on a sad girl."

Julian, who by then was a senator with a campaign being assembled, gave a short statement from the steps of a building. He said that the nation must not give in to fear, that no child should be asked to prove what she was, and that he had full confidence in the ordinary decency of ordinary people. His polls rose three points. I listened to the recording twice. His pulse did not move.

On the third night there was a panel on television, and I watched it in the staff lounge with a sandwich, as a man watches a car accident through a very large window.

There were four of them in a studio the color of an expensive bruise, seated in a row under a banner that said **THE GENE GENIE: IS IT OUT OF THE BOTTLE?** The host was a handsome man in his fifties with the clenched teeth of someone who has been told to look concerned. On his left sat a retired quarterback who had written a book about *the science of winning,* and who had been introduced as *a leading voice on human potential.* Beside him was a bioethicist who spoke in sentences that began *On the one hand.* Then a woman from a think tank, who explained that the real issue was *brand trust,* and a man in a clerical collar who said that God had always seen to it that the clever were punished.

"Let's be clear," said the host. "Are we afraid of these people?"

"I'm not afraid of them," said the quarterback. "I just want a level playing field."

"On the one hand," said the bioethicist.

"They could be anyone," said the woman from the think tank. "Your neighbor. Your child's teacher. That's why I say we need a *conversation.*"

"A conversation about what?" said the host.

There was a pause of about a second and a half. It was the pause of four people discovering that they had come on to a program to talk about a thing none of them could define.

"About whether we should be having one," said the bioethicist, and the studio audience, who had been told to applaud at certain moments, applauded.

The technician with the nose ring had stopped eating. "This is how you know we're doomed," she said. "Nobody's lying. They're all *enjoying* it."

* * *

A woman asked me, on the third week, in the middle of a consultation.

She was seventy-one, in the examination room with her husband, for a follow-up on an episode they thought had been minor. I was taking her blood pressure. She looked up at me with a clear, appraising expression and said, quite loudly:

"Doctor, are you one of them?"

I should say that I had prepared for this. I had rehearsed it in the car and in the shower and once, absurdly, in front of the bathroom mirror, as I used to rehearse telling my parents. I had a number of answers. They were all excellent. I opened my mouth to give one.

Then I listened to her.

Her pulse was fast, but it was not the fast of fear. It was the fast of someone who has asked something she wants to hear denied. Her husband, who was holding her coat, was holding it a little too tightly. Under the question she had asked, I heard another one: *Please tell me this is nothing.*

I said, "I'm one of the ordinary kind, Mrs. Rennick. I've simply had good teachers."

She breathed out. Her husband relaxed his hands. "I knew it," she said, to him. "I told you."

I wrote her prescription. I did not look up. My hand shook slightly, and I thought that this was probably the truest thing I had said all day.

* * *

Mira called from Munich on a Friday. She sounded weary and rather amused.

"They have put me on a panel," she said. "A television panel. About the 'ethics of enhancement.' The producer said they needed a scientist, a priest, and a woman who has been doing phonetics for fifteen years, and I said that I was not a scientist and he said, close enough."

"Will you go?"

"I will not. I've said I'm indisposed." I heard her smile. "Jonas, who is also invited, has said he has a bridge to inspect."

"How's Lena?"

There was a pause. Mira's voice dropped. "Her teacher asked a girl in her class whether she could hear through walls. The girl said no, she'd have noticed. And then the teacher laughed, and Lena didn't. Lena just went very still, and looked at the wall." She breathed. "She's seven. She understood that something had changed, and she's decided that the safest thing is to be boring."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. She's brilliant at it." A beat. "I've never seen anyone so good at being boring."

I realized, as she spoke, that I was holding the phone so hard my fingers had gone white. I put it on the table. I could hear her voice quite well from there.

* * *

Stone saw it on the television in the care home.

I was not present. A nurse told me, a week later, in the corridor. She had been wheeling him through the day room when a news item came on, an expert in a bow tie explaining, with a pointer, how a genome is sequenced. Stone had turned his head. He watched for eleven minutes, the nurse said, without moving, and then he had said one word, very clearly, which she did not understand.

"What was the word?" I said.

"*Slowly.*" She frowned. "Or maybe *Soon.* He said it in a sort of whisper. I don't think he knew he'd said it."

I stood in the corridor with my hands in my pockets and my eyes on the floor. I knew which word he had meant. I knew it as one knows one's own name. It was the word he had written in capitals at the top of his schedule, the one instruction he had given himself for the century. The expert in the bow tie had said *a population-level effect,* and Stone had looked at a screen and seen, perhaps for the first time in four years, a figure he recognized.

* * *

Elias was called to testify in February.

It was a committee of a legislature, in a large room with wood on the walls and a flag in the corner. They called him because he was the most visible young scientist in the country and because he had, the previous year, been asked in an interview whether he thought he was *unusual.* He had said, *Hardly.* The remark had been remembered.

I went. I sat at the back, in a row of strangers, in a coat I did not take off. I could hear everything, and I wished that I could not.

He sat at a table with a glass of water and a microphone. He wore the gray cardigan he had worn at twenty-two, under a blazer, as if the blazer were a courtesy. He looked very small. The chairman, a courteous man with a white mustache, thanked him for coming and asked, gently, as one asks an elderly relative whether they would like to sit, whether he had ever been tested.

"No, sir," said Elias.

"And would you object to a test?"

"I would," said Elias. "I would object in principle to the idea that a man who has done nothing wrong should be asked to prove it by giving up his blood. But I do not, I think, need to object in practice, because I'm quite sure it would show nothing."

I heard the lie. It was a small one, the size of a pea, and he said it perfectly. His voice did not waver. It was a very slightly lower voice than the one he used with me, a courtesy to the room. But in the second half of that sentence, on the word *nothing,* there was an eighth of a tone, no more, that did not belong.

"Dr. Elias," said a woman on the committee, "you won the highest prize in your field at twenty-nine. The world is asking how."

He took a sip of water. He set the glass down.

"I was lucky," he said. "I worked on a problem at the right time. I had good teachers. I was in a position, one afternoon, to see something that three other people could have seen." He paused. "I'd be grateful if people did not make too much of me. It's a tiresome thing to be a miracle. I'm only a man who was fortunate."

The room listened with its heart in its mouth. It was a very good answer. It was believed. People wanted so much to be told that there was no secret.

He looked up, just for a moment, toward the back of the room. His eyes found mine. I do not think he could have said how. I did not move or nod, and I did not blink. But I let him see that I had heard.

It cost him. I have known him fourteen years, and I know what it cost. It took something out of his voice that has never entirely come back, a sort of ease, as when a pianist loses the use of one finger and compensates so well that only a few people ever know.

* * *

Everything changed and nothing did.

The employers kept their tubes. The schools kept their letters. The insurer sold, in its first quarter, a good many policies. But no one was found. Months went by. No one in the entire country was shown, by a test, to be anything but ordinary, because the people who could have been shown were not taking the tests.

The hearings produced experts, who disagreed. A geneticist said that the fragment in the manuscript was *consistent with a number of explanations,* including contamination, which she described as *probable.* A philosopher said the real question was how we would treat the people if they existed. A bishop said we would all, in the end, be judged by the same standard. A man from a think tank said, with a graph, that the likelihood of anyone having been made in secret was *below the threshold of policy relevance.* The graph was well received.

The comfortable explanation spread, as the comfortable explanation does. It was a hoax, said some. A fantasy, said others. A rumor that had run away from its owner, like a dog. A sensible man could believe all of it and still go to work.

And the ones of us who had been afraid in the first weeks discovered, in the second month, that we were beginning to be glad.

Not glad of what had happened. Glad of what had not. We said so rarely, and only to one another, on the telephone, at night, in the short sentences that people use when they are afraid that someone is listening. *Nothing's happened.* *No, nothing.* *Good.* It became a sort of liturgy. I said it to Mira, who said it to the florist, who said it to the surveyor, and I knew that it was going round the whole of us, a little warm light, from kitchen to kitchen.

I did not tell my parents. I went to dinner on a Sunday in March, and my father said grace, which he did not usually do, and my mother passed me the potatoes.

"You're quiet," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

I looked at the two of them, at the table where I had eaten for more than thirty years, in the light from the lamp my father had mended, with a calendar from a bakery supplier on the wall.

"About how lucky I am," I said.

My mother beamed. My father said, mildly, "We all are, son," and went on with his dinner.

It was true. That was the worst of it. It was entirely true.


## 22. The Draft

I wrote it on a Monday, in the kitchen, in one sitting, and it was the best thing I ever wrote.

I want to say that first, so you know what you are up against. It was clear. It was calm. It had the sort of sentences that people quote at funerals. I had been writing since I could hold a pencil, and I had never once managed to make a hard thing sound as reasonable as that one did.

It began like this.

*My name is Daniel Pruitt. I am a physician. I was born on the first of January, 2000, with a genome that had been altered before my birth without the knowledge of my parents. I am one of a number of people in this country and elsewhere to whom this happened. I did not choose to say so. I am saying so now because there is a man who intends to use what was done to us, and I believe the public has a right to know who he is.*

It went on for eleven hundred words. It named the clinic. It named a doctor, now deceased in all but fact, and a program with a green leaf. It named a senator, and it set out, in careful and verifiable sentences, what the senator had done: the building, the screenings, the foundation, the pages in the glass case. It did not name Mira. It did not name Elias, or the florist, or the surveyor, or a woman in a field with a goat. I had been very careful about that. It named only me, and him.

It ended: *I am not asking to be believed. I am asking to be tested. A single sample will settle it. I will give mine today.*

I read it through four times. I had the odd feeling you get when you have hit a note exactly right on an instrument you have never played, and you know it will never happen again.

I did not notice what it was.

* * *

You will want to know why I did it, and I want to be fair to the man I was. He had good reasons. I list them because they were good.

The first was that Julian was winning. It was the spring of 2036, and he had taken seven of the first nine primaries by margins that made commentators say *historic* and then, on a second take, *inevitable.* He had a smile that people described as *honest.* He had a record of kindness that anyone could cite. He had a wife, now, a pleasant, pale, rather sad woman who ran a literacy charity and had a gift for appearing wherever the cameras were, and he had a dog.

The second was the screenings. By then the Leaf Foundation ran eleven hundred of them a month, in nine states, in clinics with tree murals and smoothie bars. They had tested, by my own estimate, a hundred thousand children. They had found, by my own estimate, a few hundred of us. I do not know what they did with the ones they found. I know that three of them had been offered *enrichment programs,* and that I had met two of those three, and that they were happy.

The third was Wren.

Wren was twenty-three and had been born in the spring of 2013. I had first met her in the autumn of 2035, when she walked into the cafeteria of my hospital on a Tuesday and sat down across from me, with a tray of fries and the clear, steady look of a girl who had once waved at me from a sandpit.

"I'd like to know," she said.

"Know what?"

"What I am." She ate a fry. "I traced the clinic from the audit. I found the program, and the list, and I found you. You're the one with the rule about not approaching people." She raised one eyebrow. "I thought I'd save you the trouble."

I had told her. I had asked first, and then I had listened, and then I had told her everything. She heard it with a calm that frightened me. At the end she said, "Right. Good. Thank you." And then, after a minute, "I don't want to hide."

It was not what the quiet ones wanted. I had known it would be a problem, one day, as one knows that a house on a slope will one day need a wall. Wren wanted to be known. She wanted to stand in a room and say *this is what I am,* with her whole face. She was twenty-three and had never been told she should not.

In March she called me, at four in the morning, from a gala dinner in a ballroom in the capital, where the Leaf Foundation had invited her as *one of our young listeners.* She was whispering.

"Danny. He's got a plan."

"Julian?"

"He doesn't know I can hear him. He's in the next room with a man in a gray suit. He's going to say it at the convention. In July. He's going to stand up in front of everyone and tell them he's one of us. He's going to call it the *Dignity Initiative.* He says it's the only way to take the fear out of it before the other side can use it." She was breathing very fast. "And then, Danny, the second part. He's going to say they'll *finish the work.* In the open. With a government laboratory, and a proper ethics board, and people like us on the staff. He says nobody's ever done it properly."

I did not say anything. I stood in my kitchen in the dark, with the phone against my ear, and I heard, as clearly as if I had been in the ballroom, the shape of the sentence he would say. *What was done in secret, we will finish in the open.*

"He asked me to stand beside him," said Wren. "On the stage. He said I'd be the face of it."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd think about it." A pause. "Danny, I want to say yes. I hate that I want to say yes."

It was the third reason, and it was the only one that mattered. He was going to do it, and he was going to do it with a state behind him, and I was the only person on earth who could read the pages he had under glass.

* * *

I called Mira on the Tuesday.

I did what I thought was the right thing. I did not send it. I read it to her, the whole eleven hundred words, in the voice I use for an unwelcome result, and when I had finished there was a long silence on the line, and in the silence, as I had half expected, a child practicing the recorder, with great determination, a quarter tone flat.

"It's very good," said Mira.

"Thank you."

"It's the best thing you've ever written." Her voice was perfectly steady. "Danny. It's the card again."

"It's not the card."

"It's the card. It's the drawer. It is a decision about all of us, made in a kitchen, by one man, and presented to us as a favor." She breathed. "Not a word of it names anyone but you. I noticed that. You think that makes it yours. It doesn't. The minute you put a single sample of that genome into a laboratory, you've put every one of us in the same room. You *know* that. You've been the cleverest person I know for seventeen years."

"He's going to restart it."

"I know."

"He's going to do it with a government."

"I *know.*" She was very quiet. "Then we'll fight him. All of us. We'll do it in a room, with the florist and the surveyor and a very large pot of coffee, and we'll argue until four in the morning. It will take us a week. It will be unbearable. And at the end of it, either we'll agree, or we won't, and you'll know."

"There isn't time."

"There is exactly as much time," said Mira, "as you decide to give us."

I did not answer.

"I'm asking you," she said, "as the person you hurt the most. Ask us."

"I'll think about it."

"Don't think about it. *Ask.*" She stopped. When she spoke again it was in a different voice, small and level, the voice of a woman at a window. "I won't speak to you if you do this. I mean it. I've been angry before, and I got over it. I don't think I'd get over this."

I had never heard her say a thing she did not mean. I knew it, standing there with my ears full of her breath. I knew it as I knew my own name.

"I'll call you on Thursday," I said.

"Please do."

I put the phone down. I looked at the screen. It was a white page, with eleven hundred words in a plain black font, and a button that said **SCHEDULE.**

* * *

I took it to Elias on the Wednesday.

He read it standing at the whiteboard, with a marker in one hand. He had grown older in the way that very bright young men do, suddenly and in the eyes. He read it twice. When he had finished he laid the marker in the tray.

"If you send it," he said, "I'll stand beside you."

"You'd have to say it was true."

"I know." He looked at the floor. "I've told one lie in my life, and I've been paying for it for a year. I'd rather pay for the other thing." He lifted his head. "But I'd rather you asked them first."

"Mira said that."

"Mira is usually right." He gave a thin smile. "It's an unbearable quality in a person."

"Wren says publish."

"Wren is twenty-three," said Elias. "She thinks being brave is the same as being right. It's wonderful. I used to think so myself." He picked the marker up again. "Danny. What are you afraid of?"

I thought about it. I thought, truthfully, for the length of a breath.

"That I'll be the one who didn't."

"Who didn't what?"

"Who knew," I said, "and didn't."

"Ah," said Elias, quietly. "That's not the same as being right either."

* * *

That night I sat in the kitchen with the laptop open and the light off.

I could hear the whole building. In 4C, where the widow had died twenty years earlier, a young couple were making love, very quietly and with great concentration. In the stairwell, someone was carrying a bicycle.

I thought about Stone.

I thought about a man at a bench at eleven minutes past midnight, with a card in his hand, certain that the world was better off if he went ahead and did it. I thought about how good his reasons had been. They had been the best reasons I had ever heard. A child who died at eleven months. A window that had stayed shut. A spreadsheet of the ways in which we fail.

*I'll ask them afterward,* he had said. *When it's done. It's only fair to ask when it's done.*

I did not hear him say it. I have no way of knowing that he did. I only know that I sat in the dark and I heard it, in a voice that was very like my own, and that the voice was quite reasonable.

I set the cursor over the button.

I had promised Mira I would call on Thursday. I scheduled the statement for six o'clock on Friday morning, so that she would have a day, and I told myself that this was fairness. I told myself that it was a margin. A man who has decided to be reasonable can always find a margin.

It said: **SCHEDULED. FRIDAY 6:00 A.M. TO 41 RECIPIENTS.**

I closed the laptop. I went to bed. I slept, to my lasting shame, extremely well.

On Thursday morning I went to work.


## 23. Duck and Rabbit

I heard it on the radio, in a kitchen, on the second Thursday of April, 2036.

The kitchen belonged to the staff lounge of the hospital where I worked, a narrow room with a kettle, a microwave that had been out of order since the previous summer, and a window that looked at an air-conditioning unit. I had come off a twelve-hour shift. I had a cup of tea that I had not yet drunk and a sandwich I had no intention of eating. A technician was washing a mug at the sink with the radio on, low, tuned to the station that played traffic and weather and, between them, a kind of anxious, well-spoken news.

The announcer's voice changed in the middle of a sentence. I want to describe that exactly, because it was the first thing I heard, before the words. A voice that has been reading a script has a particular pace, a settled sort of rhythm, like a person walking on a path they know. Then it stops. The breath comes in unevenly. The next word arrives as if from a long way off.

*We are receiving reports,* said the announcer, *of a shooting at a campaign event in*—and she named a county, and then a state—*where Senator Julian*—and she said the name.

The technician at the sink turned the tap off.

I put the cup down. I remember that it made a small, precise click against the counter, and that I heard it twice: once from the cup and once from the window, where the sound came back from the air-conditioning unit, a quarter of a second late.

* * *

I should say what Julian had become.

He was thirty-six, the same age as I was. He was the front-runner. The word in the newspapers was *unstoppable*, which journalists use when they are tired of being surprised. He had been a state representative at twenty-four, a senator at thirty, and had spent six years being so reasonable that people had begun to believe it was a policy position. He talked about the dignity of ordinary people. He talked about the future in the tone that other people use for the weather. When he laughed, a crowd laughed a half-second afterward, as if it had been given permission.

He had never, in all those years, said a word to me that I could have repeated to a stranger. He sent a card every Christmas. *Interesting how some of us are lucky. J.* I kept them in a drawer with the rock.

I will not pretend I loved him. I will say that for twenty years I had listened to a man whose pulse was as steady as a clock, and had been unable to hear anything behind it. That is its own kind of knowledge. I had come to believe that he was the only person I had ever met who did not need to lie, because he had no gap between what he said and what he was. It was the most frightening thing I knew.

The announcer said that the senator had been taken to a hospital. She said it twice. Then there was a silence of the kind that only a live broadcast can produce, in which someone is reading something that has not yet been written, and then she said, in a voice with all the weight gone out of it, that the senator had died.

* * *

He was shot, I learned over the next hours, at a rope line in a field.

It was one of those events that a campaign calls *a conversation with voters.* There was a stage with bunting, and a row of folding chairs, and a hay bale placed for effect. The senator had been shaking hands. A man in a camouflage jacket had stepped out from behind a parked truck, and that was nearly all anyone could say for certain, because a crowd that sees a thing like that sees a hundred different things.

The shooter was fifty-four. He lived alone in a cabin in the next county. For nine years he had written letters to a great many newspapers about the destruction of a particular wetland, a pipeline that was to be laid through it, and the inattention of everyone he had written to. The letters were long, and numbered, and precise. A neighbor told a reporter that he was *quiet and a bit odd, but he fed the birds.* A man at the post office said he had recently been mailing a lot of packages. A woman who ran the local feed store said, to a camera, in a voice that had no particular inflection: *He's an environmentalist. Wouldn't hurt a fly.*

He owned four rifles. This was established by the evening. By the next morning it was established that the one he had used was what the reporters called *a semiautomatic of the most advanced kind*, and that he had bought it legally, eleven months earlier, from a shop in his own county. The owner of the shop, a stout and unhappy man in a plaid shirt, was interviewed on the sidewalk outside his store. He said that the paperwork had been in order. He said the background check had come back clean. He said that the customer had been polite.

The reporters wanted to know the stated purpose of the purchase. It was a question on the form. Someone had obtained a copy. It was shown on television, in a close-up, with a red circle drawn round one line by a graphics department that had been busy that morning.

In the box marked *intended use* he had written, in a neat and rounded hand:

*Hunting ducks and rabbits.*

I watched that close-up in the staff lounge, on a small television bolted to the wall, with a half-eaten sandwich in front of me. I watched it seven times. After the third, the technician at the sink turned the volume down. After the fifth, she said, to no one: "Ducks."

I said, "And rabbits."

She looked at me. She was a young woman, with a nose ring and a blunt, honest face, and she did not smile. She said: "That's how it is. Isn't it."

* * *

I felt three things.

I have tried since to put them in order, and I cannot, because they came together, one on top of another, in a heap. The first was relief. It was physical. A muscle in my back, which I had not known was clenched, let go all at once, like a knot cut with a knife. The second was guilt, and it came a half-second later, not as an idea but as an ache, in the same place. I had spent twenty years refusing to say what I knew about that man, and now a stranger with a grievance and a legal rifle had done what I would not, and what I would have been ashamed to want.

The third thing I did not recognize. It had no name. It was a kind of vertigo: the sense of a floor that had always been there, and that I had been holding up, and that was no longer mine to hold.

I did not tell anyone. I finished the tea. It was cold. I washed the cup.

* * *

At home I opened the laptop.

I did not mean to. I meant to go to bed. But it was there, on the screen, where I had left it on Wednesday: eleven hundred words in a plain black font, and above them a small gray line of text. **SCHEDULED. FRIDAY 6:00 A.M. TO 41 RECIPIENTS.**

I read the first sentence. *My name is Daniel Pruitt.*

And I heard, quite suddenly and very clearly, what I had written. It was the same sentence. It was a different man's. It was a man at a bench at eleven minutes past midnight, calm, correct and entirely sure that he was acting for everyone's good, who has never in his life asked anyone's permission, and who intends to explain himself afterward.

I had been, for three days, Frank Stone.

I had not noticed. That was what I could not get past. I had been so careful. I had left out Mira and Elias and the florist. I had written it in the clearest and kindest prose I knew. And the voice that had told me to send it was the voice I had spent twenty-five years learning to recognize in another man, and it had sounded, from the inside, exactly like conscience.

I selected the whole thing. I deleted it. I emptied the folder. Then, because I did not trust myself, I deleted the backup, and the draft of the draft, and a note to myself that said *tell Wren,* and sat looking at a blank screen with the odd, light, sick feeling of being pulled back from a ledge by someone who did not know you were there.

I called Mira. It was past midnight in Munich, and she answered on the second ring, wide awake.

"I deleted it," I said.

"Good." She let out a long breath. "I've been sitting by the phone since the news. Elias sent me four messages. I knew you would be on your own." She was quiet. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. A man with a rifle did it."

"No." She said it very gently. "You'd have asked me at midnight. I know you. You'd have called, and you'd have heard me breathe, and you'd have stopped."

"I don't think so."

"I do." She paused. "It doesn't matter which of us is right. That's the point of asking. Nobody gets to know what you'd have done. You get to find out."

I could not answer. I held the phone in the dark, and listened to a woman I had known for seventeen years breathing, in an apartment, in a city, on a night when a man had died, and I understood that this, more than the shot, was the thing I would carry.

* * *

Elias called that night. He was in a hotel, in a city where it was three in the morning, and his voice was shaking in a way I had never heard.

"I didn't want it," he said. "You know I didn't want it."

"I know."

"I never wanted anything to happen to him. I just wanted him to go away." He took a breath. "Then why am I *glad?*"

I told him I did not know, which was true. He wept, in the quiet, dignified way that he did everything. I sat on the edge of my bed in the dark and listened to a man I had known for fourteen years cry in a hallway on another continent, and I could hear, quite clearly, in the sound of his weeping, the same three things that I had felt: the relief, the guilt, and the floor.

After a minute he stopped. We sat on the line without speaking. I do not know how long. It was long enough that I could hear his hotel's air system cycle on and off, and he could hear mine.

At last he said, in his small, precise voice, "I'm going to say I was shocked."

"You were."

"Yes." A pause. "I was."

* * *

There was a funeral, of course, the following Tuesday. It lasted six hours and was broadcast on every channel. A great many people spoke. I watched most of it, in the lounge, with the sound low.

I listened to the eulogists, one after another. A governor, two former presidents, a woman who had been his teacher, a priest. I listened to each of their pulses, as I do. They were all, in their own way, afraid. They were afraid of saying the wrong thing, of saying the right thing too well, of the cameras. One of them, a tall man with a cracked voice, was grieving, and I could tell by how his breath caught, like a coat on a nail.

His wife stood at the front in a plain black coat, with the dog's lead wound twice around her hand. She did not weep either. I listened to her pulse, and I heard, for the first time in my life, a person grieving for a man I had never been able to hear.

I did not hear anyone lie. That surprised me. I had expected a good deal of it, and there was almost none. They said that he had been kind, that he had been gifted, that he had been a man of rare warmth. Each of them believed it, because each of them had been present in the room while he was being kind, and warm, and gifted, and had felt it. That was the thing I had never been able to explain to anyone. He had not lied to them. He had not needed to.

In the last minute of the broadcast they played a clip from his final speech, the one he had given at the rope line, minutes before. His voice came through the television's small speaker, bright and unhurried, saying that the future belonged to ordinary people. The crowd laughed. I heard the half-second lag.

I turned it off.

* * *

There was a hearing, in the end, about the rifle. It lasted eleven days. The committee heard from the shop owner, a regulator, two professors, and a lobbyist for an association of people who hunted. Each of them said that the law had been followed. Each said it in a different tone. A senator on the committee, a woman I had once met at a conference, said that she wished to put on the record that the shooter had not broken a single law until the moment he fired. This was reported as a rebuke. I could not tell whom it was meant for.

Nothing was changed. I am not saying this in judgment. I am saying it as one reports the weather. The nation observed a week of mourning, a month of argument, a season of ads, and then a new thing happened, somewhere else, and its attention went over to that, like a flock of birds moving to another field.

The shooter said, at his arraignment, that he had not intended to hurt anyone in particular. He said he had wanted to be heard. He said he had always felt sorry for the rabbits.

No one connected Julian to anything I had known. No one had a reason to. In the whole of the country there were perhaps forty-one people who could have said what he was, and they were all in my notebook. They kept it, as the saying goes, to themselves.

* * *

We met in Munich in June.

It was Mira's idea. She said we ought to see each other's faces once, in the same room, without any reason. She sent a message with eleven words in it, and a date, and an address, and within a week the whole of what I called the quiet ones had answered. Not all. Perhaps thirty. They came from Ohio and Recife, from a farmhouse in Wales and an apartment in Osaka. The florist brought tulips. The violinist brought a violin and played nothing. The man who built a boat arrived by train with a duffel bag and said that he did not like airplanes.

They sat around a long table in the room where Jonas kept his drawings, under a lamp with a green shade that Mira had bought for the occasion. There were four casseroles. There was a bread that Jonas had made himself and a cake that Lena, now eight, had decorated with raisins in a pattern I recognized as the spacing of a cuckoo clock's strike.

I had expected an agenda. I had written one. I put it in my pocket and did not take it out.

For a long time nobody said anything. We ate. At some point the violinist, who was a gentle, broad-faced woman in her forties, said: "It's strange. I keep wanting to apologize to somebody."

"For what?" said Mira.

"I don't know." She looked at her plate. "I'm glad it's over."

There was a silence. The man who built the boat put down his fork. The two women who had lived on the same street all their lives looked at one another, and one of them, who had hardly spoken, said in a low voice: "I'm glad too. God forgive me."

"Nobody has to be sorry," said Mira. She said it plainly, and with a certain dryness, and I thought: *she was right to bring us here.* "He's dead. We didn't do it. We'd have been in a very bad place if he had lived. We can be glad and sorry both. People manage."

"Nobody says it out loud," said Elias.



"We just did," said Mira.

"Then why are we hiding?" said a voice from the far end of the table.

It was Wren. She had flown in from Chicago with a backpack and a very large umbrella, and had spent the first hour of the evening eating bread with the concentration of a girl who has been told she is not allowed to speak. She was twenty-three, with a sharp pale face and a short ragged haircut, and she looked at the elders around the table with the fond contempt of the young.

"I don't mean to be rude," she said. "I'm grateful. I've been very well fed. But he's dead. The man who was going to out us is dead, and the rest of you are sitting here saying how nice it is that nobody knows. I've never had a day of my life when somebody didn't know *something* about me and I wasn't allowed to say." She looked down the table. "I don't want to hide. I'd like to say my name."

There was a silence.

"Then say it," said Mira.

Wren blinked. "What?"

"Say it. Nobody here is going to stop you." Mira put down her fork. "And nobody here is going to follow you unless they want to. That's the only rule, and it took us nine years to learn it, and one of us very nearly forgot it last week." She did not look at me. "You get to choose. So does everyone at this table. So did the man in the field, who isn't here, and the man with the tractor, who's on the phone."

"The man with the tractor?" said Wren.

"Theo." Mira smiled, a little crookedly. "He called me this afternoon. He's read his mother's letters. All hundred and ninety-three. It took him four days. He's gone home."

I put my hand over my eyes. I do not often cry in company. I let the table think it was the lamp.

* * *

We decided nothing. This was, I think, the point.

It was agreed, by a general murmur, that we would continue to be what we were: a loose, unnamed network of people who could call one another at three in the morning. It was agreed that nobody would be tested, and that nobody would be asked to. It was agreed that those who wished to be visible would be visible on their own terms, and that those who did not would be left alone. The florist asked whether we should have a name. There was a pause. Jonas said, with a straight face, that it ought to be something dull. After some thought, we settled on none.

Late in the evening Lena came and sat on my knee. She was a solemn child. She asked in German whether it was true that a man had died. I said that it was. She asked if it was sad. I said that it was complicated.

"That's what grown-ups say when they mean they don't know," said Lena.

"Yes."

She considered this. Then she put her head against my chest, as children do, to listen, and I felt her go still. She was hearing my heart.

"It's going fast," she said.

"Is it?"

"Faster than the clock." She frowned. "Are you afraid?"

I thought about it, as I had learned to do.

"A little," I said. "Not of anything bad. I think I'm afraid because nothing has to happen now."

"Why is that frightening?"

"Because then," I said, "it's up to us."

She nodded gravely, as though this were quite ordinary, which, to her, it was. She slid down from my knee and went to find the cake.


## 24. The Four Minutes

I gave him the cure in October of 2036, in a room with a view of a parking lot.

It was not the cure I had imagined as a boy. I had imagined a single event: a syringe, a bright light, a man sitting up in bed and saying my name. Medicine does not work that way, and neither does he. It was a course of treatments over eleven weeks, one every second day, each of them taking about forty minutes and requiring a nurse, a pump, and a patience that I did not know I had. It did not rebuild anything. It asked what was left to wake up, and then it waited.

I should explain what I mean by *I gave him*, because it was not simple, and because the word *gave* has a long and not entirely innocent history in this account.

The molecule was not mine alone. It was the work of eleven people in four countries, and eight years, and a good deal of luck. I had been one of the eleven. My name was fourth on the paper, which my mother thought was a scandal, and which I thought was exactly right. When the trials ended and the regulators had signed what they sign, the treatment became available to anyone who qualified. Stone qualified on every criterion except one: the time since the event. Twenty-four years was a very long way outside the window. The protocol said, in the dry language of such documents, that benefit could not be expected.

So I did what I had told myself I would never do. I made an application for compassionate use, in the name of a patient identified only by a number, and I signed it as the treating physician. I wrote that he had no family, which was true. I wrote that his function was severely impaired, which was true. I wrote that the treatment was unlikely to cause harm, which was true, and that it might help.

I did not write *because I want to hear him speak.* There is no box for that.

* * *

He was eighty-six. I had been visiting him for twenty years, in a succession of chairs by a succession of windows, and had never once heard him say anything that mattered. It is a strange thing to confide in a man who cannot reply. For years I told him what I could not tell anyone else: that I had found Mira, that Elias had won his prize, that Julian had taken his seat, that the genomes were out and the children were running around in German. He listened with his one good eye. Sometimes he looked at the window. I believe now that he understood more than I gave him credit for, and less than I hoped.

On the first day of treatment he was awake and looked at the pump with deep suspicion.

On the fourth day, he said "water."

I was not in the room. A nurse told me, and I made her say it twice. She was a kind, brisk woman called Rosalind, who had worked in that building for nineteen years and had seen everything come and go. She said: "He said it as clearly as you or I would." I asked her what his voice sounded like. She thought about it. "Like someone who'd been out in the cold," she said.

On the ninth day, he said my name.

He did not say it with feeling. He said it as one says a word one has been looking for in a drawer. *Danny.* Then, after a pause, with an irritable and unmistakable correction: "Daniel."

I said, "It's Danny. It's always been Danny."

He frowned, as though he had been handed the wrong form. Then something in his face gave way, and I saw the man who had stood in my mother's examination room thirty-six years earlier and said, to the baby in the carrier, *hello.*

* * *

What came back was patchy.

He could tell me the names of the nurses. He could not tell me what he had eaten at breakfast, or whether he had eaten. He remembered the color of the card he had been holding on the day of the stroke, white, with a green line, and could not remember the year. He remembered, in extraordinary detail, a night in April 1999: the sound of the refrigerator coming on, the technician's laugh, the red light on the incubator that he had once thought, absurdly, looked like a small, patient eye.

He could not remember the index.

I had expected this, and I was unprepared for it. In the eighth week, when he could hold a pen again in his right hand, I brought him one of the four copies. I had decided that he had the right to see it, if he wanted. The originals, the ones from the cabinet, I had asked for in May. The foundation, leaderless and embarrassed, had a new chair, a retired judge, who handed them over without reading them, in a box marked *Misc.* They are in a bank now, with a lock I do not believe in. This was one of my own four copies, from the day I was eleven. I put the pages on the tray across his lap, in the careful order I had kept them in for twenty-five years, and I watched his face.

He looked at the first page for a long time.

"Whose handwriting is that?" he said.

"Yours."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "It's very neat." He turned a page, and another, and his lips moved. He was reading, I think, the way a man reads a language he studied in school, one word at a time, with great labor and no pleasure. "I don't know what any of this is."

"It's what you did," I said. "To them. To me."

He turned another page. Then he put his good hand flat on top of the stack, as one lays a hand on a sleeping animal, and left it there.

"I'd like you to have it," he said.

"I have it."

"Then keep it." He looked up at me. His eyes were pale and wet and quite clear. "I don't think I'd be good with it now."

It was the nearest thing to an apology that I ever heard him make. It was not enough, and it was not nothing, and I have learned over the years to be able to hold two such things in the same hand.

* * *

A girl called Wren has asked whether she may meet him. She is twenty-three. She says she would like to know what he looks like. I have told her that she may, when he is ready, and that I will ask him first.

On the second to last day of treatment, I brought him a newspaper.

It was a Tuesday. I had been saving it, for no reason I could have defended, in a folder in my bag. The item was on the fourth page, below the weather, in a column of international notes that most people skip. It was two sentences long.

*A research group in China has announced that it has arrived, independently, at a method of heritable genome editing in human embryos. It said the work was published openly and in accordance with its national guidelines.*

Stone read it three times, moving his lips.

I watched the light change in the window while he did. The parking lot had a tree in it, a small hard maple that had never grown properly, and in October its leaves went an angry, brilliant red. I counted them without meaning to. Eighty-one that I could see.

At last he looked up. For the first time since 2012 his eyes had the old dryness in them, the ghost of the man who timed everything.

"I was not," he said slowly, "the only one."

"No."

"I always thought," he said, "that I would be the first." He gave a short, breathy sound that might have been a laugh. "I wasn't even that."

I said that I did not think it mattered who was first.

He considered this. It took a long time. The red in the window crept a little higher on the wall.

"Is it good," he said, "or bad?"

I thought of Lena on the balcony, her head tilted, listening to a clock in another house. I thought of my father's palm, dry and rough, in a hospital corridor. I thought of Elias, crying in a hallway over a thing he could not name. I thought of a form signed in April 1999, with one procedure on it, and a blue-ink line at the bottom in my mother's hand.

"I don't know," I said. "I'm asking you."

He did not answer. He lifted a trembling hand and laid it flat on the notebook that lay beside him on the tray, and kept it there. Down the corridor a cart went past with a rattling wheel. I counted eleven seconds before it did, and I smiled. He was looking at me.

"You heard that," he said.

"I always do."

"Of course." He nodded. "That was the point."

* * *

My father was sixty-six that autumn, and he asked to come.

I do not know how he knew. I had told him only that I was seeing an old patient of the clinic who had been kind to us, and that the man was unwell. He drove the forty minutes in his old truck, which had two hundred thousand miles on it and a bakery supplier's logo scraped off the door, and he parked it carefully across two spaces, because he never trusted himself to hit the line.

He wore his good jacket. He had cleaned his fingernails.

I took him down the corridor. I had been over it in my head a hundred times, and every version ended with the same four words, and in every version I did not say them. Outside the door of the room I stopped. I said: "Dad, I have something I've been meaning to tell you."

He looked at me for a while. It was a patient look. It was the look he gave to the engine of the truck, when it made a noise he did not recognize.

"Is it about him?" he said.

"It's partly about him."

"Is it going to change anything?"

I thought about that for longer than I wanted to. I could hear, through the door, the slow, uneven breathing of a man who was trying very hard to be awake.

"I don't know," I said. "It might change how you think about some things."

"But not about you."

"No."

"Then it can wait." He put his hand on my shoulder. His palm was heavy and dry. "You've been trying to tell me something since you were sixteen. I figured you'd get to it. I'm not going anywhere." He smiled. "I'm slow, not stupid."

I could not say anything.

We went in.

Stone was sitting up. He had combed his hair, or someone had, and he held the notebook on his knee like a hat. When he saw my father he went very still. I watched the muscle in his jaw. I heard his pulse, which had been steady for weeks, go up by eleven.

"Dr. Stone," my father said. He crossed the room, and held out a hand. "Ray Pruitt. You won't remember me. You helped us have our boy."

Stone took the hand. I saw him look at it, the big, scarred hand, the nails scrubbed to the quick. It lay in his own like something being returned.

"I remember," Stone said. His voice was a rasp. "I remember you."

"Good for you." My father laughed, a deep, easy sound. "I sure don't remember much of anything from back then. Just that you were good to us. Thank you, Doc. For everything."

Stone did not let go. He held that hand for a good while longer than anyone needed to. I have thought about it many times since. I do not believe it was theater. I think that when you are given a gift you have no right to, and are thanked for it, there is nothing at all you can say.

My father stood very patiently, and let him.

* * *

I stayed late. When my father had gone I sat in the plastic chair, the same chair, by the same window, and watched the red tree go gray in the dusk.

Stone was asleep. His breath had the soft catch in it that I had learned to listen for. The notebook lay open on his knee, and I could see, in the light from the corridor, the first line on the first page. It was in his small, upright hand. I read it as I have read it a hundred times.

*The window was four minutes and eleven seconds long.*

I had answered it so many times in my head that I was not sure any longer what the answer was. That evening, for the first time, I took the pen from his tray and wrote underneath it, in my own handwriting, which is neither neat nor upright.

*It was long enough.*

I looked at that for a while. Then I crossed out *enough* and wrote the word I had been looking for.

*It was long enough to begin. It was not long enough to decide. The rest has been ours.*

I put the pen down. It was nearly dark. I sat without moving, as I always do in the evenings, with all of my attention turned outward, and I listened to the building: the pump, the cart, a woman laughing two floors down, the refrigerator in the kitchenette coming on with its small, tired shudder.

It struck me, then, that I had never once been afraid of that sound.

I let it run.

# Appendix: The Science Behind the Story

This book is fiction, and its central scientific premise is invented. The rules of inheritance it uses are real. This appendix separates the two.

## 1. How inheritance works

DNA is a double helix: two strands twisted around each other, held together by pairs of chemical "letters." James Watson and Francis Crick proposed this structure in [a two-page paper in 1953](https://doi.org/10.1038/171737a0), building on X-ray images made by others, notably Rosalind Franklin. Her best-known image, "Photo 51," was shown to Watson by a colleague without her knowledge. Franklin died in 1958, aged 37, and was not among the three men who shared the [1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1962/summary/) for the discovery. That history of credit, and of whose data was used with or without consent, is a quiet background to this book's own questions.

Every person has two copies of most genes, one from each parent. A version of a gene is called an *allele*. A person who carries a particular variant on one chromosome copy only is *heterozygous* for it. A person who carries it on both copies is *homozygous*.

When a heterozygous parent has a child, each egg or sperm cell carries one of the parent's two copies, chosen by chance. So each child has a **50 percent chance** of receiving the variant from that parent. That is the rule behind Stone's design: every insert travels by a coin toss.

**One carrier and one non-carrier.** Each child has a 50 percent chance of carrying the variant (heterozygous) and a 50 percent chance of not.

**Two carriers of the same variant.** The four equally likely combinations are shown below. A child has a 25 percent chance of carrying none, a 50 percent chance of carrying one copy, and a 25 percent chance of carrying two. This is why the book's Munich couple matter: when both parents carry the same variant, a child can receive a double dose.

| | From parent 2: variant | From parent 2: no variant |
|---|---|---|
| **From parent 1: variant** | two copies (25%) | one copy (25%) |
| **From parent 1: no variant** | one copy (25%) | no copy (25%) |

**Several variants at once.** If the variants sit on different chromosomes, they are inherited independently, and the chances multiply. The chance that a child receives all of ten independent variants from one carrier parent is one in 1,024. Variants close together on the same chromosome tend to travel together, because chromosomes are inherited in large pieces, though pieces are shuffled at each generation. This is why a full "package" is unlikely to pass intact to one child, and why the story treats reassembly as a matter of odds.

## 2. Germline and body cells

Editing a fertilized egg or early embryo changes cells that will become every tissue, including the cells that make eggs and sperm (the *germline*). That is why such a change is inherited, and why it is the most controversial kind. An edit in a body cell (a *somatic* edit) affects only that person and is not passed on.

In real life, early-embryo editing has a known difficulty called *mosaicism*: some cells carry the change and others do not. It also risks unintended changes elsewhere in the genome. The story passes over these problems, because Stone's work is secret and the narrator does not describe it.

## 3. Gene technology

Gene editing means making a targeted change to DNA. The best-known tool, CRISPR-Cas9, was described in [a 2012 paper in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1225829), and its developers Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier received the [2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2020/summary/). The first reported use of CRISPR on human embryos, [in 2015 in China](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13238-015-0153-5), used non-viable embryos. In 2018 a Chinese researcher, He Jiankui, announced that he had edited embryos that became twin girls; in December 2019 a Shenzhen court [sentenced him to three years in prison](https://www.technologyreview.com/2019/12/30/131061/he-jiankui-sentenced-to-three-years-in-prison-for-crispr-babies) and fined him.

Rules differ by country. [Section 5 of Germany's Embryo Protection Act of 1990](https://lxgesetze.de/eschg/5) makes altering the genetic information of a human germline cell a criminal offence, punishable by up to five years in prison or a fine, with narrow exceptions. In the United States, [an appropriations rider](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/congress-revives-ban-on-altering-the-dna-of-human-embryos-used-for-pregnancies) first signed in December 2015, and renewed since, bars the Food and Drug Administration from reviewing applications to begin clinical research in which a human embryo is intentionally modified to carry a heritable change. Both facts bear on the book's setting and its characters' choice to stay hidden.

## 4. Intelligence and the cognitive package

In the book, a few "dozens" of changes produce an exceptional mind. Real genetics is not like that. Intelligence is *polygenic*: studies of large populations have found thousands of common variants, each with a tiny effect, and the environment matters as well. Twin studies suggest that the heritable share of intelligence [rises from about 20 percent in infancy to perhaps 80 percent in later adulthood](https://doi.org/10.1038/mp.2014.105), but the known variants predict only a modest part of individual differences. There is no known set of dozens of edits that would produce an Einstein.

## 5. The dolphin package

Bottlenose dolphins hunt by echolocation: they emit clicks and interpret the returning echoes with very fine timing. Humans also use tiny timing differences between the ears to locate sounds; under the best conditions, [trained listeners can detect a difference of roughly ten millionths of a second](https://web.pa.msu.edu/acoustics/brughera.pdf). Dolphins and humans, however, differ in anatomy as much as in genes: the dolphin's click-producing structures, jaw and hearing apparatus have no counterpart in a human body.

The book's claim, that rewritten circuits in a human brain could give echo-resolution and social prediction from partial sounds, is invented. No such edit is known, and the book does not describe one. This is deliberate. It keeps the story about people and consequences, not methods.

## 6. Detection

Whole-genome sequencing reads a person's DNA and compares it to a reference. Stretches that do not match the human reference can stand out, which is the basis of the story's fear of detection. Consumer ancestry and health tests look at selected positions only, and would not reveal most of what the book describes.

## 7. What is fact and what is fiction

| In the book | Status |
|---|---|
| Heterozygous inheritance, 50 percent per child | Real |
| Two carriers: 25, 50 and 25 percent | Real |
| Edits in the early embryo are present in all cells | Real principle (mosaicism is a problem) |
| Germline editing is a crime in Germany and blocked from clinical review in the US | Real |
| Dozens of edits produce an exceptional intellect | Invented |
| Dolphin-derived circuits in a human brain | Invented |
| A late-recovery stroke treatment that wakes dormant circuits | Invented |
| A secret clinic doing it in 1999 | Invented |
| Stone, the Pruitts, the cohort, the senator, the foundation | Invented |

# About the Author

Frank N. Stone is a pseudonym. It differs from the name of the fertility doctor in this book by one middle initial, a coincidence the author has chosen not to annotate, in keeping with the doctor's own methods.

Lothar J. Musiol is a graduate of Munich University of Applied Sciences who spent more than forty years in the semiconductor industry, first at Germany's largest company in the field, then at a string of American start-ups. He studied physics alongside all of it, seriously enough to know exactly how much he is simplifying. Dual citizenship, put to its most sensible use, lets him split his time between San Clemente, California, and Passau, Bavaria, where Austria begins at the garden fence. The household includes one married daughter, one grumpy Teacup Pomeranian named Gucci, and eleven chickens, listed in order of seniority rather than volume.

He writes to make complex ideas clearer and more engaging than most treatments manage, without ever pretending they are simpler than they actually are.
