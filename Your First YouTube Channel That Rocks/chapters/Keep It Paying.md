*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Keep It Paying

*A monetized channel is not finished. It has to keep its counts, spread its income, and run at a pace you can hold for years. Start by finding the earliest break.*

Getting into the Partner Program feels like an ending. It is a change of job. Before the gate, the work was to be watched. After it, the work is to be watched again next month, by enough people, on videos that still qualify, while the rules and the rates move underneath you. The channels that last are not the ones that grew fastest. They are the ones that kept a pace, kept their records, and did not depend on one stream.

This chapter is for two moments: when the counts stall, and when they do not.

## I. When the counts stall, find the earliest break

The tempting explanation is “the algorithm” or “the niche.” More often the path from a stranger to a returning viewer is broken in a specific place, and another video will not repair it. Start at the top. Fix the first item that is true. Do not fix item five while item two is still true.

1. **You cannot say who the channel is for.** If the viewer sentence from chapter 1 is still vague, stop. No title, thumbnail, or schedule finds an audience for nobody in particular.
2. **Strangers do not click.** Impressions without views. Chapter 2 is the fix: the viewer’s phrase first in the title, a thumbnail readable on a phone. Read the click rate the way chapter 7 does, against your own videos, not someone else’s.
3. **They click and leave.** The thirty-second line in the retention report is low. Chapter 4’s opening is the fix: say the promise, show the result, start.
4. **They stay and do not return.** Views with few returning viewers, and almost no traffic from your own videos under Suggested. The path in chapter 5 is missing. Name the next video inside the video, point the end screen at it, build the series playlist.
5. **The wrong people are watching.** The search terms card and the comments describe a different viewer. Change the title and the first ten seconds so the poor-fit viewer leaves early. Do not chase whoever happened to arrive.
6. **The counts grow, and the money does not.** Inside the program, a low RPM. Check chapter 11’s causes first — season, country mix, unmonetized views — then the ones you control: limited-ads labels from chapter 10, claims that send the money elsewhere, long videos with no mid-rolls.

Write down which break is true today. Make the single fix it needs. Set a date to count again. Leave every other variable alone until that date.

## II. Do not spend to hide the break

Ads on your own videos, boosts, and “growth” services send more people into the same path. If the path loses them, you bought a faster loss. And chapter 8 already showed that paid campaign views do not count toward the gate, and that bought engagement is a policy risk. If you promote a video at all, cap the spend the way you would cap any test: a number written down before you start, and a date when you stop even if you are curious.

## III. Spread the income

A channel on one stream is a channel one policy change from zero. Chapter 9 recorded two such changes in a single year: the ad gate doubling for new applicants, and a Shorts revenue floor of 10 million views every 90 days. Neither was a penalty. Both changed what some channels earned.

Look at the streams in chapters 11 to 13 as a set, and add one at a time, in the order they fit the channel:

- **Ads and Premium** pay for reach. They move with views, season, and advertiser demand.
- **Memberships** pay for loyalty. They move with how many regulars want more.
- **Supers** pay for presence. They move with how often you are live.
- **Sponsors** pay for a precise viewer. They move with your record.
- **Affiliate commissions** pay for recommendations viewers act on. They move with trust.

No stream needs to be large. The goal is that losing any one of them is a bad month, not the end of the channel.

> Case study: Colin and Samir, whose channel covers the creator economy, have described their own income in interviews as roughly half to 60 percent brand deals, about 30 percent speaking, consulting, and creative work that never appears on the channel at all, and AdSense around 10 percent — a smaller share than many viewers would guess for two people who make YouTube videos for a living. AdSense only became a substantial stream for them in 2022, reaching $268,000 that year; for years before that it was a minor, inconsistent add-on to income built mostly from elsewhere. Spreading the income is not a hedge against a bad month. For a channel like theirs, it is most of the income.

## IV. Other platforms pay for the same video

YouTube is not the only platform that will pay for a video. The video you already made can be posted where you have the right to post it, and earn from that platform’s program too. Every program below shares the same basic shape — a follower floor, a recent-activity or view floor, sometimes a minimum length, almost always a geographic restriction — but the numbers differ, and they move.

Two cautions first. Read the license on every clip and track again for each site, as chapter 3 says: a grant that names YouTube does not automatically name every other logo. And post the same video, not a second channel’s worth of new work. A second platform is a second door to work you already did.

**[Facebook — Content Monetization](https://creators.facebook.com/tools/ad-breaks/v2/).** Ad revenue share. 10,000 followers, 600,000 minutes watched in 60 days, and 5 videos on the Page.

**[Snapchat — Spotlight](https://help.snapchat.com/hc/en-us/articles/7012310905876).** Ad revenue share. 50,000 followers, 25 posts a month, and 100 hours of Spotlight view time in 28 days.

**[X — Original Content Rewards](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/original-content-rewards).** Pay per qualified impression. An active Premium subscription, 500 verified followers, and 500,000 impressions in 90 days.

**[Rumble — Partner Program](https://rumble.support/help/rumble-creator-program).** Ad revenue share. No follower minimum. An account 30 days old and in good standing, and one original upload.

**[Tumblr](https://help.tumblr.com/knowledge-base/paid-feature-eligibility/).** No video ad program on that page. A place to post and point back at the long video on YouTube.

Instagram, as of the same month, does not publish a fixed follower floor for gifts, and its bonus programs are described as invite-only. This list does not treat it as an ad-share plan. If you post there, the profile website and the Story sticker can point back at the channel. Dailymotion’s creator pages did not agree on one threshold in September 2026, so this chapter gives you no number for it. Vimeo On Demand was being discontinued, and this chapter has no replacement payout page it can send you to. If you still want one of those three, open that company’s own help and read the live page. Do not plan from this paragraph.

Two programs on the list replaced an older one in the year this chapter was written. Facebook’s In-Stream Ads ended for on-demand video in 2025 and for Live in mid-2026. The Content Monetization line above is the replacement. X retired its earlier revenue-sharing program in September 2026. Original Content Rewards is the replacement, and it pays for qualified impressions on original posts. That pace is why each line carries a month.
## V. When a second channel is worth the split

Chapter 9 set the rule: a second channel starts at zero. It clears its own thresholds, under whatever thresholds apply on the day it applies, and from February 2027 the ad gate for a new applicant is 8,000 hours. That makes a second channel expensive.

It is worth the split in a few cases: a different language for a viewer who will never watch the first, a subject the first channel’s viewers actively do not want, or a placeholder that protects your name. YouTube’s rules permit several channels under one account, and a placeholder can link back to the main channel from its banner, description, and a pinned comment. What crosses the line is spam, a name that impersonates someone else, or a trick meant to inflate the main channel’s numbers.

It is not worth the split because the main channel had a quiet month. Divide the hours by two and both channels are further from the gate.

## VI. A pace you can hold

The channel has to stay active to stay paid. Chapter 9 has the rules: today, six months without an upload or a post can switch monetization off, and the 2027 help pages, as reported, describe activity in terms of hours, Shorts views, or two long videos or five Shorts every 90 days. Those are floors, not targets.

Set the pace you can hold for a year, not the one you can hold for a month. One long video every two weeks, with a Short pointing at it and a post in between, is a channel. Three videos a week that stop in March are a gap the 365-day window will remember.

Three habits keep the pace:

- **Batch the work.** Script two videos in one sitting, film them in one session, edit them in one evening. Switching between jobs is where the hours go.
- **Keep a list of the next ten.** The questions in the comments and the search terms card from chapter 7 are the list. A channel that knows its next ten videos never starts a week from zero.
- **Take a break on purpose.** Chapter 2 cites YouTube’s own page: a break is not known to hurt a channel’s long-term views. Announce it in a post, schedule one video if you can, and come back on the date you said.

## VII. Keep the records

Monetized channels are small businesses, whatever their size. Keep the money sheet from chapter 15 every month, with each stream on its own line. Complete the tax information AdSense for YouTube asks for before the first payment. Keep the license, disclosure, and notice logs from chapter 10. Ask a tax adviser where you live how channel income is treated. This book is not tax advice.

> **Key takeaway:** When the counts stall, fix the earliest break and only that one. Do not buy reach to hide it. Spread the income across streams so no single change ends the channel. Cross-post where the license allows. Keep a pace you can hold for a year, and keep the records.

## VIII. Claims that do not survive a year

- That getting into the Partner Program is the finish. The counts, the policies, and the activity rule keep applying.
- That a new niche is the fix for a stalled channel. The earliest break is the fix. A new niche is a new channel at zero.
- That one large stream is safer than several small ones. Two programs changed shape in a single year. Several streams survive that.
- That a platform’s program, once joined, is permanent. Facebook’s In-Stream Ads and X’s original revenue sharing both ended within a year, each replaced by a program with different rules.
- That more uploads always mean more income. A pace you drop in March costs more than a slower one you keep.
- That a second channel inherits the first one’s status. It starts at zero, under the thresholds of the day it applies.

A last limit. This chapter will not promise that a channel keeps paying forever. It will make the reason it stops visible, and keep any single change from ending it.
