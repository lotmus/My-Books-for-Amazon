*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Record It So They Stay

*A teaching video is a promise in the first breath, a sound people can stand, and cuts that keep the promise moving. None of it needs a studio.*

*Layer: Start now. Workbook: the opening, the hard step, and the ending on the video-job sheet (chapter 15, §III).*

A stranger gives a new video a few seconds of credit. They clicked because the title named their problem. The first thing they hear decides whether that click was a good bet. Most first videos spend that credit on a hello, a name, a request to subscribe, and a story about why the channel exists. By the time the teaching starts, the person it was for has gone back to the feed.

Every minute a viewer stays is a minute on the watch-hour bar, and later a minute in which an ad can run. This chapter is how to record the eight videos in chapter 5 so the viewer reaches the method, the proof, and the name of the next video.

## I. The first thirty seconds are a contract

The opening has one job: confirm the click. Say the result the title promised, say who it is for, and show where the video ends up. Then start.

Here is an opening that loses the viewer:

“Hey everyone, welcome back to the channel. If you’re new here, I’ve been teaching piano for about twelve years. Before we get started, make sure you hit subscribe. So today I wanted to talk about something a lot of you have been asking about…”

Forty-four words. Nothing a stranger came for. Here is the same video, opened as a contract:

“If you’re an adult who started piano and your hands still won’t play together, this is the practice order that fixes it. By the end of this video you’ll play the first line of this song with both hands — slowly, but both hands. Here’s what that sounds like.” Then four seconds of the line, played.

The second version names the viewer, names the result, and proves the result exists before asking for anything. That is what YouTube’s retention guidance in chapter 2 — the first 30 seconds match what the title and thumbnail promised — looks like written down.

Three rules for the opening (author recommendation):

- **Say the title’s promise in the first sentence,** in nearly the title’s words. A viewer who clicked “Price a bathroom repair” and hears “Let’s talk about renovation” thinks they clicked the wrong thing.
- **Show the end state early.** The finished checklist, the line played, the corrected spreadsheet. A result seen at second ten is a reason to stay for minute six.
- **Move your name and the subscribe line to later, or delete them.** Your name is in the channel header. A subscribe request before the viewer has a reason is a request for nothing. Chapter 8 puts it later, tied to the next video. The opening is for the viewer’s problem.

Not every video can open on a finished result. The objection video opens on the objection, in the viewer’s words: “You’re thinking a checklist is too thin to catch the missing line.” The comparison video opens on the two choices. The rule is the same. The first sentence is the reason they clicked.

## II. Write for the ear

A script read aloud is a different document from an essay. The listener cannot reread a sentence. They cannot see your headings. They hear one word after another and lose the thread at the first long clause.

Write it the way you would explain it across a table:

- **One idea a sentence.** If you need a comma and a “which,” you need two sentences.
- **Say a number once, then show it.** “Eight dollars a month” said aloud and written on screen at the same moment. A spoken list of four numbers is noise.
- **Signpost the steps.** “Step one.” “That’s the hard part. Here’s the easy part.” On a page, a heading does that. In a video, your voice does.
- **Name the next thing before you do it.** “Now we’ll put the left hand under it.” The viewer who knows where the next minute goes is the viewer who stays for it.

The shape below fits most of the eight jobs. It is a frame, not a template to fill with filler.

1. **The contract.** The promise, the viewer, the end state. Under thirty seconds.
2. **The first step, done on screen.** Not a preview of the steps. The first one, done.
3. **The remaining steps,** each with the reason it comes in that order.
4. **The hard part.** The step where people get stuck, named as the hard part. This is the step a withholding video hides, and the step that earns the trust that brings a viewer back.
5. **The proof,** or a pointer to the proof video, if this is not that video.
6. **The one ask.** One reason to subscribe, tied to the next video, said once. Then the name of the next video to watch.

Word for word or bullet points is a choice about you, not about the viewer. A full script keeps a nervous speaker accurate and gives you most of a caption file. Bullets sound more natural if you already teach this material out loud. A useful middle: write the opening and the ask word for word, because those two have to be exact, and speak the middle from bullets.

Read the script aloud once before you record, with a timer. Every place you stumble is a sentence the viewer will stumble on. Rewrite it then, not in the edit.

> Worked example. Spoken English for a teaching video often lands somewhere around 130 to 160 words a minute. Treat that as a starting guess and time yourself; yours will differ. A 1,200-word script at 150 words a minute is about eight minutes before any demonstration is added. If the method takes three minutes to show on screen, the video is about eleven minutes. Length has one published money rule (official rule): [YouTube’s mid-roll page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6175006) says mid-roll ads are available on monetized videos eight minutes or longer. A deep dive that genuinely needs ten minutes can carry them later. A four-minute answer stretched to eight loses the viewers who would have filled the hour bar. YouTube publishes no ideal length for ranking. Cut to fit the job.

## III. Sound first, picture second

Creator heuristic: many creators report that viewers tolerate a slightly soft picture longer than they tolerate bad sound. YouTube publishes no such finding. You can test it on yourself: watch a slightly grainy video with clean speech, then a sharp one with an echo and a humming fridge, and notice which one you finish.

Most bad sound comes from distance and from the room, not from the microphone.

- **Get the microphone close.** A phone across the room records the room. The same phone, or a cheap clip-on microphone, a hand’s width or two from your mouth records you. Distance matters more than price.
- **Pick the softest room you have.** Bare walls and hard floors echo. A room with a bed, curtains, a rug, a full bookcase, or a closet of clothes swallows the echo.
- **Turn off what hums.** The refrigerator, the air conditioner, the fan, the notification sound on the laptop. You stop hearing them after a minute. The microphone never does.
- **Record ten seconds of silence** in the room before you talk. If your editor has a noise-reduction tool, that silence is what it learns from. If it does not, those ten seconds still tell you what is in the background before you have recorded an hour on top of it.
- **Record a backup.** If the camera records the picture, let a phone’s voice-memo app record the sound as well. Clap once on camera at the start. The spike in both tracks is where you line them up.
- **Listen back on headphones** after the first minute, not after the full take. A rustle from a clip-on microphone touching a collar is easy to fix at minute one and painful at minute eleven.

If you use a generated voice instead, chapter 3, §VII applies, including consent for any voice that is not your own.

## IV. Light, frame, and a screen people can read

You do not need lights. You need one window and the discipline to face it.

- **Face the light.** Sit facing a window, or at an angle to it, so the daylight falls on your face. A window behind you turns you into a silhouette while the camera exposes for the sky.
- **Camera at eye level.** A laptop camera looking up from a desk shows the ceiling and your chin. Put the laptop or the phone on a stack of books until the lens is level with your eyes.
- **Horizontal for long videos.** A long video plays in a 16:9 frame. A Short is the vertical 9:16 frame. Decide which one this recording is before you press record, because cropping a horizontal recording into a Short cuts off whatever was at the edges.
- **Lock exposure and focus** if the camera app lets you, so the picture does not pulse every time you lean forward or lift a page. Many phone camera apps lock both with a long press on the screen. Check yours.
- **Record at the resolution you will export.** Chapter 3 lists YouTube’s recommended upload settings. Recording in a format your computer cannot edit smoothly is not quality. It is a slower evening.

Many of the eight jobs are better as a screen than as a face: the spreadsheet, the checklist, the software. A free screen recorder such as [OBS Studio](https://obsproject.com/) records the screen and the microphone together. Before you record a screen:

- Zoom the application until the text is readable on a phone. Most viewers of a demonstration are not watching on a large monitor, and a cell value nobody can read is not proof.
- Turn off notifications. A message preview sliding into the corner of a recording is a privacy problem as well as a distraction.
- Close every tab and file the viewer should not see, including anything with someone else’s name on it.
- Move the mouse slowly. Point at the thing you are talking about, then stop moving.

## V. Record in pieces, cut for pace

Nobody records eleven clean minutes. Record the video one section at a time, following the script’s shape from §II. If you stumble, pause, and start that sentence again from the beginning. Do not stop the recording. The pause is easy to find in the edit, and a clean restart is easier to cut than a correction mid-sentence.

In the edit, the job is pace, not polish:

- **Cut the gaps where nothing is said.** The breath before a sentence, the “um,” the look at the notes. A cut that jumps your head slightly between two sentences is normal on this platform. Viewers are used to it.
- **Cover a jump with the thing you are describing.** When you say “the second line of the quote,” cut to the second line of the quote. Chapter 3’s free footage and screen recordings do this job.
- **Cut the repeat.** If you explained a step twice because the first take was rough, keep the better one. Teaching a step twice in the final video is not emphasis. It is a second take you forgot to delete.
- **Leave the hard part slow.** Pace means removing what does not teach. It does not mean rushing the step the viewer came for.

Then add video chapters. Official rule ([video chapters page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9884579)): timestamps in the description, the first at 00:00, at least three in ascending order, each chapter at least ten seconds long. A viewer who wants only the hard step can jump to it. That is a viewer who did not leave. Name each chapter in the viewer’s words, the way you named the title. Later, chapter boundaries are natural places for a mid-roll break (chapter 11).

Before you export, watch the whole video once at normal speed, as the stranger. Not to admire it. To find the first moment you would have reached for the back button. Cut or fix that moment. Then look for the next one.

## VI. The end is one ask and one door

The last minute of a teaching video is where it is tempting to stack everything: subscribe, like, comment, the other channel, the newsletter, the sponsor. Author recommendation: one ask and one next video.

End like this. Summarize the result in one sentence. Give one reason to subscribe, tied to what the next video will teach. Name the one video to watch next, by its title. Stop. A viewer who starts that video adds watch time to the same session.

Official rule: [YouTube’s end screen page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789) allows an end screen in the last 5 to 20 seconds of a video at least 25 seconds long. If you will use one to point at the next video, leave those seconds on screen: talk over a calm shot, or hold on the finished result, so the boxes do not cover your face or the proof. An end screen element that opens a site outside YouTube needs the Partner Program. A video, a playlist, and a subscribe element do the work this chapter needs.

*In short:* Open on the promise and the result. Get the microphone close. Cut what does not teach. End on one ask and one named video.

## VII. Do this before you press record

- [ ] The first sentence says the title’s promise, in nearly the title’s words.
- [ ] The end state is shown in the first thirty seconds, where the video has one.
- [ ] The script was read aloud once, with a timer, and every stumble was rewritten.
- [ ] The hard step is in the script and named as the hard step.
- [ ] The microphone is close, the room is soft, and the fridge is off.
- [ ] Ten seconds of room silence and one clap are at the start of the recording.
- [ ] You are facing the light, with the lens at eye level, in the frame this video needs.
- [ ] Notifications are off, private tabs are closed, and screen text is readable on a phone.
- [ ] The ending asks once, with a reason, and names the next video by title.

## VIII. Claims that do not survive a playback

- That you need a camera upgrade before you start. A close microphone and a window do more than a new camera body.
- That a long introduction builds a relationship. A stranger has no relationship with you yet. The promise, kept quickly, is how that starts.
- That every video needs a subscribe request at the start. It costs the opening seconds the viewer uses to decide to stay. Ask later, separately, or not at all.
- That there is an ideal video length. YouTube publishes none for ranking. The job decides the length. Eight minutes is a money rule for mid-roll ads, not a quality bar.
- That a script makes you sound robotic. Reading badly makes you sound robotic. A script written for the ear, read aloud once before recording, sounds like explaining.
