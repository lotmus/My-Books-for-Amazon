*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Sponsors and Affiliate Links

*Companies can pay the channel directly: a fee for one video, or a commission when a viewer buys something you recommended. Both are income from an audience you already earned, and both need a disclosure every time.*

Ads and fan funding are paid through YouTube, on YouTube’s terms. The two streams in this chapter are paid by other companies, on terms you agree with them. That makes them the most flexible income a channel has, and the easiest to get wrong. A sponsor pays for your viewers’ trust. An affiliate program pays a commission on it. Spend that trust carelessly and every other stream in this book shrinks with it.

Neither stream is selling things of your own. You recommend a product someone else makes, or you carry a message someone else paid for, and you say so.

## I. What a sponsor is paying for

A sponsorship, or brand deal, is one company paying you to feature its product in a video: a spoken segment, a demonstration, or a whole video built around it. The sponsor is not paying for views in general. It is paying for your particular viewers, the people chapter 1 named, hearing about its product from someone they already watch.

That is why a small channel with a precise viewer can be worth more to the right sponsor than a large channel with a vague one. It is also why the first sponsor usually arrives after the channel has a record: a sponsor will look at your recent videos, how many people watch each one, and who comments.

Prepare a one-page summary before anyone asks — creators call it a media kit. It is editorial practice, not a platform form:

- who the channel is for, in the viewer sentence from chapter 1;
- average views per long video over the last ninety days, and average view duration, from chapter 7;
- where the viewers are, by country, and their age range, from Studio’s audience reports;
- two or three videos that show the kind of segment you would make; and
- how to reach you, from the email address on the channel’s About section.

## II. YouTube Creator Partnerships

YouTube runs its own matching tool between brands and creators. [YouTube’s Creator Partnerships page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9385307) describes it as the successor to BrandConnect. To see the Creator Partnerships tab in Studio’s Earn area, you must be at least 18, in the Partner Program and eligible for ad revenue sharing, based in a supported country, and without active Community Guidelines strikes. Eligible creators see the tab automatically. YouTube’s ad revenue page notes that income from Creator Partnerships appears in YouTube’s revenue reporting, unlike a deal you agree directly with a brand.

You do not need the tool to take a sponsor. Brands also approach channels directly, and many deals are agreed by email. The tool is one more door, open after the ad gate.

## III. Price a deal you can explain

There is no published rate card for sponsorships, and the numbers creators post online describe their own channels. Price from your own counts, the way chapter 11 reads RPM.

> Worked example. Invented numbers, to show a method, not a rate. A long video on your channel averages 12,000 views in its first ninety days. You decide a sponsored segment is worth $25 per thousand of those views to you, because it costs an extra day of scripting and filming and takes a minute of the viewer’s attention. 12 × $25 is $300 for one integrated segment. A whole video built around the product costs you more time and more of the viewer’s patience, so it is priced higher. The number is yours. The method — expected views times a rate you can defend — is what lets you say it without flinching and change it when the counts change.

Before you agree, write down five terms. They are where first deals go wrong, and they are editorial practice, not legal advice:

- **What you deliver.** Length of the segment, where in the video, how many rounds of review the brand gets, and the publish date.
- **Usage rights.** Whether the brand may reuse your segment in its own ads, where, and for how long. Reuse is worth more than the segment itself.
- **Exclusivity.** Whether you may work with a competitor, and for how long.
- **Payment.** The amount, when it is paid, and what happens if the brand cancels after you film.
- **Your editorial line.** You say what you actually think of the product. If the brand needs you to say it is the best, you are the wrong channel.

## IV. Disclose it twice

Chapter 10 has the rule in detail. YouTube’s [paid promotion page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235) says that if a company paid you or gave you the product, you tick the paid promotion box in Studio, and YouTube shows a disclosure at the start of the video. The box is not the whole disclosure. Local law can require more, and the FTC’s pages in the sources are the US guidance.

Say it in the video, in plain words, before the segment: “[Sponsor] paid for this video. The link is theirs.” Put the same sentence next to the link in the description.

One sponsor per video. A sponsored segment, an affiliate link to a competitor, and a membership pitch in the same video is three asks, and the viewer hears none of them.

## V. Affiliate links

An affiliate link pays you a commission when a viewer buys through it. It is a recommendation with a receipt. It does not need YouTube’s ad program to exist, which is why it is the one stream that can start before either gate. It does need an audience that acts on your recommendations, which takes the same trust everything else does.

Rules that apply wherever the program comes from:

- **Recommend what you use.** A link to a product you would not show in the video is a withdrawal from the trust account.
- **Read that program’s commission page before you film.** A high percentage of a cheap product can pay less than a low percentage of an expensive one, and a subscription can pay again next month. A roundup’s rate table is not the contract.
- **Disclose next to the link.** “Affiliate link” or “#ad,” in the same line. YouTube’s [external links policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9054257) says affiliate content does not violate its Terms of Use. The disclosure is still yours to make.
- **Keep the price where it belongs.** The price is on the merchant’s page. Your job is the recommendation and the disclosure.

Placement is where most affiliate links fail, because a pasted address is not a link until YouTube makes it clickable. [YouTube’s sharing-links page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13748639) says a long-form description and a long-form comment become clickable only after the channel has advanced features, and that an address in a Short’s description or comments is not clickable, including a comment you pin. So:

- Put the link in the first two or three lines of a long video’s description, the part a viewer sees before “more,” with the disclosure beside it.
- Repeat it in a pinned comment on that long video.
- A Short can name the product and point at the long video. It cannot carry the link.

An [info card](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6140493) or [end screen](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789) element that opens a site outside YouTube requires the Partner Program. That is a later convenience. The description works first.

## VI. The YouTube Shopping affiliate program

YouTube also runs its own affiliate program, which lets a channel tag products from participating brands inside its videos and earn a commission on purchases. On [25 March 2026 YouTube announced](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shopping-expansion-500-subscribers/) that it was opening the program to every channel in the Partner Program with at least 500 subscribers that meets the eligibility guidelines, with tags available across Shorts, long videos, and live streams. Unlike a pasted link, a product tag works inside a Short.

[YouTube’s Shopping affiliate page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13376398) lists the other conditions: a channel based in one of the listed countries, which in October 2026 included the United States; not a music channel, an Official Artist Channel, or a channel associated with music partners; and not set as made for kids or with a significant number of made-for-kids videos. Eligible creators find an invitation in Studio’s Earn area.

The same rules apply as for any affiliate link: tag what you use, say that the tag pays you, and do not let the tags crowd out the teaching. A video that turns into a catalog stops being a video people finish.

## VII. Keep the streams apart

Write sponsor fees and affiliate commissions on their own lines of the money sheet in chapter 15, separate from AdSense. They are paid on different dates, by different companies, under different contracts, and they answer different questions. AdSense tells you what YouTube paid for attention. Sponsor fees tell you what your viewers are worth to a brand. Commissions tell you whether viewers act on what you recommend.

> **Key takeaway:** A sponsor pays for your viewers’ trust. Price from your own views, write down five terms, tick the box, and say it in the video. Affiliate links pay on purchases, start before the gates, and only work where YouTube makes them clickable. The Shopping affiliate program opens at 500 subscribers inside the Partner Program, where offered.

## VIII. Claims that do not survive the disclosure rule

- That ticking the paid promotion box is the whole disclosure. YouTube says local law may require more. Say it in the video and next to the link.
- That sponsors only want large channels. A sponsor pays for a particular viewer. A precise channel can be the better fit.
- That a rate someone posted online is the market rate. It describes their channel. Price from your own counts.
- That an affiliate link in a Short earns. A Short’s description and comments are not clickable. A Shopping product tag is the exception, where you are eligible.
- That disclosure costs commissions. Hiding an affiliate relationship costs trust, and on many platforms it breaks the rules.
- That more sponsors per video is more money. One sponsor per video keeps the viewer listening and the sponsor’s segment worth paying for.

A last limit. This chapter will not find you a sponsor, and it is not legal advice on a contract. It will make sure that when a company pays the channel, the deal is priced from your own numbers, written down, and disclosed.
