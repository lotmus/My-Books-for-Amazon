*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# The Click Is the Whole Business

*What a thumbnail, a title, and ninety seconds of metadata decide before a single second of the video has played — and the words that quietly cost you money.*

*Layer: Start now. Workbook: the title and opening lines of the video-job sheet (chapter 15, §III).*

Nobody watches a video because it is good. They watch because a thumbnail and a title won a contest that lasted under a second. If the video loses that contest, nobody finds out it was good. This chapter is about winning it honestly. A few facts decide whether the win holds: a character limit, a category of words, a caption file. You can check each one in the upload form.

Every count the Partner Program asks for starts here. An impression that never becomes a view adds no watch hours and no subscribers. The title and the thumbnail are how the viewer from chapter 1 recognizes their own problem and clicks. Packaging for nobody in particular gets clicks from nobody in particular.

## I. The title: one keyword, one benefit, one hook

Official rule: a title has 100 characters, and every one counts — letters, numbers, commas, spaces, hyphens.

Author recommendation: build it in three parts, in this order.
1. **The keyword** — the exact phrase a stranger would type into the search bar, not a clever paraphrase of it.
2. **The benefit** — what the viewer walks away with, stated plainly.
3. **The hook** — the one detail that makes this version worth clicking over the other nine.

Filled in, as a pattern: keyword, price a bathroom repair; benefit, the quote matches the work; hook, line by line. Title: “Price a bathroom repair so the quote matches the work, line by line.” That is 13 words and 68 characters. Chapters 5 and 15 reuse this same illustration, so the book does not collect sample niches.

Documented platform guidance: [YouTube’s title tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300) say viewers may see only part of a title, so the most important words go near the beginning, and that an inaccurate title makes people stop watching, which can hurt discoverability. Creator heuristic: a title that leads with the hook and pushes the viewer’s phrase to the end risks being cut off before the phrase appears, and a searcher may not recognize it as the answer. When the two goals conflict, put the viewer’s phrase first.

The thumbnail is the other half of the decision. The same page says viewers usually see the thumbnail and title first, and that 90% of the best-performing videos use a custom thumbnail. That is YouTube’s observation about videos that already performed, not proof that a custom image causes performance. The picture is shown small, on a phone: one subject, large in the frame, and few words, in a font still readable at that size. YouTube says color and composition can catch the eye, and too much of either overwhelms it. Shrink the image to phone size before you upload. If you cannot tell what it is, remove something.

The thumbnail does not repeat the title. The title carries the phrase. The picture shows the result or the problem. Neither may promise what the video does not deliver. Official rule: where Studio offers a [title-and-thumbnail test](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400), YouTube keeps the version with the highest watch time, not the one with the most clicks.

## II. Tags and description: what the form limits

| Field | Character limit | What that means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Title | 100 | Front-load the viewer’s phrase. |
| Description | 5,000 | Only the first lines show before “more.” Write those as if they are the whole description. |
| Tags | 500 total | Not per tag. The whole list shares one budget, commas and spaces included. |

The description’s first lines are the only part most viewers see. Put the viewer’s phrase and the concrete benefit there. Chapters, a fuller explanation, and links come after.

Documented platform guidance: [YouTube’s description tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12948449) say to pick one or two main words that describe the video and feature them in both the title and the description, to give each video its own description, and to use the Research tab in Analytics and Google Ads Keyword Planner to find the words. A default description can carry the lines that repeat on every upload. Those go after the unique opening, not instead of it. Once a video has traffic, the search terms card in the Reach tab lists what people typed (chapter 7). If a term that brings people in is missing from the description, add it.

Documented platform guidance: [YouTube’s recommendation page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650) says tags can help but are not essential for discovery, and are mainly used to correct common misspellings. Spend the budget on phrases a viewer would type. Do not expect tags to place a video whose title and thumbnail never named the subject.

## III. The words that cost you money

Some words can limit a video’s earnings even when the video is calm and accurate. Official rule: YouTube’s advertiser-friendly guidelines treat sensitive subjects with caution, and a video can earn limited ads or none (chapter 10, §V). YouTube publishes the categories. It does not publish an exact word list.

Creator heuristic: words for desperation, crisis, or hopelessness are the ones creators most often report tripping that category, whatever the video is about. Keep the feeling, and name the searchable version of the problem. A fitness video can say “stuck at the same weight for six months.” A money video can name the bill, the quote, or the deadline. If a tag would look at home on a crisis-hotline list, leave it out of the title, the description, and the tags. Before you title a video about personal finance, health, relationships, weight, or legal trouble, read the live guidelines.

## IV. Captions and subtitles

Captions are an accessibility feature, and they give YouTube a text version of what you say. YouTube does not document whether or how it reads words shown inside the picture. Do not rely on on-screen text alone to tell the platform what the video is about. Say it, and caption it.

Viewers control closed captions with the **CC** button, a keyboard shortcut, or an account setting. **Burned-in** captions are part of the picture and stay on. They are a different choice.

Ways to add closed captions, from most accurate to least:
- **Upload a caption file** (`.srt`, `.vtt`, `.sbv`). YouTube’s supported-formats page lists those among others, in plain UTF-8. For anything scripted, your script is most of the file.
- **Auto-sync a transcript** you paste in. You supply the words, YouTube supplies the timing.
- **Edit the automatic captions.** Official guidance: YouTube says automatic captions can misrepresent speech because of mispronunciations, accents, dialects, or background noise, and that you should always review and edit them.

Publish a caption track once it is accurate. An uncorrected transcript full of misheard names is worse for a viewer than none.

## V. Retention: what YouTube publishes, and what it does not

Studio publishes one retention measure, and it is not a ranking formula. In a video’s audience-retention report, the intro line is the percentage still watching after the first 30 seconds. The report usually takes one to two days to appear. Documented platform guidance: [YouTube’s retention page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415) says a high intro percentage can mean the first 30 seconds matched what the thumbnail and title promised. Studio compares the intro with your own recent videos of similar length. That comparison is not a published cutoff for being recommended.

Documented platform guidance: [YouTube’s recommendation-system page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16533387) states two goals — help each viewer find videos they want, and maximize long-term satisfaction — and lists what the system learns from: what people watched, ignored, or dismissed, how much of a video they watched, searches, likes, comments, shares, surveys, subscriptions, and what similar viewers enjoyed. It tells creators to ask “Does my audience like this?” rather than whether the algorithm does. The same page says publish time is not known to affect a video’s long-term performance, except for a premiere or a live stream, that consistent quality beats upload frequency, and that the algorithm does not penalize creators for taking breaks. It also recommends a consistent title and thumbnail style.

## VI. Shorts and long videos together

A Short can find a new viewer. The long video is where they can stay, and official rule: only long-form time fills the watch-hour bar (chapter 9). A Short that never points at a long video ends there.

Connect them in two ways. Set the long video as the Short’s related video, and say the destination out loud inside the Short. Official rule: a Short’s description and comments are not clickable, including a pinned comment ([sharing-links page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13748639)).

Documented platform guidance: [YouTube’s discovery FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225) says the system has no opinion about what type of video you make and does not favor a format. Search still cares whether the title matches what was typed. “You need to see this” is not a search.

In Analytics, the Content tab’s Shorts view shows how often a Short was shown, how viewers found it, and your top Shorts ([Shorts analytics tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217)). A Short that held people is a candidate for the next long video, and the reverse.

If several videos answer the same viewer’s next questions, make them a [series playlist](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6084043). Official rule: the account must be verified, a video can sit in only one series playlist, and the videos must be yours. On a computer, turn on “Set as official series for this playlist.”

How many videos, and which: the eight jobs in chapter 5. Four of them must be long videos.

*In short:* Put the viewer’s phrase first in the title and in the first lines of the description, make the thumbnail readable on a phone, caption what you say, and connect every Short to a long video.

## VII. Claims that do not survive an upload form

- That tags place the video. YouTube says they are not essential for discovery.
- That the thumbnail’s job is the highest click rate. YouTube’s own test keeps the version with the highest watch time.
- That a specific retention percentage is a ranking rule. Studio’s intro comparison is against your own recent videos.
- That posting every day is the growth plan. YouTube says quality beats frequency and breaks are not penalized.
- That the hour you press publish is the strategy. YouTube says it is not known to change long-term views, except for premieres and live streams.
