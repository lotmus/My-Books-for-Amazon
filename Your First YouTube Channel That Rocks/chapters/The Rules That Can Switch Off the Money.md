*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# The Rules That Can Switch Off the Money

*A limited-ads label, a warning, a strike, a claim, and a missing disclosure are different problems with different clocks. Know which one you are holding before you react to it.*

*Layer: Set up before monetization — except the checklist in §XI, which starts with your first upload. Workbook: the rights log (chapter 15, §VII).*

A channel heading for the gate has more to lose from a rule than a hobby channel does. A hobby channel that loses a video loses a video. A monetizing channel can lose the ads on its best video, lose its place in the review queue because of an active strike, or lose the Partner Program itself for a pattern it did not notice.

The rules are not mysterious. YouTube publishes them, with the penalties and the clocks. Most first-time trouble comes from confusing one kind of problem with another and answering it the wrong way.

This chapter is the short map. Everything in it is an official rule from the policy page each section names, unless labeled otherwise.

## I. Five problems that look alike in your inbox

| Problem | What causes it | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Community Guidelines warning | A first violation of a Community Guidelines policy | No penalty at first. An optional policy training lets it expire. |
| Community Guidelines strike | A further violation after a warning | A pause on posting. Three in 90 days can end the channel. |
| Copyright strike | A valid legal removal request from a rights holder | The video comes down. Three in 90 days can end the channel. |
| Content ID claim | An automatic match against a rights holder’s reference file | Affects that video’s money or visibility. Not a strike. |
| Limited or no ads | A video that does not meet the advertiser-friendly guidelines | Fewer or no ads on that video. Not a strike. |

A disclosure is a sixth thing, and it is the one you control completely. Sections VI and VII cover the settings. The channel monetization policies sit above all of them, and section VIII covers the ones that remove a whole channel from the program.

## II. Community Guidelines: the warning, then the clock

YouTube’s [strike basics page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2802032) says the Community Guidelines apply to all content, including unlisted and private videos, comments, links, posts, and thumbnails. Notice two words in that list. **Links** means a page you point at, including an affiliate link, is inside the rules. **Thumbnails** means the picture is held to them too, not just the video.

The sequence, as YouTube describes it:

- **The first violation is typically a warning.** You can take an optional policy training. If you finish it, the warning expires 90 days after you finished the training, not 90 days after it was issued. Violate the same policy inside that window and the warning may not expire, and the channel may get a strike. Violate a different policy after the training and you get another warning. YouTube says not every warning is eligible for training, and that repeat offenders may lose the option.
- **A first strike** stops you posting for one week. YouTube’s [community guidelines tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12950271) spell out what that covers: videos, live streams, stories, custom thumbnails, and posts. The strike expires 90 days after it was issued.
- **A second strike within 90 days** stops posting for two more weeks.
- **A third strike within the same 90 days** terminates the channel.
- **A single case of severe abuse** can terminate a channel without the warning or the strikes.

If you believe the decision is wrong, appeal it from the notice. If you believe it is right, the useful response is to read the specific policy the email names, fix the pattern, and wait out the clock without testing the edge again. An active strike also blocks an application: the eligibility page says a channel cannot apply to the Partner Program until its strikes expire or are removed on appeal.

Three policies matter more than the rest to a channel built to earn.

- **Spam.** YouTube’s [spam policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973) lists, among other things, maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, and descriptions that promise what the video does not deliver; rewards offered in exchange for likes, views, or subscribers; repetitive comments posted to drive traffic; and “get rich quick” schemes. It says plainly that asking viewers to like, comment, or subscribe is fine. It also names automated or synthetic mass-production: using automated tools or AI to churn out high volumes of similar content with minimal changes. Its own example is a channel that uses the same background music and repetitive AI-generated imagery across many videos, each reading out an AI-generated script. Testing tools or posting a few variations is fine. Forty copies of the same generated video with the keyword swapped are not a channel.
- **Fake engagement.** Paying a service for views, subscribers, or likes falls under YouTube’s [fake engagement policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767). Those views do not count toward the gate either (chapter 8, §VI).
- **External links.** YouTube’s [external links policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9054257) bars links that send people to content that would break the guidelines, such as malware, phishing, or sites selling regulated goods. It covers typed links, addresses shown in the picture, spelled-out addresses, and addresses said aloud. It also says affiliate content does not violate YouTube’s Terms of Use, while excessive affiliate posting in dedicated accounts may break the spam rules. An affiliate link is a link. Point it at the merchant’s normal, secure page, and do not link a product you would not recommend in the video.

## III. Copyright strikes: a legal request, not an algorithm

> **Copyright and Content ID.** A copyright strike is a legal removal request, and three in 90 days can end the channel. A Content ID claim is not a strike, but disputing one without a valid reason can lead to one. Before you publish, read the license on every clip and track and save a dated screenshot in the rights log.

A copyright strike comes from a person or company sending YouTube a formal removal request. YouTube’s [copyright strike page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2814000) says these requests are reviewed, and that if one appears valid, YouTube has to remove the content to comply with copyright law. The [strikes FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9235777) says there is no warning step for this one. The first valid request is a strike.

Some removal requests are scheduled. YouTube’s [removal request page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13823830) says a scheduled request gives the uploader seven days to act, for example by removing the video or seeking a retraction, before the removal and the strike take effect. If you receive one, that week is the time to decide, not to argue in the comments.

Three ways a copyright strike is resolved, per YouTube:

1. **Complete Copyright School and wait.** The strike expires 90 days after it was applied, once Copyright School is done and the channel has fewer than three strikes. The removed video is not reinstated.
2. **Ask for a retraction.** Contact the person who filed the request and ask them to withdraw it. This is the route when the request was a mistake that a polite email can fix, such as a licensed clip you can show the license for.
3. **File a counter notification.** A legal statement that the removal was a mistake, or that the use qualifies as an exception such as fair use. YouTube says it cannot decide ownership or mediate between you and the rights holder. A counter notification is a legal step. Treat it as one.

Three copyright strikes within 90 days make the channel subject to termination. For a monetizing channel, the defense is upstream. Keep the proof that you read the license: the dated screenshot in the rights log. When a request arrives two years later, that screenshot is the retraction email.

> Case study: h3h3Productions, the YouTube channel run by Ethan and Hila Klein, posted a reaction video in 2015 responding to a short film by another creator, Matt Hosseinzadeh, playing short portions of it between their own commentary. Hosseinzadeh skipped YouTube’s copyright process and sued the Kleins directly in federal court for infringement. In August 2017, after roughly a year and a half of litigation, the US District Court for the Southern District of New York ruled for the Kleins on summary judgment in *Hosseinzadeh v. Klein*, finding their use fair as a matter of law because the video was, in the court’s words, quintessential criticism and comment. A counter notification or a fair-use defense can win. Reaching that answer in this case took eighteen months and real legal fees, not a form in Studio.

## IV. Content ID claims: the video, not the channel

A Content ID claim is generated automatically when an upload matches a reference file a rights holder has given YouTube. YouTube’s [claims page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6013276) says the rights holder chooses what happens: monetize, track, or block. Monetize is the most common choice for music.

A claimed video longer than three minutes that is tracked or monetized stays up. A Short between one and three minutes long with an active claim is blocked, whatever the policy. YouTube says claims affect videos and usually do not affect the channel, and that a claim is not a strike.

The trap is in the dispute. The same page says that if you dispute a claim without a valid reason, the rights holder can send a removal request, and if that request is valid, the channel gets a copyright strike. A claim you could have lived with becomes a strike you cannot ignore.

What a claim means for a channel that earns:

- **A monetize claim sends that video’s ad money to someone else.** The deep dive you spent a week on can earn for the owner of a ten-second music bed. A claimed video is also not eligible for Super Thanks, and a channel with many music-claimed videos can lose memberships. If the claimed part is a music bed, replacing the track is usually faster than disputing it, if your editor or Studio’s own tools let you remove or swap the claimed audio. Check what Studio offers on that video’s claim before you re-upload anything (author recommendation).
- **A block can break the path in some countries or everywhere.** If the path video sends people to a blocked method video, the path has a hole in it, and the hours stop. Fix the video, or point the path at another one.
- **Dispute only when you have the reason in writing.** A license you can show, music you composed, a public-domain recording you can document. A dispute is a statement, not a lottery ticket.

## V. Limited ads: the guidelines that decide the money on one video

Once you are in the Partner Program, every video carries a monetization status. YouTube’s [advertiser-friendly content guidelines](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6162278) decide it. A video can follow the Community Guidelines completely and still earn limited ads or none, because some advertisers choose not to appear beside certain subjects: profanity in the opening, violence, adult themes, controversial issues, sensitive events, and the crisis words chapter 2 warns about, among others.

That status is not a strike. One limited video does not affect the channel’s standing. A pattern can: the same page says YouTube may disable ads on an entire channel when the majority of its content is not suitable for any advertisers. It is a statement about how much that one video can earn. Three habits keep it honest:

- **Answer the self-certification questionnaire truthfully** when you upload. It asks what is in the video. Describe the video you made, not the one that would earn full ads.
- **Check the thumbnail, the title, the description, and the tags, not just the video.** The guidelines say they apply to all of it.
- **Request a human review** when you believe an automated decision is wrong. YouTube says its systems do not always get it right, and that you can ask. A review is a request, not a guarantee.

A subject with a cost is still allowed. Chapter 1 asked you to know that cost before the first upload. This is where it shows.

## VI. Paid promotion: the setting and the sentence

> **Paid promotion and affiliate disclosure.** If a company paid you or gave you the product, select paid promotion in Studio and say it in the video. Put a disclosure next to every affiliate link. YouTube’s label does not cover your legal obligations where you live.

If a video features branded content, sponsorships, endorsements, or other commercial relationships, YouTube’s [paid promotion page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235) says you have to tell YouTube by selecting the paid promotion option in the video’s details. In the upload flow it appears under “Paid promotion.” YouTube then adds a disclosure label at the start of the video. YouTube’s [branded content policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/17596007) add that if its systems detect undisclosed branded content, they may apply the label themselves and notify you.

The label is not the whole disclosure. The paid promotion page says you and the partners you work with are responsible for complying with all applicable legal requirements, and the branded content policies say you are ultimately responsible for making the disclosure clear and prominent. The sponsor sentence in chapter 13 — “[Sponsor] paid for this video. The link is theirs.” — is the part a viewer hears.

The FTC pages in the sources are the US guidance on what clear means. Do both: the setting for YouTube, the sentence for the person watching.

An affiliate link alone is a commission on a sale, not necessarily a paid placement. Whether your particular arrangement also needs the paid promotion setting is a question for the paid promotion page and the affiliate program’s own terms. The disclosure next to the link, which this book already requires, is not optional either way.

## VII. AI use: the setting for realistic generated material

Chapter 3 shows how to make a video without a camera: generated footage, a generated presenter, a synthetic voice. YouTube asks you to say so when the result could be mistaken for real.

YouTube’s [AI disclosure page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491) requires disclosure when generative AI is used to meaningfully alter or generate content that appears realistic: making a real person appear to say or do something they did not, altering footage of a real event or place, generating a realistic scene that did not happen, or creating music that is the main focus of the video. The setting is under “AI use” in the upload flow in Studio, on a computer or a phone. Answer Yes when the video meets that bar.

What does not need it, per the same page and YouTube’s [announcement of the requirement](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/): clearly unrealistic or animated content, color and lighting adjustments, beauty and special-effects filters, and production help, such as an outline, a script, a title, a thumbnail, or an infographic, generating ideas, or making captions. A script drafted with an assistant and read by you in your own voice is that case. So is a clone of your own voice used for a voice-over or a dub.

The page adds two warnings. YouTube can apply the label itself: to content made with its own AI tools, to files carrying C2PA content credentials, and to content its systems detect, and a label from the first two or from a manual review cannot be switched off. And a creator who consistently fails to disclose can be labeled by hand, have content removed, or be suspended from the Partner Program.

Two changes are worth knowing. YouTube’s [May 2026 update on AI labels](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/) moved the label for photorealistic AI content to a more prominent place: below the player on long videos, as an overlay on Shorts. The same post says that starting in May 2026, YouTube began automatically labeling videos its systems detect as having significant photorealistic AI use, where the creator had not answered either way. It also says the label by itself does not change how a video is recommended or whether it can earn money.

> **AI voice and likeness.** When a video meets the bar, set AI use to Yes. A disclosure does not make cloning someone else’s voice, or using someone else’s face, acceptable. That needs their written agreement (chapter 3, §VII).

A disclosure settles what the viewer is told. It does not settle who owns the words. In the United States, copyright needs a human author: the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit held so in March 2025, in a case over copyright for a purely AI-generated image, and the Supreme Court declined to review that ruling on March 2, 2026.

A tool’s terms may say a generated script is yours to use. That is permission, not ownership. A script an assistant wrote, read out word for word, is yours to perform and nobody’s to own, and another channel could reuse the same sentences. The lines you wrote yourself, and your own creative rewrites of a draft, are protected as usual, and so are the delivery, the edit, and the footage you shot.

The practical rule (author recommendation): put your own explanation on the track. It is what the inauthentic-content policy in §VIII rewards, and it is the only part of a script anyone can be stopped from copying.

## VIII. The monetization policies: rules for the whole channel

The policies above judge a video. [YouTube’s channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392) judge the channel, at review and continuously afterwards. The eligibility page says YouTube keeps checking channels in the program, and that a channel violating these policies loses monetization regardless of its watch hours and subscribers.

Two of them catch growing channels most often.

- **Reused content.** Someone else’s material, republished without significant original commentary, modification, or educational value. A compilation of other people’s clips with a caption on top is the standard case. Your own voice explaining something on screen is the standard cure.
- **Inauthentic content.** YouTube renamed “repetitious content” to “inauthentic content” in July 2025. It covers templated, mass-produced videos, including AI-generated videos made from generic templates with none of the creator’s own insight. The same page bars monetization for channels that use an AI persona presented as a human expert giving health, legal, financial, or political advice. If your subject is advice in one of those areas, put yourself on camera or your own voice on the track, not a generated expert.

The review looks at the channel as a whole. A channel with forty near-identical videos and eight good ones is judged as the forty.

## IX. Made for kids: a legal statement, not a reach setting

> **Made for kids.** The audience setting is a legal statement under US law, not a reach setting. Set it for who the video is directed to. A wrong answer either way is your risk, and “made for kids” switches off comments, end screens, personalized ads, memberships, and Supers.

Every channel and every video has an audience setting: made for kids, or not. YouTube’s [audience setting page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9527654) says the setting exists to comply with the US Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, COPPA, and other laws.

When a video is set as made for kids, YouTube turns off a long list of features on it. The list includes comments, cards and end screens, the notification bell, personalized advertising, the miniplayer, and saving to a playlist or Watch Later. A channel set as made for kids also loses posts and channel memberships, among others, and Super Chat, Super Stickers, and Super Thanks are not available on made-for-kids videos.

For most channels in this book the answer is plainly no. The viewer is an adult with a question. The setting describes who the video is directed to, not who it helps in the end. A channel teaching parents how to choose a reading tutor is directed to parents. A cartoon of the alphabet song is directed to children, whatever the parent who pressed play intended.

If your videos really are directed to children, the setting is not a marketing choice. Mark them correctly, and accept that the features the rest of this book relies on — comments, the end screen that names the next video, personalized ads, memberships, and Supers — will not be there. A channel that needs those features should make videos for adults.

## X. Keep the rights log

Nothing in this chapter needs a lawyer to follow. It does need a memory you will not have in two years. The rights log in chapter 15, §VII has three parts: licenses (every clip, image, and track you did not make, with a dated screenshot), disclosures (paid promotion, AI use, audience setting, affiliate line, per video), and notices (every warning, strike, claim, or limited-ads label, with the date its clock runs out).

The notices part tells you, before you upload a borderline video, whether the channel has room for a mistake this month. With an active strike, it does not, and a Partner Program application waits too.

*In short:* A limited-ads label is about one video’s money. A warning has a training. A strike has a 90-day clock and blocks an application. A copyright strike is a legal request. A claim is about one video. The monetization policies judge the whole channel.

## XI. Before every upload

This checklist is in the Start now layer. Use it from the first video.


- [ ] Every clip, image, and track has a license line and a dated screenshot in the rights log.
- [ ] The title and the thumbnail promise only what the video contains.
- [ ] The self-certification answers describe the video as it is, including the opening, the title, and the thumbnail.
- [ ] Any affiliate link points at a product you would recommend in the video, with the disclosure next to it.
- [ ] If a company paid you or gave you the product, paid promotion is selected and you said so out loud.
- [ ] If any realistic scene, person, or voice was generated or meaningfully altered, AI use is set to Yes.
- [ ] The audience setting says who the video is directed to.
- [ ] The rights log shows no active strike that this video’s subject could add to.

## XII. Claims that do not survive a policy page

- That a first mistake ends a channel. YouTube says a first Community Guidelines violation is typically a warning, with a training that lets it expire. Severe abuse is the exception.
- That a Content ID claim is a strike. YouTube says it is not. Disputing one without a valid reason is how it can become one.
- That a limited-ads label is a penalty on the channel. It is about one video’s ads. It is not a strike.
- That crediting the artist makes a track legal to use. Credit is not a license. Chapter 3’s license line is what decides it.
- That the AI label hurts reach. YouTube says the label by itself does not change recommendations or earning eligibility. Whether a viewer trusts an undisclosed fake is a separate question, and it is the one that brings them back.
- That a channel with enough hours will pass the review. Reviewers judge the channel against the monetization policies. Reused and inauthentic content fail regardless of the count.
