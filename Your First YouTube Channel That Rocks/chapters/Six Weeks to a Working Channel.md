*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Six Weeks to a Working Channel

*A calendar for getting the eight videos in front of strangers and reading the first counts. Move the dates. Do not skip the path.*

Use this as a frame, the way you would use a launch calendar for a book. If a step is not true yet — the description does not say who the channel is for, the example in the video is fake, the license on the footage is unread — move the date. A channel that publishes eight unrelated videos in six weeks has followed a posting schedule and missed the point of this book.

Hours below are a modest pace for one person using the free tools in chapter 3. If you have less time, stretch a week into two. Do not cut the deep dive to save an evening. Write the hours each video took on the money sheet in chapter 15. The decision in week six needs them.

## I. Week one: the channel and the switches

Write the viewer sentence, the poor-fit sentence, and twenty titles. Pick the eight from those twenty, one for each job in chapter 5.

The eight jobs are the only set of videos this calendar films. Chapter 2 uses that same set. Week one does not add a second quota.

Set up the channel only as far as a stranger needs: a name the viewer understands, a description whose first lines say who it helps and what arrives each week, and a banner you can read on a phone. Skip the logo exploration. Then do the switches from chapter 1 that take a minute now and a week later: 2-Step Verification, phone verification, the country, and the audience setting. In Studio, open Earn and choose to be notified when the channel is eligible. YouTube’s eligibility page says it will email you when you meet a threshold.

## II. Week two: two videos, not eight

Film “who it is for” and the deep dive. Package them with chapter 2’s limits: keyword first in the title, benefit in the first lines of the description, video chapters on the deep dive. Review the captions before you publish. A misheard name in the automatic captions is a small thing a viewer remembers.

Publish both in the same week if they are ready. Those two go up before the other six exist, on purpose: one tells a stranger whether the channel is for them, and the other is the video most likely to hold them. The order a stranger should watch is a different list. Week five’s last video names it. Show both videos to three people who match the viewer sentence, not three people who like you, and ask them where they would have stopped watching. Write the answer down.

## III. Week three: their words, then the method

Film the problem video in the viewer’s own phrase, then the method video. Show the steps you would be embarrassed to hide. Export both with the settings in chapter 3. End each one by naming the next video, and point the deep dive’s end screen at the method video.

The problem video is the one most people replace with a clever title. Use the words the viewer would type. The method video is the one most people water down, because giving the method away feels like giving the channel away. It is the opposite. The viewer who can finish a small version alone is the viewer who comes back for the next one.

## IV. Week four: proof and one objection

Film proof you actually have. If the proof is small, say it is small. Film one objection video: the reason a sensible viewer hesitates, answered without insulting them.

Make a playlist of the five videos that exist, in watch order. If the account is verified, mark it as a series playlist, as chapter 2 describes. A viewer who finishes one should be offered the next without searching.

## V. Week five: comparison, the path, then the count

Film the comparison: your method versus the usual alternative, including doing nothing. Then film the video whose only job is to name which of the other seven to watch next, in order, by title. That video may be a long video, or a Short that points at one of them. The deep dive may not be a Short. Publish both. Then stop making new videos for several days and read only these counts, written down with the date:

- Views and average view duration on the deep dive.
- Total public watch hours and subscribers, from the Earn tab or the channel’s Analytics.
- Views that came from your own videos, under Suggested videos and Playlists in the traffic sources, which chapter 7 explains.
- The question people actually asked in the comments, in their words.

Do not change the title, the thumbnail, and the opening of the same video in the same week. You would not know which change mattered.

## VI. Week six: one decision

Read the money sheet first, including the hours of every video you have filmed. Then choose one of these, and write the reason in a sentence:

- **Keep the subject and fill any gap in the eight,** because strangers watched more than one video. If all eight are already live, do not invent a ninth topic. Make the path between them clearer.
- **Rewrite the openings and the thumbnails,** because people clicked and left in the first thirty seconds.
- **Rewrite the titles,** because the videos are held well by the few who find them and almost nobody finds them.
- **Narrow the viewer,** because the comments ask for three different things. Pick one of those people and serve them.

Then schedule the next four weeks as any of the eight you still have not published, then answers to the questions in the comments, not a new niche. A second niche in week six is how a channel that might have reached the gate becomes two hobbies, each with half the hours.

## VII. Do this on paper first

Copy the six weeks onto one page with your real dates. Put the viewer sentence at the top. If a week has no task that touches one of the eight, that week is optional. The filming is not optional forever. The path between the videos is not optional at all.

> **Key takeaway:** Six weeks to eight videos on a path. Move a date if a step is not true. Read the first counts in week five. Change one thing in week six.

## VIII. Claims that do not survive week six

- That six weeks is a guarantee of the gate. At this stage the channel is far from 4,000 hours, and further from 8,000. Six weeks is a deadline for learning whether a stranger stays.
- That you should wait to publish until all eight videos are perfect. Two honest videos in public teach you more than eight private drafts.
- That a quiet week means the subject is dead. Quiet means you do not have evidence yet. Evidence is watch time and second views, not a feeling about the niche.
- That ads can replace weeks two through five. Promoted views do not count toward the Partner Program, as chapter 8 explains. If the path is missing, you paid to show strangers a gap.
- That changing everything in week six is decisive. One change, then another quiet measurement, is decisive. A new banner, a new niche, and a new style is a new project.

A last limit. This calendar will not negotiate with a missing path. If the videos do not point at each other by the end of week five, week six is spent fixing that, not filming.
