# Ads, RPM, and the Shorts Pool

*How YouTube turns watch time into ad money, which numbers in Studio describe it, and why the same views can pay twice as much on one channel as on another.*

*Layer: Use after eligibility. Workbook: the ads and Premium line, and RPM, on the money sheet (chapter 15, §V).*

Ad revenue is the stream most people mean when they say a channel is monetized. It is also the one most often misunderstood, because the numbers creators quote to each other — “my RPM is eight dollars” — mix different things, measured different ways, on channels with different viewers. This chapter is how the money is made, how Studio reports it, and which levers are yours.

Every rate below is an official rule, checked in October 2026. Your own Revenue tab is the only number that describes your channel.

## I. Where the ad money comes from

Inside the Partner Program, with the Watch Page Monetization Module accepted, a long-form video can earn from ads shown before, during, after, and around it on the watch page. That includes the video embedded on another site in YouTube’s player. Official rule: YouTube’s [partner earnings overview](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72902) pays creators 55% of net revenue from ads on their long-form watch pages.

[YouTube’s ad formats page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2467968) says that when you turn on ads for a new long-form video, YouTube shows pre-roll, post-roll, skippable, or non-skippable ads when appropriate. You no longer pick formats one by one. Two choices remain yours:

- **Monetization on or off, per video.** In Studio, Content, Monetization. Turning ads on is also a statement: YouTube’s monetization page says you confirm you have all the necessary rights to the video’s visual and audio elements. The rights log is how you know you do.
- **Mid-roll ads.** [YouTube’s mid-roll page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6175006) says monetized videos eight minutes or longer can carry ads during the video. You can let YouTube place them, place them yourself, or both. YouTube says slots at natural breakpoints — a pause in the audio, a change of scene — are more likely to serve an ad, and slots placed mid-sentence or mid-action are less likely to. Video chapters are natural breakpoints you already have.

The ad formats page also describes “Watch While” ads on mobile, which serve automatically on monetizing videos that cannot carry mid-rolls: videos between two and eight minutes, or longer videos with mid-rolls turned off. Viewers can close them.

## II. YouTube Premium and Premium Lite

A viewer who pays for YouTube Premium sees no ads, and still pays your channel. YouTube’s August 2026 announcement and its Changes page describe the split: 30% of net Premium subscription revenue, and 60% of net Premium Lite revenue, goes into a pool distributed to creators by member watch time and views. From that distribution, creators receive 55% for long-form and 45% for Shorts.

The same post says Premium Lite is expanding to every country where YouTube offers Premium, and that when a viewer signs up for Premium, partners on average earn more than when that viewer was watching ads.

You do not switch Premium revenue on separately. It comes with the Watch Page module for long videos and the Shorts module for Shorts.

## III. CPM and RPM: two numbers, two audiences

Studio shows both. [YouTube’s ad revenue analytics page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314357) defines them.

| Metric | Whose number | What it measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CPM | The advertiser’s | What advertisers paid per 1,000 ad impressions, before YouTube’s share. Ads and Premium only. Monetized views only. |
| Playback-based CPM | The advertiser’s | What advertisers paid per 1,000 playbacks that showed at least one ad. Often higher than CPM, because one playback can carry two ads. |
| RPM | Yours | Your total revenue, after YouTube’s share, per 1,000 engaged views. Includes ads, Premium, memberships, Super Chat, and Super Stickers. |

RPM is always lower than CPM, for two reasons the page gives: it is calculated after YouTube’s share, and it includes views that showed no ad at all. A view may carry no ad because the video is not advertiser-friendly, because ads are off, because no advertiser was targeting that viewer, because the viewer has Premium, or because they saw an ad recently.

So CPM answers “what is this audience worth to an advertiser?” RPM answers “what did this channel earn per thousand views, from everything YouTube pays?” Planning uses RPM. CPM explains why RPM moved.

## IV. Why the same views pay differently

The analytics page names three reasons CPM moves, and they explain most of what creators compare in forums.

- **Time of year.** Advertisers tend to bid higher before holidays. A December and a January with the same views can pay very differently.
- **Where the viewers are.** Advertisers choose geographies, and ad markets differ by country. A shift in where your views come from shifts CPM.
- **Which ad formats are available.** Different formats carry different prices.

To those, add the ones you control: whether the video is advertiser-friendly (chapter 10), whether it is long enough for mid-rolls, and whether viewers stay long enough to see a second break. The subject matters too, through which advertisers want that audience. That is why chapter 1 warned against choosing a subject only for its rate. You cannot hold an audience you do not want to make videos for.

> Case study: Deep Pocket Monster, a YouTube channel about opening Pokémon card packs, launched in January 2021 with no prior audience in that niche. Its creator has described reaching 100,000 subscribers within about a year and a typical range of $35,000 to $50,000 a month from ad revenue. One 2022 video sat well outside that range. It reached roughly 28 million views, briefly ranked third on YouTube’s trending page, and brought in more than $100,000 in ad revenue on its own, pushing that month past the usual run. A single collectibles video outearning what many channels make in a year in a lower-demand niche is not a trick of virality. Collectibles are a subject advertisers pay well to reach, which is the whole point of this section: the view count did not change. The audience behind it did.

None of that changes the mechanics underneath — advertiser demand moves the price per view, not the arithmetic that turns a view into a payment. The arithmetic itself is worth walking through once.

> Worked example. Invented numbers, to show the arithmetic only. A long-form channel has 100,000 views in a month. 60% of the views show at least one ad, so there are 60,000 monetized playbacks, carrying 80,000 ad impressions. Advertisers pay a CPM of $10, so they spend $800. The channel’s 55% is $440. Premium viewers add $60 from the subscription pool. Revenue: $500 on 100,000 views, an RPM of $5. Now the same channel’s next video passes eight minutes with two honest breakpoints, and the impressions per monetized playback rise from 1.33 to 1.6: 96,000 impressions, $960 of advertiser spend, $528 of ad revenue for the channel, $588 with the same Premium share: an RPM of about $5.90. Same views. The difference is length the job needed and breaks placed where the viewer was already pausing.

## V. The Shorts Creator Pool

Shorts ads run between videos in the Shorts feed, not beside one video, so YouTube pools them. [YouTube’s Shorts monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12504220) describe four steps each month:

1. Revenue from ads between Shorts in the feed is added together.
2. Part of it covers music licensing. A Short with no music puts all its share in the Creator Pool. A Short with one track puts half in. Two tracks, a third.
3. The Creator Pool is divided by each monetizing creator’s share of engaged Shorts views, country by country.
4. The creator keeps 45% of their allocation, whether or not they used music.

The page’s own example: in a country with $100,000 of Shorts feed ad revenue, a Creator Pool of $90,000, and a Short with 1% of engaged views, the allocation is $900 and the creator earns $405.

Three rules matter for planning.

- **The module date.** Shorts revenue accrues from the day you accept the Shorts Monetization Module. Views before that date earn nothing.
- **The 2027 floor.** From 1 February 2027, a channel earns Shorts ad and Premium revenue in a month only if it had at least 10 million qualified Shorts views in the 90 days ending on the 15th of the previous month. Below that, the channel stays in the program and keeps earning on long-form. Shorts revenue resumes automatically above the line.
- **What is ineligible.** Unedited clips of others’ films or TV, reuploads, compilations with nothing original added, bot views, and views of Shorts that are not advertiser-friendly do not count. Shorts over one minute that contain claimed content are blocked from monetization.

The same page adds one 2027 change: from 1 February 2027, Shorts ads targeted to groups of five or fewer channels earn direct revenue, with the creator keeping 45% of that ad’s net revenue, on top of the Creator Pool.

For a channel below 10 million Shorts views every 90 days — most new channels — the practical reading (author recommendation): from February 2027, long-form is the ad income, and Shorts are how strangers find the long videos.

## VI. Reading the Revenue tab

Once you are monetizing, Studio’s Analytics adds a Revenue tab. Read it once a week, and copy these onto the money sheet:

- **Estimated revenue,** all sources, for the week.
- **RPM,** for the channel and for the deep dive. Note when it moves and check the three causes in §IV before deciding the content changed.
- **Revenue sources,** to see ads, Premium, memberships, and Supers separately. RPM cannot tell you which one moved.
- **Revenue by video,** for which long videos carry the channel.

The analytics page lists what RPM leaves out: brand deals and sponsorships outside YouTube’s own partnership tool, and any other income earned through the channel. Chapter 13 covers those. They have their own lines on the money sheet.

*In short:* Long-form ads pay 55% of net revenue, Shorts pay 45% of a pooled allocation, and Premium pays from a subscription pool. Plan by RPM, explain it with CPM. Mid-rolls at natural breaks on videos eight minutes or longer are the lever you control.

## VII. Claims that do not survive the Revenue tab

- That revenue equals CPM times views. YouTube says CPM is what advertisers pay, before its share, and not every view has an ad.
- That a falling RPM means the videos got worse. It can fall when more views are unmonetized, or when viewers shift country, or after the holidays.
- That more mid-rolls are always better. YouTube says breaks at natural pauses are more likely to serve. A break mid-sentence is a slot that often stays empty and a viewer who may leave.
- That Premium viewers are lost money. They pay through the subscription pool, and YouTube says partners earn more on average when a viewer switches to Premium.
- That an RPM someone posted is a target. It describes their viewers, their season, and their subject.
