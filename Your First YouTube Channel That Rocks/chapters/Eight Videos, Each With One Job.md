*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Eight Videos, Each With One Job

*A channel that reaches the gate is a small set of videos that lead into each other, not a pile of posts.*

Once the viewer is named, the channel needs videos that do work. “Content” is not work. A video either answers a question a specific viewer already has and points them at the next answer, or it fills a calendar. Calendars feel productive. Viewers who watch three videos in a row fill the hour bar.

You do not need thirty videos to learn whether the channel can hold a stranger. You need a short set you can finish, each aimed at one question the viewer already has. Make them the cheap way chapter 3 describes. Record them the way chapter 4 describes. Package them the way chapter 2 describes. Link each one to the next.

## I. Give every video one job

Before you write a title, write the job in a single line: “After this video, the viewer can [do one thing], and knows which video answers the next question.” If the line needs a second “and,” split the video.

The list below is the order a stranger should watch. They have to recognize themselves before a method is worth hearing. They have to see the method before proof means anything. They have to see proof before a comparison is a choice. The last video names that path, so the set is not a feed. Publishing can start earlier. The calendar in chapter 6 puts “who it is for” and the deep dive up in week two, because those two are the ones a stranger is most likely to find and stay for.

The jobs, in watch order:

1. **Who it is for.** The viewer recognizes themselves in the first ten seconds. People who are not the viewer can leave without feeling tricked. This one can also be the channel trailer.
2. **The problem, in their words.** Use the phrase they would type, not the clever name you prefer. Chapter 2’s rule still applies: describe the situation, not a crisis word that can limit ads on the video.
3. **The method, given away.** Show the real steps. A withheld step is a leak in trust, and a viewer who feels tricked does not come back.
4. **Proof it can be done.** A before and after, a worked example, a walk-through with numbers you actually have. Invented results are a lie, and a lie in the comments outlives the video.
5. **The comparison.** What you recommend versus the common alternative, including “do it by hand.” Say who should choose the other one.
6. **The objection.** Time, skill, cost, “I tried this before.” Answer one objection per video. Do not stack them into a speech.
7. **The deep dive.** The long, complete version of the subject: every step, the hard part slowed down, the questions from the comments answered. This is the video built to be watched for a long time, and the one that can pass eight minutes honestly, which matters for mid-roll ads once the channel is in the Partner Program. It is never only a Short. Hours watched in the Shorts feed do not fill the hour bar.
8. **The next video.** Tell them which of the seven to watch next, by name. A channel is a path, not a feed. This video may be a long video, or a Short that points at one of them.

That is the first eight, and it is the only production load in this book. Chapter 2 uses the same eight. So does the six-week calendar. A Short may point at one of them. It does not add a second quota. You do not need a ninth idea until these eight exist and a stranger has watched two of them in a row.

## II. The deep dive is the watch-hour engine

The gate in chapter 9 counts hours. A three-minute video watched to the end adds three minutes. A twelve-minute deep dive watched halfway adds six. That arithmetic is why the deep dive gets the most care, not the most padding.

Open it with the result, the way chapter 4 opens every video. Lay out the steps with video chapters in the description so a viewer who wants one part can jump there instead of leaving. Slow down on the hard step. End by naming the one video to watch next.

Keep it as long as the job and no longer. A padded twelve minutes has a retention graph that slopes downward from the first minute, and YouTube’s recommendation pages say the system learns from how much people watched and whether they were glad. A tight nine minutes with a flat graph beats a padded fifteen on both counts.

## III. Titles name the viewer’s job

Use chapter 2’s formula — keyword, benefit, hook — and add one constraint: the keyword is the viewer’s problem, not your brand. “How I grew my channel” is about you. “How to price a bathroom repair so the quote matches the work” is about a viewer, if that is your subject. The second one can be searched. The first one can only flatter you.

Write the title before you film, and write the one action you will ask for at the end in the same sitting. If you cannot say the action, you do not know the job yet.

## IV. Stop when the set is done

Eight finished videos aimed at one viewer beat forty videos aimed at a posting streak. If you can publish one of the eight a week, do that. If a week is too fast to keep the teaching accurate, slow down. A wrong video that contradicts the method does more damage than a quiet week.

When the eight are live, look at one thing before you invent video nine: does a stranger who finds one of them watch a second? Not views. Not likes. The second video. Chapter 7 shows where Studio reports it. If nobody moves on, the next video is not a new topic. It is a clearer path from a video you already have — a better ending, a named next video, an end screen, a series playlist.

## V. Three people who started with what was on the desk

These interviews are not income promises. They are not proof that the eight jobs work. They show that the first videos of channels that later became large were small, cheap, and aimed at one person’s question.

Marques Brownlee told The Verge he turned on a webcam so the next laptop buyer would not miss a detail the reviews had skipped. On Recode he told people who ask which camera to buy to start with the gear they already have, and he recalled a Safari-for-Windows video reaching on the order of 6,000 views. Andrew Rea told Mashed the first video was one burger he wanted to taste. Hannah Hart told The Verge the first year was a webcam, the laptop on a stack of books, recorded in Photo Booth and cut in iMovie. She said the first episode reached about 80,000 views, and that she was still filming without a crew years later.

What the three share is not luck. Each first video answered a question a specific viewer had, and each channel kept answering the next one. Their later studios are not the lesson. The eight jobs are the method. The gear can be the gear you have.

## VI. Do this before you batch-film

List the eight jobs with a working title under each. Mark the deep dive. Confirm each title would make sense to the viewer in chapter 1, and that none of them require a tool or a claim you cannot show. Film “who it is for” and the deep dive first. Those two are enough to test whether a stranger stays. That pair is filming order. The numbered list above is watch order. The eighth video names the watch order.

> **Key takeaway:** Eight videos, eight jobs, one path between them. The deep dive fills the hour bar. When the set is live, count the second video watched, not the view count.

## VII. Claims that do not survive the retention graph

- That viral videos build a channel by themselves. A video can be widely watched by people who never return. Wide and one-off is a lottery ticket, not a channel.
- That you need daily uploads to reach the gate. You need videos people finish and a path to the next one. Frequency does not replace that path.
- That proof can be a screenshot you found. Proof is a result you can explain. If you do not have one yet, show the method on a small real example and do not invent a larger one.
- That the next video should chase whatever got the most views. Chase the video that led people to a second one, or fix the one that should have and did not.
- That longer is always better for watch time. A padded video loses viewers early. Length that the job needs, watched, is what counts.
- That you need the studio those channels have now. Brownlee, Rea, and Hart filmed with what was on the desk. A close microphone and a named viewer matter more.

A last limit. This chapter will not hand you a niche or a script for your life. It will refuse a content calendar that has no path between its videos.
