*A working guide for creators who want a channel, not a hobby*

# Eight Videos, Each With One Job

*A channel that reaches the gate is a small set of videos that lead into each other, not a pile of posts.*

*Layer: Start now. Workbook: the video-job sheet (chapter 15, §III) starts here — one block per video.*

Once the viewer is named, the channel needs videos that do work. A video either answers a question a specific viewer already has and points them at the next answer, or it fills a calendar. Calendars feel productive. Viewers who watch three videos in a row fill the hour bar.

You do not need thirty videos to learn whether the channel can hold a stranger. You need a short set you can finish. Make them the cheap way chapter 3 describes, record them the way chapter 4 describes, package them the way chapter 2 describes, and link each one to the next.

## I. Give every video one job

Before you write a title, write the job in one line on the video-job sheet: “After this video, the viewer can [do one thing], and knows which video answers the next question.” If the line needs a second “and,” split the video.

The list below is the order a stranger should watch. They have to recognize themselves before a method is worth hearing, see the method before proof means anything, and see proof before a comparison is a choice. Author recommendation: these eight are the whole production load in this book.

1. **Who it is for.** The viewer recognizes themselves in the first ten seconds. People who are not the viewer can leave without feeling tricked. This one can double as the channel trailer.
2. **The problem, in their words.** The phrase they would type, not the clever name you prefer.
3. **The method, given away.** The real steps. A withheld step is a leak in trust.
4. **Proof it can be done.** A before and after, a worked example, a walk-through with numbers you actually have. Invented results are a lie, and a lie in the comments outlives the video.
5. **The comparison.** What you recommend versus the common alternative, including “do it by hand.” Say who should choose the other one.
6. **The objection.** Time, skill, cost, “I tried this before.” One objection per video.
7. **The deep dive.** The long, complete version: every step, the hard part slowed down, the questions from the comments answered. The video built to be watched for a long time, and the one that can honestly pass eight minutes for mid-roll ads later.
8. **The next video.** Which of the seven to watch next, by name. A channel is a path, not a feed.

Four of the eight must be long videos, because only long-form time fills the hour bar: who it is for, the problem, the method, and the deep dive. The other four can be long videos, or Shorts that point at a long video that already does that job. The deep dive is never only a Short. You do not need a ninth idea until these eight exist and a stranger has watched two of them in a row.

## II. The deep dive is the watch-hour engine

The gate in chapter 9 counts hours. A three-minute video watched to the end adds three minutes. A twelve-minute deep dive watched halfway adds six. That arithmetic is why the deep dive gets the most care, not the most padding.

Open it with the result. Lay out the steps with video chapters so a viewer who wants one part can jump there instead of leaving. Slow down on the hard step. End by naming the one video to watch next.

Keep it as long as the job and no longer. A padded twelve minutes has a retention graph that slopes down from the first minute, and YouTube’s recommendation pages say the system learns from how much people watched and whether they were glad (documented platform guidance). A tight nine minutes with a flat graph beats a padded fifteen.

## III. Titles name the viewer’s job

Use chapter 2’s formula, with one constraint: the keyword is the viewer’s problem, not your brand. “How I grew my channel” is about you. “How to price a bathroom repair so the quote matches the work” is about a viewer, if that is your subject.

Write the title before you film, and write the one action you will ask for at the end in the same sitting. Both go on the video-job sheet. If you cannot say the action, you do not know the job yet.

## IV. Stop when the set is done

Eight finished videos aimed at one viewer beat forty aimed at a posting streak. If you can publish one of the eight a week, do that. If a week is too fast to keep the teaching accurate, slow down. A wrong video that contradicts the method does more damage than a quiet week.

When the eight are live, look at one thing before you invent video nine: does a stranger who finds one of them watch a second? Not views. Not likes. The second video. Chapter 7, §III shows where Studio reports it. If nobody moves on, the next video is not a new topic. It is a clearer path from a video you already have — a better ending, a named next video, an end screen, a series playlist.

## V. Do this before you batch-film

Fill one block of the video-job sheet per video: the job, a working title, the opening sentence, the hard step, and the next video. Mark the deep dive. Confirm none of them needs a tool or a claim you cannot show. Film “who it is for” and the deep dive first; those two are enough to test whether a stranger stays. That pair is filming order. The numbered list is watch order, and the eighth video names it.

*In short:* Eight videos, eight jobs, one path between them. The deep dive fills the hour bar. When the set is live, count the second video watched, not the view count.

## VI. Claims that do not survive the retention graph

- That viral videos build a channel by themselves. Wide and one-off is a lottery ticket, not a channel.
- That proof can be a screenshot you found. Proof is a result you can explain. Without one, show the method on a small real example.
- That the next video should chase whatever got the most views. Chase the video that led people to a second one.
- That you need a studio first. A close microphone and a named viewer matter more than a set.
