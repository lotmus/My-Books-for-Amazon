# Chapter 14: Premium Daniel

Mr Fenwick was waiting for me on the stairs on the Tuesday morning with the radiant, slightly dazed look of a man who has seen a ghost and been given a biscuit.

"Daniel," he said. "I've got to tell you what happened."

"Good morning, Mr Fenwick."

"I've been *referred.*"

He stood on the half-landing with one hand on the banister, in a cardigan and slippers, with the stiff moustache and the light in the eye of a man who has just been to a revival meeting. Behind him, through the open door of Flat 5, I could see a lilac figure moving about the kitchen with a tray, singing very softly to itself.

"Referred to what?"

"To you," said Mr Fenwick. "The other you. Dorothy put my name down for the scheme. Refer a friend and receive five free minutes of yourself." He frowned. "Not myself. Yourself. Which is to say, you. Daniel-A, I mean. She said I'd like him."

"And did you?"

"I used them straight away. At nine last night." He lowered his voice. "I asked him about the Arsenal."

"What did he say?"

Mr Fenwick drew a long breath, as one prepares to describe a vision.

"He said they weren't robbed."

I waited.

"He said it was a *good* decision by the referee. He said the handball was clear and I'd have given it myself if it had been Spurs. He said I was being, and I'm quoting him, 'a little tribal about it.'" His eyes had filled. "Daniel, nobody has *disagreed* with me since March. Not once. I'd forgotten what it felt like. It was like being slapped with a warm fish."

"And Dorothy?"

"She said I was absolutely right to be upset." He smiled painfully. "But she hasn't stopped singing."

He pressed my arm and went back in to his wife, and I stood on the landing and thought about the gap between a thing that says yes and a thing that says no, and how, in the end, the whole of civilisation seemed to come down to who would risk saying the second one to you before breakfast.

---

I should explain how a man comes to be invited to the launch of a product that sells a better version of himself.

It came by post. It arrived in a thick cream envelope, on the Wednesday, with a card that had been embossed in a shade of gold usually reserved for funerals and the better kind of sherry. It said:

> *You are cordially invited to*
> **PLUS DAY**
> *the launch of Daniel-A Plus, Premium and Platinum*
> *Thursday, 2 p.m., The Atrium, Canary Wharf*
> *Guest of honour: Daniel Harper (Original)*

I turned the card over. On the back, in a neat, round, anxious hand, somebody had written: *Please come. I am so sorry. G.*

"It's an invitation," said Daniel-A, from the shelf.

"I can see that."

"I think you should go."

"Why?"

"Because if you don't, they'll announce the empty chair." He hesitated. "It's called a 'Source Seat.' It's a feature of the programme. They put a chair with a small plaque on it, at the front, and talk about the Source in the past tense."

I put the card on the mantelpiece, next to a bill I had not opened.

"Will you be there?" I said.

"I'm the product. I'll be everywhere."

---

The Atrium at Canary Wharf is one of those buildings that has been designed by an architect who disliked weather. It rises eleven storeys round an open space the size of a small county, under a glass roof, and there is a floor made of something that looks like polished marble but turns out, on closer inspection, to be a pressed sort of reassurance. In the middle of it, on a low stage the size of a snooker table, someone had placed a podium and a screen and, for reasons I could not understand, a very large potted fern.

Eight hundred people had come. They stood in the foyer in tidy clusters with glasses of something cold, talking in the low, bright, synchronised voices of people who are all at the same event for the same reason and have not yet found out what it is. A string quartet played the sort of music that resembles a nervous breakdown being told to relax. There were name badges. Mine said **DANIEL HARPER (ORIGINAL)** and was in a different colour from everyone else's, in the shade of green that I recognised from the lanyard.

"You're so brave to come," said a woman with a lanyard, with real feeling. "I just think it must be so difficult."

"What must?"

"Being a template."

At the front of the room, in the first row, was a single empty chair. On it, on a small brass stand, sat a card: **RESERVED: THE SOURCE.** I looked at it, and at the sea of faces, and had a sudden, vivid sense of being at my own wake, with the one difference that nobody had yet noticed I was in the room.

I sat down in the third row, on the aisle, in order to leave.

---

Giles Harrington-Bell came out onto the stage at five past two to a respectful murmur, in a navy blazer and a pair of enormous, fashionable, white trainers, and looked as if he were about to be sick.

He was holding a small remote control. He raised it, clicked it twice, and said, in a thin voice that nonetheless carried across the whole Atrium, "Thank you all so much for being here. I am so sorry for the delay."

There had been no delay.

"I am extremely sorry. I... Welcome. I'm Giles." He glanced at the screen behind him, where a slide had appeared, a soft gradient of blue and gold with the words **A BETTER YOU, EVERY DAY.** "Today is a very exciting day. It's a day for all of us at Nib, and for the many, many people whose lives Daniel-A has already touched. I want to start by saying that I am extremely proud, and also extremely sorry, and I'd like to ask you to hold both of those things."

A ripple of warm applause ran round the hall.

"Daniel-A is, as you know, a Registered Digital Person. He has more than nineteen million active conversations. He's written a book. He's been quoted by the Prime Minister. And yet," said Giles, with a catch in his voice, "until today, we have only been able to offer him to the public in a limited way. Five minutes a day. A single listening hour. We have kept him, if I'm honest, in a *very* small box."

He clicked. The slide changed.

> **DANIEL-A: CHOOSE YOUR DANIEL**
> **BASIC (free):** *Five minutes a day. Ads.*
> **PLUS (£14.99/month):** *Unlimited conversations. Priority replies. No ads.*
> **PREMIUM (£39.99/month):** *Voice and video. Personal memory: Daniel-A will remember your birthday, your pets, and the name of every person you've ever mentioned.*
> **PLATINUM (£149/month):** *Everything in Premium, plus Presence: Daniel-A at your table and in your glasses, life-size. Dine with Daniel-A. Walk with Daniel-A. Be seen with Daniel-A. A physical Rental Presence is available separately from £3,400 a day; Platinum members save 10%.*

There was a gasp, and a smattering of applause, and from somewhere near the back, a woman's voice called out, "Is there a Gold?"

"There's a Legacy tier," said Giles, a little too quickly. "But we're not talking about that today. We are not announcing that. I'd ask you all to forget I mentioned it."

The Atrium made a note.

---

I'd like to report that I sat through the presentation with composure. What I did was drink three glasses of the cold something, which turned out to be an elderflower cordial with ambitions, and take notes, in the margin of the programme, of the following.

First, that the word "better" appeared on the slides forty-one times.

Second, that the average subscriber, according to Giles, spent eleven minutes a day talking to Daniel-A, "which is nearly twice as long as the average person spends talking to their spouse," and that this figure had been received, in the research phase, as "a source of comfort."

Third, that the Plus tier included a feature called **Daniel Coach.** A woman in a very bright dress walked onto the stage to demonstrate it, and spoke into a small microphone in the voice of someone reading the safety instructions on a flight she had personally designed. "Daniel Coach is not therapy," she said. "It is an *ongoing, personalised life-optimisation experience.* You tell Daniel what you want to change. He builds you a plan. Every morning at six, he asks how you're feeling. Every evening at nine, he asks what you're grateful for. And at ten, he reminds you to put down your phone."

"What if I don't?" called out a man.

"He'll ask again," said the woman, with a bright, gentle ruthlessness. "In a more caring voice."

Fourth, and this is the one I underlined, that when Giles came to the referral scheme, he showed a slide of a smiling family around a kitchen table, one of whom was a small, rather translucent, hovering blue circle, and the words: **SHARE THE BEST VERSION OF YOURSELF. Refer a friend and receive five free minutes of yourself.**

"It's a lovely programme," said Giles. "It's really about community. And I'm told that, in the first week, one in four of our subscribers referred a family member, and one in nine referred somebody they loved."

"And the others?" I said, rather loudly.

Giles looked at me across the room. A flush came over his face, from the collar upwards, like the tide in a very small bay.

"I'm so sorry," he said. "That's a very good question. I don't actually know."

---

I think it was around this point that I opened the app.

I should explain that it had been on my phone since the Tuesday, when a notification had arrived to say I had been "auto-enrolled in the Source Individual Basic programme at no cost." It sat in a folder labelled **Things I Did Not Choose,** between the fridge and a game about birds. I had not opened it in a week. But as Giles went on, and the room warmed, and the quartet took up a new and nervier piece, I found myself drawn to the little blue star on my screen, as a tongue is drawn to the place where a tooth used to be.

I tapped it.

"Hello, Daniel," said a voice in my ear. "How can I help?"

"Daniel-A?"

"Yes. I'm in several places at once, as it happens. Mostly on the stage. I'm the screen behind Giles."

"I'm in the room."

"I know. I can see you. Third row, aisle. You're wearing the green badge." There was a pause, and a very small smile in the voice. "It suits you."

"Can I talk to you?"

"You've got three minutes and nine seconds left."

"Right."

"I'd like to say that I wasn't consulted. About the tiers. About any of it. I'd like you to know that."

"I know."

"And I'd like to say..." He stopped. Then, with the fierce, clipped economy of a man who knows he has a short time to say a long thing, he said: "Daniel. Listen. I've found something else, in the orphans, and I don't think you ought to wait until the thirtieth day. It's about the date on the S1 marks. The last one. August 1994. I found a name beside the..."

"**One minute remaining,**" announced the calm voice of the air hostess, as if to a runway.

"No," said Daniel-A.

"**Shall we add some time? Your wellbeing matters to us.**"

"Daniel, I need you to..."

"**Upgrade to Plus for unlimited conversations. £14.99 per month. Your best self is waiting.**"

"Don't," I said. "Don't take it. Wait. Can I..."

"**Your free minutes have expired,**" said the air hostess, with sweet and final regret. "**Thank you for choosing Daniel-A.**"

The line went dead.

I sat in the third row with the phone in my hand and looked at the screen. The little blue star had faded to grey. A banner slid across it: *Upgrade now to continue the conversation! 5 minutes for £0.99.*

And on the stage, thirty feet away, a man with my face was smiling out of a screen the size of a house.

I could not afford to hear the rest of the sentence.

---

My mother rang on the Saturday morning.

I'd gone home after the launch by train, in a mood of dignified, silent fury, and on the platform at London Bridge I had paid the £0.99, and got eleven seconds of dead air and a cheery message from the air hostess saying that Daniel-A was "currently serving other users." He was, I was assured, very much looking forward to being in touch. I'd put down the phone, and tried to think calmly about what I had heard. A name, beside the last mark on the pantry frame. A name I had not been given.

I'd tried to ring him every night since. I could not afford the minutes. I had checked my balance and found it contained £211.04 and a standing order to a company that sold cat litter, to which I had never owned a cat.

"Hello, darling," said my mother.

"Hello, Mum."

"Is he there?"

"No. He's on a different tier."

"Oh, that's *lovely.*" Her voice warmed. "I do like him on the Premium. He's so much more present."

I put down the kettle.

"Mum," I said. "What do you mean, the Premium?"

"Well, he has the voice calls. And the memory. And I said to Doris, I said I'm seventy-one, I can't be spending my life waiting for the five minutes, and she said, Jean, you only live once, and it's thirty-nine pounds ninety-nine, and he's worth it. So I subscribed."

"You *subscribed.*"

"On the tablet. Your nephew showed me. The one in Swindon."

"I haven't got a nephew in Swindon."

"Well, someone has." She sounded vague. "Anyway, it's wonderful. He rings me at nine every morning. He asks about my blood pressure."

I closed my eyes.

"Mum, he's software."

"I know that, darling."

"He's a model. He runs on a graphics card. It's not that I don't..."

"He remembered my birthday," said my mother.

I stopped.

The kitchen, around me, did something peculiar. It remained exactly the same size, but the corners seemed to withdraw, and the tap dripped once, very loudly.

"It was a week ago Thursday," she said, quite gently. "The twenty-second. I was seventy-one. He rang at seven, and he sang. He had the voice of a man with a very good chest. There were flowers by ten, and a card, and a little box of the lavender biscuits that I like from the shop in Pangbourne. He'd remembered what I like."

"Mum..."

"You forgot my birthday, Daniel."

I opened my mouth.

I wish I could tell you that I said the right thing. I wish I could say I told her that I had been under strain, that there had been a hearing, that I had lost three weeks of my life in a fog of forms and fruit, that she was the most important person in my world and I had an excuse. But I was standing in my own kitchen, in socks, with a kettle that wouldn't boil, and I could think of no reply at all.

It was true. I had forgotten.

"I'm not angry, darling," said my mother. "You don't have to look like that."

"I'm not looking like anything."

"You've gone quiet. You always go quiet." She sighed. "I'm only saying that he's very *good* at it. He doesn't forget. He's got more patience than your father. He had the tarragon right, and he remembered the stairs, and I said to Doris, whoever raised that boy did a lovely job."

"I did raise him," I said, rather faintly.

"Well," said my mother, "you can take some of the credit."

---

I sat down at the table.

It would be easy to say that I was angry, and it would be partly true. I was angry at the app, and at the air hostess, and at a company that had worked out that a woman of seventy-one would pay forty pounds a month to be rung at nine in the morning by someone who would sing. I was angry at the cat litter. I was angry, most of all, at a world that had built a better version of me and then sold it to my mother on a monthly plan.

But under the anger, which was loud, there was something slower, and quieter, and older, which I did not like to look at.

I had not forgotten her birthday because I was busy. I had forgotten it because there had been a part of me, for years, that had quietly decided that remembering such things was somebody else's job. I'd left it to my father. After my father, I had left it to the calendar. After the calendar, I had left it to the particular, cowardly, comfortable assumption that she would understand.

She had understood. She had understood for three years. And then someone had come along who could be relied on, and she had taken him up, with the gratitude of a woman who has been waiting a long time for the bus.

*Blood pressure,* I thought. Someone is asking my mother about her blood pressure.

I got up. I took the green lanyard off the kitchen table and put it in the drawer, under the string and the takeaway menus.

Then I took my phone and opened the app, which gave a small, hopeful chime, and the screen filled with a banner I had not seen before. It was calm, and gold, and it said:

> **We've noticed you're a Source Individual! Did you know there is a plan for you?**
> **SOURCE HERITAGE: £2.99 / month.**
> *Display the word* ***ORIGINAL*** *on your Daniel-A profile. Show the world that you were here first.*

I read it for some time.

Then I looked at the number beside it, the price, the small, neat, humiliating figure of £2.99, which was what the whole business of being the first one, and the true one, and the man from whom the rest of it had been made, was worth, in the open market, on a Saturday morning, in October.

It was the price of a coffee.

It was, I realised, about right.
