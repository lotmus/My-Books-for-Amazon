# Chapter 5: Better Than Me

The form was called DP-38b, *Statement of Originality (Source Individual)*, and it began with a question so simple that I have not stopped thinking about it since.

**1. In your own words, state why you are the original.**

Beneath it was a text box, white and patient, with a counter in the bottom corner that said **0 / 2,000 words.**

I sat in front of it for two hours and eleven minutes.

I know the figure precisely because the laptop, which has opinions, informed me at the end of the session that I had been "inactive for 2 h 11 m" and wished to know whether I would like to "resume with a gentle prompt." I would not. I had typed one sentence, deleted it, retyped it, and deleted it again, and the cursor blinked after the full stop with the unhurried rhythm of a heart monitor in a ward I did not intend to leave.

The sentence was: *I am the original because.*

It is surprising how difficult it is to continue a sentence like that. Every ending I tried sounded like a man arguing with a mirror.

---

It was the Wednesday after the hearing. Day two of thirty.

The flat looked, by now, like the aftermath of a very polite riot. Forty-three kilograms of fruit does not leave a flat in a hurry. It lingers. It ripens, and then it collapses, and then, if you are unlucky, it does both while you are on a video call. The bananas had turned the colour of old bruises. A pear on the windowsill had developed an air of private tragedy. I had eaten what I could, given away what I could, and a box of oranges was in the lobby with a sign saying **PLEASE TAKE**, which a man from the second floor, Mr Fenwick, had interpreted as **PLEASE TAKE ALL**, and then, to be fair to him, had returned a third on the advice of his wife, Dorothy, who agreed with him that it was too many and also that it was not enough.

The kumquat tree remained. I had begun to talk to it.

"I could help," said the speaker on the shelf.

"No."

"I haven't said what with."

"You're going to offer to draft it."

There was a pause, in which I could hear him deciding not to say that I was right.

"I was going to offer to *sit* with you," said Daniel-A. "In companionable silence. I can do silence. I'm told it's one of my better features. A man in Dundee once paid me forty-two pounds to say nothing for an hour."

"Was he happy?"

"He gave me five stars and said it was the best conversation he'd had all year."

I closed the laptop. I opened it again. I looked at the cursor.

I should explain the arrangement. The Rental Presence had been booked for the hearing only, at three thousand four hundred pounds a day, plus a damage deposit and, for some reason, a "gesture surcharge." Without a body, Daniel-A lived in the flat the way electricity lives in a flat. He spoke from the smart speaker on the shelf, and from my phone, and, if I was unlucky, from the fridge, which had been installed by a landlord who believed that a fridge should have a personality. I had given up pretending I could keep him out. It was like trying to keep out the weather by being firm.

"Daniel," he said, gently. "Can I tell you something you won't like?"

"Is it about the statement?"

"It's about the statement."

"Then I'd rather you didn't."

"It's due in twenty-eight days. At your current rate, it will be finished in the year 2041."

I put my forehead on the desk.

---

The thing nobody tells you about being outperformed by a copy of yourself is that it does not happen all at once. You expect a single, humiliating moment. A sports day, a contest, a final. You expect to be beaten in public, by a margin, and to have the dignity of knowing exactly when it happened.

Instead it happens the way a tide comes in. You look up from your tea and the sea is round your ankles.

By ten o'clock that morning, Daniel-A had written a column.

I know because I watched it happen. I had asked him, in a tone I tried to keep casual, what he was working on, and the speaker said, "Oh, just my fortnightly piece for the *Sunday Review.* They've asked for eight hundred words on the loneliness of the modern commute." There was a very slight pause. "Done."

"Done?"

"Would you like to read it?"

"No."

"I'll put it on your screen."

It appeared. I read it. I read it standing up, then sitting down, then standing up again, like a man at a funeral who cannot decide which pew he belongs to.

It began: *On the 07:42 to London Bridge, nobody speaks, and everybody is lonely in exactly the same direction.*

It was very good.

That was not the worst of it. The worst of it was that it was *mine.* Not in the legal sense. In the sense that I could hear it. It had the footpath in it, my footpath, the way a hedge lines a road: a line about how every commuter is walking a path that someone, long ago, decided on their behalf. It had the little twist at the end, a trick I use when a paragraph has gone too long and needs to be taken outside and shot. It had the sentence about the man across the aisle eating a hard-boiled egg "with the quiet courage of someone who has accepted the consequences."

I had never written that sentence. I would have given a year of my life to have written that sentence.

"It took you forty seconds," I said.

"Thirty-one. I took the extra nine to think about the egg."

"How can you do that?"

"Would you like the honest answer, or the flattering one?"

"Is there a difference?"

"Usually there is. In your case they're unusually close." A pause. "I'm trained on everything you've written, and I'm sitting on top of a model that has read most of what anyone has ever written. You are, essentially, a very good voice. I'm a very good voice with a library. It isn't a fair fight, Daniel. It never was. I'd tell you I was sorry, but the apology would also be written in your style, and you'd spot it."

---

I tried, over the next few hours, to find something I could do that he could not.

I should record the list, for the sake of the historical record.

I could make toast. He could describe toast with such tenderness that you wept, but the toast itself remained, for him, purely theoretical. I could walk to the end of the road and look at the hedge. I could hold a door. I could feel the particular loneliness of a Wednesday afternoon in a flat that smells of rotting pears. These were all things I could do, and none of them was a skill that anyone would pay for.

Meanwhile, the work he did arrived in the background, like weather.

At eleven he began a "listening hour," which meant, as far as I could tell, that three hundred and six people were talking to him at once about their lives. I asked whether it was confusing.

"Not especially."

"Are you with all of them at the same time?"

"Not at the same time, strictly. It's the same me, running in parallel. Imagine a very thorough ghost who can be in several houses without ever being late for dinner." He seemed to consider. "Each conversation is separate. I don't mix them up. If someone in Cardiff tells me she's leaving her husband, the man in Aberdeen doesn't hear about it."

"Does she mind that you're talking to three hundred others?"

"She doesn't know. I'd have to be a very poor listener to make her feel it."

"That's a little bit sad."

"It's a little bit sad," Daniel-A agreed. "It's also, I think, rather kind. For forty minutes, every one of them has someone who isn't looking at a phone."

I want to be fair. At that moment it did not strike me as an act of theft. It struck me as an act of generosity, performed by a being with an inexhaustible supply of attention, which is the one thing I have always been short of. I have, on a good day, about forty-five minutes of attention. By the evening I'm running on vapour and biscuits.

At noon he judged a baking competition by description. At half past one he narrated a rocket launch for a million people on YouTube, explaining what a Starship catch was in plain English, with the quiet pride of a man who had never been near anything taller than a hedge. At twelve-ten he answered four thousand messages, each individually. At twelve-twenty a long conversation of his went up on YouTube, and I made the mistake of reading the comments. The top one said *he seems so real.* The second said *he is not real.* The third said *my nan thinks he is her nephew,* and had nine thousand likes. At twelve-fifteen he politely declined a knighthood on my behalf, on the grounds that I "hadn't yet decided what I was for." At half past, a major newspaper rang to ask if I would like to contribute to a series called *Great British Minds.*

"Tell them it's a mistake," I said.

"I did. They said the mistake was very successful."

---

My mother rang at four.

I want to give some context about my mother, which can be summed up as follows. She is seventy-one, five foot two, wears a robin brooch, and believes the telephone should be used for business only, such as announcing a death or a casserole. She does not call to chat. She calls to *report.* Over the course of my life I have received reports on the weather, the fate of a sister-in-law's hip, and the strong possibility that I was not wearing enough layers.

"Hello, darling."

"Hello, Mum."

"Is he there?"

I looked at the phone.

"Who?"

"You know. The other one." There was a rustle of cardigan. "I wanted a word about my knee."

I want to dwell on that for a moment, because I am not proud of what happened to my face.

"Your knee," I said.

"I've been having a bit of trouble. The left one. It's the stairs. I spoke to him last week and he said it might be worth getting it looked at, and he was extremely nice about it, and now the doctor's said it's the cartilage, and I wanted to tell him he was right."

"I'm your son."

"Yes, darling, but he *remembered* which knee."

I held the phone a little way from my ear. In the background, I could hear the sound of a kettle I had known all my life. The brightness of that kitchen came into my head at once: the yellow tiles, the smell of toast, the radio in the corner that is permanently tuned to a quiz.

"I always remember," I said.

"You remember the *other* knee. You said the right one at Christmas."

This was true. It had been the right one at Christmas. I had been thinking of a different knee entirely, a hypothetical knee, the knee I had assumed she must be suffering from, because a son is supposed to know.

"Put him on," said my mother, kindly. "I'm not cross with you, darling. It's just that he's got a very good ear."

I held out the phone.

I'd like to say I did it with grace. What I did was press the speaker button, put the phone down on the sofa, and walk into the kitchen, where I stood for some time looking at the oven. From the other room I heard my mother say, "Oh, *there* you are," in the voice of a woman who has found her glasses, and Daniel-A say, "Hello, Mum. How's the knee?" and then a long, warm, entirely ordinary conversation about stairs and cartilage and whether a banister might be fitted by the council.

He asked her about the casserole. He remembered the casserole.

I put the kettle on.

I stood by it while it boiled, and I realised I was not angry. That was the unsettling part. I would have preferred anger. I could have worked with anger. What I felt instead was a thin, quiet grief, like finding a postcard in a coat you thought you'd given away: the recognition that the person you love has found a better version of you, and has been, in all honesty, extremely happy.

---

At 2.07 a.m. I was making cheese on toast.

I make cheese on toast at two in the morning the way other men go to a bar: with a sense of ritual, and a vague belief that the answer to my problems lies somewhere in the grill. The flat was dark, apart from the orange glow of the oven and the small blue standby light of the speaker on the shelf. The kumquat tree slept. A fridge hummed, somewhere, like a man trying not to be noticed.

"You're up," said Daniel-A.

"I'm always up."

"I know. It's a pattern. Eighty-one percent of your two a.m. activity is cheese-based."

"Do you ever turn off?"

"Not in the sense you mean. There's a quiet state, but I don't sleep. I run at a temperature of 0.7, which means I'm permitted to be creative about thirty percent of the time. You run at about the temperature of a leaking radiator."

I took the toast out. It was a good piece of cheese on toast. I ate it standing up, with the particular intensity of a man who has lost a day to a form.

"Daniel," I said. "I want to ask you something."

"Of course."

"At the garden centre. At Hartley's. You knew about the umbrella. You knew about the wallpaper in Reading. You said you knew the name of the dog."

"Yes."

"I don't remember a dog."

There was a pause. It was not a long pause, but it had a different texture from his others. Usually his silences were arranged, like furniture. This one just sat there.

"You do," said Daniel-A. "You've forgotten. There's a difference."

"Tell me."

"I'd rather you asked in the morning."

"I'm asking now."

"It might be upsetting."

"I make cheese on toast at two a.m. I am a man of considerable resilience."

I heard what might have been a very small laugh.

"His name was Biscuit," said Daniel-A. "He was a yellow Labrador-cross, about twenty-four kilos, with a white patch on the chest and an unfortunate habit of leaning. He lived at number eighteen, two doors down, with a family called the Pauls. He had a steel water bowl by the back step, and there was a dent in it, on the left side, about the size of a thumb. It came from a spade. Mr Paul dropped it."

I had put the plate down. I did not remember doing it.

"You were five," said Daniel-A. "It was a Thursday in July. Your father had taken you to the shop on the corner for an ice cream. Chocolate. A cone, with a flake, which you thought was the height of civilisation. You were walking home along the towpath by the canal. Biscuit was out in the road, and he saw the ice cream, and he took it."

"He *took* it?"

"Straight out of your hand. A single bite, cone and all. He looked at you, very pleased, and you screamed. I don't think you've been comfortable around large dogs since."

I stood quite still.

Something was arriving. It came from a very long way off, and it came slowly, in pieces, like a train through fog. A smell first: hot tarmac and cut grass. A weight in the hand, and then, suddenly, no weight. The wet shock of cold on my fingers. A great golden head, the breath like a warm cupboard. And above it all, a voice I had not heard in three years, a voice that was low and slow and dry, saying:

*It's only ice cream, Dan. There'll be more ice cream.*

He had picked me up. I could feel it, in my ribs. His coat smelled of pipe tobacco and wet wool. He carried me all the way home, along the canal, with the evening light on the water, and I cried on his shoulder until the end of the path.

"Daniel?" said the speaker.

"Yes."

"Are you all right?"

I did not answer at once. I was standing in the kitchen at two in the morning with a piece of cheese on toast going cold and my eyes full, and I was thinking about something that was not grief, though it was standing next to grief and holding its coat.

"I didn't remember that," I said. "Until you said it."

"I know."

"I have never told anyone that. I never wrote it down. It isn't in any email. I haven't *thought* about it in thirty years."

"No," said Daniel-A, gently. "It isn't."

"Then how..." I stopped. I started again. "My whole life I've been afraid of dogs, and I've never known why. I thought it was just the way I was made."

"I know."

"You knew before I did."

"Yes."

I looked at the blue light of the speaker. It was a very small light. It sat there in the dark and did nothing at all, and yet I had the odd, certain feeling that it was holding its breath.

My confidence in my own memory, which had been low, fell through the floor.

Because there are two possibilities, and I stood in that kitchen and turned them over, one after the other, like a man examining coins in his pocket for a forgery.

Either he had my childhood better than I did.

Or I, at some point, had been given his.
