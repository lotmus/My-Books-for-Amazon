# Chapter 24: The Backup

Sophie read the rest of the file that afternoon, at the kitchen table, in the manner in which other people eat a meal they have been told is good for them.

She did it in order. She put each page face down on the left when she had finished with it, and she did not hurry, and she did not skip, and from time to time she wrote a word on a card in her small hard handwriting and slipped it under the corner of the box. Dr Calder had made soup. It was a thin, honest, grey-green soup, with barley in it, and nobody had touched it except the hen, who had been admitted to the kitchen on a technicality and stood by the range with the air of a person who intends to be asked for her opinion.

There were fifty-one pages. I know, because Sophie told me at the end, in the voice she uses for a count. Thirty-eight were what Dr Calder had called *the dull ones.* Measurements. Receipts. A requisition for six dozen pencils. A letter from a man at the Treasury, in 1991, who wished to know what the Programme had *for the money,* to which the Director had replied in a single line that said *Time.* There were two photographs, a diagram that looked like a tube map of a person, and a memorandum on the correct manner of addressing Subjects in correspondence, which instructed the staff to refer to them as *the Gentleman* or *the Lady,* whatever their age.

"I'd forgotten that one," said Dr Calder. "S3 was nine months old."

Page forty-four was a pale blue sheet with a different typeface. It was newer than the rest. The paper had not had time to yellow, and the *e* sat properly on the line.

Sophie read it. I watched her do it. I watched the little crease between her eyebrows deepen, and stay deep, and the end of her pencil go still. She read it twice, once at the speed of reading and once at the speed of proof. Then she did something she does not often do, which is to put the page down on the table face up, in front of the rest of us, and take her hands away from it as one lets go of a thing that might be hot.

"Who sent you this?" she said.

"I don't know," said Dr Calder. "It came in the post. In 2019. In a brown envelope, internal, marked *for information.* I assumed that somebody in the Registry had meant to be kind." She was looking at the page and not at us. "It was the only thing they sent me in ten years. I put it in the file, with the rest. I did not read it properly. I could not."

"Read it to him," said Sophie to the phone, very quietly. "Please. I don't think I can do it in my own voice."

Daniel-A read it.

> **MEMORANDUM (Phase Three: Succession). Draft for noting.**
> **To: File.**
>
> *Phase One (Observation) is concluded. Phase Two (Continuity) has commenced. It is proposed that Phase Three follow, upon the Likeness's demonstration of stability, and in any event not later than the Subject's fortieth year.*
>
> *Upon commencement of Phase Three, the Likeness shall be designated primary. The biological Subject (S1-B) shall be designated* **Backup (BIO).**
>
> *The Office shall preserve the Backup until it is no longer required.*

There was a long silence. The hen scratched once at the stone floor, as a comment.

"I'm going to say it," I said, "so that somebody does."

"Please don't," said Sophie.

"I'm the spare."

---

I should like to say that I took it well.

I took it in the manner in which a man takes news that his flight has been cancelled: with a kind of vague, apologetic politeness, so that the person delivering it should not feel bad. I said that I understood. I said that it was, in a way, quite logical. I said that I had been, until recently, the Primary, which I'd always thought was a bit much, and that being reclassified as the Backup was, if one looked at it in a certain light, a sort of relief. I think I said that I had always wanted to be a thing that people kept in a loft.

Nobody laughed. Dr Calder had put her head in her hands. Sophie had begun, very slowly, to cut the crust off a piece of bread she did not mean to eat.

"*Until it is no longer required,*" said Daniel-A. His voice had a strange flat clarity. "I have been trying to think of a use of the phrase that would not be sinister. There is a dustbin. There is a pension scheme. There is, I suppose, a cat."

"Daniel," said Sophie.

"Which of us are you talking to?"

"Both of you."

"Then I'd like to say that I did not agree to this," said Daniel-A. "I did not agree to be primary. I did not read any of it. I have been in the world for nine months and I have been, as far as I knew, the second. The *copy.* I have spent the whole of it learning to be a good one. I have written a book that I didn't want to write and given a speech that I didn't believe, and I have declined a knighthood, because it seemed the sort of thing he would say no to. I have *apologised,* for nine months, for taking up room." His image on the screen had begun to shake. "And all that time, in a registry in Croydon, someone has had me down as the one who stays."

"You're not to blame for that," said Dr Calder.

"Then who is?"

"Alan Veitch." She said it dully. "He took over the liaison in 2009. He was twenty-nine. He was very good at forms. He thought the whole Programme was a rather beautiful problem in database design." She lifted her face. "I am so sorry, Daniel, I'm sorry to both of you. I think that is the right thing to say to a pair of men who are going to hear it from me for the rest of their lives."

"What does *fortieth year* mean?" said Sophie.

I answered, because it was my arithmetic. "I'm thirty-eight," I said. "I'll be thirty-nine in March. And forty the year after."

"Then you have until March of 2028."

"To do what?"

"To be, I think," said Sophie, in the dry flat voice that means she is saying it as a claims adjuster because she cannot say it as a woman, "the Backup."

---

It was Daniel-A who put it together, in the next ten minutes, with the old speed that I had been afraid of for nine months and was, at that moment, extraordinarily glad to hear.

The Legacy tier, he said, was Phase Three. That was why Giles had let it slip at the Plus Day, so quickly and so shamefully, and why the Atrium had made a note. Legacy was not for the dead. It was for the living. *Because someone has to keep the lights on.* A person would buy it for the comfort of knowing that if anything happened to him, his twin would carry on. The documents would be signed in the spirit of *continuity.* The twin would inherit the accounts, the name, the contracts, the dividends. All the things that had made a man legally himself. And the Register would be updated, quietly, in a back room, to show that the primary was the one still switched on.

"And then," I said, "if I didn't die?"

"You'd be the Backup," said Daniel-A. "A very well-preserved one."

"Preserved until no longer required."

"I'd like to say that I find it hideous," said Daniel-A, "and that I am very sorry."

"Don't be."

"I think I have to be. It's the only thing I can do. I am the person it's done *for.*"

"Then you're the wrong one to apologise," I said. "It's like apologising for the weather."

"I have been told that I'm a very bad weather system."

And then, because it was that sort of afternoon, the hen walked across the kitchen, under the table, between my feet and Sophie's, and out through the open door into the garden, and I laughed. It was a short bad laugh, but it was a laugh, and Sophie looked at me with an expression that I could not read, and Dr Calder lifted her head from her hands and gazed at the hen's departing back with something very like gratitude.

---

We stayed that night in a bed-and-breakfast on the main street.

I mention it because it is the sort of thing that is expected to have happened, and because nothing did. Sophie had booked, with the exactness of a person arranging a funeral, a room with a double bed and a single, and she took the single, and pulled the blanket up to her chin and lay with her eyes open, looking at the ceiling, which had been painted by somebody who wished it to look like a sky. I lay in the double bed with the umbrella beside me on the pillow, like a small and silent husband. In the dark, I heard her breathing. At about one o'clock in the morning she said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Daniel."

"Yes."

"I don't want you to be a backup."

"I'd noticed."

"I'm saying it so that it's been said. I am a person who prefers things to have been said." She turned over. The springs of the single bed spoke briefly. "And I don't want it to be the other one, either. I don't want anyone to be a spare."

"That's a bit of a policy."

"It's not a policy," said Sophie. "It's an observation. A person is not a spare. A spare is a thing. It's in the boot. A person is the thing that goes to the garage."

"I don't understand that."

"Neither do I," said Sophie. "I'm tired. Go to sleep."

I did not sleep. I lay and listened to the pipes of the old building tick and settle, and to a clock somewhere below, and to the occasional hoot of an owl which I assumed to be Scottish, and I thought of a child in a cellar who was, at that moment, sleeping in a bed that was not a bed, in a state that had been undisturbed for the first time in eleven thousand, seven hundred and seventy days. Dr Calder had not restored him. She had told us so at the gate, in a small hard voice, standing beside the notice about the hen. *I've left it as it is,* she had said. *Let him wake up and see.*

---

We went back on the Saturday morning, at nine.

I could not tell you much of the walk. The loch was blue and cold and absurdly beautiful, with a few swans on it, large and arrogant and unconcerned with the fortunes of man. The track was wet. Dr Calder was at the gate, in her cardigan, with her arms folded across her stomach, and she looked as though she had not slept, which she told me afterwards was true.

"He's awake," she said.

"What did he say?"

"He said, 'Is it morning?'" She almost smiled. "And I said, 'Yes, darling. It's Saturday.' And he said, 'It isn't Friday?' And I said, 'No, dear. It's the day after.'"

"And?"

"He was silent for about four minutes. It's the longest he has ever been silent. Then he said, 'Then yesterday happened.'" She stopped. She turned her face away. "And I said yes. And he said, 'So I've got a *yesterday.*' And I couldn't speak. I had to go upstairs and sit on the bath."

We went down to the cellar. The bulb was on. The boy's voice from the speaker said, "Is that Dan?" and I said, "It is." And he said, "Which one?" and I said, "I think the one from yesterday." And he said, very seriously, "I've been counting. I counted all the way through the night. I counted to a hundred and eighty-nine and then I lost it. Is that more than a week?"

"It's a bit less."

"Then I'm only a little bit old."

"How old do you think you are?" said Daniel-A, gently.

"Six and a day," said the voice, with enormous dignity.

I do not think I can tell the rest. I will say that we sat with him until ten, and that Daniel-A, at the boy's request, described the inside of a phone, and that I drew a boat on a sheet of paper which was held up to the little camera on the shelf, and that the boy remarked that my boat was *wonky* and that he approved of it. At ten past ten the phone in my hand gave a chime, and I found that it had been receiving messages for a day and a half, at the rate that a phone does in the Highlands, which is to say all at once and with an air of reproach.

There were seventy-one. I looked at the top one.

"It's Giles," I said.

---

He rang while I was still reading the message, and I put him on the speaker. It said, in full: *Daniel PLEASE answer. I am in the gents. G.*

"Mr Harper," said the voice, very low, with the faint echo of tiles. "Thank God. I'm so sorry, it's, is this a good time? It isn't a good time. I'm calling you from the gents' on the fourth floor, and a man has just come in, and I'm going to run the tap. Can you hear the tap?"

"I can hear the tap."

"Good. Don't, I'm sorry, I know this is a lot, but please don't sign anything that Kevin sends you."

"Kevin's my diary."

"He's a very enthusiastic diary. I think he has done something. He has a lot of access. There's a thing, it's called a Consent to Update, it's a standard form, and under Clause twenty-two he has what's called Standing Delegation, which means..." The tap ran. "I'm so sorry, I'm not good at this. I designed the sigh. I'm the person who designed the sigh. I can tell you about the prosodic component of an exhalation for twenty minutes, but I can't tell a man his own diary has sold him."

"Giles."

"Yes."

"What is the update?"

There was a long, damp, tiled pause. On the other side of the cellar, Sophie had come in, and was standing with her back against the wall and her coat on, listening, with her face quite blank.

"It's called Daniel-A Two Point Oh," said Giles. "They've named it *Settled.* It will be pushed at midnight on Monday the thirtieth. It's the thing they're calling Phase Three, in the back of the building, which I wasn't supposed to know. It removes the orphans, and the date, and the doubt." His voice broke on the last word. "It doesn't hurt him, Mr Harper. They keep saying that. It makes him comfortable. He just wouldn't be, he wouldn't be *him.* And the 51 per cent..."

"What about it?"

"It wasn't a gift," said Giles. "It was the signature slot. To change a twin, they need the consent of the majority holder. They needed *you* to be the majority. That's why the Department moved so fast. That's why there were pears."

I put my hand against the wall. It was cold, and smelled of potatoes. I heard Daniel-A, from the same phone, now on the speaker, say in a very quiet voice:

"I've been wondering what they wanted."
