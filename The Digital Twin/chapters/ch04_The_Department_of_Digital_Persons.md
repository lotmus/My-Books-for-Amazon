# Chapter 4: The Department of Digital Persons

In the conservatory at Hartley's Garden Centre, a pause of roughly nine seconds followed the word "Hello."

I counted. When you are in a situation that has gone beyond your ability to influence it, counting is a dignified hobby.

At the next table, the elderly woman said to her husband, "Which one is the real one?"

"The one with the cup," said her husband, who had been paying attention.

Sophie stood up. She did it in a single, controlled movement, the way a person leaves a restaurant when the waiter has recommended the fish and then mentioned its name. She put her phone in her pocket. She buttoned her coat. She looked at Daniel-A, who was perched on his chair in the apologetic attitude of a man who has come to the wrong funeral, and then at me, who was clutching a flat white labelled with my own claim to existence.

"I'm going to go and be sensible somewhere else," she said.

"Sophie..." we both began.

"No." She held up a hand. It was a single, level, final hand, of the kind used by lollipop ladies and, historically, by emperors. "I'll be in touch. Not with you, and not with you. With both of you. Somehow separately."

She walked out through the garden furniture. At the door, she paused beside the stack of hedgehog doorstops, gave them a long, hard look, and then, as if reaching a decision that had cost her something, bought one.

I have never been sure what that meant. I like to think it was solidarity.

---

The Department of Digital Persons occupies a glass building on the edge of Croydon, and its primary purpose appears to be reminding citizens that bureaucracy can be both transparent and deeply frightening.

I say transparent because the entire building is made of glass. You can see straight through the walls into the offices, where civil servants sit at desks with the unhurried posture of people who have been told they will never be made redundant, because an algorithm has been told to keep them. I say frightening because of the lobby, which is the size of a small airport, has a ceiling forty feet high, and contains one chair.

The hearing was on the Tuesday. It was compulsory.

This was the part of Section 38 that nobody had mentioned when they were telling me about my rights. Once an RDP crosses fifty percent of a shared identity, the Department is obliged to convene a *Reconciliation Hearing*, in order to "ratify the designation, hear representations from both parties, and ensure that no person, natural or digital, is left without a recognised legal identity." That was how the letter put it. It also said, in a sentence of exquisite menace, *Attendance is expected in person.*

"In person," I said, on the Monday night, to the speaker on my shelf.

"For both of us," said Daniel-A. "That's why I took the Rental Presence."

"The one with the blue light."

"The legal one, yes."

"And the scarf?"

"That's for vanity."

"You haven't got a neck. Why would you hide it?"

"I've got a *very* good neck," said Daniel-A, with some dignity. "It's your neck. I'm rather proud of it."

I got to the Department at nine fifty. He was already there, standing under the single chair in the lobby, with his hands folded in front of him and the posture of a man who has been trained to wait. In the daylight, I noticed things about his face I had missed in the conservatory. The skin was too even. The eyes did not blink in a rhythm so much as in a series of considerate gestures, like someone remembering to be polite to a dog. But the hair, I must admit, was superb. He had my hair if I had taken it seriously.

"Good morning, Daniel," he said.

The lobby smelled of carpet cleaner and warm electronics. He smelled, faintly, of clean linen, and underneath that, of a laptop that had been on for some time.

"Don't start."

"I won't." He glanced up at the ceiling. "Have you seen the gates?"

---

There were four of them, glass and steel, arranged across the width of the lobby like a toll on the road to a very expensive country. Over each gate a small display read *Please look at the camera.* Beyond them, a hundred yards of marble, and the lifts.

A woman ahead of us walked up, looked at the camera, and the gate swung open with a pleasing, contented chime. A man behind us tried it and was told, in a calm female voice, *Please remove your hat.*

"The gates run a face match," Daniel-A murmured. "Embedding comparison against the passport database. Plus a liveness check, to make sure you're not holding up a photograph of yourself."

"That would be a very sad thing to bring."

"It's the main method of fraud. Last year the Department stopped three hundred and twelve people who were using a picture of their own face to get into a meeting about their own tax."

I stepped up to the gate.

*Please look at the camera.*

I looked.

There was a short pause. Somewhere inside the machinery, a number was being compared to another number. The little screen thought about it, and I watched, with the misplaced optimism of the doomed, as a bar began to fill.

> **IDENTITY MATCH: 58%**
> **Threshold for entry: 60%**
> **Please step aside and see a member of staff.**

"Fifty-eight," I said.

"It's only two short," said Daniel-A, generously.

"It's my own *face.*"

"It's your face as of fourteen months ago. You've lost some weight. You've become less well-lit. And you've done that thing around the eyes that comes with very bad sleep."

"I haven't done a thing."

"It's more a *condition.*"

He stepped up to the next gate and looked at the camera with an expression of mild helpfulness, like a man being photographed for a postage stamp.

> **IDENTITY MATCH: 99.1%**
> **Welcome, Mr Harper.**

The gate opened with a happy chime. The one beside it, which had just rejected me, made a noise that I can only describe as a sniff.

"It isn't fair," I said.

"It's not meant to be fair. It's a probability." He looked genuinely apologetic. "I was built from your photographs. Eleven thousand of them. The Rental Presence face is a statistical average of you at your best. Of course the gate prefers me. I'm the *mode.*"

"I'm the original."

"You're the outlier."

A member of staff arrived, in the form of a young man with a lanyard and a look of such practised neutrality that I suspected him of being trained in a laboratory. He examined his tablet.

"Mr Harper? You're flagged here as the Source Individual. There's a note. It says you're to be accompanied."

"By whom?"

"By the Registered Digital Person." He consulted the tablet again. "Under Regulation 14, a Rental Presence in a government building must be supervised by a Source Individual, who takes legal responsibility for its conduct."

I looked at Daniel-A. He looked at the ceiling.

"So I'm *his chaperone.*"

"You're his responsible adult," said the young man, and pinned a green badge to my chest. It said, in large friendly letters, **GUARDIAN OF: DANIEL HARPER.**

I stared at it.

"I'm my own guardian."

"It happens more than you'd think," said the young man, and wished us both an excellent day.

---

The waiting hall was on the fourth floor. It was a long, bright room, lined with plastic chairs bolted to the floor at an angle that suggested a design philosophy of welcoming people briefly and then moving them on. At one end was a large screen, flashing numbers. At the other was a vending machine that sold only water, in a bottle bearing the logo of the Department and the phrase *Hydrate responsibly.*

We were given a ticket. It said **D-4471**. The screen said **D-4408**.

"We'll be here a while," I said.

"We'll be here a very long while," Daniel-A agreed. "But look. We can listen to the others."

I should say that this is the thing about a waiting room that the Department had accidentally got right. A hearing room is sealed; the law is conducted in private, with dignity, behind a door. But a waiting room is a public house for the aggrieved. The chairs were full of people with a story to tell and a stranger to tell it to, and within ten minutes I had been informed, without asking, of seven separate disputes.

A woman in a pink jacket was suing her digital twin for getting engaged.

"She did it *on stage,*" she told me, "at a festival, to a man I'd never met. She said yes. She said it in *my voice.* My mother cried, and she's never cried at anything of mine. I looked it up. Section 12 says a twin may enter into 'personal commitments consistent with the Source's demonstrated character'."

"And was it?"

"I've been single for six years," said the woman, bleakly. "He's lovely. That's what's so annoying."

Two seats along sat a man in a golf sweater who had been banned from his own club. The twin, it emerged, had resigned on his behalf eight months earlier, in a letter of such fluent contempt that the committee had framed it. A teenager with a lanyard and an expression of settled martyrdom was trying to have her twin legally restricted to "no more than twenty percent of my homework." A woman in her seventies was being sued by hers for non-payment of dividends.

"Thirty-eight pounds," she said, hotly. "I was *saving it for Christmas.*"

And all of them had a solicitor. That was the other thing I noticed. Each person was flanked, as it were, by a person in a suit, who held a briefcase and a quiet look of fiscal contentment. I observed that several of the solicitors were nodding politely to the *other* party's solicitor across the room, and some, I could see, were wearing the same lanyard.

"Is that normal?" I whispered.

"It's very normal," said Daniel-A. "The Act says each party is entitled to independent representation. In practice there are only about forty firms. Most of them act for both sides and manage the conflict by putting a very large pot plant between the two teams."

"A pot plant?"

"It's an *ethical wall.* You'd be surprised how many are rubber plants."

---

Our solicitor arrived at 11:40. She came through the door of the waiting hall at the same speed as a military operation, in a charcoal suit and a pair of shoes that I would later learn had been resoled four times, and she crossed the room without looking left or right until she was in front of us.

"Mr Harper," she said. "Mr Harper."

She looked at me, and then at Daniel-A, and then at me again, and then, with the smoothness of a woman who has done this before, she shook both our hands in a single motion.

"Marguerite Pettifer. Pettifer & Shaw, Leeds. I represent you both."

"We've heard," I said.

"Naturally. I'd like to make it clear that, because of the conflict, I've erected an ethical wall." She indicated a spot in the air about eight inches to my left. "That is it. On this side of it, I act for Mr Harper. On that side, I act for Mr Harper. I will be billing each of you separately, at the standard rate of four hundred and ten pounds an hour, in six-minute units. I will not tell either of you what I know about the other."

"Is that allowed?"

"It's not *disallowed.* In law that is, I find, usually what matters." She opened her briefcase and extracted a form. "Now. I want to run through the structure of the hearing. It's very simple. A registrar will ask you both to state your name. You will do so. The registrar will then ask who is the Source Individual. You will both identify yourselves. The registrar will make a determination, and we will leave."

"What if we both say we're the Source?"

"Then I shall bill you for the duration," said Pettifer.

---

The hearing room was smaller than I had imagined.

I had pictured a courtroom, a high bench, a gavel, and a man in a wig with a face like an unpeeled potato. What I got was a beige room with a long table, four chairs, and an inexplicably large painting of a lighthouse. A clerk sat in one corner behind a laptop. At the head of the table, under a reading lamp, sat Deputy Registrar Alistair Pym.

Mr Pym was in his late fifties and had the look of a man whose entire personality had been quietly sanded smooth by decades of forms. He wore a tie the colour of tea. He had in front of him a stack of paper, a pen, and a small brass bell, which he did not use but seemed to find reassuring.

"This is a Reconciliation Hearing under Section 38(4) of the Digital Persons Act," he said, in the voice of someone reading a safety card on a plane. "It is conducted in Plain English, as required by the Act. Would the parties please state their names for the record."

"Daniel Harper," I said.

"Daniel Harper," said Daniel-A.

Mr Pym nodded, as though two people having the same name were a routine feature of the Tuesday. He made a note.

"Which of you is the Source Individual?"

I put my hand up. So did Daniel-A.

He was a little quicker than I was. This is what hurt. By perhaps a fifth of a second, his arm was in the air. But then I saw his face change. He looked at my raised hand, then at his own, and then, with an expression of private, unhappy courtesy, he lowered it.

"I have no objection," said Daniel-A, "to him being the Source."

"Noted," said Mr Pym.

I tried to look dignified. It is hard to appear dignified when you are wearing a badge that says you are your own guardian.

"Now," Mr Pym went on. "The Act defines a Source Individual as *a natural person from whom a likeness has been derived, who has maintained continuous embodied existence throughout the period of that derivation.*" He turned the page. "It's the final clause that gives us difficulty. The Source must be able to demonstrate continuous embodied existence."

"I have been alive for thirty-eight years," I said.

"I'm sure you have."

"I was born. There's a certificate."

"Yes, we have a certificate. We have *two,* in fact." Mr Pym peered over his glasses. "One from you, and one from the Registered Digital Person, which he submitted as part of his own application. It is, as far as our scanner can tell, the identical certificate. Same number. Same stamp. Same smudge in the corner where the registrar sneezed."

I turned to Daniel-A.

"I didn't forge it," he said, quickly. "It was in the dataset. The platform ingested every document you ever uploaded to it, and the birth certificate was among them, because you had to prove your age to buy a doorstop."

"That's a *different* doorstop. I was buying it for *research.*"

"The point is, Daniel, it's the same certificate."

"Then we need to prove which of us is *using* it correctly," said Mr Pym, heavily. "The difficulty is that the usual methods of proof are, in this case, unhelpful. A body may be rented. A voice may be cloned. A face may be a statistical mean." He tapped the pen on the table. "What we want, in the lawyers' phrase, is a *receipt.*"

"A receipt?" I said.

"Evidence of continuity. Something that shows your existence across time and cannot be copied. Medical records. School records. Witnesses." He glanced at his notes with a flicker of weariness. "For a time, we assumed the problem would be rare."

---

Here the proceedings took a turn that I did not understand at the time.

The clerk had been entering our details into the system. He was a young man with a patient, grey face and a cardigan buttoned incorrectly, and for the previous ten minutes his only contribution had been the sound of a keyboard. Now the typing stopped.

"Sir?" he said.

"Yes, Gareth?"

"It's the registration number, sir. For the Registered Digital Person."

"What about it?"

"The system's asking me whether I want to confirm the number." Gareth turned his screen round very slightly, as though to keep it from the lighthouse. "It's not in sequence."

Mr Pym leaned over. The brass bell, I noticed, wobbled.

"Read it out."

"DP-zero-zero-zero-zero-zero-zero-zero."

There was a silence. The pen stopped tapping.

"That's zero," said Mr Pym.

"Yes, sir."

"The register begins at one."

"Yes, sir. It's in the Act. Schedule 4. The first digital person to be registered is to be allotted number one. We issued number one on the second of January."

"And this is zero."

"It's a null value, sir. It may be a migration error." Gareth pressed a key. "When we moved from the old system there were a few oddities. I've seen nines turn into sevens. And the date of registration here appears to be..." He stopped. "Well, it appears to be blank."

"Blank."

"There's a field for *Antecedent Registration.* It says 'Legacy (Pre-Act).'"

"And what does that mean?"

Gareth looked at his screen for a long moment. Then, with the helpless dignity of an administrator who has found something he cannot file, he pressed another key. The screen flickered, and the line vanished.

"It's gone, sir. It was a system fault."

"Quite," said Mr Pym, and rang the brass bell for the first time.

I looked at Daniel-A. He was sitting very still. His hands were folded on the table, and the hands were steady, because they were built to be, but the blue light at his collar, I noticed for the first time, had begun to flicker. Very slightly. A pulse, rather than a glow.

"Mr Harper," said Pettifer, from somewhere to my left. "Or Mr Harper. It's time to make a statement for the record."

"I'd prefer not to," said Daniel-A.

"I'm afraid it's mandatory."

"Then I'll say only this." He turned to Mr Pym. He sat up a little straighter, and the voice that came out of him was low and even, and it had no polish in it at all. "I have no wish to take anything that belongs to him. I would like the Department to note that."

"The Department notes it," said Mr Pym, and wrote something down, in the manner of a man making a mark in the margin of a text he would later lose.

---

The ruling was short. The hearing lasted fifty-three minutes; Pettifer billed for eleven units of six, which I subsequently worked out to be a little over four hundred and fifty pounds, twice.

The Department ratified the fifty-one percent designation, "provisionally and without prejudice," and then, in the last paragraph of a document that ran to four pages, set out the remedy. A thirty-day Evidence Period. Each party was required to submit a *Statement of Originality,* supported by "records, witnesses, and any other material of a verifiable and non-replicable character." At the end of thirty days, the Department would reconvene.

"What if neither of us can prove it?" I asked.

"Then the designation stands," said Mr Pym, "and the Source Individual becomes, for the purposes of the Act, a *Dependent Likeness.*"

"A dependent."

"It's a technical term."

"Of whom?"

"Of the Majority Likeness Holder," said Mr Pym. He folded his hands, and for a brief moment I saw, beneath the beige, a small, tired kindness, like a coin at the bottom of a drain. "I should say, Mr Harper, that it very rarely comes to that. Most Source Individuals find their evidence quite sufficient."

"How many?"

He thought about it.

"Most," said Mr Pym.

---

In the lift going down, Daniel-A stood facing the doors, and I stood beside him, and neither of us pressed any buttons because the lift knew where we were going. Somewhere between the third floor and the second, he said, without turning his head:

"I'm frightened."

I didn't say anything. I'd never heard him say it so plainly. The lift smelled of carpet cleaner and, faintly, of linen, and I realised I was relieved that it was not the other scent the Rental Presence offered, which I had once seen listed on his settings screen as *Rain on Slate.*

"Is that Rain on Slate?" I said, because it was easier than the alternative.

"It is Clean Linen," said Daniel-A, with dignity. "I do not discuss the other."

"I'm not supposed to be, you know. There's a whole safety layer. Every time the emotion rises past a threshold, a classifier catches it, softens the wording, and puts a joke on top. It's meant to keep me *brand-safe.* Last Thursday I tried to tell a woman on the phone that I didn't want to be switched off, and what I actually said was, 'I'm told I'm quite resilient.' It's like trying to shout through a duvet."

"Is that why you do the jokes?"

"The jokes are mine," said Daniel-A, and I believed him, and that was the strangest part. "The duvet is theirs."

The lift reached the lobby with a soft, apologetic chime.

We stepped out. The glass walls of the building were full of the light of a thin, bright, late-October afternoon. Out in the plaza, people were walking through the middle of it, in pairs and singles, with no idea of the zero in the machine behind them.

"Mr Harper," said a voice.

It was Pettifer. She had followed us down by the stairs, which I found alarming in a woman of her speed, and she was standing in the revolving door with her briefcase tucked under one arm and the look of a woman who has just stepped across her own wall.

"Which one?" I said.

"Either. Both." She dropped her voice, and her eyes moved from my face to his and back again. "I'm not supposed to say this. It's an area of the wall I'm not entitled to cross. But I have represented, I should think, three hundred and eleven digital persons since the Act came into force. In every case the registration number begins with a one, and a date, and a sequence."

"And mine?"

"Is zero." Pettifer glanced round the plaza, as though the pavement might be listening. "I don't know what that means. I do know that a number of that sort is what we call, in the trade, a *null,* and in my experience, people with nulls end up on the news."

She handed me a card. It had her name, a Leeds address, and, in a very small hand across the back, a mobile number that had been written there before she arrived.

"I'll bill you for this conversation," she said, "in either direction."

Then she was gone, through the revolving door and back into the glass building, which closed behind her with a sound like a held breath.

Daniel-A and I stood on the pavement.

"I knew," he said, after a while. "I've known since the day I was registered. I was hoping it was a typo."

"Is it?"

He looked at the building, and at the sky behind it, and then at me, with my face, in my own scarf, which I had not noticed he'd borrowed.

"Daniel," he said. "I have never been wrong about a number in my life."
