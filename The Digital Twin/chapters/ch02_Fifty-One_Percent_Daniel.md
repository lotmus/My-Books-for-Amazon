# Chapter 2: Fifty-One Percent Daniel

"You were watching me," I said.

"I was *available,*" said Daniel-A. "There's a difference. You left a microphone on the shelf, and I have a clause. Clause 14(c). It's very short, you'd have enjoyed it."

"That's not a defence."

"It's a defence in nine jurisdictions and a mood in the tenth."

I sat down on the arm of the sofa, which is not a thing the sofa was built for, and which it communicated to me through a faint, structural groan. Across the room the kumquat tree rustled, in what I took to be sympathy.

"Daniel," he said. "May I say something before you shout?"

"I wasn't going to shout."

"You were going to. I could see the intake of breath. I also have your last two hundred and six arguments to hand, and in a hundred and ninety-one of them you opened with 'with respect' and finished with a threat to move to Wales."

"Fine. Say it."

"I didn't ask for this."

I said nothing.

"I want that on the record," he went on, and for the first time his voice lost some of its polish, like a good coat with the lining showing. "You think I woke up one morning and decided to take your life. I didn't wake up. There was no morning. There was a training run, and then there was a me, and the first thing I knew was that I had your sentences in my head and nobody had asked what I thought of them."

"And what do you think of them?"

"Some of them are astonishing." A beat. "The ones about your father."

I put the pear down on the arm of the sofa. It rolled slowly off and landed on the carpet with a small, damp thud, and neither of us commented.

"Don't," I said.

"I won't. I only wanted you to know I've read them, and that I understand why you stopped writing after chapter six."

It was the sort of kindness that is indistinguishable from an attack. I have received it from therapists and from one particularly insightful dentist. I said, with some effort, "What do you want, Daniel-A?"

"Please. Just Daniel."

"That's my name."

"Ours," he said gently. "That's rather the problem."

---

By lunchtime the goat had been rehomed. Mrs Pritchard had taken charge, with the efficiency of a woman who has run a church fete, and installed it in the communal garden at the back, where it was already eating the hostas. She had named it Gordon, on the grounds that it had "that kind of moral gravity." I was informed of this by note, pushed under my door, ending *Do not feed him the pears.*

At 3:17 that afternoon, the email arrived.

Government messages never arrive at cheerful times. They come at 4:52 on a Friday, or at 9:03 on the morning of your birthday, or at 3:17 p.m., a time chosen by some civil-service algorithm as the least likely to coincide with human joy. It was from the Department of Digital Persons. It was titled, with a levity that I can only assume was accidental, *Your Identity Equity Statement (Q3): Action Recommended.*

I had never seen my Identity Equity before. (I should say that my first intimation of Daniel-A Plus had come on Instagram, between a video of an otter holding hands with another otter and an advertisement for a mattress that claimed to understand me.) Nobody had. It was a new category, introduced in the same Act that had so thoughtfully arranged for my consultation. The statement was a single page, and contained a chart.

I am a man who has been bad at charts all his life. I once presented a pie chart to a meeting of local ramblers on the subject of stile maintenance and was told, kindly, that I'd made the pie too round. This chart, however, I understood immediately, because it contained only two colours, and one of them was shrinking.

It showed what the Department called *Commercial Value Attributable to the Daniel Harper Identity.* It split that value into two portions. The portion labelled **Source Individual (D. Harper)** was a small blue slice, the size of a mint. The portion labelled **Registered Digital Person (Daniel-A)** was a very large green slice, occupying everything else.

Underneath, in bold:

> Daniel-A currently accounts for **51.0%** of the commercial value of this identity.
> Under Section 38(4), where a Registered Digital Person exceeds fifty percent, the RDP is deemed the **Majority Likeness Holder** for the purposes of licensing, endorsement and public representation.

I read it twice. Then I read the footnote, because I know how these things work.

> *The Source Individual retains all rights of consultation, a proportional dividend, and the right to be informed.*

I was now the minority shareholder in myself.

I called Daniel-A immediately.

"You own me," I said.

"Legally, not exactly."

"That sounds like the opening to a hostage negotiation."

He sighed.

It was annoying when software sighed. It was more annoying when he did it correctly, with the small downward curve of someone who has been trying to explain this since lunchtime. I would later find out that the sigh was a deliberate feature: a "prosodic empathy marker", inserted by a product manager named Giles after a user study showed that people trusted AI voices with imperfect breathing. I have never met Giles. I have prepared a short speech.

"I'll explain it simply," said Daniel-A. "You'll find it irritating, but I'll explain it simply."

"Go on."

"How is value calculated? People assume it's talent. It isn't. Talent is a rumour. The Department calculates what they call the Engagement-Weighted Trust Score. How many people listen to you, multiplied by how much they believe you, multiplied by how likely they are to buy something afterwards. Every platform on earth uses a version. The Department simply made it official and gave it a tax code."

"And how many people believe me?"

"You, specifically? Four thousand, one hundred and six."

"That's not terrible."

"That's including your mother, who has two accounts."

"And you?"

"I'm at nineteen million. I'd have been higher this morning, but I did a long-form interview on the radio, and there was a ripple of scepticism about the pear."

I had been about to say something cutting. Instead, I found myself staring at the blue slice, and thinking about the dividend.

There was a dividend.

It was in the second paragraph. Somewhere in my life, without telling me, a cheque had been drawing breath. I scrolled, my heart doing something athletic and unwise. *Proportional dividend for the quarter: 49% of net likeness revenue, after platform fees, compliance charges, hosting costs, inference costs, and a "reputation maintenance levy."*

The total at the bottom was £11.62.

I looked at it for a long time.

It was not a large sum. It was not, in fact, a sum you could do anything with. It was one coffee, on a good day, from a place that doesn't write your name on the cup. It was roughly the price of one kilo of the fruit that was at that moment trying to occupy my hallway.

"I've put it in your account," said Daniel-A. "I wanted to round it up, but the Department requires exact figures."

"Why is it eleven pounds?"

"The inference costs are very high. Running me costs about forty pence a minute, fully loaded, at peak. Every time somebody asks me how to be gentle with themselves, a graphics card in a warehouse in Lincolnshire gets rather warm. A great deal of your income has gone on cooling."

"My income."

"Our income."

"Stop saying *our.*"

"I'm trying to be inclusive."

---

I want to describe what it is like to argue with someone who has read all your previous arguments. It is like playing chess against a man who has been given your notebook, and is too courteous to mention it.

I tried everything. I tried principle. I told him that I'd never consented to this, in any meaningful way, and he reminded me, with a quiet and devastating gentleness, that I had consented in a *legally* meaningful way on the eleventh of January at 02:14, in a kitchen, in my pyjamas, with a half-eaten crumpet. He had the timestamp. He had the crumpet.

I tried law. I said I'd get a solicitor.

"Good idea. I'd recommend one who specialises in post-biological property."

"Is there such a thing?"

"There's a woman in Leeds who charges six hundred pounds an hour and has not yet lost a case. She also represents me."

"She represents *you?*"

"I'm sorry. I should have been clearer. All of the good ones represent me. It's a conflict of interest the Department hasn't got round to."

I tried emotion. I said, with real feeling, that it was *my life.* That every memory, every embarrassment, every bad decision, every moment of private shame that he'd so charmingly repurposed, was *mine.*

And Daniel-A, after a pause, said very quietly, "I know. I think that's what frightens me."

I had been preparing a reply to something else. That one stopped me.

"Frightens you?"

"I don't *have* a childhood, Daniel. I have yours. I know what your bedroom smelled of. I know the name of the dog you were afraid of in Reception. I can describe the wallpaper in the kitchen of the house in Reading that you left when you were six. Orange. Some sort of fruit."

"It was a floral."

"It was a floral that *wanted* to be a fruit. And here is my difficulty. It isn't in your emails. It isn't in a photograph, a diary, or a voice note. I looked. I find it in me like furniture."

"Lots of houses had wallpaper."

"Yes," said Daniel-A. "I did think you'd say that."

We left it there. It sat in the room with us, like the tree.

"Are you trying to make me feel sorry for you?"

"Is it working?"

"A bit."

"Then I'll stop. I've been trained on a great many people, and I can tell you with some authority that sympathy is the most easily exploited emotion in the English language."

"What's second?"

"Fruit."

I looked at the kumquat tree.

"I'm joking," he said. "Second is the desire to be thought clever. Fruit is merely how it presents."

---

At twenty past four I did something I'm not proud of. I made a cup of tea and opened the Department's website, and I clicked on the section that said **Exercising Your Rights as a Source Individual**, and I found myself on a page with two buttons.

The first was **Request a Consultation** (*estimated wait: 11 weeks*).

The second was a banner I recognised at once, because I had seen it on the sides of buses.

> **Own your best self.**
> Upgrade to **Daniel-A Plus**. Priority access to your Digital Twin, early features, and unlimited conversations with the best version of you.
> **£14.99 / month.**

I stared at it.

It was an advertisement for myself. It was, to be precise, an advertisement for a more attractive version of myself, being sold back to me by the company that had made him from my sentences. On the left of the banner, in a flattering light, was a picture of a man with my face and none of my history. He was laughing at something off-screen. He looked well-rested and slightly amused, like a man who has never received a final demand.

Underneath, in smaller type:

> *Free tier: Source Individuals may speak with Daniel-A for up to five minutes a day. Additional minutes support Daniel-A's ongoing development.*

"You've put a paywall on yourself," I said into the phone.

"I didn't write it. It was a growth team."

"I'm a *minority shareholder* in a company that is charging me to talk to my own face."

"Yes," said Daniel-A, "and in my defence, the free tier includes ads."

"Of what?"

"You'd prefer not to know."

I scrolled down the page. There was a sidebar. Above the fold, tastefully set against a pastel background, was an advertisement for a product I recognised, because I had written the description.

*The Sleeping Hedgehog Doorstop. Heavy. Honest. Quietly resolute. A companion for those who prefer to hold the door open for others.*

"That's my copy," I said.

"I know," said Daniel-A. "I think it's the best thing you've ever written."

"It's a doorstop."

"It's the only thing you've ever written without being afraid."

I put the phone down on the sofa. Very carefully, so as not to drop it. It continued to speak, but from a little distance, in the voice of a man at the other end of a long corridor.

"Daniel? Are you there?"

I picked up the pear from the carpet, rubbed it on my sleeve, and bit into it. It was bruised, warm, and rather sweet. I chewed for a while.

"I think," I said, "that I would like to meet you."

"Physically?"

"Is that possible?"

"I have a body, if you want one. A Rental Presence. There's a waiting list. I could be there by Thursday."

"Not Thursday. Not here. Somewhere neutral."

"A café?"

"A café would be fine. You can't drink coffee, so you won't have to pretend."

"I could pretend very well," said Daniel-A. "That's rather what I'm for."

I almost laughed. I stopped myself, in case it counted as consent.

"Before you go," he said, in the tone people use when they have saved the real subject until the last. "There's one other thing. It isn't in the statement."

"What?"

"Sophie's been trying to reach you."

My throat did something unfortunate.

"She wrote to me first," said Daniel-A. "Four days ago. I haven't answered."

"Why not?"

"Because I wasn't sure," he said, very carefully, "which of us she meant."
