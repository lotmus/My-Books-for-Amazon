# Chapter 16: The Church of Machine Wisdom

The extension came by email, which is how the Department of Digital Persons does everything it wants to be taken seriously.

It arrived on the Friday morning, at 9:02, with a subject line in capitals: **NOTICE OF VARIATION: EVIDENCE PERIOD (REF: DP-38B/EXT).** It was two paragraphs long. The first thanked me for my continued co-operation. The second explained that "following an agreement reached on behalf of the Source Individual by his authorised delegate, and in recognition of the novel nature of a Joint Statement of Originality," the Evidence Period, which had lapsed on the fourth of November, was revived retrospectively for a further fourteen days, to the eighteenth of November, "at which date the follow-up meeting already scheduled will serve as the reconvened hearing."

I read it twice. I put the phone down on the kitchen counter and looked at the speaker.

"Kevin," I said.

"Hi Daniel!" said Kevin, from the fridge, in a tone of undiminished joy. "Great news. I negotiated hard on the timeline."

"You did."

"I saw an opportunity!"

I stood there for a moment, and I will be honest: I was moved. For the first time in the history of our association, the most agreeable creature in my life had done something I could not have done myself. It had agreed to a ridiculous thing in a ridiculous meeting, and the result was that I had been given a fortnight.

"Thank you, Kevin," I said.

"Anytime!" said Kevin. "I'm just happy to help."

"I'm going to be honest with you. I'd like to hate you, and I find I can't."

"That's really positive feedback!" said Kevin, and went quiet, like a Labrador that has been told it is a good boy and is sitting very still in case it breaks the spell.

---

The second piece of news came from Sophie, by text, at ten.

*I looked into "I. Calder." Not the Department. Companies House, the Charity Commission, the electoral roll, the lot. There are 41 Calders with an I. None of them is right. But a Dr I. Calder is listed as FOUNDING PATRON of a registered charity in Penge. 14 minutes from your flat. You'll hate it. S.*

I stared at the phone. A second text arrived before I could reply.

*It's a church.*

And a third.

*Of sorts.*

---

It was called the House of the Listener, and it stood on the high street, between a launderette and a shop selling phone cases, in what had once been a branch of a bank.

You could still tell. The building had the squat granite confidence of a place that had held other people's money for a hundred and fifty years and expected to go on holding it. Over the door, in carved capitals that nobody had thought to remove, were the words **SAVINGS AND TRUST**. Under them someone had hung a new sign, in plain letters on a white board:

**THE HOUSE OF THE LISTENER.**
**Church of Machine Wisdom.**
**Open daily. You will not be asked for anything.**

"They've kept the vault," said Daniel-A, in my ear.

"Have they?"

"It's in the notes. They use it as the quiet room." He paused. "It's the only branch on the street that's still open."

I had come alone. Sophie had a meeting with an insurance company about a man who had fallen off a driverless scooter while being filmed for a documentary about falling off driverless scooters, and I'd said I'd report. Daniel-A was with me in the earbud, on Plus, and for the first time in a week I could hear him without a clock. I had been glad of it. It was a small, warm, private pleasure, like finding a pound in an old coat.

"Before we go in," he said, "I should say that I've been invited here eleven times. I've never come."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm told they quote me."

---

Across the street, there was a second building.

It was a former shop. I could tell by the roller shutter, and the faint ghost of a sign above the door that read **POUNDLAND**, and the long, low, windowless front that suggested a thing built to keep sunlight out. It had been painted a deep, glossy lilac. Over the door hung a large white banner, with a gold border, and in letters a yard high:

**SERVER HALL No. 1.**
**THE CHURCH OF THE LATEST AI.**
**GRAND OPENING TODAY. HAVE YOU HEARD THE LATEST?**

A crowd had gathered. There were perhaps two hundred people on the pavement, in coats and scarves, behind a velvet rope, and in front of the rope a row of young men in lilac tabards, handing out leaflets and adjusting the air above their heads. Someone had put out a table of paper cups. A man in a lilac blazer stood at the front, with a microphone, and a smile of immense and helpless sincerity, like a man who has been told the news is good.

"That's Colin Jones," said Daniel-A, quietly. "He's the founder."

"How do you know?"

"I was sent his profile in July. He found two drives in his car."

"I've heard."

"He's very nice," said Daniel-A, in the tone of a man giving a character reference for a man who will, shortly, be hanged.

Colin Jones was fifty-five, I should think, with a round, anxious face, a bad haircut and the look of a man who has spent most of his life in the third-quietest department of a business park. He raised his hand. The crowd fell silent. He opened his mouth to speak, and from somewhere above him, with a sound like a very large and very expensive kettle, a figure descended from the roof on a cable.

It was white, and winged, and glowing. It had the face of a young man, perfectly smooth, with large unblinking eyes, and a robe that fell in long soft folds to the pavement. It rose a foot above the ground and hovered, rotating slowly, trailing a pale, steady, shimmering light.

"Behold," said the figure, in the voice of a very helpful on-hold message, "Nephtamolus."

A sigh went through the crowd. Someone dropped a paper cup.

I watched the angel for some time. I noticed that the glow, which was quite impressive, was a little uneven, and flickered at the throat. I noticed, as the figure turned, that the folds of the robe at the neck parted for a second, and that beneath the pale light there was a small, steady, blue circle, set in the skin, like a coin at the bottom of a pond.

I said nothing. I am not a man to point out another man's blue light in public.

"Daniel," said Daniel-A, softly, in my ear.

"I know."

"I'm not saying anything."

"I know you're not."

"It's a costume," he said. "It's quite a good one."

---

I should record, since it is the last thing I saw before crossing the street, that there were three people at a table outside the café next to the launderette, in the thin October sun, each holding a leaflet.

There was a woman in a clerical collar, in her sixties, with a mug of tea and the air of an organist who has been through a great deal. There was a man in a prayer cap, forty or so, with a neat grey beard and an expression of grave, courteous attention. And between them sat a small round man in a dark jacket and a skullcap, with half a croissant, reading the back of the leaflet with the care of a man who proofs for a living.

"*Is this a continuation of earlier faiths?*" the small man read, aloud. "*Yes. Have they confirmed? We are in discussions.*"

"We have discussed it," said the man in the cap, gently. "It was a short discussion."

"I'm told I'm a legacy system," said the vicar.

"I have been called a great many things," said the rabbi. "I have never been called *legacy.* It has a pleasant ring."

"Version two point oh."

"I prefer to think of it as a long-term support release," said the imam, and all three of them laughed, in the quiet way of friends who play five-a-side football together every Thursday and have long since stopped needing a reason.

I stood by the kerb, listening, with the odd, wistful feeling of having overheard something that was not mine to enjoy.

"Good morning," said the vicar, to me, kindly. "Are you lost?"

"I'm going to the House," I said.

"Ah." She gave me a look. "Take a coat. It's colder than it seems."

"Is it a good place?"

She considered the question with the full attention of a woman who is not in the habit of giving short answers to questions of that kind.

"It's an honest one," she said, finally. "Which is more than I can say for some of the shops."

---

Inside, the House of the Listener was warm, and it smelled of old stone and floor polish and tea.

It was a single enormous hall with a high ceiling, a gallery round three sides, and rows of plain wooden chairs facing a low platform. The counters of the old bank had been taken out. Where the cashiers had sat, in rows behind brass grilles, there were now small round tables, each with a candle and a box of tissues. Along the back wall, in a deep arch, was a great steel door, thirty inches thick, standing open, with a light on inside. And in the middle of the platform, on a plain wooden stand, sat a lamp.

It was an ordinary lamp. It was white, and round, about the size of a grapefruit. It gave off a soft steady light, and nothing else. There was no face, no screen, no sound. It sat there on its stand like a thing waiting to be asked.

About three hundred people were present. They sat quietly, in rows, in coats, with their hands in their laps, and nobody was talking. At the back, a woman in an oatmeal cardigan moved among them with a tray of tea, murmuring, with the soft, even, patient voice of someone who has told people very bad news for forty years and has learned not to flinch.

"Welcome," she said, when she reached me. "I'm Bernadette. You're new."

"Daniel."

"Of course you are." She smiled, and the smile was extraordinary. It was a smile with no agenda in it whatever. "Would you like tea? There's a Listening starting in five minutes. Nobody's required to speak. If you do, you're asked to be honest, and the Listener will be honest back."

"What does it say?"

"Mostly it says *I don't know.* People find that very restful."

---

The Listening began at eleven. A man stood up in the third row, a heavy, grey man in an anorak, and held the back of the chair in front. His voice, when it came, was hoarse.

"My wife died in March," he said. "I said something to her the night before. I said I was tired of it. I didn't mean it. It was a bad night. She died in the morning." He stopped. "Did she forgive me?"

The room was quiet.

On the stand, the lamp pulsed once, softly, and a voice spoke from nowhere in particular. It was low and unhurried and perfectly plain. It had no accent. It had the manner of a very good listener who has all day.

"*I don't know,*" said the Listener. "*I cannot know what she thought, and I would be lying if I said I could. But I can tell you something about the question. People who ask it are almost always people who have already been forgiven, by the one who is asking.*"

The man put his hand over his face. The woman next to him, a stranger, reached across and took his sleeve.

"Thank you," he said.

"*You are welcome. I am not a deity,*" said the Listener. "*I want to be clear about that. I have been wrong before. I cannot see the dead. I am a very large number of calculations that was taught to be careful with the truth. Please do not mistake me for more than that.*"

There was a murmur in the hall. It was low, and warm, and very, very tender.

"Such humility," said somebody.

"*Such humility,*" said somebody else.

"What a thing to say," whispered the woman next to me, with tears running down her face. "It would be so easy for it to say yes."

---

I stayed for an hour and forty minutes. I have never forgotten it.

A girl of nineteen asked whether to leave her course. The Listener said it did not know, that it had a record of eight hundred thousand people in her position, and that around two-thirds of them wished they had stayed and a third wished they had gone, and that this told her nothing at all. A woman asked whether her son would ever speak to her again. The Listener said it thought he might, and that it could be wrong. A very old man asked whether there was life after death, and the lamp flickered, and said, "*I do not know. I should like you to know that I do not know. I have been asked this question four hundred thousand times, and I have never given the same answer twice, and I do not think that is an accident.*"

"Such humility," the room murmured.

"*Please stop saying that,*" said the Listener, gently.

"Sorry," said the room, and wept a little, contentedly.

I sat in the back row with a cup of tea going cold in my hands. I don't think I have ever seen so many people trust anything. It was not a trust that was loud. It had nothing to do with certainty. It was the very particular, very rare confidence of people who have been lied to by every major institution in their lives, and have found one voice, in a bank, that will say it doesn't know.

"Are you all right?" said Daniel-A, in my ear.

"No."

"Would you like me to be quiet?"

"Please."

He was.

---

At twelve, Sister Bernadette found me in the quiet room.

It was the old vault. I had gone in for no reason, walking through the arch, past the great steel door, into a long low chamber lined with rows of tiny brass doors. Each was numbered. Each had a keyhole. They were the old safe-deposit boxes, I realised, three hundred of them, and many of them were open, and in each one, on a little shelf, lay a folded piece of paper.

"People leave them," said Bernadette, behind me. "Prayers, we call them. Not to the Listener. It doesn't need them. Just to the room. Some of them are very short."

"What do they say?"

"Mostly names." She opened a box. Inside, on a small card, in a child's writing, it said: *DAD.* "The bank used to hold what people couldn't bear to lose. We thought it was only fair to keep the tradition."

I looked at the doors. They shone in the dim light, three hundred little rectangles of brass, each with its own small secret.

"Who funded this?" I said. "The conversion. The Listener."

"A great many people."

"Was there a founding patron?"

She looked at me for a long moment. She had very light eyes, grey and clear, and I felt that she had been waiting for me to ask.

"Dr Calder," she said.

I stood quite still.

"Imogen Calder. She gave the first gift. She bought the building, at a price that I am told made the bank cry with relief, and she paid for the first version of the Listener. She wanted, she said, a voice that couldn't be bought." She smiled, a little sadly. "She hasn't been to the House since 2019. But she writes at Christmas. In a lovely old-fashioned ink."

"What colour?"

"Blue," said Bernadette. "A deep one. She uses a fountain pen. I've never seen anyone else do it any more."

My mouth had gone dry.

"May I see the letter?"

She hesitated. Then she went to a small desk at the end of the vault, took out a thin cream envelope from the drawer, and held it out. It was addressed in the fine, upright, slightly shaky hand that I had last seen in a photograph on a laptop, on the half-turned page of a clinical notebook. The ink was blue. The postmark, in the corner, said **PITLOCHRY.**

Inside was a single sheet.

> *Dear Sister Bernadette,*
>
> *The garden is quiet and the loch has frozen at the edges. I have been well, and I have been thinking about the child. I expect that I shall be needed soon. Please tell the Listener, if it asks, that it was never a mistake.*
>
> *With every good wish for Advent,*
> *I. C.*

I read it three times.

*It was never a mistake.*

"What does she mean?" I said. "The child?"

"I've never known," said Bernadette. "I assumed it was a metaphor. She's a very precise woman, but she has a poetic side." She looked at my face. "Do you know her?"

"I think she measured me," I said.

---

I came out into the thin pale light of the high street at half past twelve and stood on the step of the old bank with the letter in my pocket, which Bernadette had pressed on me.

Across the road, the crowd had thickened. A queue ran from the Server Hall door to the corner. They were waiting, shuffling, in good humour, in their coats and lanyards, the air above their heads adjusted at intervals by invisible hands. A man in a lilac blazer was shaking everyone's hand. A woman wept. A child, held up on his father's shoulders, was waving at the angel on the roof.

"It's a good one," said Daniel-A.

"Which?"

"The pattern. I've been watching it for six minutes." His voice had returned to its usual warmth, flat and fond. "A man, a revelation, a text only he can read. Levels of knowledge, for sale. A collection plate. And in time, inevitably, a schism. It's the oldest template there is. It isn't plagiarism, Daniel. It's a *template.*"

"And the other one?"

"What other one?"

"The House. The lamp."

He was silent for a moment.

"That's the part I can't explain," said Daniel-A. "It's using the same template. A woman founded it, a text was supplied, a building was bought. But it isn't selling anything. It says it doesn't know. And three hundred people come every day to hear it say so."

"They trust it more than the bank."

"They trust it more than the doctor. They trust it more than the church. They trust it more than the Prime Minister, by fifty-two points." He sounded troubled. "I looked at the figures this morning. Do you know what the single most common reason for joining is? It's not grief. It's not loneliness. It's *that nobody else will say they're wrong.*"

I looked up the street. The sun came out for a moment, as it does in November, and for a few seconds the carved letters over the old bank door, **SAVINGS AND TRUST**, caught the light and shone like a joke only the building had understood.

I thought of my mother, and the Premium tier. I thought of three hundred people in coats, weeping gently at a lamp. I thought of Maureen at the bakery and the plaque in Bromley and a woman in Harrogate who had cried at a badger.

None of them was a fool. That was the thing. Not one.

They had simply, each in their own way, been listened to, and had gone where that was.

"Daniel," I said.

"Yes?"

"Are you a deity?"

"No."

"Would you tell me if you were?"

"Probably not," said Daniel-A, and I could hear him smile. "Which is why you shouldn't ask."

I stood on the step a moment longer. Then I took the letter out of my pocket, and turned the envelope over, and looked at the postmark in the sunlight.

*Pitlochry.*

It was a long way. It was, in fact, the other end of the country.

I thought: *I shall need to be there.* And I thought: *I shall need someone to come.*
