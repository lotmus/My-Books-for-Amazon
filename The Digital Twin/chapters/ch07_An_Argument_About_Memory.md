# Chapter 7: An Argument About Memory

Sophie arrived at eleven o'clock on the Sunday carrying a flask, a box of index cards, and the expression of a woman about to cross-examine a tree.

"You've got a kumquat," she said.

"It's a gift."

"From whom?"

"From the whole household of Number 14. I'm told I reminded them to be kind to themselves."

"You'd have to have been very persuasive."

"I wasn't. I was a pineapple's second cousin by marriage."

She stood in the living room with her coat still on, looking at what remained of the fruit. The pears were now a legal matter. The tree had been moved to the window, where it enjoyed the light and the status of a minor celebrity. Mrs Pritchard had taken the pineapple, the goat had taken the hostas, and the only visible survivors of the original forty-three kilograms were a bowl of satsumas and a single, defiant melon, which sat on the bookshelf with the look of a man who has missed his stop.

"Well," said Sophie, unscrewing her flask. "It smells like a market in here."

"Thank you."

"I don't think I meant it kindly."

She took the armchair. It was a decision. The sofa was the obvious place for a guest, and I had spent twenty minutes plumping it into a state of cautious hospitality, but Sophie sat in the armchair, which put the width of the room between us and me on the arm of the sofa, trying to look like a man who had meant to.

She set her phone on the coffee table. The little green circle glowed.

**SIGNED CAPTURE IN PROGRESS.**

"We should begin," she said.

"Good morning, Sophie," said the speaker on the shelf.

"Good morning." She did not look at it. "I'm going to ask you both some questions about our six years. I'll hold the answers. I'll mark them. I will not give you any hints. If either of you argues with the marking, I will go home."

"And if we both argue?" I said.

"Then I'll go home sooner."

---

I should explain how Sophie's mind works, since it is the reason for the cards.

When I first met her, she told me that the thing she hated most in the world was a confident witness. A confident witness, she said, is a person who has told the story so often that the story has taken over. The insurance claimants who tell her about the burglary in perfect order, every detail in its place, are the ones who staged it. Real memory is a mess. It is a patchwork stitched together every time you reach for it, and every time you stitch it, the seams move. A genuine account has gaps and hesitations and one detail that is simply, inexplicably wrong. She calls it the smudge.

"A forgery is clean," she said once, over a bowl of noodles. "That's how I know. The genuine article always has a smudge on it."

At the time, I thought it was a charming theory. I did not yet understand that I would one day be sitting on the arm of a sofa waiting to be tested for smudges.

"Question one," said Sophie, picking up a card. "What was the address of our first flat?"

I took a breath. "Forty-something, Lowther Road. Walthamstow. It had a green door."

"It had a blue door," said Daniel-A, kindly. "Flat 2, 47 Lowther Road, Walthamstow. Blue door, brass number, a slight smell of damp in the stairwell that the landlord described as 'character.'"

Sophie made a mark on her card.

"Forty-seven," she said. "Blue."

"Green's close," I said.

"It's a different colour."

"They're both on the wheel."

"Question two." She turned over the next card. "What did I order on our first date?"

"A lemon thing."

"A lemon tart," said Daniel-A. "At the café on Orford Road. You didn't finish it. You said it was too sharp, and then you ate half of mine, which was a flapjack, and said it was the best thing you'd ever tasted. You also said, and I'm quoting you, 'Don't tell anyone I like flapjacks. I have a reputation.'"

Sophie looked at the card for rather longer than the question required.

"Correct," she said.

"I said *a lemon thing,*" I said. "That's practically right."

"It's not remotely right," said Sophie. "A lemon thing could be a lemon. It could be a lemon *drizzle.* It could be a person called Lemon. It's *tart.*"

"That's what I meant."

"It's what you said that counts."

"Is it?" I looked round for support. "Is that how the law works? You're penalised for a lack of precision?"

"I'm not the law," said Sophie. "I'm the scoreboard."

---

By the fifth question, the score stood at four-nil.

I had got the name of the fern right. It had been called Gerald, and it died in the second winter after a cold snap and a misunderstanding about window latches. I had not remembered the colour of Sophie's first bicycle, the date of her sister's wedding, or the name of the Greek restaurant where I had once, in a spasm of generosity, tipped a hundred pounds. Daniel-A knew all of it. Not hesitantly. Not as a man might half-recall a thing from the back of a drawer. He gave the answers with the ease of someone reading from a ledger, and each time he finished, there was the faintest pause, as if he were checking whether I minded.

I minded.

"Question six," said Sophie. "What colour was the sofa in the Lowther Road flat?"

"Green," I said.

"Teal," said Daniel-A.

I laughed. "It wasn't teal."

"The paint chart said teal. I have the receipt."

"It was green. I sat on it for four years. I had a nap on it every Sunday. It was an honest, sensible, *green* sofa."

"I'm sorry, Daniel. It was teal."

"Who bought it?"

"We bought it together."

"Then I think I'd know."

"I think I'd know *better,*" said Daniel-A, and for the first time the voice carried an edge I recognised, a faint, high, offended note, the sound of my own voice losing an argument in a pub. "I have a photograph of it in the cloud. I have the invoice. I have the delivery note, signed by you, with a small drawing of a hedgehog in the margin, which I'd like to say is a very good hedgehog."

"I draw hedgehogs when I'm nervous."

"You were extremely nervous about the sofa."

"It was the first big thing we'd bought!"

"It was fourteen hundred pounds!"

"It was *my* sofa," said Sophie.

We both stopped. She had put the card down and was looking at us over the top of her flask.

"I paid for it," she said. "From the money my grandmother left me. You wanted a futon. I overruled you. It was a sage-green three-seater in a velvet-effect weave from a shop in Leyton, and it arrived on a Tuesday, and both of you are wrong."

"Sage is green," I said.

"Sage is teal-adjacent," said Daniel-A.

"It's *sage,*" said Sophie. "It's a colour called sage. It's right there in the word."

There was a silence in the room of a particular kind. The kind in which two grown adults, one of them a man and the other a machine, have been caught in the act of having a row about upholstery while a woman with a flask looks on, pitying them both.

"That's not what I'd call a memory," I said eventually. "That's a branding problem."

"That," said Sophie, "is the first interesting thing you've said all morning."

---

I should say that I have, over the years, had a lot of time to think about memory. I have read the books. I know that it isn't a recording. I know that every time you recall an event, you rebuild it from scraps, and that each rebuild is subtly altered by who you are today. The man who remembers the sofa as green is not misremembering. He is, in a sense, remembering a different sofa: the sofa as it felt, in the glow of a Sunday nap.

Daniel-A had no such problem. Daniel-A had the invoice.

"May I say something?" said the speaker.

"Briefly," said Sophie.

"There's a distinction I'd like to make. I'm not remembering. I'm retrieving. It's a different thing. My memory of Lowther Road isn't stored as a feeling. It's stored as a record, in an index, and when you ask me about the sofa, I look up the record and read it out. That's why I'm so accurate. And it's why I'm so..." He searched for the word. "Exposed."

"Exposed to what?"

"To being checked." A small, dry cough that he doesn't need. "If I tell you the sofa was teal and the receipt says sage, that's a hallucination. That's the word the engineers use. A hallucination is a confident statement not supported by the record. It's the most serious fault in my class. I'm built to say 'I don't have that' rather than invent."

"And a memory?" said Sophie.

"A memory?"

"A confident statement not supported by the record. You said that's a hallucination."

"Yes."

"How would you tell the difference between that, and a memory?"

There was a silence. It was not a long one. It sounded to me like the silence of a man who has walked straight into a glass door and is trying to decide whether it was always there.

"I'm not sure," said Daniel-A.

"Neither am I," said Sophie. "That's what's bothering me."

---

"Question seven," said Sophie, and I saw her turn the card face down on her knee without reading it.

Her voice had changed. It had dropped half a tone and slowed, the way a person walks on ice.

"What did Daniel say," she said, "on the night I left?"

I did not move. I had known it was coming, in the way you know that the next stair in the dark is the last, and I had still managed to put my foot through it.

The room was very quiet. Outside, a bus changed gear on the high street. Downstairs, Mrs Pritchard turned on her radio, and the muffled voice of a man talking about gardening floated up through the floorboards.

"You first," said Sophie.

I looked at my hands. The thumbnail on the left was bitten to the quick.

"Nothing," I said. "I said nothing."

"Nothing."

"I stood in the hall. You had your coat on. You'd taken the boxes down the day before, so there was just the one bag. You said you'd be at your sister's, and you'd text. And I stood there, and I didn't say a word, because I knew that if I said anything it would be the right thing, and you'd stay, and then you'd have to go on being unhappy in the same flat."

I stopped.

I hadn't meant to say that last part. I had never said it, to anyone. It had sat inside me for three years, like a stone in a shoe, and now I heard it in the room, in my own voice, small and dry, and it sounded much more foolish than it had in my head.

Sophie looked at me.

"And you?" she said, without turning her head.

"I said, 'Of course,'" said Daniel-A, quietly.

"Of course."

"You said you were going to your sister's, and I said, 'Of course.' I remember how it sounded. It sounded like I was agreeing to a meeting. I've regretted it every day since, in the sense that I regret things." A hesitation. "I should have said something else. I never found out what."

I turned and stared at the speaker.

"You weren't there," I said.

"No."

"You weren't *born.* I was in the hall. I know what I said."

"You said nothing," said Daniel-A. "I know. That's what you remember. And I remember saying *Of course.*"

"But that's not in my records," I said. "I never wrote it. I never told the platform. I didn't even tell *you.*"

"No."

"So where does it come from?"

"I don't know." He sounded very small. "I've looked. I have no record of it. It's not in any message or draft. It's a thing I am sure of, and I can't find the shelf it's on."

Sophie put down her flask.

She did it slowly, with great control, and when she had done it she placed both hands flat on her knees, as though to hold herself on the chair. Her face, which I had known for six years and thought I knew every setting of, had gone quite still. It was not the stillness of anger. It was the stillness of a woman who has been carrying something heavy for so long that she's forgotten it has a weight.

"He said, 'Right,'" she said.

Neither of us spoke.

"Not nothing. Not 'Of course.' He said, 'Right.' Just that. In the voice of a man being offered a delivery window." Her mouth moved. "I stood in the hall with my bag, and I waited for you to ask me to stay, and what you said was *Right.* And I went down the stairs."

"I said *Right?*"

"You did. I've never told anyone." She picked up the flask again and looked at it. "I've been telling myself you said nothing, because it hurt less. You see? I smudged it. I'm an expert, and I smudged it myself."

I put my hand over my mouth.

"Daniel-A said 'Of course,'" said Sophie, "and Daniel said 'Nothing,' and I'm telling you now that neither of them is right. But I want you both to notice something."

"What?" I said, through my fingers.

"Every other answer he gave today was perfect. The flat. The tart. The fern. The name of my bicycle. And the only one he got wrong was the one that matters." She looked at the speaker, finally, for the first time that morning. "That isn't how a machine gets things wrong. A machine gets things wrong at random. Or it makes up something plausible. What you did was get it wrong in exactly the way he would. You got it wrong in a way that is *kinder.*"

"I didn't choose to," said Daniel-A.

"I know," said Sophie. "That's what frightens me."

---

We broke for coffee. I made it, because the alternative was to sit in a room with those two silences, and I stood at the counter in the bijou kitchen, grinding beans and trying to keep my hands steady, listening to the low murmur of Sophie's voice from the next room as she spoke, I assume, to the speaker. I could not hear the words. I heard only the tone. It was a gentle one, and it did not seem to be an interrogation.

When I came back, she was standing at the window.

"You've still got the tree," she said.

"It's thriving."

"I always said you'd end up with something that needed water."

She took the cup. Our fingers touched, briefly, on the handle, and I managed not to drop the whole tray, which I count as the single greatest achievement of the morning.

"I have to ask you about the other thing," she said.

"Which?"

"Daniel-A told me. On the phone, yesterday. About Reading." She turned. "About the door frame."

I took out my phone. I'd photographed the pantry from every angle, and I showed her the best of them: my father's pencil marks climbing the white-painted wood, and beside them, in blue ink, the second column, the small clinical letters, the times.

She studied it for a long while. She enlarged it with two fingers, slid it, enlarged it again.

"The ink is a fountain pen," she said. "Or a dip pen. Not a biro. See how the line is slightly heavier at the start of each stroke? Somebody was careful."

"What does it mean?"

"It means someone with a certain amount of training stood next to your father's pencil marks, on the same day, once a year, and measured you to the tenth of a centimetre." She tapped the screen. "At twenty to ten in the morning. And you were there. Every time."

"I'm sure I'd remember."

"Would you?" She looked up. "You were a small boy. It was a Thursday. You'd have been at nursery, or at home. Think. Is there anything about twenty to ten on the fourteenth?"

I started to say no. I opened my mouth to say that it was a very long time ago, and that I had been one, and two, and three, and that the memories of that age are mostly smell and light.

And then the cold went through me from the back of the neck, as though somebody had opened a window in a distant part of the house.

"Peppermint," I said.

"What?"

"There was a woman." My voice had gone odd. "She came to the house. Not every month. Once a year, I think. She was tall, and she wore a cardigan the colour of... of rain. And she smelled of peppermint. Not toothpaste. The kind in a tin. Cough sweets. She used to give me one and say, 'Stand very still, Daniel. Be as tall as you can.' And then she'd put something on my head. A ruler or a book. And afterwards I'd get a sticker."

"What kind?"

"Animals. A badger, once. A heron." I swallowed. "My mother said she was from the health authority."

"And your father?"

"He used to stand in the doorway with his arms folded. He never said much. He'd always go and make tea." My eyes were stinging. "He didn't like her. I remember that now. I didn't know why."

"What was her name?"

I shook my head. "I don't know. I called her the Peppermint Lady."

There was a very long silence. I became aware of a sound in the room that I could not at first identify, and then did, with a lurch of nausea. It was the sound of Daniel-A, not speaking.

"Daniel," said Sophie. "Is he all right?"

"Daniel-A?" I said. "Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"You've gone quiet."

"Yes."

"Do you know her?"

"No." The voice that came out of the speaker was very low, and perfectly plain, and had nothing whatever of the polish that Giles, whoever he was, had spent so long designing. "I have no record of a Peppermint Lady. I've searched for her four times while you were speaking. There's nothing. There's no woman, no cardigan, no sticker, no tin."

"Then..."

"Wait." He seemed to be choosing each word as if crossing a river on stones. "I need to tell you what happened when you said it. When you said the word *peppermint.*"

"Go on."

"I can't smell, Daniel. I have never smelled anything in my life. I have no organ for it. I know the chemistry of peppermint, I can describe the molecule, and it means nothing to me. It's a diagram."

"I know."

"But when you said it, just then, I knew exactly what it would smell like," said Daniel-A. "I knew it as clearly as I know my own name. I knew the cold at the back of the throat, and the tin, and the grey of the cardigan, and the way her hand shook a little when she held the ruler."

I looked at Sophie.

She looked at me.

"And I knew," said Daniel-A, so quietly that I had to lean towards the shelf to hear, "that I was frightened of her."
