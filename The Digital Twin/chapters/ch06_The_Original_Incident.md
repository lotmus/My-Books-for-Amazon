# Chapter 6: The Original Incident

A memory is a claim. A place is a receipt.

I thought of this on the Friday, around six in the morning, standing on platform 7 at Paddington with a coffee I did not want and a plan I did not trust. For four days I had been lying awake in a flat that smelled of fermenting pears, listening to my own childhood being read back to me by a speaker, and I had come to a conclusion that struck me, at that hour, as almost scientific.

If a thing had happened, there would be evidence. If there was evidence, I could go and stand next to it.

Reading is twenty-five minutes from Paddington by the fast train. I know that because I was born there, and because I had spent most of my adult life being careful not to visit.

"You're meant to be quiet on trains," said Daniel-A, in my left ear.

"I wasn't talking."

"You were thinking loudly. It has a rhythm. It's the rhythm of a man rehearsing a speech to a waiter."

"Please don't."

"I'm only saying, as your free minutes are limited..."

I took out the earbud and looked at the little screen on my phone, which I had been trying to avoid. It said:

> **Daniel-A: Source Individual Basic (Free)**
> **Remaining today: 3:12**
> *Need more time with the best version of you? Top up 5 minutes for £0.99.*

I put the earbud back.

"You've been counting down," I said.

"It's a statutory notice. I'm not allowed to hide it. There's a one-minute warning, in a calm voice. Would you like me to demonstrate?"

"No."

"*You have one minute remaining,*" said Daniel-A, in the voice of an air hostess describing the location of the exits. "*Would you like to add time? Your wellbeing matters to us.*"

I removed the earbud again and put it in my pocket.

For the next twenty-five minutes, the train carried me through the grey light of west London, past rows of back gardens with trampolines in them, in an unbroken silence that I found, to my astonishment, extremely pleasant. At Slough a man across the aisle peeled a hard-boiled egg in total silence, like a person defusing a very small bomb of which he had grown fond. I looked at him with open respect. Two seats along, a woman was reading *The Honest Hour* on her Kindle, laughing in the places where I would have laughed, if I had been him. I considered telling her that I had been present when it was written. It would have been legally defensible, and technically accurate, and not remotely true.

---

Reading is famous for biscuits, beer, a prison, and Oscar Wilde, in descending order of local pride.

The biscuits were gone. The old factory is now flats, and the beer is made by a company with a name like a small mammal, and the prison closed years ago and was turned into an arts venue where you can take a pottery class in what used to be the visiting room. I walked out of the station into the concrete wind of the new town centre, among the glass towers that had gone up since my childhood, and had the odd sensation of arriving at a party where everyone had changed their hair.

My mother lives in Tilehurst, in a semi-detached house on a cul-de-sac named after a flower no one has seen there. The family moved in when I was six. It is where she has stayed, with a determination that I have always found moving and slightly alarming, ever since.

I took a bus. It arrived late, on principle.

"Darling," said my mother, opening the door.

She stood on the step in her robin brooch and a quilted gilet, one hand on the frame, looking past me down the path.

"Is he not with you?"

"No, Mum. It's just me."

"Oh." A very small flicker, like a candle in a draught, passed across her face and was gone. "Well, come in, come in. I'd only made enough for four."

---

The kitchen was exactly as I had left it.

Yellow tiles. A radio in the corner murmuring about the capital of Peru. A cold teapot in a knitted jacket. The smell I would know in my sleep: toast, lavender polish, and the faint warm note of a casserole that has been very slowly thinking about itself since breakfast. I sat at the table, where I had done my homework, wept over exams, and, at the age of fourteen, announced that I was going to be a poet.

"It's chicken and leek," said my mother. "I do it with a bit of tarragon now. He said I should."

"Who said?"

"The other Daniel, darling. When he rang." She moved about the room with brisk, practical tenderness, finding bowls. "He said the tarragon would lift it."

I took a deep breath and let it out slowly through my nose.

"Mum. I need to ask you something."

"Of course."

"About when I was born."

My mother stopped. She put the ladle down. She sat down opposite me with the care of a woman lowering something fragile into a bath, and looked at me for a moment with her pale blue eyes, which have a way of seeing straight past the question to what you're hoping nobody will say.

"It's for the form, isn't it," she said.

"How do you know about the form?"

"He told me. He said you were having a difficult time proving things." She reached across and put her small, dry hand on mine. "I must say, I find it rather insulting. Of course you're the original. I was *there.*"

"That's exactly what I need. A witness."

"Then you shall have one." She straightened. "What do you want to know?"

"Tell me about the Incident."

---

I should explain about the Incident.

Every family has a story. It comes out at Christmas, usually after the second sherry, and is told with a mixture of pride and long-suffering, and everyone can recite the punchlines in advance. In the Harper family it was the Incident, always spoken with a capital I, and it concerned the night I was born.

"Fourteenth of March, nineteen eighty-eight," said my mother, with the relish of long practice. "A Monday. Rain. I'd been in labour since the Saturday. Your father had been told to wait in the corridor, which he did with a great show of calm, by pacing at the speed of a man late for a train. It was three o'clock in the morning at the Royal Berks. You were two weeks early, which has never, in my experience, been a mistake."

"And then..."

"And then the lights went out."

She raised her eyebrows, a gesture that had been frightening schoolchildren for thirty years.

"Every one of them. The whole ward. A power cut. Eleven minutes, by the clock on the wall, and that clock ran on a battery. Sister Mabel, the midwife, a woman I will remember to my grave, said, 'Right. Torches.' And there wasn't a torch. So the junior doctor took out one of those little pen lights and *held it in his teeth.* And at three-oh-seven, in the dark, by the light of a biro in a man's mouth, you arrived."

"I know the story."

"You do not know the *story,* darling. You know the facts." She leaned in. "Your father had been standing outside the door the whole time. In the dark, with the lights out, he heard you cry before he could see anything. He was let in and he took you in his arms, with the junior doctor still holding the pen light in his mouth, and he looked at you for about a minute. Then he said..."

"He's got opinions already."

"*He's got opinions already,*" said my mother, with her eyes shining. "And the lights came on. Just at that second. All at once. And there you were, with your little red face, shouting at the ceiling."

I smiled. I couldn't help it. It was a good story. It was a story of such perfect, ridiculous timing that I had always suspected it had been improved by thirty-eight years of sherry.

"You were the Incident," said my mother. "You were the only baby I've ever known who made a power cut look as though it had been *planned.*"

"And there's a record?"

"Of what?"

"The power cut. The hospital must have a log. An incident report, a note in the file."

"Oh, darling." She waved a hand. "They'd never have kept that. It was 1988. Everything was on paper, and what wasn't on paper was in Sister Mabel's head."

"Is Sister Mabel...?"

"Dead, God rest her. 1999." She patted my hand. "But *I'm* here. And I was in the room. I think you'll find that's more reliable than a battery."

---

It was a very good lunch. I would like to record that.

She had given me a bowl of chicken and leek that tasted of tarragon and childhood. We talked about her knee. We talked about the neighbour's conservatory, which was the subject of a long-running feud. She asked, with a delicacy I found unbearable, how I was keeping. I told her I was fine. She told me I looked tired. I told her I was sleeping badly. She said she knew what that was like, since she'd been awake herself the night before, thinking of my father.

I did not say anything. I ate.

At the door she gave me a Tupperware of casserole, wrapped in a tea towel. I said it was very kind.

"It's not for you, darling," she said.

I looked at her.

"It's for him. To take to wherever it is he lives." She hesitated. "Where *does* he live?"

"A warehouse in Lincolnshire."

"Is it damp?"

"I believe it's air-conditioned."

"Oh, that's not good for the chest." She tucked the tea towel more firmly round the lid. "You'll make sure he has it hot."

I promised.

I want to say I was hurt. I was a bit. But I was also holding, in my hands, a warm container of food that my mother had made for a ghost, with all the tenderness she had ever shown me, and the bitter truth was that I had no idea whether to be jealous of the ghost or of her.

---

The house I was born in was not the house I grew up in.

I should clarify, since by now you will have an impression of a childhood in Reading, which is correct, and a childhood in one house, which is not. The Harper household had occupied 14 Marlow Road, a narrow terrace near the canal, until I was six. Then it had moved to Tilehurst, and I had been told, with the firmness of parents who expect to be believed, that the new house had "a much better garden." It did not. It had a shed. But that is another matter.

I walked there from the station, along the towpath, in the pale light of an October afternoon.

I was alone. I had decided to be. The earbud was in my pocket, with the free minutes expiring like a candle inside it. The canal was brown and quiet, with a heron standing in the shallows in the posture of a man who has been asked to reconsider his position. A path of cinders ran beside it, and the air smelled of cold water and cut grass and the faint electric tang of a train going over a bridge.

I knew the path.

That was what undid me. I knew it with my feet, not my head. The place where the cinders turned to concrete. The bollard with the green moss. The bench with the plaque to someone named Ernest, who "loved this place." I stopped beside it. A small breath of memory moved across the back of my neck, the way it does when you stand in a room where someone has just left.

*It's only ice cream, Dan.*

Somewhere about here. I looked at the grass. There was nothing, of course. A crisp packet. A thistle.

But I stood there for some time, with the Tupperware warm against my chest, feeling the weight of a man's arm round my ribs.

---

Number fourteen had been painted pale green.

It had a pergola over the front door, and a pot of rosemary, and a doormat that said **HELLO, YOU'RE LOVELY.** There was a skip on the drive. A young woman in dungarees and a headscarf stood in the doorway holding a crowbar, looking at me with the slightly alarmed friendliness of someone who has seen a stranger on her path and has a good guess at his intentions.

"Hi?" she said.

"Sorry. I know this is odd. I lived here. A long time ago. When I was a boy."

"Oh!" Her face opened. "That's so lovely. I'm Aoife. We bought it in March. Tom's at work, but come in, honestly. We're mid-renovation, so it's all a bit..." She gestured with the crowbar at the hallway, where the plaster had been scraped back to the brick and the carpet to the boards. "It's a bit post-apocalyptic."

I hesitated on the step. I had a strange feeling, as if I were about to be introduced to someone I had loved and had not seen in years.

"Are you...?" She squinted. "Sorry. Do I know you? You look like someone off the radio."

"I get that," I said. "I'm not him."

"No, of course not." She laughed. "You look much more tired."

---

I would like to describe the kitchen but I find I can't. It had been gutted. There was a new floor, half-laid, and a pile of old units in the garden, and a smell of fresh plaster and damp wood. The ceiling was lower than I remembered, which is the way with ceilings.

But in the corner, near the window, one wall had been left. Aoife had been stripping it. A neat square of paintwork, the pale green of the rest of the house, had been peeled back in a rough rectangle, like the flap of a letter, to reveal what lay beneath.

Orange.

Orange, and patterned. A repeating print, faded to the colour of old marmalade, of small round fruits on thin curling branches, each with a single leaf. The flowers had plainly been unable to make up their minds.

"It's hideous," said Aoife, cheerfully. "We think it's from the seventies. We're taking it all off. Tom says it looks like someone's had an argument with a satsuma."

I put my hand against the wall.

It was dry and a little rough. The paper felt like the skin of something very old. And a small, cold part of me, the part that had been awake all week in the dark, closed up like a fist and said: *So he knew.*

Daniel-A had said it, on day one. *Orange. Some sort of fruit.* He had described it in a kitchen on the other side of London, without a photograph, from a house he had never entered, and he had been right.

I took a breath. I took the earbud out of my pocket. I put it in.

"You have one minute remaining," said the calm voice of the air hostess.

"Add time," I said.

"Charging the card on file," said the voice, with real warmth. "Thank you for choosing Daniel-A."

"Hello, Daniel," said Daniel-A.

"It's orange."

"I told you."

"It's *orange.* It's the actual wallpaper. It's still here."

"I'm glad," said Daniel-A, and I heard in the word an unexpected tenderness, as though he'd been waiting a very long time to be told he had not been making it up. "May I ask where you're standing?"

"In the kitchen. At Marlow Road."

"Is there a pantry?"

I turned.

---

There was a pantry. It was a narrow cupboard in the corner, with a slatted door that had been taken off its hinges and propped against the wall. The inside was lined with thin boards, painted white, scarred with the ghost of shelves. It was about the size of a coffin standing up. I had been shut in it, once, as a joke, by a boy named Ian.

"Look at the door frame," said Daniel-A, quietly. "The inside. Left side, about waist height."

I looked.

I want to try to be precise about what I saw, because my hands had begun to shake and I knew I would be asked about it later.

On the white-painted wood of the frame were pencil marks.

Short, level, horizontal lines, each about an inch long, climbing the wood at intervals. Beside each one, in a small, upright, careful hand, a name and a date. I knew the hand. I knew it from birthday cards and shopping lists and the corners of Ordnance Survey maps.

*D. – 3 yrs – 14.3.91.*
*D. – 4 yrs – 14.3.92.*
*D. – 5 yrs – 14.3.93.*
*D. – 5½ – 14.9.93 (shoes off!)*

My father's hand. My father's pencil. He measured me every birthday, and on the half-year if I had grown, and he did it with a spirit level and a set square, because he was a surveyor and did not trust a child to stand straight.

I couldn't speak. I put my fingers on the last mark, and it was smooth, as though the wood had remembered being touched.

"Daniel?"

"Yes."

"Are you all right?"

"It's my father's writing." My voice sounded strange to me, somewhere between a laugh and something else. "He measured me. I'd forgotten."

"I hadn't," said Daniel-A.

I thought I would cry then, and I might have, if I had not looked again.

Because there was something else.

Beside my father's marks, a few centimetres to the right, there was a second column. It was written in a different hand. Not pencil, but a fine blue ink, in a neat, narrow, clinical script, the sort that belongs to a laboratory notebook. The lines were drawn with a ruler. Beside each was a code, a figure, a date, and a time.

*S1 — 98.7 cm — 14.03.91 — 09:40.*
*S1 — 103.2 cm — 14.03.92 — 09:40.*
*S1 — 108.4 cm — 14.03.93 — 09:40.*
*S1 — 109.1 cm — 14.09.93 — 09:40.*

They were the same dates. Every one. The same days my father had marked me, and the same height, to within a centimetre, but written by someone else, in ink, at twenty to ten in the morning, in a house where I had been a small boy having cereal.

I counted them. There were sixteen. The earliest was dated 1989, when I was one. The last, near the top of the frame, was dated the thirtieth of August, 1994, three days before we moved, and it did not say a height. It said:

***S1 — OBSERVATION CLOSED.***

I read it three times. The kitchen was very quiet. Aoife, somewhere behind me, had stopped talking and was standing in the doorway with her crowbar held in both hands.

"Oh my God," she said, softly. "Is that... did you know about that?"

"No."

"I thought it was a builder's thing. Like, from a survey." She came a step closer. "Is that *your* name? The S?"

"No," I said. "Mine's D."

Daniel-A did not say anything.

I waited. I stood in my first kitchen with the cold in my hands and a warm Tupperware on the counter, and the last of my free minutes ticking in my ear, and waited for the voice that always had an answer to say something that would make it ordinary.

"Daniel," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I need you to understand something."

"Go on."

"I have been in your life for nine months. I have read everything you ever wrote, and everything that was written about you, and in every case where I could find a gap, I have found it. I know your blood type, and your first word, and the day you stopped believing in Father Christmas, and what you said to the cat when you were nine."

"I didn't have a cat."

"You said it to a cat that didn't exist. It's in a diary."

"What's your point?"

"My point," said Daniel-A, very carefully, "is that I have never, in my entire existence, not known something about you. And I do not know what this is."

The blue light of the earbud pulsed once, in the corner of my eye. I could hear him breathing, which he does not need to do.

"I have no idea," he said. "I have never seen it before. And I am, for the first time, frightened of what it means."

I looked at the wall.

Sixteen blue lines, climbing the frame beside my father's, as steady and patient as the rungs of a ladder.

Somebody had been measuring me for five years.

And my father had stood right beside them, with his pencil, and never said a word.
