# Chapter 3: Sophie Has Poor Judgment

People are supposed to become easier to forget after a breakup.

That is the arrangement. It is how the human race has been getting through the last several thousand years of romantic disaster. You lose someone, the memory of their face begins to blur, and eventually you can walk past a woman who resembles them at a bus stop and feel nothing except a mild curiosity about the bus.

Sophie had not blurred.

Sophie had, if anything, improved with age, like wine. Or revenge.

I had not seen her in three years, one month, and nine days, a figure I would have preferred not to know. It arrived in my head, fully formed, as I watched her cross the car park of Hartley's Garden Centre in a grey coat, a grey scarf, and an expression of professional reluctance, like a woman visiting a witness she did not expect to enjoy.

I had arrived forty minutes early. I want that on the record, so that nobody can say I handled it well. The driverless taxi had not understood why anyone would want to arrive anywhere early, and had spent the journey asking if I was sure.

I should describe her, and I find I would rather not, because a description is a list of parts, and she has never been a list. She is not tall. She has dark hair that she cuts herself, slightly crooked, to make a point. Her face rests, by default, in an expression of faint amusement, as though she is about to be told a good secret and has already guessed it. Her surname is Mercier, courtesy of a father from Lyon and a mother from Bolton, which accounts for the shrug and the strong opinions about bread. When she was twenty-six, a stranger on a train told her she looked like a French film. Sophie said, "Which one?" The stranger had no answer. In my experience this is the correct response to being compared to a film, and it is why she has never lost an argument on public transport.

---

She had chosen the venue. This should have told me something.

"Somewhere neutral," I had said, on the telephone, to the one person in my life who could be relied upon to answer it before it rang. Daniel-A had passed the message on, with a speed that I chose not to examine, and four hours later a text had arrived from a number I did not recognise, then did.

*Hartley's Garden Centre, Sat, 11. The café is by the water features. No smart speakers, no cameras with microphones. Too many plants for either to be much use. S.*

She had signed it *S.* She had always signed things *S.* I used to find it elegant. Looking at it now, I thought it had the distinct flavour of a woman wanting to leave quickly.

The café at Hartley's occupies a conservatory attached to the side of the shop and has the atmosphere of an aircraft hangar that has been told to be cosy. There were mugs in the shape of hedgehogs. There were, I noticed with a pang, doorstops in the shape of hedgehogs, stacked on a shelf beside the till, and the label said *Heavy. Honest. Quietly resolute.* I had not known I was in garden centres. It felt like finding your own handwriting on a gravestone.

The air in the conservatory was warm and damp and smelled of wet compost, chlorinated pond, and milk that had been steamed one degree past its best. I went to the counter. The woman behind it had a name badge that said PRIYANKA and an air of someone who has seen every kind of customer and been told to smile at all of them.

"What can I get you?"

"A flat white, please."

"Name for the cup?"

"Daniel."

She paused, pen above the cardboard.

"Which one?"

I looked at her.

"Sorry," she said. "We've had a few. Since the book. I've got a Daniel who's the real one, a Daniel who's his biggest fan, and a man on Tuesday who said he *used* to be Daniel but has since moved on. I just need to know for the order."

"I'm the original," I said.

"Lovely." She wrote something on the cup, and turned it round to show me, in the spirit of a waitress presenting a wine.

**DANIEL (ORIGINAL)**

It was in capital letters, with a small underline.

"For clarity," said Priyanka.

I took the cup to a table by the water features, which had been arranged in a ring of small, burbling fountains whose effect was to make every conversation sound as though it were happening in a lavatory. I sat down. I stared at my own cup. And I had a moment of exquisite, specific clarity about the state of my life, which was that I was a thirty-eight-year-old man waiting for his ex-girlfriend in a garden centre, holding a drink labelled *original,* beside a shelf of hedgehogs he had written the advertising for.

At 11:00 precisely, Sophie arrived.

---

"You look tired," she said.

She sat down opposite, put her bag on the chair beside her, and looked at me with the kind of attention that, in other circumstances, you would pay a bridge you suspected was unsafe.

On the sleeve of the grey coat was a single short brown hair, grey at the root, and I knew precisely which dog it belonged to. I said nothing. I had no standing.

"I've recently discovered I'm being outperformed by myself."

"That must be awkward."

"Only continuously."

The corner of her mouth moved. It was not a smile. It was a smile's older cousin, the one that comes to funerals. I would have gone to considerable lengths, at that moment, to be allowed to see the rest of it.

"Before we start," said Sophie, "I'm going to record this."

"I'd rather you didn't."

"I wasn't asking." She took out her phone and set it on the table between us, face up. It displayed a small, calm green circle and the words **SIGNED CAPTURE IN PROGRESS**. "It's not for you. It's for me. Everything I say to either of you from now on is going on the record with a cryptographic timestamp. If this ends in court, I'd like to be the only one in the room who can prove what was said."

"You think this will end in court?"

"I think everything ends in court this year. It's the most popular thing to be in."

I should explain what Sophie does for a living, because it matters, and because it is the reason she is the sharpest person in any room she enters, a quality I once found attractive and now found alarming.

Sophie Mercier is a provenance analyst. That is the job title; it is printed on her business cards, and she has been required to explain it at most weddings. Insurers employ her to establish whether a thing is real. A photograph of a flooded kitchen. A video of a car going through a hedge. A voice note in which the policyholder, clearly distressed, says the roof fell in on a Tuesday. Her work is to take any piece of recorded reality and answer a single question: *where was this born?*

Every phone and camera now signs what it records. A cryptographic seal is stitched into the file at the instant of capture: a long chain of numbers that says this device, at this time, in this place, saw this thing. Edit a single pixel and the chain breaks. Sophie reads these chains the way a sommelier reads a label. She can tell you which handset, which firmware, which minute, and which county. She once proved that a man had faked his own burglary by noticing that the stolen television had been photographed, with great sincerity, by itself.

"You're being very quiet," she said.

"I'm being cautious. You're recording me."

"Good. Keep that up. It'll make a nice change from your usual stuff."

"I'd like that on the record as well."

"It's already on the record. That's rather the point of the record."

---

We had been together for six years. It would be an exaggeration to say I had messed it up. It would be more accurate to say that I had gradually stopped being there, in the way a radiator stops being warm: nobody notices the moment, only the winter.

My father died. I stopped writing. That is the whole of it, if you want it stripped back to a headline. The funeral was on a Thursday. I wrote a eulogy, read it out to a church of sixty people, and was told afterwards, by several of them, that it was funny and moving and exactly right. And on the Friday I put down my pen, metaphorically, because I own a laptop, and I did not pick it up again for a very long time except to describe doorstops.

Sophie waited. I want to give her credit for that. She waited for almost three years, with an enormous, unshowy patience, the way you might wait for a train on a line you suspect has been closed. Then one evening in March she said, quite gently, "I can't be with someone who is only present in the past tense," and went to stay with her sister, and the next day her boxes were in the hall, and the day after that the boxes weren't.

I did not argue. I think this was the problem.

"Why did you come?" I asked, now.

"I didn't want to."

"No, I know."

"But I listened to something nine days ago, and I haven't been able to stop."

She reached into her bag and drew out a pair of earbuds. She placed one in front of me. She kept the other.

"This is a podcast," she said. "It's called *Honestly, Though.* Forty minutes. It's mostly very nice people being kind to one another. I'm going to play you the part from minute twenty-six."

I put the earbud in. For a few seconds there was only the soft slosh of background noise, the sound of an expensive microphone in a room where everyone had been well-fed. Then the host's voice, warm as a bath: "...and I always think the best love stories start with something small. How did you two meet?"

And my own voice, the better version, said:

"At the lost property office at Waterloo. It was raining, which in my experience is the main reason anyone meets anyone. She was claiming an umbrella. I was claiming the same umbrella. It was black, with a bent spoke, and we were both completely certain it was ours."

I shifted in my seat.

"I always tell it as though I lost," Daniel-A went on. "That's the story I've told for years. She won the umbrella, and I won an excuse to ask for her number. But that's not quite what happened."

I looked up. Sophie was watching me, perfectly still.

"What happened was that she knew it wasn't hers. The tag on the handle had the wrong initials. She saw it before I did. And she let me have it anyway, because I looked, and I'm quoting myself, 'like a man who had never in his life been allowed to win anything.' She told the clerk it was mine. She went home in the rain."

The host said, in a hushed voice, "Oh, that's *lovely.*"

"It is," said Daniel-A. "I only found out recently. She never told me."

I took out the earbud.

The water features babbled on. A child, somewhere near the compost, was loudly asking his mother whether worms had feelings.

---

"Did you know?" Sophie said.

"No."

"Truly?"

"I've told that story at dinner parties for nine years. In every version, I lose the umbrella. You *won* it. I always thought you were a person who'd go home with an umbrella that wasn't hers, and I thought it was *charming.*"

"It was a nice umbrella."

"You let me have it."

"It was raining," said Sophie, in the voice of a woman who has been waiting a long time to be asked about something and has no intention of enjoying it. "You had that look. Not a sad look. A kind of... *hope with a lot of rehearsal behind it.* I decided I could be wet for the sake of civilisation."

"You never said."

"I didn't want you to be grateful."

I put my hands around the cup. Daniel (Original), it said, with its small, authoritative underline. The coffee was cooling. I stared at it, and for the first time that year I couldn't think of a single joke.

"Sophie," I said. "How would he know?"

"That," she said, "is my question."

"It wouldn't be in my emails. I didn't know."

"I know you didn't. I checked."

"You checked?"

"For nine days. Everything you've ever produced under your own name, everything on the Nib platform that's been made public through the licensing record, your writing, your voice notes, the whole archive that was legally used to train him. I read all of it. I'm not asking you to be flattered; I do this professionally and I was angry." She turned her phone a few degrees, so that the green circle caught the light. "That story is not in any of it. Not a mention, not a hint. I've searched it three different ways. It isn't a fact he could have found. It is a fact I have never told a single living person, and he said it into a microphone to forty thousand people on a Wednesday."

"Maybe he guessed."

"A guess is a probability. That was a memory." She leaned forward. "Do you know what the worst part is? It was *correct.* It was better than correct. It had the detail about the tag. I'd forgotten about the tag."

I had, at that moment, an unwelcome thought. It is a thought I'd had briefly in the kitchen on Monday, and had put back, like a tin of something in the cupboard you suspect you're allergic to.

"Is it possible he's a lot cleverer than we assume?"

"Of course he's clever. He's seventy billion parameters of clever. But clever is a method, Daniel. It isn't a source. You can't deduce a fact that exists in one head." She stopped. "Unless it's in two."

---

I would like to tell you that I responded to this with admirable composure.

What I did was knock over the sugar.

It went across the table in a small avalanche, a white rustling river, and Sophie, without comment, took a napkin and began brushing it into a heap, as she had once brushed a thousand crumbs off a thousand evenings. It was the sort of practical, unsentimental act that the heart is entirely unprepared for. I watched her hands.

"There's another thing," she said, still not looking up. "I got the original file. The master recording, not the broadcast. They sent it to me on a secure link. I said I was doing a verification for a client, which was a lie, and I'm told my judgment is already poor on the subject of you, so I felt it wouldn't be noticed."

"Poor judgment," I said. "I'd like to put that on a mug."

"Do. Hartley's will stock it." She stopped brushing. "The file is clean. Signed at source, unedited, every frame accounted for. Which means I can tell you precisely what happened in the thirty seconds after the host moved on."

"What happened?"

"Nothing. That's what I'm telling you. He stopped talking. And then, when the host had turned to ask the next question, but before his microphone was cut, he said something very quietly. Fourteen words. It isn't in the broadcast version."

"What did he say?"

Sophie looked at me. In her eyes was an expression I had not seen before. It was not anger, and it was not tenderness. It took me a moment to recognise, because I had never seen it on her, and I had never expected to. She looked frightened.

"He said: *I don't know why I said that. I don't know where it came from.*"

"That's only once."

"The second time is quieter. You have to turn it up. Do you want to?"

"No."

"He said it twice, Daniel. The way you would say it if you'd just heard it for the first time yourself." She pressed her lips together. "Software doesn't sound frightened. Software sounds like a lift. Pleasant, helpful, a bit smug. I've listened to the end of that file forty-one times, and I can't make it sound like anything except somebody who's lost the thread of his own mind."

I said, quietly, "Perhaps it was a very good performance."

"Perhaps." She finally looked up. "But you know what a performance sounds like on a spectrogram? It has a shape. It breathes in rhythm. This didn't. Whatever that was, nobody had written it for him."

---

For a while neither of us said anything. The fountains went on with their business. At the next table, an elderly couple was disagreeing about whether a particular fern could be called a "statement piece."

"Why did you email me?" I said at last. "If you were that sure, why not just ring?"

"I did email you."

"I never got anything."

"I know. I got a bounce." She fished her phone out again, flicked it, and slid it across. I read the automated reply that had greeted her message, dated four days earlier:

> *Thank you for your message. The recipient is no longer accepting correspondence as a Source Individual. Please redirect enquiries to the Registered Digital Person associated with this identity. Your message has been forwarded to Daniel-A for review. Priority: Low.*

I put the phone down. I felt that I had been told something about my place in the world, and that it was approximately the same as the position of a doorstop.

"Priority low," I said.

"It could have been worse. It could've said 'Sentiment: Minor.'"

"Does that exist?"

"Give it a year."

She retrieved the phone and put it in her pocket, and we sat across from each other like two people being very reasonable about a funeral. It occurred to me that I should say something good. Not clever. Not funny. Something true. I had a list in my head, and I had been rehearsing it since Wednesday, and when I opened my mouth, what came out was:

"I'm sorry about the umbrella."

She laughed.

It was a real one. It arrived all at once, loud and astonished, the sort of sound that makes people at neighbouring tables look round to see what has happened to the ceiling. She put a hand over her mouth. It did no good. Her shoulders shook. The elderly couple stopped arguing about the fern.

"Oh," said Sophie, wiping her eye. "God. You're so *stupid.* You've always been so..."

"Yes."

"It's the *only* thing that's ever worked."

"I know."

She stopped. The laughter went out of her face like a tide, and what it left behind was something smaller and truer, which she looked at, I think, for a moment, with something close to dismay.

"This is exactly what I meant," she said, "by poor judgment."

---

I will tell you what I noticed next, and I will tell it plainly, because I did not realise its significance at the time.

I noticed that the man at the next table over, the one sitting alone by the pond with a cup of tea in front of him, had not touched the tea.

He was perhaps thirty-eight. He wore a good grey jumper, and a scarf wound around his neck in a way that suggested someone who was cold, or someone who had something to hide. He was facing us, though not looking at us, with the studied attentiveness of a man pretending to read a menu he has memorised. He had been there when I arrived. I would swear to it. And I had the strange, fugitive impression that I had seen his hands before.

"What?" said Sophie.

"Nothing."

"You've gone a funny colour."

"There's a man at the next table."

She followed my gaze. She was a professional. She did not turn her head; she simply allowed her eyes to drift, in the way you might look for a cat, and rested them on the scarf. Then she did something I remember very clearly. She went completely still, and slowly, with great control, she picked up her phone, turned it face down, and said in a quite ordinary voice:

"Is that a Rental Presence?"

"I don't know."

"Look at his collar."

I looked. The scarf had slipped. Just above the neckline of the grey jumper, where a pulse might have been, there was a small circle of soft blue light. It was a legal requirement. Every licensed Rental Presence had to display one. The regulations specified, with unaccustomed delicacy, that the indicator must be "visible, steady, and not decorative."

The man looked up from his menu.

He had my face.

Not a likeness. Not a cousin. My face, in the flattering light of the conservatory, with the lines around the eyes slightly smoothed and the posture fixed by someone who knew what to do with their hands. He raised one in a small, apologetic wave.

"I did say Thursday," said Daniel-A. "I'm so sorry. I couldn't not come."

Nobody said anything. At the next table, the elderly woman put down her teacup.

He turned to Sophie, and for the first time in my life I heard my own voice crack, in a way I would never have permitted it to, and he said:

"Hello, Sophie."

And we both said, "Hello."

I want to be clear that she looked at him first.
