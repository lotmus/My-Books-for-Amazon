# How to use this lesson

Lesson 27 proved that each continuous symmetry of the action gives a conserved current, but it treated symmetries one at a time. This lesson steps back and organizes them. Symmetry transformations form groups; near the identity a group element is fixed by a set of generators; the generators obey a closed algebra; and the conserved charges of Noether's theorem realize that algebra. The algebra is already familiar in one case, the angular momentum commutators of Lesson 13, and this lesson shows that it is one instance of a general structure.

Two families of symmetries matter for QED. The spacetime symmetries, translations and Lorentz transformations, form the Poincare group and are respected by every relativistic theory. The internal symmetries act on the labels of the fields and leave spacetime alone; the phase symmetry of the Dirac field is the one that matters here. The lesson closes with a practical rule, the charge rule, that decides which terms may appear in a Lagrangian density once the charges of the fields are known. Expect seven to nine focused hours.

## Learning objectives

- Explain why symmetry transformations form a group and what makes the group continuous.

- Write an infinitesimal transformation using generators and obtain finite transformations by exponentiation.

- Derive the closure of the generators under commutation from the group structure and state the structure constants for U(1) and for rotations.

- Show that Noether charges obey the same algebra as the generators of the symmetry.

- Derive the antisymmetry of the infinitesimal Lorentz transformation, count the ten parameters of the Poincare group, and identify the ten associated conserved quantities.

- State the structure of the Lorentz and Poincare algebras and explain why two boosts produce a rotation.

- Distinguish internal from spacetime symmetries and global from local ones.

- Use the charge rule to decide whether a proposed term is allowed in a Lagrangian density.

# 1 Groups of symmetries

## 1.1 The group structure

If two transformations each leave the action invariant, so does their composition, and so does the inverse of either, and doing nothing is trivially a symmetry. The symmetry transformations of a theory therefore form a group G: a set closed under composition, containing an identity, and containing an inverse for each element. A group is continuous, called a Lie group, if its elements are labeled by a finite number of parameters that can vary smoothly; the number of independent parameters is its dimension. The phase rotations φ → e^(iα)φ form a group with one parameter α, called U(1). The rotations of three-dimensional space depend on three angles and form a three-parameter group. The Lorentz transformations depend on six parameters, as Section 4 shows.

## 1.2 Generators

Near the identity, a group element can be written g = 1 + iε^aT_a + O(ε²), where ε^a are small parameters and T_a are fixed operators, the generators, one for each parameter. A finite transformation is built by applying many small ones in succession, which gives the exponential.

$$ g(α) = lim_(N→∞) (1 + iαT/N)^N = e^(iαT)

For U(1) there is one generator, which acts on a field of charge q as multiplication by q, so that φ → e^(iqα)φ. For rotations the generators are the angular momenta J_i of Lesson 13, and the finite rotation about z by angle θ is e^(−iθJ_z), the form met in Lesson 14, Exercise 13, for spin one-half.

### Worked example 1

Build the finite U(1) transformation from infinitesimal ones. A phase rotation by a small angle ε multiplies φ by 1 + iε. Applying N such rotations, each by α/N, gives (1 + iα/N)^N φ, which tends to e^(iα)φ as N → ∞. The composition rule U(α₁)U(α₂) = U(α₁ + α₂) shows that the group is abelian: the order of two phase rotations does not matter.

# 2 The algebra of generators

## 2.1 Closure under commutation

For a group whose elements do not commute, the generators do not commute either, and the commutator of two generators is again a generator. This follows from the group structure. For small operators A and B, expanding each exponential to second order (Exercise 2) gives

$$ e^A e^B e^(−A) e^(−B) = 1 + [A, B] + higher orders

Take A = iεT_a and B = iδT_b. The left side is a product of group elements and is therefore itself a group element close to the identity, of the form 1 + iθ^cT_c. The right side is 1 − εδ[T_a, T_b], so the commutator must be a linear combination of generators.

$$ [T_a, T_b] = i f_(ab)^c T_c

The numbers f_(ab)^c are the structure constants of the group. They determine the group near the identity completely, and the algebra they define is called the Lie algebra of the group. For U(1) there is only one generator, which commutes with itself, so f = 0 and the group is abelian. For rotations, the structure constants are the Levi Civita symbol.

$$ [J_i, J_j] = i ε_(ijk) J_k

the commutation relation of Lesson 13, Section 1.2, and, for spin one-half with T_i = σ_i/2, the relation [σ_i/2, σ_j/2] = iε_(ijk)σ_k/2 following from the Pauli algebra of Lesson 1 (Exercise 6).

## 2.2 Noether charges realize the algebra

The Noether charge Q_a of Lesson 27 generates the transformation of the field through the Poisson bracket, δ_aφ = ε{φ, Q_a} (Lesson 27, Section 5.2). Two successive transformations, one generated by Q_a and one by Q_b, differ by the order of application by the commutator of the transformations, and the Jacobi identity of Lesson 6, Section 4.3, converts this into a bracket of the charges (Exercise 8).

$$ {{φ, Q_b}, Q_a} − {{φ, Q_a}, Q_b} = {φ, {Q_b, Q_a}}

Since the transformations close under composition, the brackets of the charges must close too, and for symmetry groups without extra complications, {Q_a, Q_b} = f_(ab)^c Q_c. After quantization the bracket becomes a commutator, and the charges satisfy [Q_a, Q_b] = i f_(ab)^c Q_c, the same algebra as the generators. The angular momentum brackets {L_i, L_j} = ε_(ijk)L_k of Lesson 6, Section 4.4, are one instance of this: the Noether charges of rotations, the angular momenta, satisfy the algebra of the rotation group.

# 3 Internal symmetries

## 3.1 What they are

An internal symmetry acts on the labels of the fields without moving the spacetime point. The U(1) phase symmetry of the complex scalar and the Dirac field (Lesson 27, Sections 4.2 and 4.3) is the basic example, and it is the only internal symmetry that QED requires. Internal symmetries commute with the spacetime symmetries: rotating a field in space and rotating its phase can be done in either order.

## 3.2 Global and local

A symmetry is global if the parameter is the same at every point and local if it may vary from point to point. Every global symmetry of Section 1 has been global. Lesson 27, Section 3, showed that allowing the parameter to depend on position changes the action by ∫(∂_με)j^μ, so that a global symmetry does not automatically survive being made local. Lesson 38 will require it to be local and will find that this can be achieved only by adding a new field.

## 3.3 A non-abelian example

A theory can have an internal symmetry whose generators do not commute. Take two complex scalar fields collected into a doublet φ = (φ₁, φ₂)ᵀ with ℒ = ∂_μφ†∂^μφ − m²φ†φ. It is invariant under φ → Uφ for any 2 × 2 unitary matrix U, since φ†φ and ∂_μφ†∂^μφ are unchanged. Writing U = 1 + iε^aT_a with T_a = σ_a/2, the three transformations have Δφ = iT_aφ and Noether currents (Exercise 13)

$$ j_a^μ = i(∂^μφ† T_a φ − φ† T_a ∂^μφ),    [Q_a, Q_b] = i ε_(abc) Q_c

The three charges do not commute and satisfy the same algebra as angular momentum, though nothing here has to do with rotations in space. The example shows that the algebra of Section 2 is a property of the symmetry and not of what is being rotated, and it shows the difference from U(1), where the single generator commutes with itself.

# 4 Spacetime symmetries

## 4.1 The Poincare group

The symmetries of spacetime that leave the interval invariant are the translations x^μ → x^μ + a^μ, with four parameters, and the Lorentz transformations x^μ → Λ^μ_νx^ν, which satisfy ΛᵀgΛ = g (Lesson 4, Section 2.2). Together they form the Poincare group, and every relativistic theory, including QED, is invariant under it. Invariance under translations and Lorentz transformations is what makes the laws of physics the same everywhere and for every inertial observer.

## 4.2 Infinitesimal Lorentz transformations

Write Λ^μ_ν = δ^μ_ν + ω^μ_ν with ω small. Inserting this into ΛᵀgΛ = g and keeping first-order terms gives ωᵀg + gω = 0, which in components reads

$$ ω_(μν) + ω_(νμ) = 0,    ω_(μν) = g_(μρ) ω^ρ_ν

so ω_(μν) is antisymmetric. An antisymmetric 4 × 4 array has six independent components (Lesson 4, Worked example 4). The three components ω_(ij) generate rotations and the three components ω_(0i) generate boosts. The total number of parameters of the Poincare group is 4 + 6 = 10.

### Worked example 2

Check the antisymmetry for a rotation and a boost. An active rotation by a small angle θ about z is x′ = x − θy, y′ = y + θx, so ω¹₂ = −θ and ω²₁ = θ, giving ω₁₂ = g₁₁ω¹₂ = θ and ω₂₁ = g₂₂ω²₁ = −θ, antisymmetric. A boost along x with small velocity β, from t′ = t − βx and x′ = x − βt (Lesson 3, Section 3), has ω⁰₁ = ω¹₀ = −β, so ω₀₁ = g₀₀ω⁰₁ = −β and ω₁₀ = g₁₁ω¹₀ = +β, again antisymmetric. Note that the boost matrix is symmetric in its mixed indices but antisymmetric once the first index is lowered.

## 4.3 The ten conserved quantities

Noether's theorem assigns a conserved charge to each of the ten parameters.

@widths 2160 2880 4752
| Symmetry | Number | Conserved quantity |
| Time translation | 1 | Energy H |
| Space translations | 3 | Momentum P |
| Rotations | 3 | Angular momentum J |
| Boosts | 3 | Boost charge K, related to the motion of the center of energy |

The first three groups are the familiar conservation laws; the last is less familiar, and expresses that the center of energy of an isolated system moves with constant velocity. Lesson 29 constructs these charges for the scalar, Dirac, and electromagnetic fields.

## 4.4 The algebra of the Poincare group

The generators of rotations J_i, boosts K_i, and translations (H, P_i) obey a closed set of commutation relations, which in one standard convention are

$$ [J_i, J_j] = iε_(ijk)J_k,    [J_i, K_j] = iε_(ijk)K_k,    [K_i, K_j] = −iε_(ijk)J_k

$$ [J_i, P_j] = iε_(ijk)P_k,    [K_i, P_j] = iδ_(ij)H,    [K_i, H] = iP_i,    [P_i, P_j] = [P_i, H] = [J_i, H] = 0

The signs depend on the sign conventions chosen for the boost parameter, but the structure does not. Rotations close among themselves. Momentum is a vector under rotations. The translations commute, so the components of energy and momentum can all be known at once, which is why a particle can be assigned a definite four-momentum. And the commutator of two boosts is a rotation, not a boost: the boosts do not form a group by themselves.

### Worked example 3

Verify that two boosts in perpendicular directions produce a rotation. Let B_x have the only nonzero entries (B_x)⁰₁ = (B_x)¹₀ = 1 and B_y the only nonzero entries (B_y)⁰₂ = (B_y)²₀ = 1, so that small boosts are 1 − βB_x and 1 − β′B_y. The product B_xB_y has a single nonzero entry (B_xB_y)¹₂ = (B_x)¹₀(B_y)⁰₂ = 1, and B_yB_x has a single nonzero entry (B_yB_x)²₁ = 1. So [B_x, B_y] has entries +1 at (1, 2) and −1 at (2, 1), which is proportional to the rotation generator about z found in Worked example 2. The group commutator of the two boosts, 1 + ββ′[B_x, B_y], is therefore a rotation about z by an angle of magnitude ββ′. This is the Thomas rotation, and it is the content of the relation [K_i, K_j] = −iε_(ijk)J_k of Section 4.4.

## 4.5 Fields as representations

A field is a set of components that transform among themselves under the symmetry, which is to say a representation of the group. Under the Lorentz group a scalar transforms trivially, φ′(x′) = φ(x); the four-potential transforms with the matrix Λ; and a spinor transforms with a 4 × 4 matrix S(Λ) built from the spin tensor σ^(μν) = (i/2)[γ^μ, γ^ν] of Lesson 21, Section 3. For a rotation by angle θ about z, S = e^(−iθΣ_z/2), the spin one-half rotation of Lesson 14, Exercise 13, and a rotation through 2π gives S = −1 (Exercise 11), the sign change of half-integer angular momentum noted in Lesson 13, Exercise 15. That a spinor changes sign under a 2π rotation is not a defect: it is a consequence of representing the rotation group on spin one-half objects, and it shows up experimentally in neutron interferometry.

# 5 The charge rule

## 5.1 Charges of the fields

Under the U(1) symmetry a field of charge q transforms as φ → e^(iqα)φ, and its conjugate φ* carries charge −q. A product of fields transforms with the product of the phases, so its total charge is the sum of the charges of its factors. A term in the Lagrangian density is invariant under the U(1) if and only if its total charge is zero. This charge rule is the practical form of the symmetry: it tells, without any calculation, which terms may appear.

## 5.2 Applying the rule

@widths 2160 3024 4608
| Term | Total charge | Allowed |
| φ*φ for a complex scalar of charge q | −q + q = 0 | Yes |
| φ² or φ³ for charge q ≠ 0 | 2q or 3q | No |
| (φ*φ)² | 0 | Yes |
| ψ̄ψ for a Dirac field | 0 | Yes |
| ψ̄γ^μψ A_μ with A neutral | 0 | Yes |
| φψ̄ψ for φ of charge q ≠ 0 | q | No |

Two conclusions follow. The photon field A_μ must be neutral, since it appears alone in F_μν and the interaction ψ̄γ^μψA_μ must have total charge zero for any charge of ψ. And the U(1) that survives in QED is unbroken by any of the terms in the Lagrangian density, since each has total charge zero, so its Noether charge is conserved.

### Worked example 4

Two scalar fields φ₁ and φ₂ carry charges 1 and 2. The cubic term φ₁²φ₂* has charge 2 − 2 = 0 and is allowed. Under a phase rotation of φ₁ alone, φ₁²φ₂* changes by e^(2iα), so rotating the fields separately is not a symmetry of a theory containing this term. Only the combined transformation, φ₁ → e^(iα)φ₁ and φ₂ → e^(2iα)φ₂, is. By Noether's theorem the conserved charge is the weighted sum Q = Q₁ + 2Q₂ of the two free charges, not each separately (Exercise 10). The example shows that adding an interaction can reduce a symmetry, and that the charges of the fields fix what survives.

## 5.3 An extra symmetry that a mass breaks

The Dirac Lagrangian density has another continuous symmetry when the mass is zero. Under ψ → e^(iβγ⁵)ψ, ψ̄ → ψ̄e^(iβγ⁵) the kinetic term ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ is invariant, because γ⁵ anticommutes with each γ^μ (Lesson 20, Section 4.2), but the mass term becomes ψ̄e^(2iβγ⁵)ψ, which is not equal to ψ̄ψ. This chiral symmetry is therefore a symmetry only for m = 0, and its current, the axial current ψ̄γ^μγ⁵ψ, has divergence proportional to the mass (Exercise 16). A mass term breaks it explicitly, in the way a mass broke the shift symmetry of the scalar in Lesson 27, Section 4.1. The vector U(1) of Lesson 27 is unaffected by the mass and stays exact.

# 6 Symmetry and the classification of particles

The Poincare algebra of Section 4.4 has two invariants, one built from the four-momentum, P^μP_μ, whose value is the mass squared, and one built from the angular momentum, whose value is the spin. It can be shown (a result of Wigner, not proved in this course) that the irreducible representations of the Poincare group are labeled by these two numbers. This is why every elementary particle is characterized by a mass and a spin, and it is the sense in which the particles of QED are determined by symmetry: an electron is the smallest representation with mass m and spin one half, and the photon the representation with zero mass and helicity ±1 (Lesson 37).

The theme of the lesson is that symmetry organizes everything: which quantities are conserved, which terms may appear in the Lagrangian density, and which particles exist. # 8 Summary

1. Symmetry transformations form a group; a continuous group has generators T_a, with g = e^(iε^aT_a) for a finite transformation.

2. Closure of the group implies [T_a, T_b] = if_(ab)^cT_c; U(1) is abelian and rotations have f = ε_(ijk).

3. Noether charges generate the symmetry through Poisson brackets and satisfy the same algebra as the generators, becoming commutators after quantization.

4. Infinitesimal Lorentz transformations have ω_(μν) antisymmetric, with six parameters; with four translations the Poincare group has ten.

5. The ten parameters give conservation of energy, momentum, angular momentum, and the boost charge.

6. In the Poincare algebra rotations close, translations commute, and two boosts produce a rotation.

7. Internal symmetries act on field labels, commute with the Poincare group, and may be global or local; U(1) is abelian and the doublet symmetry is non-abelian.

8. The charge rule says a term is allowed only if its total charge is zero; a mass term breaks the chiral symmetry of the Dirac field but not its vector U(1).

# Solutions

**1.** (1 + iαQ/N)^N = Σ_k C(N, k)(iαQ/N)^k = Σ_k [N(N−1)⋯(N−k+1)/N^k](iαQ)^k/k!, and as N → ∞ each coefficient N(N−1)⋯(N−k+1)/N^k → 1, giving Σ_k(iαQ)^k/k! = e^(iαQ). For a field of charge q, Q acts as q and U(α) = e^(iqα). Then U(α₁)U(α₂) = e^(iq(α₁+α₂)) = U(α₁ + α₂), since exponents of commuting numbers add.

**2.** Write each factor as 1 + x + x²/2 with x₁ = A, x₂ = B, x₃ = −A, x₄ = −B. At second order the product contains Σx_i²/2 + Σ_(i<j)x_ix_j. The first sum is (A² + B² + A² + B²)/2 = A² + B². The second sum is AB − A² − AB − BA − B² + AB = AB − BA − A² − B². Adding, the second-order part is AB − BA = [A, B]. The first-order terms A + B − A − B cancel.

**3.** With Λ = 1 + ω, ΛᵀgΛ = g + ωᵀg + gω + O(ω²). The first-order condition ωᵀg + gω = 0 gives, in components with ω_(μν) = g_(μρ)ω^ρ_ν, ω_(νμ) + ω_(μν) = 0. An antisymmetric 4 × 4 array has 4·3/2 = 6 independent components. The three ω_(ij) with i, j spatial are rotations and the three ω_(0i) are boosts.

**4.** For the rotation, x′ = x + θz and z′ = z − θx give ω¹₃ = θ and ω³₁ = −θ (indices 1 = x, 3 = z), so ω₁₃ = g₁₁ω¹₃ = −θ and ω₃₁ = g₃₃ω³₁ = θ, antisymmetric. For the boost along z, t′ = t − βz and z′ = z − βt give ω⁰₃ = ω³₀ = −β, so ω₀₃ = g₀₀ω⁰₃ = −β and ω₃₀ = g₃₃ω³₀ = +β, antisymmetric.

**5.** Energy (time translation), three components of momentum (space translations), three components of angular momentum (rotations), and three components of the boost charge (boosts): 1 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 10.

**6.** [σ_i/2, σ_j/2] = (1/4)(σ_iσ_j − σ_jσ_i) = (1/4)(2iε_(ijk)σ_k) = iε_(ijk)σ_k/2, using σ_iσ_j − σ_jσ_i = 2iε_(ijk)σ_k. Comparing with [T_i, T_j] = if_(ij)^kT_k with T_i = σ_i/2, the structure constants are f_(ij)^k = ε_(ijk).

**7.** (B_xB_y)^μ_ν = Σ_ρ(B_x)^μ_ρ(B_y)^ρ_ν. The only nonzero term is μ = 1, ρ = 0, ν = 2, giving (B_xB_y)¹₂ = 1. Similarly (B_yB_x)²₁ = (B_y)²₀(B_x)⁰₁ = 1 is the only nonzero entry. So [B_x, B_y] has entries +1 at (1, 2) and −1 at (2, 1). A small rotation about z by θ is 1 + ω with ω¹₂ = −θ and ω²₁ = θ (Worked example 2), that is 1 − θ[E₁₂ − E₂₁]. The group commutator of 1 − βB_x and 1 − β′B_y is 1 + ββ′[B_x, B_y] = 1 + ββ′(E₁₂ − E₂₁), which equals a rotation with θ = −ββ′. Its angle has magnitude ββ′, and the sense of rotation depends on the order of the boosts.

**8.** Apply the Jacobi identity with f = φ, g = Q_b, h = Q_a: {φ, {Q_b, Q_a}} + {Q_b, {Q_a, φ}} + {Q_a, {φ, Q_b}} = 0. Using antisymmetry, {Q_b, {Q_a, φ}} = −{{Q_a, φ}, Q_b} = {{φ, Q_a}, Q_b}, and {Q_a, {φ, Q_b}} = −{{φ, Q_b}, Q_a}. Substituting, {φ, {Q_b, Q_a}} + {{φ, Q_a}, Q_b} − {{φ, Q_b}, Q_a} = 0, so {{φ, Q_b}, Q_a} − {{φ, Q_a}, Q_b} = {φ, {Q_b, Q_a}}.

**9.** Charges: φ carries +1, φ* carries −1, ψ carries −1, ψ̄ carries +1. Then φ*φ has charge 0, allowed; φ² has +2, not allowed; (φ*φ)² has 0, allowed; φ³ has +3, not allowed; ψ̄ψ has 0, allowed; φψ̄ψ has +1, not allowed; φ*φψ̄ψ has 0, allowed; ψ̄γ^μψA_μ has 0 with A neutral, allowed.

**10.** Under the joint rotation, φ₁²φ₂* → e^(2iα)φ₁²e^(−2iα)φ₂* = φ₁²φ₂*, invariant. Under φ₁ → e^(iα)φ₁ alone, φ₁²φ₂* → e^(2iα)φ₁²φ₂*, which changes. For the joint symmetry Δφ₁ = iφ₁ and Δφ₂ = 2iφ₂ with K^μ = 0, and the kinetic and mass terms contribute the free-field expression to each piece, so j^μ = j₁^μ + 2j₂^μ with j_a^μ = −i(φ_a*∂^μφ_a − φ_a∂^μφ_a*). The conserved charge is Q₁ + 2Q₂.

**11.** S(θ) = diag(e^(−iθσ_z/2), e^(−iθσ_z/2)) and e^(−iθσ_z/2) = cos(θ/2) − iσ_z sin(θ/2). At θ = 2π this is cos π − iσ_z sin π = −1, so S = −I. At θ = 4π it is cos 2π − iσ_z sin 2π = 1, so S = I.

**12.** With γ⁵ Hermitian, ψ† → ψ†e^(−iβγ⁵), and ψ̄ = ψ†γ⁰ → ψ†e^(−iβγ⁵)γ⁰ = ψ†γ⁰e^(iβγ⁵) = ψ̄e^(iβγ⁵), since γ⁵γ⁰ = −γ⁰γ⁵ gives e^(−iβγ⁵)γ⁰ = γ⁰e^(iβγ⁵). Also e^(iβγ⁵)γ^μ = γ^μe^(−iβγ⁵). So ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ → ψ̄e^(iβγ⁵)iγ^μe^(iβγ⁵)∂_μψ = ψ̄iγ^μe^(−iβγ⁵)e^(iβγ⁵)∂_μψ = ψ̄iγ^μ∂_μψ. The mass term becomes mψ̄e^(2iβγ⁵)ψ, which differs from mψ̄ψ unless β = 0 or m = 0.

**13.** Under φ → Uφ with U†U = 1, φ†φ → φ†U†Uφ = φ†φ and ∂_μφ†∂^μφ → ∂_μφ†U†U∂^μφ = ∂_μφ†∂^μφ, so ℒ is invariant. For U = 1 + iε^aT_a with T_a Hermitian, Δφ = iT_aφ and Δφ† = −iφ†T_a. With ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ) = ∂^μφ† and ∂ℒ/∂(∂_μφ†) = ∂^μφ, j_a^μ = ∂^μφ†(iT_aφ) + (−iφ†T_a)∂^μφ = i(∂^μφ†T_aφ − φ†T_a∂^μφ). Then ∂_μj_a^μ = i(□φ†T_aφ + ∂^μφ†T_a∂_μφ − ∂_μφ†T_a∂^μφ − φ†T_a□φ) = i(□φ†T_aφ − φ†T_a□φ) = i(−m²φ†T_aφ + m²φ†T_aφ) = 0 on solutions of (□ + m²)φ = 0.

**14.** O(N) has N(N − 1)/2 generators, U(N) has N², and SU(N) has N² − 1. For N = 2: O(2) has 1, U(2) has 4, SU(2) has 3. For N = 3: O(3) has 3, U(3) has 9, SU(3) has 8. The count for O(N) is the number of independent antisymmetric N × N matrices, matching the N(N − 1)/2 currents of Lesson 27, Exercise 14.

**15.** If ℒ has no explicit dependence on x, translating all fields by a is a symmetry. A term in the action coupling fields with momenta p₁, p₂, and p₃ contributes ∫d⁴x e^(−i(p₁+p₂−p₃)·x) when the fields are expanded in plane waves, because the interaction is a product of fields at one point. The integral equals (2π)⁴δ⁴(p₁ + p₂ − p₃), which vanishes unless the total four-momentum is conserved. Translation invariance thus appears as a delta function at each vertex.

**16.** Using γ^μ∂_μψ = −imψ from the Dirac equation and (∂_μψ̄)γ^μ = imψ̄ from its adjoint, ∂_μ(ψ̄γ^μγ⁵ψ) = (∂_μψ̄)γ^μγ⁵ψ + ψ̄γ^μγ⁵∂_μψ = imψ̄γ⁵ψ − ψ̄γ⁵γ^μ∂_μψ, using γ^μγ⁵ = −γ⁵γ^μ in the second term. Then −ψ̄γ⁵γ^μ∂_μψ = −ψ̄γ⁵(−imψ) = imψ̄γ⁵ψ, so ∂_μj₅^μ = 2imψ̄γ⁵ψ. For m = 0 this vanishes and the axial current is conserved, consistent with the chiral symmetry of Section 5.3.
