# How to use this prologue

Feynman's fourth lecture admits what the three actions still leave unfinished. An electron can emit a photon and reabsorb it: a loop. A photon can become an electron–positron pair and back: another loop. Those diagrams shift the electron's magnetic moment from the Dirac value 2 to 2.00232…, and they produce infinities that this course will absorb into m and e (Lessons 67–68). This prologue names the puzzles and where they are paid. Expect three to five hours.

## Learning objectives

- Draw the self-energy and vacuum-polarization loops in the three-action language.

- State that Dirac's g=2 (Lesson 75, from Lesson 39) is corrected by the vertex loop to a=α/(2π) at one loop.

- State that infinities in loops are not ignored: they are absorbed into unmeasurable bare parameters.

- Distinguish this easy picture (which predicts the existence of those effects) from the calculations that produce the numbers.

# 1 Extra diagrams the three actions already allow

Once emission and absorption are allowed, they are allowed in combination. An electron travelling from A to B can emit a photon at one point and absorb it at a later point. A photon travelling from A to B can spend part of the trip as a pair. Those are not new verbs. They are the same three actions, used twice more.

# 2 The magnetic moment

A spinning electron is a magnet. The Dirac equation alone, with the vertex of Prologue 3 but no loops, gives g=2 (Lesson 75, Section 1). The vertex correction — a photon emitted before the external magnetic photon is absorbed, and reabsorbed after — adds F2(0)=α/(2π). That is Schwinger's number, the first quantitative prize of the easy picture once the arrows are turned into integrals. This prologue does not evaluate the integral; it says why a number slightly larger than 2 has to appear.

### Worked example 1

Is 2.00232 a failure of g=2? No. g=2 is the three-action tree. 2.00232 is the same theory with one extra photon on the vertex. Experiment (Lesson 78) sits at 2.002319… The residual is higher loops, not a different coupling.

# 3 Infinities

The loop diagrams, computed with no cutoff, diverge. The easy lectures leave that as a scandal. This course's answer is Lessons 66–68: a cutoff, then a redefinition of m and e so that what is measured is finite. The leftover dependence on how hard a photon is off shell is the running of α. Mead's later view (the Interlude after Lesson 40) will insist that the primitive object is still A, not a new infinity in E and B. Both statements can be true: the infinity is in a loop integral over A, and the measured coupling of A is finite.

**1.** Electron line with a photon emitted and reabsorbed.

**2.** Photon line interrupted by an electron loop.

**3.** g=2, Lesson 75 from the Dirac Hamiltonian of Lesson 39.

**4.** a=α/(2π).

**5.** Bare mass m₀ and bare charge e₀.

**6.** No; it means a naive integral needs a cutoff and a redefinition of parameters already in the three actions.
