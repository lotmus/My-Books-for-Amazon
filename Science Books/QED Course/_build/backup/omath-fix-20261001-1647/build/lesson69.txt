TITLE: Renormalization Group
TAGLINE: The logarithm of Lesson 68 is the integral of a differential equation, μ de/dμ = β(e), and that equation is what survives when the logarithm is no longer small
NUM: 69
NEXT: Lesson 70, Gauge Fixing, returns to the photon field itself and writes the covariant gauge-fixing term that Lesson 37 postponed, so that the Ward identity of Lesson 71 can be stated in a covariant language.

# How to use this lesson

Lesson 68 ended with a formula, α(Q²) = α/[1 − (α/3π) ln(Q²/m²)], and a claim it did not prove: that the logarithm belongs in the denominator rather than as a correction α[1 + (α/3π) ln(Q²/m²)]. This lesson proves the claim. It does so by showing that the running coupling obeys a first-order differential equation in the scale, μ dα/dμ = β(α). Once that equation is in hand, the denominator form is simply its solution, and it sums an infinite series of logarithms that fixed-order perturbation theory would truncate. The same equation explains how each charged particle switches on as the scale passes its mass, what the sign of β says about screening, and why the Landau pole of Lesson 68 is a statement about the equation rather than about nature. In QED the effect is small because α is small. In the strong interaction it is the whole story, which this lesson mentions but does not derive. Expect six to eight hours.

## Learning objectives

- Differentiate Lesson 68's running formula and obtain μ dα/dμ = 2α²/(3π), hence β(e) = e³/(12π²) for one charged Dirac fermion.

- Define the coupling at an arbitrary subtraction scale μ, and state why a physical observable cannot depend on μ.

- Write the renormalization-group equation for a dimensionless observable and check it on a one-loop amplitude.

- Show that solving the equation sums the leading logarithms α(α L)ⁿ, and explain when that matters.

- Integrate β with the coefficient b = (4/3)Σ N_c Q_f², include thresholds as each fermion is crossed, and reproduce the leptonic running of Lesson 68 to leading logarithm.

- Classify the possible signs of β, locate the one-loop Landau pole, and quote, as a result not derived here, the two-loop term and the running mass.

# 1 From a logarithm to a derivative

## 1.1 Differentiating Lesson 68

Write L = ln(μ²/m²), so that Lesson 68's leading-logarithm formula, evaluated at Q = μ, reads α(μ) = α/[1 − (α/3π)L], with α on the right the long-distance value. Since dL/d ln μ = 2,

$$ μ dα/dμ = (2α²/3π) / [1 − (α/3π)L]² = 2α[μ]² / (3π)

The second equality is exact for this formula, because α/[1 − (α/3π)L] is α(μ) itself, so the squared denominator produces α(μ)². The right-hand side no longer mentions the low-energy α or the electron mass. It depends only on the coupling at the scale where the derivative is taken.

## 1.2 Translating to e

With α = e²/4π, dα = (e/2π) de, so μ de/dμ = (2π/e) μ dα/dμ. Substituting,

$$ β[e] ≡ μ de/dμ = e³ / (12π²)

for one charged Dirac fermion at one loop, at scales well above its mass.

### Worked example 1

Check the arithmetic from μ dα/dμ = 2α²/(3π) to β = e³/(12π²). With α = e²/4π, μ dα/dμ = (e/2π) μ de/dμ. The right side is 2(e⁴/16π²)/(3π) = e⁴/(24π³). So μ de/dμ = (2π/e)·e⁴/(24π³) = e³/(12π²). The numerical coefficient is 1/(12π²) ≈ 0.0084, and with e ≈ 0.303 the slope μ de/dμ is about 2.3×10⁻⁴: the charge changes by a fraction of 10⁻³ per e-fold in scale.

# 2 Why a scale appears at all

## 2.1 Defining the coupling at μ

Lesson 68 defined e by Coulomb's law at Q² = 0, so that e_eff²(Q²) = e²/(1 − Π̂(Q²)) with Π̂(0) = 0. Nothing forces that choice. One may equally define the coupling at any spacelike momentum Q² = μ², by declaring α(μ) ≡ α_eff(μ²). The two definitions are related by the finite function Π̂, and in terms of the new coupling,

$$ α_eff[Q²] = α[μ] / (1 − (α[μ]/3π) ln[Q²/μ²])

for Q, μ ≫ m. The logarithm is now of the ratio of the physical scale Q to the chosen scale μ, and it is small whenever μ is chosen close to Q. The low-energy α is the special case μ → 0, where this large-Q formula must be replaced by Lesson 68's full Π̂.

## 2.2 Observables do not depend on μ

The scale μ is a bookkeeping choice, the point at which the finite remainder is subtracted. It is not a particle mass, and nothing measured can depend on it. A dimensionless observable R, say a cross section multiplied by Q², therefore depends on μ in two ways that must cancel: explicitly, through logarithms ln(Q²/μ²), and implicitly, through α(μ). The requirement dR/d ln μ = 0, written with the chain rule,

$$ [μ ∂/∂μ + β[α] ∂/∂α] R[Q²/μ², α] = 0

is a renormalization-group equation. The name is historical: changes of μ compose like the elements of a group. The content is simple. The explicit μ-dependence of a calculation is fixed by β.

### Worked example 2

Check the equation on the one-photon exchange between two heavy charges at large Q. The amplitude is proportional to α_eff(Q²), so at one loop R = α(μ)[1 + (α(μ)/3π) ln(Q²/μ²)]. Then μ ∂R/∂μ = α(μ)(α(μ)/3π)(−2) = −2α²/(3π), and β ∂R/∂α = (2α²/3π)[1 + O(α)]. The sum vanishes at order α², the order to which R was computed. A truncated calculation is μ-independent only up to the first omitted order, and that residual dependence measures the size of the omitted terms.

# 3 What the equation buys: leading logarithms

## 3.1 Fixed order against resummed

Expand Section 2.1's formula in powers of α(μ):

$$ α_eff = α[μ] [1 + (αL/3π) + [αL/3π]² + [αL/3π]³ + ⋯]

with L = ln(Q²/μ²). A one-loop calculation produces only the first correction, αL/3π. The higher terms, one for every power of α multiplied by the same power of L, are the leading logarithms. They come from diagrams with n vacuum-polarization bubbles chained along the photon line, exactly Lesson 68's geometric series, now justified by the differential equation rather than by assumption. Solving μ dα/dμ = 2α²/(3π) produces the whole series at once.

## 3.2 When resummation matters

Each term is smaller than the last by αL/3π. If α is small but L is large, so that αL/3π approaches 1, truncation fails while the resummed formula is still under control. The practical rule is to choose μ near the physical scale of the process, which makes L small and puts the large logarithms into α(μ), where the equation has already summed them.

### Worked example 3

Compare the fixed-order and resummed predictions for the electron loop from μ = m to Q = M_Z, using Lesson 68's numbers: αL/3π = 7.74×10⁻⁴ × 24.18 = 0.0187. Fixed order gives α(M_Z)/α = 1.0187. The resummed formula gives 1/(1 − 0.0187) = 1.0191. The difference is (0.0187)² ≈ 3.5×10⁻⁴. That is small, but it is several times the relative uncertainty, about 10⁻⁴, with which α(M_Z) is quoted, so precision work does need the resummed form. In the strong interaction, where the coupling is already 0.2 to 0.3 at a few GeV, resummation is not a refinement but a necessity.

# 4 Integrating the equation

## 4.1 Several charged species

Every fermion lighter than μ contributes its own vacuum-polarization loop, multiplied by N_c Q_f². The one-loop equation becomes

$$ μ dα/dμ = b α² / (2π) qquad b = (4/3) Σ_f N_c Q_f²

For one lepton, b = 4/3 and bα²/(2π) = 2α²/(3π), as in Section 1.1.

## 4.2 The solution

Separating variables, dα/α² = (b/2π) d ln μ, and integrating from μ₀ to μ,

$$ 1/α[μ] = 1/α[μ₀] − (b/2π) ln[μ/μ₀]

The inverse coupling runs linearly in the logarithm of the scale. This is Lesson 68's formula rewritten (Exercise 6), and it is the most useful form in practice: a plot of 1/α against ln μ is a straight line between thresholds, with slope −b/2π.

## 4.3 Thresholds

The sum in b runs only over fermions lighter than μ. A heavy particle's loop contributes only Lesson 68's (α/15π)Q²/m_f², which vanishes as its mass grows. This is decoupling. Below each mass the particle is absent from b, and above it the particle is present. In the simplest treatment b jumps at μ = m_f. Matching at μ = m_f e^(5/6) instead reproduces Lesson 68's constant −5/3 exactly at one loop (Exercise 10).

### Worked example 4

Run 1/α from the electron mass to M_Z with the three charged leptons. Between m_e and m_μ, b = 4/3. Between m_μ and m_τ, b = 8/3. Between m_τ and M_Z, b = 4. The logarithms are ln(m_μ/m_e) = 5.33, ln(m_τ/m_μ) = 2.82, and ln(M_Z/m_τ) = 3.94. The total change is Δ(1/α) = (1/2π)[(4/3)(5.33) + (8/3)(2.82) + 4(3.94)] = (1/2π)(7.11 + 7.53 + 15.75) = 4.84. That is the leading-logarithm version of Lesson 68's leptonic shift, (1/3π)Σ ln(M_Z²/m_ℓ²) = 4.84, computed now as three straight-line segments. Lesson 68's constants −5/3 reduce it to 4.30. Quarks add their own segments with N_c Q_f² = 4/3 for up-type and 1/3 for down-type quarks, but below a few GeV the strong interaction makes those segments unreliable, which is why Lesson 68 took the hadronic part from data.

# 5 The sign of β

## 5.1 Three possibilities

If β > 0, the coupling grows with μ and shrinks at long distance. This is QED, and it is screening restated. If β < 0, the coupling shrinks at short distance, which is called asymptotic freedom. The strong interaction behaves this way: its one-loop coefficient has the form 11 − (2/3)n_f, where the 11 comes from gluon self-interaction. That result is quoted here, not derived. If β = 0 at some value of the coupling, that value is a fixed point, where the theory looks the same at every scale. Lesson 68's Exercise 16 asked what Π̂ would have to be for β to vanish: independent of q², so that no logarithm appears.

## 5.2 The Landau pole as a property of the equation

Setting 1/α(μ) = 0 in Section 4.2 gives

$$ μ_L = μ₀ exp[2π / (b α[μ₀])]

For the electron alone, with μ₀ = m and α(μ₀) = 1/137.036, this is m exp(3π/2α) ≈ 10²⁸⁰ m, matching Lesson 68 apart from the matching constant e^(5/6). The one-loop solution blows up there, but the equation was derived assuming α small, so near μ_L the equation itself is no longer trustworthy. Whether the full theory has such a pole cannot be decided from one loop. Lattice studies of QED-like theories suggest that a consistent QED with nonzero charge at low energy may require a cutoff, which is one meaning of the statement that QED is an effective theory. In practice the question never arises, because QED is part of the electroweak theory of Lesson 86 long before μ_L.

### Worked example 5

Explain why the Landau pole moves to lower scales as more charged species are added, and estimate the effect of the three leptons alone. A larger b gives a steeper line in 1/α versus ln μ, so 1/α reaches zero sooner. Starting from 1/α(M_Z) ≈ 132.7 with leptons only (Lesson 68) and b = 4, the pole sits at ln(μ_L/M_Z) = 2π × 132.7/4 ≈ 208, so μ_L ≈ 10⁹⁰ M_Z. That is far lower than 10²⁷⁷ GeV, but still incomparably beyond the Planck scale.

# 6 Beyond one loop, quoted

At two loops, for one charged Dirac fermion,

$$ β[e] = e³/(12π²) + e⁵/(64π⁴) + ⋯

or equivalently μ dα/dμ = (2α²/3π)[1 + 3α/(4π)]. The correction is about 0.2 percent of the leading term. For a theory with a single coupling and massless fermions, these first two coefficients do not depend on the subtraction scheme. From three loops on, they do. Masses also run: in the modified minimal-subtraction scheme the electron mass obeys μ dm/dμ = −(3α/2π)m at one loop, so the running mass decreases slowly with scale. Both results are standard, and neither is derived in this course. They are quoted so that a reader meeting them elsewhere knows where they belong.

# 7 What this lesson has not done

This lesson derived the one-loop β from Lesson 68's explicit loop, not from a general renormalization of the Lagrangian. It has not computed the two-loop coefficient or the mass anomalous dimension, and it has not derived the strong-interaction coefficient 11 − (2/3)n_f. It has treated thresholds as sharp jumps, which is accurate to the leading logarithm, and has relied on Lesson 68 for the constants. The general framework, in which every parameter of the Lagrangian has its own running, is the subject of the field-theory texts named in the Bibliography.

# 9 Summary

1. Differentiating Lesson 68's running formula gives μ dα/dμ = 2α²/(3π), or β(e) = e³/(12π²).

2. The coupling can be defined at any scale μ. Physical observables cannot depend on μ, and that requirement is the renormalization-group equation.

3. Solving the equation sums the leading logarithms α(αL)ⁿ, which justifies the denominator form of the running coupling.

4. With b = (4/3)Σ N_c Q_f², 1/α runs linearly in ln μ, with a change of slope at each threshold.

5. β > 0 in QED: screening, and a one-loop Landau pole far beyond any physical scale, where the equation itself no longer applies.

6. The two-loop term e⁵/(64π⁴) and the running mass are quoted, not derived.

# Solutions

**1.** Let D = 1 − (α/3π)L, with dL/d ln μ = 2. Then dα(μ)/d ln μ = α(α/3π)(2)/D² = (2/3π)(α/D)² = 2α(μ)²/(3π).

**2.** α = e²/4π gives μ dα/dμ = (e/2π)μ de/dμ. Setting this equal to 2e⁴/(16π² × 3π) = e⁴/(24π³) gives μ de/dμ = (2π/e) × e⁴/(24π³) = e³/(12π²).

**3.** From Lesson 68, 1/α_eff(Q²) = 1/α − (1/3π)ln(Q²/m²), and in the same way 1/α(μ) = 1/α − (1/3π)ln(μ²/m²). Subtracting, 1/α_eff(Q²) = 1/α(μ) − (1/3π)ln(Q²/μ²). Inverting gives Section 2.1.

**4.** μ ∂/∂μ of ln(Q²/μ²) is −2, so μ∂R/∂μ = α(α/3π)(−2) = −2α²/(3π). β ∂R/∂α = (2α²/3π)(1 + 2αL/3π) = 2α²/(3π) + O(α³). The sum is O(α³), beyond the order computed.

**5.** α(1 + x + x² + x³ + ⋯). The term xⁿ comes from n vacuum-polarization bubbles chained on the photon line, each contributing one power of α and one logarithm.

**6.** dα/α² = (b/2π)d ln μ, so −1/α(μ) + 1/α(μ₀) = (b/2π)ln(μ/μ₀). With b = 4/3, b/2π = 2/(3π), and (2/3π)ln(μ/μ₀) = (1/3π)ln(μ²/μ₀²), which is Lesson 68's logarithm with μ₀ = m.

**7.** Leptons: (4/3)(1 + 1 + 1) = 4. Quarks: u and c give 3 × 4/9 each, and d, s, b give 3 × 1/9 each, so the quarks add (4/3)(4/3 + 4/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3) = (4/3)(11/3) = 44/9. The total is 4 + 44/9 = 80/9 ≈ 8.9.

**8.** From Exercise 7, b = 80/9 ≈ 8.89. ln(10/1.777) = 1.73, so Δ(1/α) = (8.89)(1.73)/(2π) = 2.45, and 1/α(10 GeV) ≈ 132.3. The free-quark treatment ignores confinement and quark-mass ambiguities below a few GeV. That is why Lesson 68 takes the hadronic piece from data.

**9.** A loop with m_f ≫ Q contributes only (α/15π)Q²/m_f² to Π̂. This carries no logarithm, so it adds nothing to the slope dα/d ln μ, and it vanishes as m_f/Q → ∞.

**10.** Below threshold the particle contributes nothing. Above it, the step-function rule gives (α/3π)ln(Q²/μ_th²). Choosing μ_th = m e^(5/6) gives ln(Q²/m²) − 2(5/6) = ln(Q²/m²) − 5/3, which is Lesson 68's large-Q² result.

**11.** 1/α(μ_L) = 0 gives (b/2π)ln(μ_L/μ₀) = 1/α(μ₀). For the electron, b = 4/3 and ln(μ_L/m) = (3π/2) × 137.036 = 645.8, so log₁₀(μ_L/m) = 280.4.

**12.** The ratio is 3α/(4π) = 3/(4π × 129) = 1.85×10⁻³, about 0.2 percent.

**13.** ln(m(100)/m(1)) = −(3α/2π)ln 100 = −(3/(2π × 137))(4.61) = −0.016. The mass decreases by about 1.6 percent, using α ≈ 1/137 as a rough constant.

**14.** Setting μ = Q removes the large logarithm from the one-loop correction, but the two-loop terms, of order α², remain uncomputed. Varying μ by a factor of 2 around Q changes the truncated result by an amount of that same order, so the variation estimates the size of the missing terms. It is not an error to be eliminated.
