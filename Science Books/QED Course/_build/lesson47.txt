# How to use this lesson

Lessons 45 and 46 built the scalar and electron propagators by the same two-step method: form the un-time-ordered two-point function from the field's mode expansion, then time-order it. This lesson attempts the identical method for the photon and finds it genuinely harder, for a reason specific to gauge theories: Lesson 37 quantized A^i in Coulomb gauge, with A⁰=0 and ∇·A=0 imposed from the outset, so the object this lesson can honestly build is a purely spatial, non-covariant propagator carrying an explicit transverse projector. The lesson constructs that object carefully in momentum space, where it is clean and fully checkable, and then introduces the covariant Feynman-gauge propagator −ig^μν/(k²+iε) as the simpler, calculationally preferred alternative used throughout the rest of this course, with a first, honest argument for why the difference between the two does not affect physical results — an argument this lesson can only partially complete, with the remainder deferred to Lesson 71's Ward identity. Expect five to seven hours.

## Learning objectives

- Construct D^ij(x−y)=⟨0|A^i(x)A^j(y)|0⟩ from Lesson 37's mode expansion and the transverse polarization sum Σ_λε_λ^iε_λ^j=δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j.

- Time-order this two-point function to define D_F^ij(x−y), using the bosonic (unsigned) time-ordering rule.

- Verify, in momentum space, that D_F^ij(k)=i(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)/(k²+iε) satisfies the expected Green's function property as ε→0⁺.

- Explain why the position-space Green's function involves a nonlocal "transverse delta function," and why this course does not need its explicit form.

- State the covariant Feynman-gauge propagator D_F^μν(k)=−ig^μν/(k²+iε) and compare its spatial block to the Coulomb-gauge result.

- Give a first, partial argument, based on current conservation, for why the difference between the two propagators should not affect physical amplitudes, and identify what is still missing from that argument.

- State both propagator forms from memory and explain the relationship between them.

# 1 The photon two-point function

## 1.1 Constructing D^ij(x−y)

Lesson 37 established ⟨0|A^i(x)|k,λ⟩=ε_λ^i(k)e^(−ikx), with the mode expansion built from two real, transverse polarization vectors satisfying Σ_λε_λ^i(k)ε_λ^j(k)=δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j. Since the photon is its own antiparticle (Lesson 37), A^i(x) contains a single kind of ladder operator pair, a_λ(k) and a_λ†(k), each multiplying the same real ε_λ^i(k), unlike the complex Dirac field's distinct b and d operators. Following exactly the method of Lesson 34, Section 4.1,

$$ D^(ij)(x−y) := ⟨0| A^i(x) A^j(y) |0⟩ = ∫ d³k/((2π)³2ω) Σ_λ ε_λ^i(k)ε_λ^j(k) , e^(−ik(x−y)) = ∫ d³k/((2π)³2ω) (δ^(ij)−k̂^ik̂^j) , e^(−ik(x−y))

with ω=|k| for the massless photon, and k(x−y)=ω(x⁰−y⁰)−k·(x−y).

## 1.2 What is different here

D^ij(x−y) carries an extra structure D(x−y) never had: a momentum-dependent matrix factor, δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j, sitting inside the integral. This factor depends only on the direction k̂ of the three-momentum, not on k⁰ at all, a fact Section 3 relies on directly.

### Worked example 1

Explain why the photon's self-antiparticle property (Lesson 37) simplifies Section 1.1's calculation relative to the analogous step for the Dirac field in Lesson 36. For the Dirac field, ⟨0|ψ(x)ψ̄(y)|0⟩ picked out only the b-b† pairing, distinct from the d-d† pairing that would appear in the reversed order; for the photon, there is only one kind of ladder operator, so ⟨0|A^i(x)A^j(y)|0⟩ and ⟨0|A^j(y)A^i(x)|0⟩ are built from the identical a-a† pairing, with the two point functions related simply by exchanging x and y, with no separate "antiparticle piece" to track.

# 2 Time-ordering

## 2.1 The definition

Since A^i is a bosonic field, the time-ordering rule carries no extra sign, exactly as for the scalar field in Lesson 45:

$$ D_F^(ij)(x−y) := ⟨0| T{A^i(x)A^j(y)} |0⟩ = θ(x⁰−y⁰) D^(ij)(x−y) + θ(y⁰−x⁰) D^(ij)(y−x)

## 2.2 A scope note on A⁰

A fully covariant treatment of the photon propagator would need to include A⁰ as well, and account for how the Coulomb-gauge condition A⁰=0 is itself not preserved by a general Lorentz boost. Lesson 37 imposed A⁰=0 directly, as a gauge choice, without treating A⁰ as an independent dynamical operator with its own conjugate momentum; consistent with that choice, this lesson's construction concerns only A^i, and no separate propagator for A⁰ is built here. This is a genuine simplification relative to the most careful treatments of Coulomb-gauge electrodynamics, and it is the reason Section 4 turns to the covariant Feynman-gauge propagator, which handles all four components of A_μ uniformly, for the calculations of later lessons.

### Worked example 2

State why Section 2.1 needed no analogue of Lesson 46's minus sign. Lesson 46's minus sign traced directly to the fermionic anticommutation structure of Lesson 36, Section 6.2; A^i is built from bosonic ladder operators satisfying ordinary commutation relations (Lesson 37, following the pattern of Lesson 32), so no such sign is generated when its two orderings are combined into a time-ordered product.

# 3 The momentum-space propagator

## 3.1 The proposed form

By direct analogy with Lesson 45, Section 4.1, propose

$$ D_F^(ij)(x−y) = ∫ d⁴k/(2π)⁴ , i (δ^(ij)−k̂^ik̂^j) / (k²+iε) , e^(−ik(x−y))

the massless (m=0) specialization of the scalar Feynman propagator's pole structure, multiplied by the fixed transverse projector.

## 3.2 Checking the Green's function property

Applying □_x=∂_t²−∇² to the exponential factor gives □_xe^(−ik(x−y))=−k²e^(−ik(x−y)), exactly as in Lesson 45, Exercise 9, since this step uses only the exponential's structure, not the photon's polarization content. So

$$ □_x D_F^(ij) = − i ∫ d⁴k/(2π)⁴ (δ^(ij)−k̂^ik̂^j) , k²/(k²+iε) , e^(−ik(x−y))

As ε→0⁺, k²/(k²+iε)→1 away from the pole, exactly as in Lesson 45, Section 4.2, giving

$$ □_x D_F^(ij)(x−y) → − i ∫ d⁴k/(2π)⁴ (δ^(ij)−k̂^ik̂^j) e^(−ik(x−y))

Unlike Lesson 45's analogous result, this is not simply −iδ⁴(x−y)δ^ij, because (δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j) is a nontrivial function of k̂ that does not integrate out to a constant. Separating the k⁰ integral, which depends on none of the polarization structure, ∫dk⁰/(2π)e^(−ik⁰(x⁰−y⁰))=δ(x⁰−y⁰) (Exercise 9), and what remains is a specific, well-defined but nonlocal function of x−y built from the remaining three-dimensional integral, called the transverse delta function. This course does not need its explicit position-space form; every later calculation uses D_F^ij(k) directly, in momentum space, exactly as written in Section 3.1.

### Worked example 3

Explain briefly why the appearance of a nonlocal object in position space is not, by itself, cause for concern. Coulomb-gauge electrodynamics is well known to involve nonlocal-looking objects in position space (the instantaneous, action-at-a-distance-looking Coulomb potential between charges is the most familiar example, arising from the same A⁰=0 gauge structure), reflecting the fact that the gauge choice itself, not the underlying physics, is responsible for the nonlocality; physical, gauge-invariant predictions built from this propagator remain local and causal, and Section 3.1's momentum-space form is entirely sufficient for the diagrammatic calculations this course actually performs.

## 3.3 The massless pole

Comparing D_F^ij(k)'s denominator, k²+iε, with Δ_F(k)'s k²−m²+iε (Lesson 45) and S_F(p)'s p²−m²+iε (Lesson 46), the photon propagator is exactly the m=0 specialization of the same pole structure, consistent with the photon's masslessness established since Lesson 37.

# 4 A simpler, covariant alternative

## 4.1 The Feynman-gauge propagator

For calculations, this course uses the covariant object

$$ D_F^(μν)(k) = − i g^(μν) / (k²+iε)

a single, Lorentz-covariant expression with no explicit projector, treating all four components of A_μ on an equal footing.

## 4.2 Comparing the spatial block

The spatial-spatial part of −ig^μν is −ig^ij; using this course's metric convention g^ij=−δ^ij, this is −i(−δ^ij)=iδ^ij, differing from Section 3.1's Coulomb-gauge numerator i(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j) by exactly ik̂^ik̂^j, a term built entirely from the momentum direction k̂ (Exercise 13 verifies this directly).

## 4.3 A first plausibility argument

A term proportional to k^ik^j (or, covariantly, k^μk^ν) in a propagator's numerator vanishes when contracted against a conserved current at each end of the internal line: Lesson 41, Section 5, established ∂_μj^μ=0 for the electron current, which in momentum space reads k_μj^μ(k)=0; a piece of the propagator proportional to k^μ, dotted into such a current, gives exactly zero. This suggests, without yet fully proving, that the difference between D_F^μν(k) and the honest Coulomb-gauge result has no effect on any physical amplitude, every internal photon line in this course's diagrams being attached at both ends to a conserved electron current.

### Worked example 4

State precisely what this section's argument has not yet shown. Section 4.3's argument treats the current at each vertex as exactly conserved, ∂_μj^μ=0, which is a property of the classical field equations (Lesson 41); a complete argument needs to confirm that this conservation survives intact at the level of the full quantum scattering amplitude, including all the bookkeeping of the perturbative expansion not yet developed (Lesson 49 onward), and stated precisely as the Ward identity. This lesson gives the physical idea; Lesson 71 gives the proof.

# 6 Summary

1. D^ij(x−y)=⟨0|A^i(x)A^j(y)|0⟩ is built directly from Lesson 37's mode expansion and carries the transverse projector δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j.

2. Time-ordering, with no extra sign (A^i is bosonic), gives D_F^ij(x−y).

3. In momentum space, D_F^ij(k)=i(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)/(k²+iε), verified by the same substitution check used in Lesson 45, up to a nonlocal position-space structure this course does not need explicitly.

4. The photon propagator's pole, k²+iε, is the massless specialization of the pole structure already seen in Lessons 45 and 46.

5. The covariant Feynman-gauge propagator D_F^μν(k)=−ig^μν/(k²+iε) is used in practice; its spatial block differs from the Coulomb-gauge result by a term proportional to k^ik^j.

6. Such k^μk^ν terms vanish when contracted with a conserved current, a first, physical argument for gauge independence, made fully rigorous only in Lesson 71.

# Solutions

**1.** A^i(x)=∫d³k/(2π)³(2ω)^(−1/2)Σ_λ[ε_λ^i(k)a_λ(k)e^(−ikx)+ε_λ^i(k)a_λ†(k)e^(ikx)] (Lesson 37's mode expansion, with real ε_λ^i since the photon is its own antiparticle). Sandwiching ⟨0|A^i(x)A^j(y)|0⟩, only the a_λ(k)-a_λ†(k′) term survives (the a_λ† piece of A^i(x) annihilates against ⟨0| and the a_λ piece of A^j(y) annihilates |0⟩ directly, leaving only the cross term), giving, after using [a_λ(k),a_λ′†(k′)]=(2π)³δ³(k−k′)δ_λλ′ to collapse one momentum integral, D^ij(x−y)=∫d³k/((2π)³2ω)Σ_λε_λ^i(k)ε_λ^j(k)e^(−ik(x−y)).

**2.** Substituting Σ_λε_λ^i(k)ε_λ^j(k)=δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j directly into Exercise 1's result gives D^ij(x−y)=∫d³k/((2π)³2ω)(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)e^(−ik(x−y)), exactly Section 1.1's formula.

**3.** Since only one type of ladder operator (a, a†) appears in A^i, the calculation of ⟨0|A^i(y)A^j(x)|0⟩ proceeds by the identical steps as Exercise 1 with x and y (and correspondingly i and j) exchanged, using the same polarization sum Σ_λε_λ^iε_λ^j, unlike the Dirac case where ⟨0|ψ(x)ψ̄(y)|0⟩ and ⟨0|ψ̄(y)ψ(x)|0⟩ pick out different operator pairings (b-b† versus d-d†).

**4.** D_F^ij(x−y)=θ(x⁰−y⁰)D^ij(x−y)+θ(y⁰−x⁰)D^ij(y−x), a plain sum with no relative minus sign, because A^i's ladder operators satisfy ordinary bosonic commutation relations (Lesson 37, following Lesson 32's pattern), and Lesson 46, Section 1.1, traced its minus sign specifically to fermionic anticommutation, a structure absent here.

**5.** Lesson 37 sets A⁰=0 as part of the Coulomb-gauge choice, without treating A⁰ as an independent operator with its own conjugate momentum and mode expansion; since this lesson's propagator is built entirely from the two-point functions of fields already quantized in Lesson 37, and A⁰ was never one of them, only A^i appears here. A fully covariant treatment would need to restore A⁰ as a dynamical (or constrained, Lagrange-multiplier-like) field with its own consistent quantization, an additional ingredient not developed in this course.

**6.** ∂_μ∂^μe^(−ikx)=(−ik_μ)(−ik^μ)e^(−ikx)=−k_μk^μe^(−ikx)=−k²e^(−ikx), the identical computation as Lesson 45, Exercise 9, with the electron's four-momentum p replaced by the photon's four-momentum k, and with k²=(k⁰)²−|k|² using the same metric convention.

**7.** □_xD_F^ij=∫d⁴k/(2π)⁴ i(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)/(k²+iε) · □_xe^(−ik(x−y)) = ∫d⁴k/(2π)⁴ i(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)/(k²+iε) · (−k²)e^(−ik(x−y)) = −i∫d⁴k/(2π)⁴ (δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j) k²/(k²+iε) e^(−ik(x−y)), using Exercise 6 and pulling the fixed matrix factor (δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j), which the □_x operator does not act on, outside the differentiation.

**8.** For any k with k²≠0, as ε→0⁺ the ratio k²/(k²+iε) approaches k²/k²=1, by the identical reasoning as Lesson 45, Exercise 11; substituting this limiting value into Exercise 7's result gives □_xD_F^ij→−i∫d⁴k/(2π)⁴(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)e^(−ik(x−y)).

**9.** Since (δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j) depends only on the spatial direction k̂, not on k⁰, the k⁰ integral factors out: ∫d⁴k/(2π)⁴(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)e^(−ik(x−y)) = [∫dk⁰/(2π)e^(−ik⁰(x⁰−y⁰))]·[∫d³k/(2π)³(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)e^(ik·(x−y))], and the bracketed k⁰ integral is the standard Fourier representation of the one-dimensional delta function, δ(x⁰−y⁰).

**10.** If the factor were simply δ^ij (a constant matrix), the remaining spatial integral ∫d³k/(2π)³δ^ije^(ik·(x−y)) would collapse directly to δ^ijδ³(x−y); the actual factor δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j depends on the direction of k, a nontrivial function of the integration variable that cannot be pulled outside the integral, so the result is instead a genuinely k-dependent (and therefore position-space nonlocal) function of x−y, not a simple delta function.

**11.** All three propagators share the same denominator structure, "(momentum)²−(mass)²+iε," with S_F(p) and Δ_F(k) carrying the electron's mass m and D_F^ij(k) carrying zero mass (the photon being massless since Lesson 37); setting m=0 in either p²−m²+iε or k²−m²+iε (Lessons 46 and 45 respectively) gives exactly k²+iε (or p²+iε), matching D_F^ij(k)'s denominator.

**12.** D_F^00(k)=−ig^00/(k²+iε). Using this course's metric convention diag(+,−,−,−), g^00=+1, so D_F^00(k)=−i/(k²+iε), the timelike component of the covariant propagator.

**13.** The spatial block of −ig^μν is −ig^ij; substituting g^ij=−δ^ij (this course's metric convention for spatial indices), −ig^ij=−i(−δ^ij)=iδ^ij. Comparing to Section 3.1's Coulomb-gauge numerator i(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j), the difference is iδ^ij−i(δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j)=iδ^ij−iδ^ij+ik̂^ik̂^j=ik̂^ik̂^j, confirming the claim.

**14.** Lesson 41, Section 5.1, derived ∂_μj^μ=0 from the antisymmetry of F^μν; in momentum space, a derivative ∂_μ becomes multiplication by −ik_μ under a Fourier transform, so ∂_μj^μ=0 becomes k_μj^μ(k)=0. A term in the propagator's numerator proportional to k^μ, contracted with such a current, gives k^μj_μ(k)=0 directly, by exactly this relation.

**15.** Using a single, fixed covariant expression D_F^μν(k)=−ig^μν/(k²+iε) for every internal photon line, regardless of that line's specific momentum direction, avoids having to recompute or re-orient the momentum-dependent transverse projector δ^ij−k̂^ik̂^j separately for every line in every diagram, substantially simplifying the bookkeeping of multi-vertex calculations.

**16.** Section 4.3's argument assumes that the current j^μ(k) appearing at a given vertex inside a full scattering amplitude satisfies k_μj^μ(k)=0 exactly, treating the vertex as if it were simply the classical, conserved current of Lesson 41; it has not verified that this conservation survives intact once the vertex is embedded in a complete quantum-mechanical amplitude built from the perturbative machinery of Lesson 49 onward, including any subtleties from how internal lines and other vertices in the same diagram interact with it. This more complete verification, stated precisely as the Ward identity, is supplied by Lesson 71.
