## How to Read This Book

Every claim in these pages wears one of three temperatures. The difference matters more than any date on a slide.

**Hot — established.** Measured, repeated, and used to predict new measurements. People and firms are spending real money on lunar crew systems. Uncrewed landers and sample capsules have already kissed dust in this century. Light to the Moon takes about a second and a third. Light to Mars takes minutes. A Hohmann path between the two planets is a season, not a weekend. Most humans will stay on Earth for any leaving we can actually staff. A few thousand settlers do almost nothing to the air.

**Warm — working program, real gaps.** A crew around the Moon this decade is a class of mission, not a month. A landing *attempt* in the 2030s is the honest noun for a cabin that still owes a demonstration of the ugly parts. A heavy stack that becomes cheap per tonne *if* it learns routine would change the mass you can throw. If industry collapsed, emissions would fall fast; so would the aerosol parasol. The infrastructure of going can worsen the bill for the people who stay.

**Cold — speculation sold as inventory.** A city on the Moon or Mars this decade. Children who do not need a cargo cult. Settlement as something you can buy by Friday because a founder named a year. Leaving as a cleanup. Earth bouncing back as a garden once the ambitious have gone.

A temperature can change. A stack that has flown is hotter than a rendering. A date that has slipped is not, by itself, a con, and it is not destiny. It is an invoice the slide did not want to show.

On one standing rule. **String theory, if it is named in this book, cannot currently be tested.** It will barely be named. Destinations do not need extra dimensions. They need dirt, delay, and dates.

A few scenes are stories: a woman on a Mars-like dead world, an airlock that sticks, basil under an LED, a radio that answers a quarter-hour late, a meeting room that tries to fuse three nouns. They are thought experiments with dirt on them. The numbers in them are real. The people are invented. They are here so a program has somewhere to sit besides a poster.

The main chapters keep the math in the sentence. A handful of relations are important enough to number. Those numbered relations live in the Appendix, where tables are allowed and a clock is allowed to sit still. If you want the scaffolding, it is there. If you want the argument, you can finish the book without opening the back.

You do not need a physics degree. You need a willingness to let a downtown wait until a kitchen works.

## This Book Stands Alone

This is Volume 2 of *Look First*. It is a complete book. You are not being assigned homework.

A longer book already retired a cosmic “now” and treated elsewhere as a *permit*. You do not need that book. The permit here is simpler: a world you can weigh, a delay you can time, a date that will slip.

If you liked the clocks of air and ice and wanted a body that keeps its own, that want has a later kitchen: *The Body Keeps Its Own Clock*. That book stands alone too. It reuses three temperatures and a habit of looking first. It does not reopen this spine. You can stop here. The airlock will still stick tomorrow.

## Author’s Note

Most people meet the Moon and Mars in the wrong order.

First comes a poster. Then a year. Then a city. Then — if the book has nerve — a sentence about oxygen, and a sentence about who stays behind. Time is treated as the thing a founder can name. A slip is treated as a scandal or a destiny. Earth is treated as a kitchen you can abandon because a dead world has been called a spare.

That order is commercially understandable. It is the order that sells a downtown. It is not the order that makes the invoices easiest to see.

This book runs a different sequence on purpose. It starts with dirt, delay, and dates, because those are what a program actually is. It keeps three nouns unfused — *sortie*, *outpost*, *settlement* — because fusing them is how a flyby becomes a school district in a single slide. Only after the Moon has been allowed to be close, and after the hardware that has already flown has been named, does it turn to the next crew, the landable year, the station that is a choice, the other flags, the heavy stack that is a vehicle, and the planet where posters go to breed. The questions people actually want — How soon? Will Earth recover if we leave? — are delayed until the words have meanings.

John D. Barrow’s short book *The Origin of the Universe* is still the formal model: brief chapters, no swagger, a clean line between a measurement and an idea for ideas. The neighbors in this lane are the people who treated a camp as a bill and a biosphere as a clock, not as a sermon. They are used. They are not copied. Where a year will move, the year is written as a *kind of clock*. This kitchen is dated September 2026. Official pages will need a new page. The temperatures should survive the pass.

A book like this should not succeed because a reader can recite a launch window. It should succeed if a sequence of recognitions arrives and stays:

that a program is not a poster;  
that a slipped date is an invoice, not a morality play;  
that the Moon is first because it is three days away, not because it is a spare Earth;  
that a flag is not a shift;  
that a crew around a dead close world is a sortie;  
that a landable year is not a town;  
that a station in lunar orbit is a choice, not a law;  
that another ledger’s camp on paper is still a camp;  
that a large cheap stack is a vehicle, and routine is the unpaid invoice;  
that Mars rhetoric is ruder than Mars engineering only in the slides;  
that sortie, outpost, and settlement must not be fused;  
that most of us stay;  
that damages keep different clocks;  
that leaving is not a cleanup;  
and that the world that already has a staff is this one.

That last recognition is not a counsel against going. It is the opposite of a write-off. If a city is not scheduled for Friday, the kitchen you already staff still has to be washed. Look first. Seed later. The basil still has to live.
