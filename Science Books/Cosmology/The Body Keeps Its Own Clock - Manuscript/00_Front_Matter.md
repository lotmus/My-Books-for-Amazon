## Prologue: The Capsule and the Hands

A capsule arrives on the same delay as the orbiter’s voice. The label is optimistic. Her hands are not.

She works a greenhouse on a dead world — wet soil under LEDs the color of an overcast noon, a tray of basil that does not belong to this dirt, a radio that answers a quarter-hour late, or four minutes, depending on the week. There is no conversation. There is a stack of monologues that eventually, if everyone lives, amount to a plan.

The capsule is a local win in a blister pack. A joint that still works. A season she can still hit an airlock for. She takes the dose because local wins are the only kind biology has ever reliably sold. She does not become a different species. She names the basil, which is sentimental, and logs the pH, which is not.

This book is about what that scene is allowed to mean, and what a slide deck is not allowed to sell you on the back of it.

The short version, before it has been earned:

Lifespan is not healthspan. Aging is many clocks, not one fuse. A gene fix is a local repair — this instruction, this tissue, this invoice — not a fountain. The sentence *we only use ten percent of our brain* is false. You do not have a spare ninety percent in the cupboard waiting for a password. You have the organ you already use, plus sleep, plus practice, plus tools, plus other people. The carrot that makes a body chase a cue has no off-switch. A long life does not install one. And the last fifty years of real, uneven progress are not a method for drawing a straight line to the year 12,000.

Those are recognitions, not moods. They wear temperatures. The first chapter teaches the temperatures. The rest of the book spends them on mornings you would vote to repeat, on scissors with paperwork, on an energy bill the skull already pays, and on the walls that have names: energy, materials, attention, institutions, wars.

If you came for helium, you will not get helium. You may leave with a colder, better want: more mornings that are still worth logging.

## How to Read This Book

Every claim in these pages wears one of three temperatures. The difference matters more than any slogan.

**Hot — established.** Measured, repeated, and used to predict new measurements. Lifespan and healthspan are different pots. The “ten percent of the brain” sentence is false. A sickle-cell-class gene edit can, in a growing list of cases, correct a broken instruction in a lineage of cells that will keep the correction. The last half-century produced real, uneven gains: vaccines that closed rooms of death, a genome you can read without a monastery, packets, cheaper compute.

**Warm — working account.** The best story we have, with real gaps. Aging is many clocks, not one fuse: the pieces are real; a single kitchen switch is not on the counter. Modest further gains in healthy years this century, in rich-country medicine, if institutions hold. Progress that is more often an S-curve than a ray. Whatever is invented will still pay energy, coordination, and the body’s invoices.

**Cold — speculation dressed as a product.** Allowed as a wish. Not selected by present levers. A body-wide rewind. A scheduled 150-year, 200-year, or thousand-year ape. Unlocking a hidden ninety percent of mind. Last fifty years × 200 = the furniture of the year 12,000. A file that is you, forever. A required last mind at the end of time. Progress as a law that fires if we wait.

A claim can change temperature. A local gene fix that was a paper in 2012 can be a clinic in 2023. A fountain does not get warmer because the local fix did. Do not let a hot win launder a cold catalog.

On a standing rule from the rest of this series: **string theory, if it is named, cannot currently be tested.** It is a large mathematical framework. It has not produced a unique, risky prediction that would kill it if the experiment came out otherwise. This book barely needs it. If a slide uses “quantum” as incense over a longevity claim, ask for the invoice, not the incense.

A few scenes are stories: a greenhouse on a dead planet, a radio that answers late, a capsule that may keep a joint useful, a committee that cannot agree, a founder with a straight line on a wall. They are thought experiments with dirt on them. The numbers in them are real. The people are invented. They are here so the clocks have somewhere to sit besides a diagram.

The main chapters keep the numbers in the sentence, the way a good explainer talks at a table. A handful of relations are important enough to sit still.

where HALE is healthy life expectancy — years you would, on present evidence, vote to repeat — not the years a wall clock can still be embarrassed by a pulse.

Those numbered marks, and the tables behind them, live in the Appendix. If you want the scaffolding, it is there. If you want the argument, you can finish the book without opening the back.

You do not need a medical degree. You do not need Book 1. You need curiosity and a willingness to let a miracle come apart into pots.

## The Carrot, If You Start Here

Book 1 of this series taught a kitchen fact about wanting: a lineage that must copy, search, and pay will not last if the staff treats the invoice as optional. Pleasure is staffing. The cue can be turned up until a fake outbids the thing. The carrot has no off-switch labeled *this is only a picture*.

That book said it about a feast and a fake. You do not need those pages. The fact is still true if the feast is a long life.

A Pleistocene wanting-loop — seek, tag, chase, get a stamp, seek again — in a body that lasts 120 years is not a wise king. It is a mismatch with more calendar. Sugar, a slot, a feed that never ends, a slide that says *enhancement*: same family. Wanting is one juice. Liking is another. The off-switch was never hired. Look first before you call the extra years an upgrade. Chapter 8 teaches the loop in full. One courtesy is enough. The rest of this book will not assign Book 1 as homework.

## A Note on the Series

This book is Volume 3 of *Look First*. It is a complete book. You are not being assigned homework.

If you already have Volume 1, *The Universe Has No Now*, you will recognize a woman, three temperatures, and a habit of looking first. You do not need its leftover glow, its courtroom about the first three minutes, or its map of other solvents. Those rooms stay there. This kitchen is the body, the organ you already use, and the lie of the straight line.

If you already have Volume 2, *A Permit Is Not a City*, you will recognize the same greenhouse and a ruder question about camps. This book does not reopen landing windows. A pill is not a downtown.

A backward point in these pages is a courtesy, not a chore. The loaf — the idea that all the times are already on the inventory — gets at most one sentence here. You can finish without it.

You can stop here. Her hands will still age tomorrow. That is not a tragedy the cosmos owes you a refund for. It is the starting fact.

## Author’s Note

Most people meet the future of the body in the wrong order.

First comes a headline about a gene. Then a chart that goes up and to the right. Then a sentence that says we only use ten percent of the mind, so the other ninety is a tank. Then — if the talk has nerve — a straight line from the last fifty years of gadgets drawn across ten thousand years of furniture nobody can name. Time is treated as a fuse you can pull. DNA is treated as a fountain. The brain is treated as a cupboard. Civilization is treated as a transistor with a calendar.

That order is commercially understandable. It is roughly the order a slide deck discovers these things. It is not the order that makes the invoices easiest to see.

This book runs a different sequence on purpose. It starts with a pair of hands that still age after a useful dose, because that is what a local win looks like when you refuse helium. Only after lifespan and healthspan have been split into two pots does it turn to the many clocks, the local scissors, the organ you already staff, the carrot that will not switch off, and the cartoon that multiplies a real half-century by two hundred and calls the product a kitchen.

John D. Barrow’s short books are still the formal model: brief chapters, no swagger, a clean line between a measurement and an idea for ideas. The biology neighbors are the honest histories — the gene as a local object, medicine as an S-curve, the brain as an energy bill — not the startup keynote. Where a clinic and a keynote disagree, the disagreement is the point.

A book like this should not succeed because a reader can recite a hallmark of aging. It should succeed if a sequence of recognitions arrives and stays:

that years on a wall clock are not years you would vote to repeat;  
that aging is many clocks, and a shout at one does not reset the kitchen;  
that a gene fix is this tissue, this invoice — a class of win, not a fountain;  
that a 150-year scheduled product is a wish, and a thousand-year ape is colder still;  
that the ten-percent sentence is false, and idle-looking tissue is still on duty;  
that plasticity is real and expensive, not a spare tank;  
that education, sleep, a hearing aid, a stimulant with a known bill are tools, not unlocks;  
that the carrot has no off-switch, and a longer life is more calendar for the mismatch;  
that the last fifty years were real and are not a method;  
that progress is more often an S-curve, and diminishing returns are a kitchen fact;  
that fifty into ten thousand is two hundred, which is arithmetic, not physics;  
that the limit is often a meeting, a ledger, a famine, a front;  
that a file is not a worldline;  
and that the honest product, if you want one, is a morning still worth hitting an airlock for.

That last recognition is not a counsel of despair. It is the opposite. If helium is not scheduled, meaning is made in the useful season — this copy, this joint, this pH.

Readers of *The Universe Has No Now* will notice a family resemblance: start with the variable that makes the hygiene simple, not the variable that got sold first. There, the variable was the event. Here, it is the clock the body actually keeps — many of them — and the difference between a local fix and a sermon.
