# PART III — A Burst, Then Seeds

---

## 12. Three Puzzles the Hot Bang Cannot Solve

The leftover glow and the leftover helium make the hot, expanding past look inevitable. They do not make it comfortable. Three puzzles sit in the model like bills on a table, and the three-minute workshop does not pay them.

The first is the *horizon problem*. Look at the microwave sky in two opposite directions. The temperatures match to a part in a hundred thousand. For two patches to agree that tightly, they usually need to have talked — to have exchanged light, mixed, come to terms. In the simple hot bang, those opposite patches have never been in each other’s past light cones. They are like two strangers who have never met and have never had a friend in common, wearing the same shirt to the same size. You can call it a coincidence. Physicists get hives.

The second is the *flatness problem*. The universe’s geometry — whether parallel lines stay parallel on the largest scales — is astonishingly close to “flat,” which is another way of saying the density is astonishingly close to a special critical value. In the simple bang, that closeness is unstable. A little too much density in the first second and the universe would have recollapsed before helium was done. A little too little and it would have become so empty, so fast, that galaxies would never have found each other. We live on a knife-edge that the equations keep trying to throw us off. Either someone balanced a pebble, or something *drove* the pebble to the middle of the blade.

The third is the *relic problem*. Grand unified theories — warm-to-cold ideas about how the forces were once one force — tend to produce leftover monsters: magnetic monopoles, heavy stable particles, knots in fields. The simple bang would have left the universe ankle-deep in them. We do not trip over monopoles on the way to the mailbox. Something diluted the relics, or they were never made in our past, or the grand theories are wrong. The first option is the one inflation was built to offer.

![Figure 12. A circle for the last-scattering sky. Two opposite patches, A and B. A short mark showing they never met in the simple bang.](Figures/figs/fig12.png)

None of these puzzles says the hot bang is false. They say it is incomplete as a story of *initial conditions*. Weinberg could reconstruct the nuclear minutes without knowing why the sky was so even. Guth, in 1980, asked whether a brief, violent stretch *before* those minutes could set the table so the minutes would look inevitable.

That is a different kind of move. The three minutes are a reconstruction. Inflation is a proposed preface. Prefaces can be wrong even when the book is right. Keep the temperatures straight. The puzzles are hot. The leading solution is warm. The details of the preface — what field, what potential, what first 10⁻³⁶ second — are still being written in pencil.

## 13. Inflation, Without the Hype

Here is inflation without the poster.

Suppose, in a sliver of time so short that “sliver” is already too romantic, a patch of the universe was filled with a field that behaved like a temporary cosmological constant: a stuff whose tension was so odd that gravity, locally, *repelled*. The patch doubled in size, and doubled again, and again, sixty or more times. Distances that had been smaller than a proton became larger than a grapefruit, then larger than a solar system, then larger than the part of the universe we can see. Then the field decayed. Its energy dumped into particles. The dump is called reheating, which is a calm word for setting a universe on fire. After the dump, you have a hot bang: soup, light, the three minutes, the leftover glow.

Why bother? Because the three puzzles go pale.

The opposite patches of sky *did* talk. They talked when they were neighbors, before the burst pulled them out of conversational range. The burst then stretched their agreement across the whole sky. The horizon problem becomes a memory of an old neighborhood.

The knife-edge of flatness gets driven toward the middle. Stretch a curved surface enough and any small patch looks flat. A basketball, to an ant the size of an atom, is a plane. Inflation is the move that makes us the ant.

The relics get diluted. One monopole per pre-burst patch becomes one monopole per observable universe, or fewer. We stop tripping.

![Figure 13. One curve: a steep rise labeled burst, then a gentler slope labeled after.](Figures/figs/fig13.png)

Alan Guth’s first version had a graceful-exit problem: the burst did not know how to stop without making a mess. Andrei Linde, and independently Albrecht and Steinhardt, found a smoother hill for the field to roll down — *slow-roll*. Linde later suggested that the starting values of the field could be chaotic, different in different places, some patches inflating a lot, some not. That idea will grow a maybe-multiverse in Chapter 15. Do not let it jump the line. The *local* burst is the working theory. The froth of other bursts is a rumor the local burst can tell if you let it drink.

What inflation has not done: name the field. We have no particle in a detector called the inflaton. We have a shape that works and a sky that looks like the shape predicted. That is more than a mood and less than a conviction. Appendix A13 writes the e-folds and the slow-roll parameters. Here it is enough to know that “inflation” is not a brand. It is a job description: a brief, repulsive stretch that sets the table for the hot bang we can actually reconstruct.

String theory sometimes offers inflatons the way a catalog offers furniture. The catalog is large. **It cannot currently be tested.** We will not pay the book’s rent with a catalog.

## 14. How a Quantum Twitch Became a Galaxy

If inflation had stretched the universe perfectly smooth, there would be nothing to look at. No galaxies, no stars, no kitchens. Perfection is sterile. The universe is saved by a twitch.

Quantum fields cannot sit still. Even in their quietest state they jitter. During the burst, those jitters — differences in the field from place to place, at the smallest scale the quantum rules allow — get stretched from subatomic to cosmic the way a note written on a balloon becomes unreadable when you inflate it, except here the note becomes a *continent*. A region that jittered a little dense stays a little dense. After reheating, gravity, which has all the time in the world, starts calling in the debt. The dense patches pull. The thin patches get thinner. After a few hundred million years, the dense patches are making the first stars. After a few billion, they are making galaxies that look, cropped and gray, like Figure 14.

This is Mukhanov’s territory, and Guth’s, and a workshop of people in 1982 who realized that inflation does not just solve puzzles. It *makes the seeds*. The leftover glow’s freckles are those seeds, photographed at 380,000 years, before gravity had done much sculpting. The pattern has a sound: almost the same power at every scale, a little redder on large scales, a spectrum written *P(k)* in the appendix and “almost scale-invariant” in conversation. Planck measured the tilt. The tilt matches slow-roll inflation’s favorite song. That is the warm claim at its warmest.

![Figure 14. One nearby spiral, disk filling the frame. A quantum twitch, stretched and pulled for thirteen billion years.](Figures/figs/fig14.png)

A detail that should not be allowed to stay abstract: the twitch is not a metaphor for “something small.” It is the same kind of uncertainty that keeps an electron from having a definite path in a wire. Inflation is the amplifier. Gravity is the sculptor. You are a later exhibit.

Chaos belongs here as mechanism. A jitter of one part in a hundred thousand is nothing in a kitchen and everything in a cosmos. Change the jitter’s pattern and you get a different web of galaxies — still a web, different rooms. That is not destiny. It is sensitivity. Chapter 16 will give chaos its own chair. Here, notice that the most important structures you will ever see began as noise.

Hot claim: the leftover glow has a pattern of freckles whose statistics we have measured. Warm claim: those freckles are stretched quantum jiters, grown by gravity into galaxies. Cold claim: we have watched a twitch become a spiral in a laboratory. We have not. We have watched the intermediate photograph and done the math. That is allowed to be enough, if we say so.

## 15. Bubbles That May Never Stop

Once you have a field that can burst, you can ask whether the bursting ever takes a holiday.

In many versions of slow-roll, the field reaches the bottom of its hill in some places and keeps rolling, or keeps jittering, in others. The places that keep going keep stretching. The places that stop become hot bangs — pocket universes, bubbles, whatever noun you can stand. Our observable sky would be inside one stopped place. Outside, the bursting might still be hiring.

This is eternal inflation, Linde’s and Vilenkin’s child, Guth’s grown rumor. It is a live hypothesis. It is not a photograph.

If the underlying physics has many ways to stop — many valleys for the field to fall into, many sets of effective constants — then different bubbles can freeze out with different laws. That is the expensive copy machine of Chapter 39, and it is where the anthropic filter of Chapter 41 gets its only real job. Weinberg’s argument that the cosmological constant should not be much larger than what still allows galaxies *needs* an ensemble. Eternal inflation plus a landscape is the ensemble people reach for.

The landscape, in its most advertised form, comes from string theory. **String theory cannot currently be tested.** A landscape of valleys inherited from an untestable framework is not a measurement. It is a maybe stacked on a maybe. This book will let the stack sit on the table. It will not serve it for dinner.

![Figure 15. Three large circles in a spreading field. A picture of a possibility.](Figures/figs/fig15.png)

There is a further rumor: that the bursting has no beginning, that bubble has always begotten bubble. The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin result says otherwise for a wide class of stretching spacetimes: if the average expansion is positive, the spacetime is past-incomplete. Eternal inflation, even if it has no end, still faces a boundary in the past. The bubbles do not give you a free forever-behind-you. They give you a messier loaf.

Measure problem, in one sentence: if there are infinitely many bubbles and infinitely many observers, the word “typical” breaks. You cannot say “most universes are like this” until you say how you count. No one has a count that everyone else has to accept. Until they do, “the multiverse predicts X” is a hope in a lab coat.

Warm claim: inflation *can* be eternal in many models. Cold claim: it is, and we live in a bubble with neighbors we will never meet. The sky we have does not require the neighbors. It permits them. Permission is not a postcard.

## 16. Chaos Is an Engine

Chaos, in physics, is not a mood and not a mess. It is a precise kind of sensitivity: two starts that differ by a breath end in rooms that do not recognize each other. Weather is chaotic. A pendulum swinging over a magnet can be chaotic. The approach to a final crunch, if the universe had been headed for one, would have been chaotic — Barrow proved things in that vicinity, and Belinski, Khalatnikov, and Lifshitz gave the mixmaster a name. A recollapsing cosmos would not politely reverse the film. It would scramble.

At the beginning, chaos is an engine.

The quantum twitch of Chapter 14 is sensitivity as a gift. Stretch it, and you get a web. The cosmic web — filaments of galaxies around voids like holes in a sponge — is what gravity does with a nearly scale-invariant jitter after thirteen billion years. It is not a design. It is what you get when pull is allowed to run on noise.

![Figure 16. A cropped survey map of galaxies, thresholded so the filaments read as dark on light. A web grown from twitches.](Figures/figs/fig16.png)

At the end, if there were a crunch, the same family of sensitivity would be a theft. Tiny differences near a final singularity would be amplified until no one, not even a civilization of perfect computers, could steer. That fact will matter when someone tries to sell you a required Omega Point: a last moment in which mind takes the controls and computes forever. Chaos at the crunch is not a control panel. It is a warning that control panels melt.

In the middle — which is where we live — chaos is why forecasts have horizons. We can reconstruct the three minutes because the soup was mixed and the laws were simple. We cannot forecast a galaxy’s weather in year 10¹². We can forecast the stretch. The stretch, with dark energy in the budget, looks dull and huge. Dull and huge is a kind of mercy. The web freezes in place as the shove between filaments wins. Galaxies go dark on their own clocks. The engine that built them idles.

Keep chaos in the book as a tool, not a vibe. It made the rooms. It will not let anyone own the ending. It is the opposite of a watchmaker and the opposite of a destiny. It is what a twitch can do when the universe gives it time.
