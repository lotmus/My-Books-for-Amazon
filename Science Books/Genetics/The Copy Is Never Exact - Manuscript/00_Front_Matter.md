## How to Read This Book

This is a book about a text. The text is written in four letters, it is 3.1 billion letters long in your case, and there is a full copy of it in almost every cell you own, a cell being one of the tiny living units your whole body is built from, too small to see, trillions of them working at once. It is copied every time a cell divides in two, which is how a body grows and repairs itself. The copy is never exact. That last fact is the hinge of the whole story, so it is worth saying once, plainly, before anything else: if the copy were perfect there would be no evolution, no cancer, no difference between you and your sister, and nothing for this book to be about.

The chapters run in roughly the order the world found things out. Part I is the shape, discovered in 1953. Part II is the molecule itself, its chemistry, and how it copies and miscopies. Parts III and IV are inheritance and the monk who measured it first. Part V is how we learned to read the text, Part VI what we can now do with it, and Parts VII and VIII are copies and dinner: cloning, and the plants and animals we have been editing for ten thousand years. Part IX is the whole human text, read once at great cost and now read for the price of a night in a hotel. Part X is what your own text can and cannot tell you. Part XI is correction, and the argument about where correction should stop.

Where the status of a claim matters, I say which of three things it is. *Settled*: measured, repeated, and used every day. *Working*: the best current account, with real gaps. *Speculative*: allowed by what we know, not shown. Most of the book is settled. The last chapters are not, and say so.

Three people appear throughout: Ruth, born in 1948, her daughter Anna, born in 1978, and Anna's son Theo, born in 2011. They are invented. Their freckles, their milk tolerance, and their arguments at the table are there so that the mechanisms have somewhere to happen. Nothing bad is done to them for drama.

Gene names are in italics (*BRCA1*). The proteins they encode are not (the BRCA1 protein). Prices are in United States dollars and carry a year. Where I give my own opinion, I mark it as mine.

## Author's Note

I am not a geneticist. I am someone who wanted to know what was in the tube, and kept reading. The people who did the work are named in the text, with the year and the journal where it matters, so that anyone who wants the original can find it. Where the record is disputed, as it is for 1953, I have tried to give each side its best case and then say what I think.

## Prologue — The Tube in the Mailbox

It is a Tuesday in early spring, and a woman named Anna is standing at a mailbox with a padded envelope. Inside the envelope is a plastic tube. Inside the tube is about two millilitres of her saliva, mixed with a blue stabilising fluid that will keep what is in it from rotting on the way to a laboratory in another state.

Anna is invented. So is her mother, Ruth, who is seventy-eight and lives twenty minutes away, and so is Anna's son Theo, who is fifteen and thinks the whole thing is slightly disgusting. The tube is not invented. Several million people have mailed one, and the company that sells the kit has been through a data breach, a bankruptcy, and a rescue, all of which come later in this book. What matters at the mailbox is what Anna has just posted.

Two millilitres of spit contains, among the enzymes (working molecules that speed up a chemical job, the way a key turns a lock) and the bacteria and the crumbs of a late breakfast, a few hundred thousand cells shed from the lining of her cheeks. Each of those cells is about fifteen microns across, which is roughly a fifth the width of a human hair. Each has a nucleus, a small compartment at the cell's center that holds its instructions the way a safe holds a document. Inside each nucleus, coiled and folded and packed with a precision no engineer has matched, are forty-six threads of a molecule called deoxyribonucleic acid, DNA for short. Laid end to end, the forty-six threads from one cell would stretch about two metres. They carry, in a four-letter alphabet, a text of about 3.1 billion letters. It is the same text, with tiny differences, in every cell from her scalp to her heels. There are about thirty-seven trillion such cells in her body, so Anna, walking back from the mailbox, is carrying something like seventy billion kilometres of the stuff. That is more than four hundred times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

None of this was known when Ruth was born. In 1948 the molecule had been named, its ingredients were listed, and a majority of the people who thought about such things believed it was too dull to carry information. Proteins, the working molecules that do almost every job inside a cell, from digesting food to carrying oxygen in blood, were the interesting ones. DNA was, in one chemist's word, a "stupid" substance, four ingredients repeated over and over like a wallpaper pattern. Five years later, in the spring of 1953, two men in Cambridge built a model out of metal plates and wire that showed what the wallpaper was for, and the sentence they wrote about it is one of the most quietly loaded in the history of science: it had not escaped their notice that the pairing they proposed suggested a copying mechanism.

That is the first thing to hold onto. The shape of the molecule and the way it copies are the same fact. DNA is built like a twisted ladder split down its length into two matching halves, known as strands. Pull the two strands apart, and each one is a template, a mold the cell can rebuild the missing half from, so that one ladder becomes two identical ladders. A cell does this every time it divides. Anna's body does it roughly four million times a second, day and night, and has been doing it since a single cell in 1978 became two.

Here is the second thing, and it is the reason the book has the title it has. The copying is astonishingly good. After the copying enzyme has checked its own work and a second set of enzymes has checked it again, the error rate is about one wrong letter in a billion. That is a proofreader who reads two thousand hefty novels and misses a single letter. It is also not zero. Multiply a billionth by 3.1 billion letters and by trillions of cell divisions over a lifetime, and the errors add up to something you can measure. Theo, when he was conceived, carried somewhere between sixty and seventy changes to the text that neither Anna nor his father had. Most came from his father's side. Every human being who has ever lived carried a similar number. The copy is never exact, and everything that has ever evolved, including us, is downstream of that.

The tube in the mailbox will not be read in full. The laboratory will look at a few hundred thousand of the 3.1 billion positions, the ones known to vary between people, and it will report back on ancestry, on a handful of health risks, and on the relatives who have also mailed a tube. Anna will learn that she carries one copy of a variant, meaning a spot in the text where her letters differ slightly from the average, that makes her a little more likely to develop a certain disease, and one copy of a variant that lets her drink milk as an adult, which she already knew. She will also be told, by a web page, about a second cousin in Ohio she has never met. What she will not be told, because no laboratory can tell her, is most of what she wants to know: how long she will live, whether Theo will inherit Ruth's temper, and whether the thing she is a little more likely to develop will in fact develop.

The gap between what the tube can say and what people hope it will say is one of the subjects of this book. So is how the tube got here in the first place. In 1990, when the reading of a complete human text began, it was priced at three billion dollars and scheduled to take fifteen years. Today a full genome, all 3.1 billion letters and not a sample of them, costs a few hundred dollars and takes a day. Reading turned out to be the easy part. The hard part, which is only now beginning, is writing.

In 2012, two women published a paper describing how a bacterial defence system could be turned into a pair of programmable scissors that cut DNA at a chosen address. Within eleven years, the first patients had been cured with it of sickle cell disease, a painful inherited blood disorder caused by a single wrong letter, at a list price of $2.2 million each. In 2025 a baby in Philadelphia became the first person to receive an edit designed for his one mutation, from diagnosis to infusion in about six months. In 2018 a scientist in Shenzhen used the same scissors on embryos and produced two girls whose altered text will pass to their children, if they have any, and was sent to prison for it. The question of what we should correct, and whether "correct" can shade into "improve," and who decides, no longer belongs only to novels.

So the plan of the book is the plan of the tube. First, the shape: the pub in Cambridge, the photograph in London, the model on the bench. Then the molecule: what the four letters are made of, how the chemistry under them works, how the text copies, how it becomes protein, and where the mistakes come from, including whether the mistakes are, at bottom, quantum events. Then inheritance, and the monk with the peas who worked out its rules while Darwin was writing his book and never knew he had done so. Then the tools: scissors, ladders, a copying machine that needs no cell, a fingerprint, and the fall in price that put a genome within reach of a hospital. Then what we can do now: edit, cure, write from scratch, read the dead. Then copies, from a sheep in 1996 to the two girls in Shenzhen. Then dinner, which is the oldest genetic engineering there is. Then the whole human text, read once and now read by the million. Then Anna's tube, and what comes back. Then correction, the line, and the hundred years after this one.

Anna walks home. Somewhere between the mailbox and her front door, in the lining of her gut, about two billion of her cells divide and copy the text, and a few of them get a letter wrong.
