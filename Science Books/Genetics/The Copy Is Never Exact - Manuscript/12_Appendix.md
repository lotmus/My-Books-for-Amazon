# APPENDIX

## A Short Timeline

1865 — Gregor Mendel presents his results on pea crosses to the Brünn Natural History Society; published 1866.
1869 — Friedrich Miescher isolates "nuclein" from white blood cells in Tübingen.
1900 — Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak independently rediscover Mendel's paper.
1902–03 — Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri propose that chromosomes carry Mendel's hereditary factors.
1910 — Thomas Hunt Morgan finds a white-eyed mutant fly and begins the *Drosophila* work that founds classical genetics.
1913 — Alfred Sturtevant draws the first genetic map.
1928 — Frederick Griffith discovers the bacterial "transforming principle."
1933 — Nazi Germany passes a compulsory-sterilisation law modelled partly on American statutes.
1937–40s — Fisher, Haldane, Wright, Dobzhansky, Mayr, and Simpson build the Modern Synthesis, joining Darwin and Mendel.
1944 — Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty identify DNA as the transforming substance.
1950 — Erwin Chargaff publishes the base-ratio rule.
1952 — Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase's phage experiment; Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling take Photo 51.
1953 — Watson and Crick publish the double-helix structure (25 April) and the copying mechanism (30 May).
1956 — Arthur Kornberg isolates a DNA polymerase; Vernon Ingram finds the single amino-acid change behind sickle cell disease.
1958 — Meselson and Stahl demonstrate semiconservative replication.
1961 — Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei crack the first codon; Christian Anfinsen shows sequence determines protein folding.
1962 — Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins.
1964 — Genetics research resumes in the Soviet Union after Lysenko's fall from official favour.
1966 — The genetic code is fully worked out.
1970 — Restriction enzymes characterised (Arber, Smith, Nathans); Howard Temin and David Baltimore find reverse transcriptase.
1972–73 — Paul Berg makes the first recombinant DNA molecule; Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer clone a gene in bacteria.
1975 — The Asilomar Conference sets voluntary safety guidelines for recombinant DNA.
1977 — Frederick Sanger sequences the first DNA genome (phage phi X174); Sharp and Roberts discover introns.
1978 — Recombinant human insulin produced in bacteria.
1983 — Kary Mullis conceives PCR; Barbara McClintock receives the Nobel Prize for transposons.
1984 — Alec Jeffreys develops DNA fingerprinting.
1990 — The Human Genome Project formally begins; the first gene-therapy trial (Ashanti DeSilva).
1994 — The Flavr Savr tomato reaches supermarket shelves.
1996 — Dolly the sheep is born on 5 July at the Roslin Institute.
2000 — The draft human genome is announced at the White House on 26 June.
2003 — The Human Genome Project is declared complete in April, the fiftieth anniversary of the double helix.
2003–05 — He Jiankui is not yet part of this story; Hwang Woo-suk's cloning claims are exposed as fraudulent.
2007 — Shinya Yamanaka reprograms adult cells into induced pluripotent stem cells.
2010 — J. Craig Venter's team boots up a cell with a fully synthetic genome; Svante Pääbo's group publishes the draft Neanderthal genome.
2012 — Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier publish CRISPR-Cas9 as a programmable editing tool; ENCODE publishes its functional-genome estimate.
2013 — CRISPR editing demonstrated in human cells; Angelina Jolie's op-ed on *BRCA1*; the U.S. Supreme Court rules on gene patents in *Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics*.
2018 — He Jiankui announces germline-edited twins in Hong Kong in November.
2019 — Victoria Gray becomes the first sickle cell patient dosed with CRISPR-edited cells.
2020 — Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Doudna and Charpentier; AlphaFold2 solves protein folding at the CASP14 competition.
2022 — The telomere-to-telomere human genome (T2T-CHM13) is completed; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Svante Pääbo.
2023 — Casgevy (exa-cel), the first CRISPR-based therapy, approved in the UK and the United States.
2024 — Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Demis Hassabis, John Jumper, and David Baker for protein structure prediction and design.
2025 — KJ Muldoon receives a personalised base-editing treatment for CPS1 deficiency, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in May.

## Glossary

**Allele** — one of the alternative versions of a gene at a given position.
**Amino acid** — a small building-block molecule; twenty kinds are strung together to make proteins.
**Base** — one of the four chemical units, adenine, thymine, guanine, or cytosine, that form the "letters" of DNA.
**Base pair** — two bases joined across the double helix, A with T or G with C.
**CRISPR** — a bacterial immune system, repurposed as a programmable gene-editing tool guided by RNA.
**Chromatin** — DNA wound around histones, compact spool-like proteins that help pack it tightly.
**Chromosome** — a single long DNA molecule, together with its packaging proteins; humans have forty-six.
**Codon** — a group of three DNA or RNA letters that specifies one amino acid or a stop signal.
**Enhancer** — a stretch of DNA that boosts a gene's activity, sometimes from far away along the chromosome.
**Enzyme** — a protein (occasionally an RNA) that speeds up a chemical reaction.
**Epigenetics** — chemical marks on DNA or its packaging proteins that change gene activity without changing the underlying letters.
**Exon** — a coding segment of a gene, the part kept once the non-coding pieces are cut out.
**Gene** — a stretch of DNA that specifies a protein or a functional RNA molecule.
**Genome** — the complete set of DNA in an organism.
**Germline** — the cells that give rise to eggs or sperm; changes here pass to offspring.
**Heritability** — the fraction of variation in a trait, within a specific population, attributable to genetic differences.
**Heterozygous** — carrying two different alleles of a gene, one from each parent.
**Histone** — a spool-like protein that DNA winds around for compact packaging.
**Homozygous** — carrying two identical alleles of a gene.
**Intron** — a non-coding segment of a gene, cut out before the gene's message is used.
**Meiosis** — the cell division that produces eggs or sperm, each with half the usual number of chromosomes.
**Messenger RNA (mRNA)** — the RNA copy of a gene that carries instructions to the ribosome, the cell's protein-making machine.
**Mitosis** — ordinary cell division, producing two genetically identical daughter cells.
**Mutation** — a permanent change to the sequence of DNA letters.
**Nucleotide** — the basic unit of DNA or RNA: a sugar, a phosphate, and a base.
**Nucleus** — the compartment of a cell that holds its DNA.
**Pangenome** — a reference genome built from many individuals rather than one, to capture human variation more fully.
**Penetrance** — the probability that a person carrying a particular genetic variant actually develops the associated trait or disease.
**Phenotype** — an observable trait, as distinct from the genetic makeup that produces it.
**Point mutation** — a change to a single DNA letter.
**Polygenic** — influenced by many genes acting together, each with a small effect.
**Polymerase** — an enzyme that builds a new strand of DNA or RNA using an existing strand as a template.
**Promoter** — the DNA address where the copying of a gene into RNA begins.
**Recombination** — the exchange of DNA segments between paired chromosomes during meiosis.
**Restriction enzyme** — a bacterial enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific sequence.
**Ribosome** — the cellular machine, built of RNA and protein, that translates mRNA into protein.
**Semiconservative replication** — the copying scheme in which each new DNA molecule contains one old strand and one newly made strand.
**Somatic cell** — any cell of the body other than the germline; changes here do not pass to offspring.
**Splicing** — the removal of introns from a freshly built RNA transcript.
**Tautomer** — an alternative chemical form of a molecule, differing in the position of a hydrogen atom.
**Telomere** — a repetitive, protective stretch of DNA at the end of a chromosome.
**Transcription** — the copying of a DNA gene into RNA.
**Transcription factor** — a protein that binds DNA to help control transcription.
**Transfer RNA (tRNA)** — an adaptor molecule that matches a codon to its amino acid during translation.
**Translation** — the process by which a ribosome builds a protein from an mRNA sequence.
**Transposon** — a DNA sequence capable of copying and inserting itself elsewhere in the genome.
