## Prologue — The Tube in the Mailbox (Fig 0)
Hook: a person spits into a plastic tube, screws on the cap, drops the padded envelope into a mailbox. In those two millilitres are a few hundred thousand cells, each carrying about two metres of DNA, 3.1 billion letters, a text 70 years old as a known shape and about 3.8 billion years old as a running program. Promise of the book: how we found the text, how it copies, why the copy is never exact, how we learned to read and now to write it, and what the tube can and cannot tell the sender. Ruth, Anna, Theo introduced (invented). Fig 0: photo, a saliva collection tube in a hand.

# PART I — The Shape of It

## 1. The Eagle, 28 February 1953 (Fig 1)
Hook: a lunchtime pub in Cambridge; Crick announces they have found "the secret of life." Watson (born 1928, aged 24) and Crick (born 1916, aged 36) at the Cavendish under Bragg. The model completed 7 March 1953; the Nature paper 25 April 1953, about 900 words, one sentence on copying ("it has not escaped our notice"). The cardboard bases, the metal plates, Jerry Donohue's correction on keto forms. Pauling's wrong triple helix (February 1953). Why a shape was the answer: complementary pairing gives copying for free. Fig 1: photo, the Eagle pub exterior or a period pub interior.

## 2. Photo 51 (Fig 2)
Hook: a photograph taken at King's College London in May 1952 by Raymond Gosling under Rosalind Franklin; an X, the signature of a helix. Franklin (born 1920, Cambridge chemist, Paris X-ray training), Wilkins, the misunderstanding about who ran what. The A and B forms; Franklin's measurements (34 Å repeat, 20 Å width). Watson shown the photo by Wilkins on 30 January 1953 without Franklin's knowledge; the MRC report via Perutz. Franklin's own paper in the same Nature issue. Her move to Birkbeck, tobacco mosaic virus, death 16 April 1958 at 37. Nobel 1962 (Physiology or Medicine) to Watson, Crick, Wilkins; the prize is not awarded posthumously. Fair account: what she had, what she was close to, what was taken. Fig 2: diagram, the X-pattern schematic of a helix diffraction (drawn), with layer lines.

## 3. What the Shape Explains (Fig 3)
Hook: pull the two strands apart and each is a mould for the other. Chargaff's rules (1950: A=T, G=C) and how the model made them obvious. Hydrogen bonds (two for A–T, three for G–C), antiparallel strands, major and minor grooves, about 10 base pairs per turn, 3.4 Å rise. Crick's 1958 "central dogma" and the sequence hypothesis. The Cold Spring Harbor 1953 talk. The shape as a prediction machine. Fig 3: diagram, the double helix opened into a ladder with A–T and G–C rungs.

## 4. The Race Before the Race (Fig 4)
Hook: 1869, Friedrich Miescher in Tübingen works on pus-soaked bandages and finds "nuclein." The long neglect: Levene's tetranucleotide idea (DNA too boring to carry information; proteins the favourite). Griffith 1928, transforming principle in pneumococcus. Avery, MacLeod, McCarty 1944: the transforming substance is DNA. Hershey–Chase 1952 blender experiment (phosphorus-32 and sulfur-35). Astbury's 1938 X-ray fibres. Chargaff's visit to Cambridge in 1952 and his contempt. Why the world was slow to believe DNA. Fig 4: photo, a Petri dish with bacterial colonies.

# PART II — The Molecule

## 5. Four Letters (Fig 5)
Hook: Theo's whole instruction set would fill about 1,000 phone books; his cell stores it in a sphere about 6 microns across. Nucleotides: base + deoxyribose + phosphate. A, T, G, C. 3.1 billion base pairs, 23 pairs of chromosomes, about 2 metres per cell, roughly 37 trillion cells. Packaging: nucleosomes (147 bp around eight histones), chromatin, about 10,000-fold compaction. Chromosome 1 largest (about 248 million bp), 21 smallest. RNA differences (ribose, uracil). Fig 5: diagram, one nucleotide and the four bases drawn as shapes.

## 6. The Chemistry Under the Letters (Fig 6) — biochemistry chapter
Hook: a cell is a bag of salt water in which thousands of different reactions run at body temperature, most of them impossible without help. Covalent bonds hold the backbone (phosphodiester); hydrogen bonds hold the pairs and can be opened near 90 °C or by an enzyme. Water, pH, ions. The 20 amino acids, peptide bonds, protein folding (Anfinsen 1961: sequence determines fold), why shape is function. Enzymes as catalysts, lock-and-key vs induced fit, rate speed-ups of a million-fold and more. ATP as the currency (the body turns over roughly its own weight of ATP daily). Metabolism: glycolysis, mitochondria, why breathing matters. Hemoglobin as a worked example: four chains, one mutation (Glu6Val in *HBB*) makes sickle cell (Pauling 1949 "molecular disease"; Ingram 1956 found the single amino-acid change). Fig 6: diagram, an amino-acid chain folding into a protein with an active site.

## 7. The Copying Machine (Fig 7)
Hook: every time a cell divides it copies 3.1 billion letters in a few hours with only a handful of errors. Kornberg's DNA polymerase 1956. Meselson–Stahl 1958 (nitrogen-15, semiconservative). Helicase, primase, leading and lagging strands, Okazaki fragments 1968, ligase. Speed: about 50 nucleotides per second per polymerase in humans, about 1,000 in *E. coli*; tens of thousands of origins at once. Proofreading (3′ exonuclease) and mismatch repair take the error rate to about one per billion. Telomeres and the end problem; telomerase (Blackburn, Greider, Szostak; Nobel 2009). Fig 7: diagram, replication fork.

## 8. From Letter to Protein (Fig 8)
Hook: 1961, Nirenberg and Matthaei feed a poly-U tape to a cell extract and get a chain of phenylalanine. Transcription (RNA polymerase, promoters), messenger RNA, splicing, the ribosome (Yonath, Ramakrishnan, Steitz; Nobel 2009). Codons: 64 triplets, 20 amino acids, 3 stops; the code cracked by 1966 (Khorana, Holley, Nirenberg; Nobel 1968). Redundancy. tRNA as the adaptor Crick predicted. Near-universality of the code (and the small exceptions in mitochondria). Fig 8: diagram, the codon table drawn as a grid.

## 9. Most of the Text Is Not Genes (Fig 9)
Hook: only about 1.5 percent of the text codes for protein; what is the rest? Introns and exons (Sharp and Roberts 1977; Nobel 1993). Regulatory DNA, promoters, enhancers, transcription factors. Repeats and transposons (about 45 percent of the genome; Alu about 10 percent; McClintock, Nobel 1983). The "junk" argument and the ENCODE claim of 80 percent function (2012) versus the evolutionary view (perhaps 8–15 percent conserved). Epigenetics defined carefully: methylation, histone marks, X-inactivation. Same genome, about 200 cell types. Fig 9: diagram, a bar showing the genome's budget: coding, introns, repeats, regulatory, other.

## 10. Where Mistakes Come From (Fig 10) — mutation, Darwin, and the quantum question
Hook: Theo carries roughly 60 to 70 new mutations neither parent had; most came from his father's side, about two more per year of paternal age. Sources: replication error (about one in a billion after repair), spontaneous chemistry (depurination about 10,000 per cell per day, cytosine deamination), UV pyrimidine dimers, ionizing radiation, chemicals (aflatoxin, tobacco benzo[a]pyrene), transposon jumps. Repair: base excision, nucleotide excision (xeroderma pigmentosum), mismatch repair (Lynch syndrome); Lindahl, Modrich, Sancar Nobel 2015. Types: point, insertion/deletion, copy-number, chromosomal. Darwin (1859) needed heritable variation and had no mechanism; Mendel supplied particulate inheritance; mutation supplies novelty; the Modern Synthesis (1930s–40s) joined them. **The quantum question, stated honestly:** Watson and Crick's second 1953 paper already noted that rare tautomeric forms of bases could mis-pair. Löwdin 1963 proposed proton tunnelling across the hydrogen bonds as a route to tautomers. Slocombe, Sacchi and Al-Khalili 2022 (Communications Physics) computed that tunnelling makes tautomers form and vanish fast, with a steady-state fraction large enough to matter in principle. Not shown: that this survives the polymerase's checks in a living cell. Status: working hypothesis, not settled. Also note the settled quantum role: photons and radiation chemistry are quantum events. Closing: mutation is the raw material; selection is the editor; neither alone is the theory. Fig 10: diagram, a base pair with a proton hopping across the hydrogen bond, normal vs tautomer.

# PART III — Inheritance

## 11. Why Theo Has Ruth's Freckles (Fig 11)
Hook: a family photo across three generations. Chromosomes, 23 pairs, one set from each parent. Mitosis vs meiosis; crossing over; independent assortment; about 8 million (2^23) possible combinations before recombination; each child a shuffle, not a blend. Why siblings share about 50 percent on average, with a real spread (measured sibling pairs run from roughly 37 to 62 percent). Fig 11: diagram, meiosis with crossing over in two colours.

## 12. Who Gets What: X, Y, and the Mother's Line (Fig 12)
Hook: hemophilia in Queen Victoria's descendants (Leopold, the Russian court). Sex chromosomes, *SRY*, X-linked traits (red–green colour blindness in about 8 percent of men), X-inactivation and calico cats. Mitochondrial DNA, 16,569 letters, maternal only, haplogroups; the Y chromosome and surnames. Mitochondrial Eve and Y-chromosomal Adam explained as statistical ancestors (roughly 150–200k and 200–300k years ago; wide error bars), not a couple. Fig 12: diagram, a pedigree chart of an X-linked trait.

## 13. Dominant, Recessive, and the Messy Middle (Fig 13)
Hook: eye colour is not one gene. Simple Mendelian traits (cystic fibrosis, *CFTR* ΔF508, carrier frequency about 1 in 25 in people of European ancestry; sickle cell and malaria; Huntington's as dominant). Incomplete dominance, codominance (ABO blood groups). Polygenic traits: height (about 80 percent heritable; about 12,000 variants found in 5.4 million people, Yengo 2022), skin colour. Heritability defined correctly (a population statistic, not a personal fraction). Twin studies and their limits. Fig 13: diagram, a Punnett square next to a bell curve.

## 14. What Is Not in the Genes (Fig 14)
Hook: the Dutch Hunger Winter 1944–45 and the children conceived in it (higher rates of obesity and schizophrenia decades later; Heijmans 2008 *IGF2* methylation). Environment, development, chance. Epigenetic inheritance: robust in plants and worms; weak and contested in mammals across generations. Lamarck's ghost and why it is mostly still a ghost. Identical twins are not identical (somatic mutations, fingerprints). Gene–environment interaction (PKU: a genetic disease managed by diet). Fig 14: photo, a bare winter field.

# PART IV — The Monk

## 15. Brno, 1856: Peas in a Garden (Fig 15)
Hook: a friar in a Moravian abbey garden with a paintbrush. Gregor Mendel born 1822 Heinzendorf; the Augustinian abbey of St Thomas in Brno; physics training in Vienna under Doppler; failed the teaching exam. Eight years of crosses (1856–1863), about 28,000 plants, seven traits (seed shape, seed colour, pod shape, pod colour, flower colour, flower position, stem height). Why peas: self-fertilising, true-breeding lines, big flowers. Fig 15: photo, pea plants with pods and flowers.

## 16. The Ratios (Fig 16)
Hook: 5,474 round seeds to 1,850 wrinkled. The 3:1 ratio; dominant and recessive; the 1:2:1 underneath; the 9:3:3:1 for two traits. Segregation and independent assortment (with the later catch: linkage breaks assortment). Mendel's factors as particles, against blending. His 1865 talks to the Brünn Natural History Society, 1866 paper "Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden." Why nobody understood. Fig 16: diagram, a two-generation cross with counts.

## 17. Lost for Thirty-Five Years (Fig 17)
Hook: 1900, three botanists (de Vries, Correns, Tschermak) independently rediscover the paper. Mendel's later life: abbot 1868, tax dispute, death 1884; his papers burned. Bateson coins "genetics" (1905 letter, 1906 congress); Johannsen "gene" (1909). Fisher's 1936 argument that Mendel's numbers are too good, and the modern verdicts. Sutton and Boveri 1902–03: chromosomes carry the factors. Fig 17: photo, the abbey in Brno or a period botanical print of peas.

## 18. Flies in a Bottle (Fig 18)
Hook: Columbia University, 1910, a single white-eyed male fly. Thomas Hunt Morgan, the Fly Room, *Drosophila*. Sex-linkage, linkage, Sturtevant's first genetic map (1913, drawn in one night as an undergraduate), the centimorgan. Muller's X-ray mutations (1927; Nobel 1946). Morgan's Nobel 1933. The Modern Synthesis (Fisher, Haldane, Wright, Dobzhansky 1937, Mayr, Simpson): Darwin and Mendel reconciled. Fig 18: photo, fruit fly close-up.

## 19. Galton's Shadow (Fig 19)
Hook: Francis Galton, Darwin's cousin, coins "eugenics" in 1883. The Eugenics Record Office (1910), state sterilisation laws, Buck v. Bell (1927; Holmes's "three generations of imbeciles are enough" is a documented phrase), about 60,000 sterilisations in the US; the Nazi law of 1933 modelled partly on American statutes; the road to murder. The Soviet mirror image: Lysenko, Lamarckism as state doctrine, Vavilov dead in prison 1943, genetics suppressed until 1964. Why the science was bad as well as the ethics. Modern echoes picked up in Part XI. Fig 19: photo, an old courthouse or archive cabinets (avoid rights-risky archival images).

## 23. The Fingerprint (Fig 23)

## 24. Fast, Cheap, and Everywhere (Fig 24)

# PART VI — What We Can Do Now

## 25. Scissors from Bacteria (Fig 25)
Hook: a yogurt company's problem in 2007 (Danisco; phage resistance). Repeats noticed 1987 (Ishino); named CRISPR 2002; Mojica proposes immunity 2005; Barrangou 2007. Doudna and Charpentier, *Science*, June 2012, guide RNA plus Cas9; Zhang and Church in human cells January 2013; Nobel Chemistry 2020. How it works (guide, PAM, double-strand break, NHEJ vs HDR). Base editing (Komor and Liu 2016), prime editing (Anzalone 2019). Off-target effects, mosaicism. Fig 25: diagram, Cas9 with guide RNA on DNA.

## 26. The First Cures (Fig 26)
Hook: Victoria Gray, July 2019, first sickle-cell patient dosed with CRISPR-edited cells. Gene therapy history: the 1990 ADA trial (Ashanti DeSilva); Jesse Gelsinger's death 17 September 1999; the 2000s SCID leukaemias; the comeback: Luxturna (2017, about $850,000), Zolgensma (2019, $2.125 million), Hemgenix (2022, $3.5 million). Casgevy (exa-cel) approved UK November 2023, FDA 8 December 2023, list price $2.2 million; Lyfgenia $3.1 million. In-body editing: NTLA-2001 for transthyretin amyloidosis (2021). KJ Muldoon: personalised base editing for CPS1 deficiency, dosed February 2025, published NEJM May 2025 (CHOP; Musunuru, Ahrens-Nicklas), about six months from diagnosis to dose. What "cure" means so far. Fig 26: photo, a hospital infusion bag or lab pipetting.

## 27. Writing from Scratch (Fig 27)
Hook: 2010, a cell boots up with a chemically synthesised genome (Venter's JCVI-syn1.0, 1.08 million bp; syn3.0 in 2016 with 473 genes). Synthetic biology; Sc2.0 yeast synthetic chromosomes (first 2014; most complete 2023). DNA as storage (Church 2012, Goldman 2013; hundreds of petabytes per gram in principle). mRNA vaccines (Karikó and Weissman; Nobel 2023). AlphaFold2 (CASP14 November 2020; Nobel Chemistry 2024 to Hassabis, Jumper, Baker) and protein design. Where "writing" is still hard. Fig 27: diagram, a protein ribbon predicted next to its sequence.

## 28. Reading the Dead (Fig 28)
Hook: a finger bone from a Siberian cave, reported in 2010, from a girl of a population nobody had seen. Pääbo, Neanderthal draft genome May 2010, Denisovans, Nobel 2022. About 1–2 percent Neanderthal DNA in non-Africans; *EPAS1* in Tibetans from Denisovans. Ancient pathogens (plague in Bronze Age teeth). De-extinction: Colossal's "dire wolf" pups (April 2025; 20 edits in 14 genes of grey wolf), woolly mice (March 2025); what such animals are and are not. # PART VII — Copies

Hook: a lamb born in a shed near Edinburgh, announced February 1997. Wilmut, Campbell, the Roslin Institute; nuclear transfer from an adult mammary cell; 277 reconstructed eggs, 29 embryos, one lamb. ## 30. What a Clone Is and Isn't (Fig 30)
Hook: Barbra Streisand's two dogs (2018) cloned from her late Coton de Tulear. Costs: ViaGen dogs about $50,000. Livestock cloning (FDA 2008 safety), polo horses, camels in Dubai. Zhong Zhong and Hua Hua (macaques, January 2018). Efficiency still low. Fig 30: photo, two similar dogs or cats side by side.

## 31. The Human Question (Fig 31)

Reproductive cloning bans (UN declaration 2005, national laws); Hwang Woo-suk's 2004–05 fraud; Mitalipov's 2013 human SCNT stem cells; Yamanaka's iPS cells (2006 mouse, 2007 human; Nobel 2012) as the technology that made therapeutic cloning nearly moot. # PART VIII — Dinner

## 32. Ten Thousand Years of Editing (Fig 32)
Hook: a supermarket ear of corn next to a spike of teosinte. # PART XI — Correcting the Text

## 45. Enhancement (Fig 45)
Hook: a German boy born in 1999 and reported in 2004 with a *MSTN* mutation and unusual muscle. Fig 45: photo, a racing whippet or an athlete.

## 46. The Next Hundred Years (Fig 46) — Lothar's question: what will life look like in 100 years
Hook: 2126, a child's genome read before the umbilical cord is cut. Written as labelled scenarios with the evidence today: (1) settled trajectory: universal newborn genomes, somatic cures for most single-gene diseases, pharmacogenomic prescribing, engineered crops and livestock as default, cheap sequencing of everything (air, water, sewage). (2) working: polygenic embryo selection normalised in some countries and banned in others; partial epigenetic reprogramming (Yamanaka factors; Ocampo 2016; Altos Labs 2022) extending healthspan modestly; de-extinction as a conservation tool; synthetic organisms in industry. (3) speculative: germline edits for a few diseases, inequality by genome, human variants for space or climate, the "designer" argument. Evolution has not stopped: lactase persistence (5–10,000 years), *EPAS1* in Tibet. The author's guess, marked as his. Coda: the copying theme closes the book. Fig 46: photo, a newborn's hand or a sunrise over a field.
