# PART THREE — The Photon and the Path

## 9. The Photon

"Photon" is one of those words that feels perfectly clear right up until someone asks you what it actually means. Call it a particle of light, and you've said something true. You have not said very much. A photon is, more precisely, a quantum excitation of the electromagnetic field — which is a more accurate sentence, and also one that immediately raises a new question: excitation of *what*, exactly? What is the electromagnetic field, such that it has excitations?

The electromagnetic field, in the quantum theory, is not a classical electric field with a small quantum packet riding on top of it like a cork bobbing on water. Quantum field theory changes the meaning of the word "field" itself. A quantum field is an object whose disturbances (its excitations) correspond to particles. The electron field has electron and positron excitations. The electromagnetic field has photon excitations. This single move buys an enormous amount of explanatory power: it lets the theory describe processes in which particles are created and destroyed. An electron can emit a photon that did not exist a moment before. A photon can be absorbed and simply cease to exist. At high enough energies, matter can convert into other forms of matter entirely. The particle picture and the field picture read like two competing stories here; they are really two views of one quantum theory.

This is a good place to be honest about a temptation this book has been resisting since Chapter 1, and needs to keep resisting. It would be convenient to say that everything is really collective phase underneath, and that photons are just a convenient bookkeeping device for talking about phase changes — that once you've absorbed the argument of the last eight chapters, you no longer strictly need photons at all. That would be a mistake, and not a subtle one. Real photons are experimentally real. They carry measurable energy and momentum. They travel through the vacuum of space for billions of years without decaying. They eject electrons from a metal surface on contact, one at a time, in the way a particle would — the very fact Einstein used, in 1905, to argue that light itself must be quantized in the first place.^3

Shine dim light on a metal plate, and classical wave theory predicts electrons should trickle out gradually as the wave's energy slowly accumulates, with brighter light simply speeding up the trickle. That is not what happens. Below a sharp threshold frequency, no electrons come out at all, no matter how bright the light; above it, electrons come out immediately, one per absorbed quantum, with an energy that depends on the light's color, not its intensity. Turning the brightness up only makes more electrons come out, not more energetic ones. That pattern makes no sense for a continuous wave delivering its energy smoothly. It makes complete sense if light arrives in discrete packets, each carrying a fixed energy set by its frequency, and an electron either absorbs one whole packet or none at all. A single photon of visible light carries only around two electron-volts — an almost comically small amount of energy, and yet exactly enough, delivered all at once instead of trickled in, to knock a particular electron loose from a particular atom. A photovoltaic solar cell runs on the same one-photon-one-electron logic, though the threshold there is a semiconductor's bandgap rather than a metal's work function: each photon above that energy frees one electron into the circuit. For light of a single color, that's also why a panel's output current climbs in step with how many photons arrive each second — which, for a fixed color, is what turning up the intensity means in the first place. Quantum electrodynamics describes all of this, and a great deal more, with a precision that borders on the absurd — predictions checked against experiment to better than one part in a billion. This book's phase-centered reframing isn't trying to talk that precision away; it rests on top of it.

What the reframing does buy us is a sharper distinction, and it takes some care to state correctly. When two charged particles interact in a Feynman-diagram calculation, the diagram typically shows a wavy line connecting them, conventionally drawn as a photon being exchanged. It is tempting to read this literally: electron A emits a tiny photon, the photon flies across the gap, electron B catches it. As a rough mnemonic, that picture is harmless. Taken literally, it becomes actively misleading. The internal photon line in a diagram like this represents a mathematical contribution to a quantum amplitude (a term in a calculation), not an independently detectable particle in flight. A *real* photon, the kind that can arrive at a detector, satisfies a strict relationship between its energy and momentum. An internal, *virtual* photon in a diagram is under no such obligation; it is an intermediate piece of bookkeeping inside a perturbative calculation, not a mini-projectile crossing the space between two electrons. Feynman diagrams are not, in this sense, tiny movies of what physically happened. They are diagrams of the terms in a sum, an extremely useful organizing scheme for an amplitude that is, underneath, the kind of object Chapter 1 introduced: a number with a size and a phase.

This is, if anything, a point in favor of the phase-centered picture rather than against it. The fundamental object being calculated is still an amplitude. The diagram is a bookkeeping device for organizing the contributions to that amplitude (a superb one, arguably the most successful bookkeeping device in the history of physics), but it is not handing you a photograph of a mechanism.

The *real* photon, the one that actually arrives, loses nothing in any of this. And there is an elegant bridge between the microscopic photon and the classical electromagnetic wave that this book has not yet earned the right to describe carefully, but can preview: a classical-looking beam of light (a radio wave, a laser beam) isn't so much a rival description competing with the photon picture as what an enormous, highly coherent collection of photons looks like from far enough away that counting them individually stops being useful. The wave did not replace the photon. The photon did not replace the wave. One is the large-scale coherent limit of the other, in much the sense that a classical trajectory turned out, in Chapter 1, to be the large-scale coherent limit of a sum over quantum histories.

That word — *coherent* — keeps reappearing. It is not a coincidence, and the next two chapters are going to lean on it hard.

## 10. The Path Is Not a Track

Suppose an electron travels from point A to point B. The classical instinct is to ask which path it took, as though the answer were a single line you could, in principle, trace with a pencil. Feynman's path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics^4 asks a different question entirely: what amplitude is associated with *each* of the infinitely many possible paths the electron could have taken between A and B?

Every conceivable route — the straight line, the gentle curve, the path that loops backward on itself, the path that visits the Moon and comes back — contributes an amplitude to the total. Each amplitude has a phase, set by the *action* accumulated along that particular history: a quantity — roughly, the running difference between kinetic and potential energy, tallied up moment by moment along the path — that measures something like the total "cost," in a technical sense, of that specific trajectory. Add every contribution together, weighting each by its phase, and you get the full quantum amplitude for the electron to go from A to B. The observable outcome depends on that combined sum, not on any single path considered in isolation.

Feynman arrived at this picture partly by taking a much older idea more seriously than anyone before him had. Paul Dirac had noted, in passing, that a quantum amplitude ought to be related to the classical action by something like the factor *exp(iS/ħ)* (the same expression Chapter 28 will meet again in a more formal setting, sitting directly inside the machinery of QED) without pushing the observation very far. Feynman, still a graduate student at the time, pushed it all the way: if every possible history really does contribute a phase set by its action, then summing over all of them, rather than singling out one, ought to reproduce the whole of quantum mechanics from scratch. It did. The Schrödinger equation, the interference patterns, the uncertainty principle — all of it falls out of the sum-over-histories picture as a consequence, not an additional assumption bolted on afterward.

Here is where it gets interesting. Most of the wildly different paths in that infinite sum have wildly different actions, and so wildly different phases, and when you add a huge collection of essentially random phases together, the contributions cancel each other out almost completely — arrows pointing in every direction, canceling in aggregate. But paths that lie close to the single path a classical, Newtonian particle would have taken share almost the *same* action, and therefore almost the same phase, and phases that are nearly equal reinforce each other instead of canceling. The result is that the classical trajectory emerges as the trajectory around which quantum contributions pile up and survive, while everything else washes out, rather than being fed into the theory as a separate assumption. A "path," in this picture, was never really a physical track the electron slid along. It was always this: the one neighborhood of histories where the interference stopped canceling and started adding.

Now bring electromagnetism back into the picture, and the payoff of this whole formulation becomes concrete. The electromagnetic contribution to the action for a charged particle's history takes the schematic form *q times the integral of A along the path* — the same loop integral from Chapter 3, except now measured along one open history rather than around a closed circuit. The exact notation matters less than what it says in plain language: the electromagnetic potential contributes directly to the phase built up along a charged particle's history, on exactly the same footing as every other contribution to the action.

Everything from the first three chapters clicks together at this point. The Aharonov–Bohm effect is no longer an isolated curiosity requiring its own special explanation — it is what you'd expect the moment you take path integrals seriously. The potential shifts the phase of every possible history. The phase shift changes how those histories interfere with one another. The changed interference changes what you observe at the far end. The path integral is the language in which all three of those steps fit together as one continuous statement instead of three separate facts that happen to be true.

And it reveals something the earlier, more piecemeal version of the argument only gestured at. The electromagnetic potential does more than dictate the force a particle feels at each instant — it reaches into the phase of every possible history that particle might have taken, before any single history has been singled out as "the" one that happened. That is a far deeper role for the potential to be playing than anything a force law, by itself, could ever capture.

## 11. From Individual Histories to Collective Phase

Now widen the picture. Instead of one electron summing over its possible histories, imagine a macroscopic coherent system (the superconductor from Part Two) whose microscopic state is a staggeringly complicated sum over the histories of an enormous number of particles, but whose *collective* low-energy behavior can be captured by a single macroscopic phase field, as Chapter 5 described. The same basic structure from the last chapter reappears, essentially unchanged in outline: a phase shifts as you move through space; the electromagnetic potential modifies how it shifts; and the system's momentum and current end up tied to the combination of the phase gradient and the potential, the same *p ∝ ħ∇θ − qA* relationship from Chapter 7, now describing a collective variable shared by billions of particles instead of the private phase of one electron's wavefunction.

This is one of the more beautiful points of contact in the entire synthesis this book is attempting. At the microscopic level, quantum electrodynamics says that charged matter couples to the electromagnetic potential through the phase of individual quantum amplitudes, summed over every possible history. At the macroscopic level, a coherent quantum system carries a collective phase that couples to that very same potential, through what is structurally the same relationship. At bottom, this is one electromagnetic relationship viewed at two different scales, through two different mathematical machineries built for those scales, not two separate interactions that happen to look alike.

Calling the macroscopic phase description quantum electrodynamics wearing a disguise would overstate the case: enormous amounts of microscopic detail have been averaged over, discarded, or rendered irrelevant to get from one picture to the other, and that discarding is not free; it is a genuine physical process with its own story, one this book will come back to directly when it takes up renormalization. Think of it the way a weather forecaster relates to the underlying physics of individual air molecules: nobody doubts that a forecast obeys the same laws of thermodynamics and fluid motion that govern every molecule in the atmosphere, and nobody expects the forecaster to track any of those molecules individually to produce an accurate forecast. Wind speed and barometric pressure are what survives, systematically, once the irrelevant microscopic detail has been accounted for and set aside. The collective phase of a superconductor survives that same kind of process from full quantum electrodynamics. But the macroscopic phase picture isn't borrowed from a different part of physics because the mathematics happens to look similar — it's built from the same underlying quantum structure, all the way down, with most of the microscopic detail integrated away rather than ignored.

That gives us a powerful organizing principle to carry forward: the electromagnetic potential couples to quantum phase, full stop — whether that phase belongs to a single quantum amplitude summed over the wild multiplicity of paths available to one electron, or to a macroscopic coherent state shared by a trillion of them. The scale changes enormously. The mathematics gets simpler in some ways as you move to the macroscopic case, and more subtle in others. But the underlying relationship — potential shapes phase, phase determines physical behavior — survives the crossing intact.

With that relationship established at both scales, return once more to the superconducting loop from Chapter 3 and look at it again, now with more machinery in hand. The collective phase has to remain self-consistent all the way around a closed path — the same requirement that produced flux quantization the first time around, but now derived from the more general phase-and-potential relationship developed across this Part rather than asserted as a special case. Integrate that relationship around the loop, demand the phase return to an equivalent value, and the quantization condition falls out again, this time as a direct consequence of everything built up since Chapter 7 rather than as an isolated result.

The re-derivation itself matters less than what it reveals about how the different descriptions in this book connect to one another. At the microscopic level, quantum mechanics is a story about amplitudes and phase, summed over every possible history a particle could have lived. At the collective level, that same phase becomes a single, directly useful macroscopic variable, shared by an enormous coherent population of particles. At the classical level — the level of ordinary circuits, voltages, and currents — the electromagnetic response can be written down entirely in terms of fields, currents, inductances, and fluxes, with no explicit mention of quantum phase anywhere in sight.

The three levels are nested descriptions of one underlying structure, and the remarkable thing is that the same mathematical role — phase, organized through its electromagnetic connection — keeps showing up as the thread connecting them, whether or not the language at any given level bothers to mention it by name.

This is the point where the synthesis this book is attempting stops being a juxtaposition of two clever ideas and starts being something closer to a single argument. Feynman's path-integral picture insists on taking phase seriously for every conceivable history available to a quantum system, however microscopic. Mead's collective-electrodynamics picture insists on taking phase seriously the moment an enormous number of charges start behaving coherently, however macroscopic the resulting system becomes. The two insights aim at the same target from two different distances and land in the same place: electromagnetism runs on phase — it isn't decoration sprinkled on top of the theory — from a single electron's sum over histories to a superconducting ring you could hold in your hand.

One piece of the picture is still conspicuously missing, though, and it has been missing since the very first chapter. Everything so far has explained how *matter's* phase responds to an electromagnetic potential that was simply handed over as a given, already fixed, already sitting there in space waiting to be coupled to. Nothing has yet explained where the familiar electric and magnetic fields — the ones every physics student meets first, with their field lines and their right-hand rules — come from, or how Maxwell's own four famous equations fit into a story told almost entirely in the language of phase and potential. That gap is the next one to close.
