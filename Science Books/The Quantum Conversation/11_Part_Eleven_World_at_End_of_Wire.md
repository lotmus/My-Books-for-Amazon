## 40. From QED to a Superconducting Circuit

It's time to come back to something more tangible than a Lagrangian. A wire. A loop. A voltage. A current — the kind of thing anyone could build on a lab bench without needing a particle accelerator anywhere nearby. Make that wire superconducting, and cool it down far enough, and the humble circuit turns into a genuine quantum system: its current becomes tied to a collective phase, its enclosed magnetic flux becomes quantized as Chapter 3 derived, it acquires discrete energy levels, and it becomes capable of interacting with individual microwave photons one at a time. A device that would otherwise sit comfortably in an undergraduate electrical engineering course becomes, at low enough temperature, a fully functioning quantum laboratory. This is the point where the book's two central strands move from compatible in principle to inseparable in practice.

An ordinary classical circuit is described using a small, familiar set of quantities — voltage, current, capacitance, inductance — and those variables remain enormously useful for almost every practical purpose imaginable. A superconducting circuit, cooled into its quantum regime, does something stranger: those same variables become quantum operators. Energy levels turn discrete. Superpositions become physically realizable. The circuit can become entangled with a microwave field the way an atom can. Nothing mystical happened to get here: the scale and temperature were adjusted until the relevant quantum coherence survived long enough to matter, as Chapter 32 described for coherence in general. What used to be a handful of symbols an electrical engineer might sketch on a whiteboard has become a quantum system.

Inductance is normally introduced as a property of a coil's geometry, telling you how much magnetic flux accompanies a given current. In a superconducting circuit, it acquires a deeper quantum meaning: the current is tied to the collective motion of the coherent charge condensate, the electromagnetic energy depends on that current, and the phase dynamics driving it all are coupled directly to the electromagnetic potential, as Chapter 31 described. The inductor, which looks like the most passive possible piece of engineering, turns out to be part of the system's quantum Hamiltonian — classical circuit concepts becoming quantum variables, emergence running in reverse from where this book usually finds it. Capacitance follows the identical pattern. Classically, a capacitor stores electrostatic energy,

*E_C = ½CV²*.

In a quantum circuit, charge itself becomes a quantum variable, paired with the phase as its conjugate partner — voltage is just how fast that phase changes, the circuit's stand-in for velocity, not momentum — and the capacitor's energy becomes one term in a quantum Hamiltonian — the whole circuit turning into a genuine quantum harmonic oscillator, mathematics and all. This is a systematic quantization of the circuit's actual electromagnetic degrees of freedom. The old engineering variables survive completely intact. They simply acquire quantum meaning they never carried in an ordinary undergraduate circuits course.

What turns this quantum oscillator into something useful is a single added component: the Josephson junction, predicted in 1962 by Brian Josephson^13, then a twenty-two-year-old graduate student at Cambridge, a prediction that won him a share of the 1973 Nobel Prize, and that rhymes more than a little with a young Feynman working out radiation reaction as Wheeler's graduate student back in Chapter 16. A Josephson junction is a thin barrier separating two superconductors, across which the superconducting phase on one side can differ from the phase on the other by some amount *δ*. The resulting current, familiar from Chapter 31, and the energy stored in the junction as a function of that same phase difference, are:

> **Equation (12).** The Josephson relations:
> *I = I_c sin(δ)*,
> *U(δ) = −E_J cos(δ)*,
> *V = (ħ/2e) dδ/dt*.

That single cosine is doing more work than it looks like it should. It makes the circuit's potential energy genuinely *nonlinear* — unlike a plain inductor-capacitor oscillator, whose energy levels are evenly spaced forever, a Josephson junction's cosine potential gives the circuit unevenly spaced energy levels, which means two particular levels can be addressed individually with a carefully tuned microwave pulse without accidentally exciting every other level in the ladder at once. That nonlinearity is what makes those levels individually addressable, with transitions between specific pairs controllable — the circuit behaving, from this point on, as an artificial atom, with everything about that behavior traceable back to collective quantum phase and nothing else. An ordinary atom has discrete energy levels because its electrons are bound electromagnetically inside a quantum system. So does a plain LC circuit, once its charge and phase become quantum operators — any bound quantum oscillator has discrete levels. What the Josephson junction's nonlinearity buys a superconducting qubit on top of that is the uneven spacing worked out above, which is what turns "discrete" into "individually addressable." The two systems could hardly be more different physically. Mathematically, they share the structure that matters: both have quantized energy levels, both can absorb and emit photons, both can hold superpositions, both can become entangled with an electromagnetic mode. Calling the superconducting circuit an "artificial atom" is a precise description of what engineers actually built. And this is what's sitting inside the quantum processors Google and IBM have spent the past decade racing to scale up. The qubit at the center of a real, physical quantum computer is this chapter's circuit, wired up at scale — not a metaphor borrowed from it.

That third relation, *V* = (ħ/2e) *dδ/dt*, is the one that turns an abstract phase difference into something an engineer reads straight off a meter: a changing phase produces a voltage, and a steady voltage drives the phase forward at a constant rate, oscillating the current at a frequency of exactly 2*eV*/*h* — a relationship measured precisely enough that it now defines the volt itself in the modern SI system. Without this relation, a Josephson junction would be nothing more than an unusually nonlinear inductor. With it, phase becomes a voltmeter reading.

![Figure 10. A Josephson junction: two superconductors separated by an insulating barrier a few atoms thick. A supercurrent tunnels through anyway, driven entirely by the phase difference across the gap — no voltage required.](fig10_josephson.png)

Feynman would recognize this device immediately, for a very particular reason. At the microscopic level, it contains an enormous number of electrons embedded in a complicated material environment, and yet its relevant low-energy dynamics collapse into a small handful of collective variables, the kind of compression this book has been chasing since Part Two. The full microscopic system is complicated. The effective quantum circuit built from it is simple. And the moment that circuit couples to a photon, full QED reappears right on schedule — not competing with the collective description, but participating in it as a low-energy degree of freedom operating comfortably alongside everything else. Mead would recognize the very same device for the opposite reason: the electromagnetic potential enters the phase dynamics the way his whole program insists it should, flux matters, phase differences matter, loops matter, collective coherence matters, and the entire electromagnetic behavior can be understood by starting from quantum phase relationships rather than from a classical force law. Both men would be looking at the same circuit and recognizing their own intuition in it — not a contradiction, but the merger this book has been building, sitting on an actual lab bench.

## 41. One System, Several Descriptions

Take a superconducting circuit coupled to a microwave cavity — a physical apparatus you could point to on a bench in a real laboratory — and it can be described, correctly, at any of several distinct levels at once. At the microscopic level: electrons, ions, electromagnetic fields, and their full quantum interactions. At the condensed-matter level: a superconducting condensate, quasiparticles, an order parameter. At the collective circuit level: phase, charge, capacitance, inductance, Josephson energy. At the quantum-optical level: a qubit coupled to photon modes. At the classical level: ordinary currents, voltages, electromagnetic waves. All five descriptions apply to the very same physical object simultaneously. The art lies entirely in knowing which one is useful for the question being asked, which is the real, working meaning behind the phrase "hierarchy of theories."

The coupling between the circuit and the cavity makes the connection between two of these levels experimentally concrete, not just conceptual. The cavity holds one quantized electromagnetic mode; the circuit carries its own quantum transition; and when the two interact, the Hamiltonian describing them contains a term schematically like

*H_int ~ g(a + a†)X̂*,

*where a and a† describe the cavity mode and X̂ is the relevant circuit variable.*

The exact form depends on the specific system, but the conceptual content is what matters here: collective quantum matter, coupled directly to a quantized electromagnetic mode, in one Hamiltonian, in one piece of apparatus. This is quantum electrodynamics in one of its most accessible low-energy laboratory realizations — not full relativistic QED, but concrete enough to make the interaction between quantized matter and quantized electromagnetic modes something you can point to on a bench. It is, in the very same breath, collective electrodynamics. Here the two descriptions are combined experimentally, running simultaneously inside the same physical device.

![Figure 11. A superconducting circuit built to act like one atom: a capacitor and a Josephson junction give it a controllable phase and discrete energy levels, and it couples directly to a microwave photon in an on-chip cavity.](fig11_circuit_qed.png)

Even when the cavity contains no real photons at all, the quantized electromagnetic mode itself doesn't disappear. Its vacuum state remains, and that vacuum state can influence the coupled system: shifting energy levels, modifying transition rates, contributing measurably to observable effects, as the Casimir effect and the engineered vacuum of Chapters 26 and 34 already established. Even in the naive classical sense, the cavity is never truly empty: it contains a genuine quantum electromagnetic mode sitting in its ground state, and once again there's no need to picture tiny photons bouncing around inside an ostensibly empty box — the vacuum state itself is the relevant quantum object doing the work.

It sounds almost self-contradictory to say a macroscopic object can have "a" quantum state — surely something built from trillions of atoms should carry countless independent degrees of freedom rather than one tidy state to point to. It does carry all of those microscopic degrees of freedom; nothing about this description erases them. What happens instead is that the entire system's many-body quantum state organizes itself so that a small number of collective variables (the superconducting phase chief among them) dominate its low-energy behavior completely. Saying a superconductor "has a phase" is shorthand for a precise statement about how its enormous many-body quantum state happens to be organized, not a claim that the collective variable is somehow the whole wave function in miniature. The collective variable is entirely real; it simply never had to be the whole microscopic story.

The same phenomenon shows up running in the opposite direction inside a laser. A laser contains an enormous number of photons, and yet its electromagnetic output is described beautifully by a simple classical field carrying a stable amplitude and phase — a complicated microscopic quantum state producing a startlingly simple coherent optical field, the pattern *many quantum degrees of freedom → one collective coherent variable* already familiar from superconductivity. A superconductor and a laser look, on the surface, like they belong to different branches of physics. The mathematics of coherence connects them anyway: one organizes matter into collective behavior, the other organizes light, and the underlying trick is identical in both cases. Matter and light mirror each other here with real precision (microscopic charged particles collapsing into a collective quantum phase on one side, quantized electromagnetic modes collapsing into a classical field amplitude and phase on the other), and that mirroring shows the quantum-to-classical transition works as a pattern that can happen through this kind of collective organization — a richer idea than the familiar shorthand "large things are classical" ever managed to capture on its own.

Turn the temperature back up on the same circuit, introduce noise, let environmental interactions back in, and coherence gets destroyed — the quantum circuit begins behaving classically again, its discrete energy levels becoming irrelevant, its phase coherence lost, until it can once more be fully described using ordinary voltages and currents with no quantum vocabulary required at all. In a real superconducting qubit sitting in a dilution refrigerator, that coherence typically survives for only tens to a few hundred microseconds before the environment wins — an almost unimaginably brief window, and also, with enough such qubits working together before it closes, long enough to run an actual calculation. Nothing magical happened in reverse here either. The quantum theory underneath never stopped being true for a single instant; the environment simply made the classical variables sufficient for whatever question was being asked. The classical circuit is the same quantum circuit, viewed the whole time through a coarser lens — never a separate, simpler object waiting at the end of some transformation.

## 42. The Same Wire Contains All Four Worlds

Here, perhaps, is the strangest conclusion this book has reached so far: the very same physical wire can correctly be described as a quantum many-body system, a collective superconducting phase, a quantum circuit, or an ordinary classical electrical conductor, depending entirely on the regime and the question being asked of it. The material itself stays exactly what it is, regardless of who's looking at it or how; what changes is the description. That is the deepest lesson any of these effective theories has to offer: reality does not arrive with one compulsory vocabulary stapled to it. The vocabulary has to match the scale of the question.

It follows directly that this hierarchy was never meant to be a ladder of truth. Saying "QED is more true than Maxwell's equations," or "Mead's framework is more fundamental than classical electromagnetism," gets the whole structure backward. The better description running underneath every chapter so far has been: different theories answer different questions. QED answers microscopic quantum questions with a precision nothing else can match. Collective theories answer questions about organized many-body behavior that QED's own formalism tends to leave stubbornly opaque. Classical electromagnetism answers macroscopic field questions so efficiently that no engineer needs anything more elaborate for the overwhelming majority of real problems. These theories are related to one another, tightly, but their explanatory strengths differ, and the point here was to understand the translations connecting them, since crowning a winner was never the goal. Physics turns out to be just as much about discovering how different descriptions of the same physics connect to each other as about discovering equations: quantum theory to classical mechanics, QED to Maxwell, microscopic electrons to superconducting phase, photons to classical light, field descriptions to scattering amplitudes, one energy scale to another through renormalization. The translation between descriptions is itself a piece of physical knowledge in its own right, and once that translation is understood, most of the apparent contradictions that seemed to be lurking between chapters simply evaporate.

Here, plainly, in one place, is what each half of this book's central pairing actually contributed. Mead's great contribution ran alongside QED rather than against it. He forced electromagnetic phenomena to be seen through the eyes of coherent quantum matter, a shift that makes phase, flux quantization, inductance, electromagnetic momentum, collective scaling, and the special status of the potential all much more intuitive at once: beginning with the quantum state itself rather than treating classical electromagnetism as the natural starting point and quantum mechanics as a correction bolted on afterward. That reversal turns out to be powerful pedagogically, independent of whatever else it does. Feynman's contribution runs broader. He supplied a complete, staggeringly successful formulation of quantum electrodynamics — and, just as importantly, a whole way of thinking: histories, amplitudes, phase, interference, diagrams, propagators, and a settled unwillingness to accept an explanation just because it happened to sound intuitive. Feynman's deepest lesson may ultimately be the methodological one: if the picture in front of you is confusing, change how you've formulated the problem rather than forcing the confusing picture to make sense. Mead took that lesson and ran further with it than almost anyone else in the field. This book has tried to take it just as seriously.

The most natural way to hold the whole synthesis in your head at once might be as an imagined conversation, so here it is, staged directly. Feynman opens: *start with quantum amplitudes, add the electromagnetic interaction, sum over every possibility.* Mead answers: *good — now watch what happens once an enormous number of those quantum degrees of freedom become coherent. The phase itself becomes the macroscopic variable.* Feynman: *fine, but don't forget the electromagnetic field is quantum too.* Mead: *and don't forget that in plenty of collective problems, you can understand the physics without ever making an independently existing field the center of the story.* Wheeler and Feynman walk in together: *why assume the field has to be an independent actor at all?* Gauge theory interrupts from the back of the room: *because the potential is exactly the connection required to compare phases consistently from point to point.* Renormalization sighs, from wherever renormalization sits: *you're all arguing about the wrong scale.* And classical electromagnetism, sitting quietly in the corner the whole time, adds: *whatever the rest of you decide, my equations still work.* Everyone in the room is a little annoyed. Everyone in the room is right about something. That, as much as anything else this book has offered, is what physics actually is.

The real achievement here was learning, chapter by chapter, why each voice exists in the first place, not finding one sentence that makes them all agree. That is what genuine understanding looks like, as opposed to its cheaper imitation. This book began with the familiar world of electric and magnetic forces, passed through potentials, discovered quantum phase, worked through the Aharonov–Bohm effect, met superconducting loops, entered full QED, learned what photons and virtual photons mean, survived vacuum polarization and renormalization, and finally arrived at a concrete superconducting circuit where all of it operates at once, measurably, in one piece of laboratory apparatus. Two centuries of apparently unrelated discoveries turn out to be a hierarchy of quantum relationships, one whose large-scale manifestations can look, from the outside, like wildly different subjects, without ever quite being one.

At the very beginning, the goal was a synthesis of Feynman and Mead that an ordinary, curious reader could actually follow. Mead did not derive QED here, QED does not prove Mead's ontology, and photons and the field have not been argued away anywhere in these pages. What this book has tried to do, chapter after chapter, is show a continuous conceptual path connecting phase, to potential, to interaction, to field, to photon, to collective coherence, to classical electromagnetism, and then make one final, essential point: every arrow in that chain can be traveled in both directions, depending entirely on which question you're asking. That, and nothing grander than that, is the real merger this book set out to build; Chapter 47 draws the full boundary around it.
