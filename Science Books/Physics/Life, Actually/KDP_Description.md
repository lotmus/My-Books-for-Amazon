## Product description

On Mars, a rover has sealed a rock with strange spots on it into a titanium tube, and nobody knows when it will come home. On Earth, more than thirty million people have spat into plastic tubes and mailed them to laboratories. One tube holds a question we cannot yet answer: has life ever begun anywhere else? The other holds the answer to a question nobody could even ask properly a century ago: what is life, and how does it keep itself going?

Life, Actually is the story between those two tubes. It runs on one idea: life is chemistry that copies itself, imperfectly, and lets the imperfections be judged.

It starts with the chemistry. Why carbon and water. Why biological energy is electrons running downhill. How, about four billion years ago, chemistry that merely happened might have become chemistry that copies, and why life then stayed microscopic for three billion years.

Then the judging. How Darwin's machine works, why sex is evolution's most expensive habit, why cooperation evolved at all, and how the great transitions built cells with engines, bodies, brains and us, a branch still being edited by milk, altitude and disease. It goes where most evolution books stop: Hofstadter's strange loop, Dennett's dangerous idea, and whether a mind could be built from something other than neurons.

Then it opens the copier. The double helix of 1953 and Rosalind Franklin's photograph. Four letters, a copying machine that makes one mistake in a billion, a friar's peas, a bottle of flies and the dark turn of eugenics. How we learned to read the text, then to cut it, copy it in a test tube, clone a sheep and rewrite a single letter in a living patient. What a spit kit really reads, who else is reading it, and whether we should ever edit our children.

Finally, it leaves Earth. What life needs. Why Mars dried out, and what Perseverance found in a rock called Cheyava Falls. The buried oceans of Europa and Enceladus, and the methane lakes of Titan. How the James Webb Space Telescope reads the air of planets around other stars, and why the claim of life on K2-18 b did not hold up. Sixty years of listening for signals, Fermi's question "Where is everybody?", and what a second, independent beginning of life would tell us about the first.

Written for a reader who never studied biology. Short sentences, every term explained the moment it appears, dry humor, and claims marked as settled, working or speculative. Current to October 2026. A volume in the Physics, Actually series.

## About the author (for the KDP author page)

Lothar J. Musiol is a graduate of Munich University of Applied Sciences. He spent more than forty years in the semiconductor industry, first at the largest company in the field Germany had to offer, then at a string of American start-ups.

He studied physics alongside all of it, seriously enough to know exactly how much he is simplifying in his books, and how much he is not. He writes to make complex ideas clearer and more engaging than most treatments manage, without ever pretending they are simpler than they actually are.

Dual citizenship lets him split his time between San Clemente, California, and Passau, Bavaria, where Austria begins at the garden fence. The household includes one married daughter, one grumpy Teacup Pomeranian, and eleven chickens.
