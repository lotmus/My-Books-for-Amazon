# PART II — The Moon First

---

## 4. The Moon First, Because It Is Close

The Moon is first because it is close. That sentence is allowed to be boring. Boring is how you do not die of a poster.

Three days, not months. A second and a third for the radio, not four minutes, not twenty. One-sixth of the weight you grew up in, which is a kindness to the legs and a rudeness to the dust: everything you kick stays in the conversation longer than you wanted. A test kitchen, not a spare Earth.

![Figure 4. Earth and Moon on a simple line; a three-day tick, a 1.3-second tick. Close is a clock, not a romance.](fig04.png)

where light-time to the Moon is about 1.3 seconds one way — a delay you can talk through, if you are patient, and still call a conversation.

Mars will not give you that. Mars gives you a monologue with a pause that can eat a lunch. The Moon gives you a slightly drunk phone call. That difference is not poetry. It is why a medical emergency, a jammed airlock, a software load that needs a human who is still on Earth, is a different sport at three days than at a season.

The cheap path to the Moon is not a Hohmann argument with a twenty-six-month sulk. You wait for a monthly-ish window, you light, you coast a few days, you arrive in a gravity well that is shallow compared with home and still perfectly able to kill you if you arrive wrong. Abort, in many of the early hours, still means *fall back to Earth* with a heat shield and a prayer that is really a number. Abort from a Mars transfer, once you are weeks in, is a different novel. Chapter 11 writes that novel. This chapter writes the reason the novel is not the first homework.

---

Close is not kind. Close is *reachable*.

The Moon has no air you can hire. It has a vacuum that will boil your spit and a dust that is glass, not potting soil. The dust is electrostatic, sharp, and in love with seals. It will find the airlock that already sticks. It will find the pump. It will find the lung if you let it. The temperature swings like a door left open on a winter night and a summer noon, because there is no blanket of air to argue with the Sun. Two weeks of day, two weeks of night, if you sit on the equator like a tourist. The poles, which the current posters love, have a different kitchen: low Sun, long shadows, craters that have not seen light since before anyone named a king, ice in the dark if the maps are right, and a power problem the moment the Sun sits on the horizon like a stingy coin.

Ice at the poles is the sentence that turned the Moon from a flag stand into a maybe-kitchen. If the ice is there in useful amounts, and if you can mine it without spending more water than you gain, you have propellant and drink and air, in principle. In principle is not a tank farm. ISRU — in-situ resource utilization, the kitchen word for *make it there* — is the middle noun trying to become the third. Papers and prototypes exist. A city does not.

One-sixth g is a playground and a clinic. Bones and legs that spent a career under Earth will not thank you for a long stay unless you work them. Fluids shift. Dust hangs. A fall is slower and still a fall. The playground photographs well. The clinic is why a winter-over is a medical program, not a camping trip.

Radiation is the unpaid guest. The Moon has no magnetic kindness worth mentioning and no air to spend the first of the particles. A solar storm can arrive as a homework assignment you fail with your bone marrow. A habitat is a pile of dirt, or water, or regolith bags, or a lava tube if you are lucky and the tube is not a fairy tale for your latitude. Apollo stayed a few days and came home. A few days is a sortie. Months is a different dosimeter.

---

Why first, then, if it is a vacuum with a dust problem?

Because every ugly part of going somewhere else is cheaper to learn three days away than a season away. Landing. Leaving. Suits. Dust. Power through a night. A radio that is almost a conversation. A missed landing that is still a missed landing, but the inquiry can have the people in the same room as the families within a week. A test kitchen is where you burn the first loaf. You do not rent a hall for a wedding and then learn to bake.

The Moon is not a spare Earth. It will not take the species if the kitchen at home fails. It has no biosphere to recover, no forest to grow back, no grandmother of a bird to un-extinct. It is a rock with a useful well, a useful ice rumor, and a useful closeness. Calling it a spare is how you stop washing the one you have. Chapter 15 is that sermon’s funeral. Here, keep the three days.

Mara is not on the Moon. She is on the ruder neighbor, or on a thought-experiment that wears the neighbor’s butterscotch sky. The book keeps her there so the delay stays honest. The Moon would have given her a radio that almost answered. She would still have logged the pH. The basil would still not have cared about the logo. Close does not make a downtown. Close makes a classroom.

A note on “why not Mars first,” because the posters ask it with a straight face.

Mars has days that last about a day, a little carbon dioxide you can point a box at, ice at the poles and in the dirt, and a romance that photographs in butterscotch. The Moon has none of that romance and a radio you can almost use. The first kitchen you burn a loaf in should be the one you can phone from, and abort from, and hold an inquiry in without a twenty-six-month sulk. That is not timidity. That is how you do not spend a crew on a first lesson that could have been a third-day lesson. If a later decade makes Mars a sortie, it will still have been cheaper that the suits, the dust, the leave, and the habit of not fusing nouns were learned on the close rock.

Appendix A4 writes the 1.3 seconds, the three days, the one-sixth, a sketch of lunar Δv, and why polar ice is a maybe-tank, not a city charter. Here, keep the line with two ticks. Close is a clock. Romance can sit in the hall.

## 5. What Has Already Flown

Uncrewed loops, landers, sample returns. A flag is not a shift. A kiss of dust is not a lease.

Hot: this century has already put machines on the Moon, driven them, failed in public, succeeded in public, and brought rock home from both the familiar face and the face we used to call the back. That sentence should be taught before any founder’s year. The hardware is not a rumor. The downtown is.

![Figure 5. A sample capsule and a small lander, both large in frame, on bright ground. Rock in a box is inventory. A skyline is not.](fig05.png)

Start with the loops, because a loop is the dress rehearsal people skip when they are in a hurry to walk.

A cabin that flies a long cold path around the Moon and comes home without a crew is a test of stack, shield, software, and the habit of hitting the thin air of Earth at a speed that will kill you if the shield is a story. Artemis I did that in 2022. Other nations have sent uncrewed craft on lunar paths for decades. The new fact is not that a loop is possible. The new fact is that a *crew-shaped* cabin, on a stack meant to carry people later, has done the loop and splashed. Warm as a step. Hot as a class of thing that has now happened. Not a sortie. The seats were empty on purpose.

Landers are ruder than loops. Loops do not have to negotiate the last kilometer of a well with no air to help you regret. The last kilometer is a sport. Some of this century’s landers have been tidy: Chang’e-class craft that set down, deployed rovers, and, in the sample-return editions, threw a bit of the Moon into a can and sent the can to a desert you can drive to. Chang’e-5 brought near-side rock home in 2020. Chang’e-6 brought far-side rock home in 2024. Those are flown facts. They are not shifts. No one stayed. The rock is in a box. The box is hotter than a rendering of a dome.

Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south in 2023 and drove a rover. Japan’s SLIM arrived with a precision dare and a landing that taught a lesson about ending up on your head and still phoning home. Commercial landers — an American class that wants to be a truck service — have begun to try the same dirt. Some have stood. Some have tipped, leaked, or gone quiet. Mixed manners are what a new truck service looks like. They are not a proof that “the Moon is open for business” in the sense a Chamber of Commerce means business. They are a proof that kissing dust is no longer a national once-a-decade miracle. It is becoming a thing companies fail at in public. Failure in public is how trucks get better. It is not a town.

---

A sample return is a special kind of flown.

You must land, or at least collect. You must launch again from a well, or throw the can to a waiting orbiter. You must hit Earth’s air and not cook the rock. You must find the can. The rock, once you have it, is a library: isotopes, water histories, a date, a fight about whether the poles are a tank farm. Chang’e-class returns are the modern masters of this sport. Apollo brought more mass, and also people, and also a flag. Apollo is last century. This century’s returns are robots. Robots are the honest near-term staff. Chapter 25 says a crew that does not sleep can wait. The same crew can also fetch a box so a person in a desert can open it.

What has *not* flown, while we are cleaning the inventory:

A crew around the Moon in this century, as of this kitchen. That is still a warm class, not a photograph.

A crew landing in this century. Warm as an attempt in the 2030s. Not flown.

A winter-over. Not flown.

A tank farm of lunar ice turned into a tanker of oxygen. Not flown. A small experiment that makes a breath of oxygen from lunar dirt or from a box of regolith in a lab is a paper and a prototype. Keep it warm as a class of trick. Do not cash it as a city.

A child. A school. A law court. A farm that feeds a town without a cargo cult. Cold. Not flown. Not scheduled as a product this decade.

---

Rohan, on the other side of Mara’s tin, used to work payloads. He is invented. He still flinches when a slide shows a flag and a skyline in the same frame. He has opened boxes that came from space and found, inside, a gram of something that cost a career. He respects the gram. He will not let the gram rent a halo.

A flag is a photograph of a sortie that ended. A shift is a roster that repeats, a pump that has a spare, a person who is replaced when the person is spent. The Moon has had flags. It has not had shifts. This century’s robots are the beginning of a shift for *machines*. A machine shift is not a town. It is how you learn the dirt before you ask a body to log the pH for a year.

She hears, on the delay, that another lander has tipped. Someone on Earth is already writing the sentence that the tipped lander means the program is a con, or the sentence that the tipped lander means the downtown is one failure closer to being inevitable. Both sentences are costumes. The tipped lander means the last kilometer is still a sport. She logs the pH. The basil does not require a flag.

A ledger, so a kiss of dust has a weight.

Last century: twelve people walked, stayed hours to a few days, and the last of them came home in 1972. They brought back on the order of a few hundred kilograms of rock, and a photograph the century has been trying to rent as a lease. The lease expired when they left. That is hot. It is also over.

This century’s returns are smaller and ruder, which is why they are the honest inventory. Chang’e-5, 2020, a near-side sample on the order of a couple of kilograms, in a box you can drive to. Chang’e-6, 2024, a far-side sample of the same modest mass. A kilogram is a library. A habitat that keeps a person through a lunar night is tonnes: shell, power, air, water, a spare pump, the dirt you pile on the roof when the Sun throws a fit. The gap between the box and the tonnes is the whole sport. Do not let a press kit step over it.

The truck service, named so it cannot hide in “commercial.”

In 2024 a private lander left Earth, sprang a propellant leak, and never got the chance to negotiate the last kilometer. The same year another private lander did negotiate it, tipped, and still phoned home with a science haul from a crooked floor. Japan’s SLIM, the same season, landed with a precision the old program would have envied and then spent its career on its head, solar panel in the wrong argument with the Sun, and still worked. In 2025 a third private lander stood up and worked a lunar day like a shift. Another, aimed at the south, ended in a crater on its side. Hot, all of them, as a class: the last kilometer is now a thing companies do in public, including the failures. Cold as a town. A shift for machines is a machine that is replaced when it dies, on a calendar, with a spare already on the pad. We are at the beginning of that calendar. We are not at the babies.

What a machine shift would have to show before a body is asked to log a year. A lander that can be wrong and still leave a working radio. A rover that survives the night, not as a miracle, as a second unit already designed. A sample can that comes home often enough that a missed one is a slip, not a decade. A map of the ice that a miner could stake a shift on, not a radar rumor. None of those is a school. All of them are hotter than a dome.

Rohan’s gram is this paragraph. He will put the gram on a bench and tell you what it cost. He will not let you multiply the gram by a press conference until it becomes a street.

## 6. The Next Crew Around the Moon

Warm: a crew around the Moon this decade — a flyby, a cabin, a look at a dead close world that is three days away, not three seasons. Call it an Artemis-II-class morning. The exact month is a mood. The class is the fact: flesh, cis-lunar, home in a week if the stack behaves. That is a *sortie*. It is not a town.

![Figure 6. A cabin on a long loop, Earth and Moon both small. Seats occupied. No dome.](fig06.png)

The seats, this time, are not empty on purpose. That is the whole increment. Four bodies, or a number like four, in a cabin that has already flown empty. They will see the Moon as a disk that grows, then as a wall, then as a disk that shrinks. They will not land. They will not log a pH on the dirt. They will test the life-support the empty cabin could not confess: the smell, the CO₂, the toilet, the tempers, the suit drill that is a pantomime of a later morning. They will come home, if the shield and the numbers hold, and they will be called pioneers, which is a kindness, and a crew on a sortie, which is the noun.

A flyby is a crew sortie. It is not a town.

Keep the nouns unfused even here, before Chapter 12 makes a courtroom of them. The people in the cabin go, they work, they come home, or they die on a schedule Earth still owns. That is the first noun. The slide will try to marry it to the third. Refuse the marriage. A photograph of four faces in a window with the Moon in it is a masterpiece of a certain kind of engineering. It is also a week. A week is not a school district.

---

Why does the month stay a mood?

Because the stack that throws the cabin is a machine with a temper, and the cabin is a machine with a temper, and the two must agree, and the abort modes must be more than a slide, and the suits — even if they are not walking — must not be a rumor, and the weather in the political kitchen must hold long enough for a count-up. This kitchen is dated September 2026. Official pages have named years that later needed a new page. By the time you read this, the morning may have happened, or slipped, or slipped again. The class does not care. The class is: flesh, around the Moon, home in days, this decade as a warm bet, not as a prophecy.

If the morning has already happened when you open these pages, the chapter is not wasted. You now own a flown sortie. You still do not own a town. The temptation will be to treat the splashdown as a lease. It is a splashdown.

If the morning has not happened, you still own the empty-cabin loop, the landers, the boxes of rock. You own a warm class. You do not own a fraud just because a date moved. Chapter 3 already billed that invoice.

---

Jules Adeyemi is the invented name on a crew-shaped roster. He runs, in this story, the kind of checklist that makes a cabin less likely to kill you with a valve. He does not think of himself as a founder of a city. He thinks of himself as a person who will be very far from a hospital and very close to a heat shield. That is the correct self-portrait. The poster will paint a different one. The poster is not invited on the stack.

What the sortie is for, if you want a purpose that is not a sermon: to make the next landing attempt less of a first time. Life support with breath in it. Navigation with a crew that can be sick. A communications stack that has to carry a voice, not a beep. A public that has to remember that spaceflight is a sport in which the ball can kill you. Those are program reasons. They are enough. They do not require a downtown on the far side of the loop.

Mara hears the crew on a relay that is not for her. Someone reads a name. Someone reads a slipped date for a later landing that the flyby was supposed to make inevitable. Inevitable is not a temperature we use. She logs the pH. The basil is a little too alkaline. That is a problem she can bully. A town on the Moon is not.

What the morning has to prove, if you want a list that survives a slipped month.

A cabin that has already flown empty must now fly with breath in it. Life support has to keep four people — the number can be three or six; the class is a handful — alive for about ten days, not for a season. The heat shield has to come home from a speed the empty test already tasted, with a crew who can be sick on the way. A free-return path, if that is the architecture, is a loop that falls back toward Earth if you do not light a later burn. That is a kindness. It is also a confession: the first crewed loop is designed so that *not landing* is the success. Success is splashdown. Splashdown is not a lease on a crater.

Communications will be almost a conversation. One-way light-time is still about 1.3 seconds at the Moon, more when the geometry is rude, and the loop may take them around the far side where the Earth is a memory until a relay or a geometry brings it back. A few minutes of silence is a planned blackout, not a town going dark. Medical help is a checklist and a doctor on a delay, not a hospital. If someone bleeds, the hospital is days away, on the other end of a heat shield. That is why Jules thinks about the valve. The poster thinks about the window.

The public test is ruder than the engineering test. A flown sortie will be asked to mean a settlement by Friday. It will not mean that on Saturday either. Apollo 8 flew people around the Moon in 1968 and came home. The landings that followed were still landings, still short, still a cargo cult from Earth. A second crewed loop, half a century later, is a resumption, not a promotion. Hardware that had to be invented again is not a civilization that was waiting in the cupboard.

## 7. A Landable Year Is Not a Town

Warm: a landing *attempt* in the 2030s — a cabin that goes down and is supposed to come up, a lander that still owes a demonstration of the ugly parts: refuel in orbit, survive the last kilometer, light the engines in a gravity well with no atmosphere to help you regret. HLS, Starship-class, whatever logo wins the week. “Attempt” is the honest noun. A flag is not a greenhouse. A greenhouse is not a school district.

![Figure 7. A lander on bright dirt, one figure, a ladder. The caption: attempt. No skyline.](fig07.png)

The long poles have names. They do not have a single year that is a landing gear.

**Refuel.** If the lander is a heavy stack that arrives in pieces, someone must pour propellant in orbit the way you pour a second pot of coffee: many times, without making a bomb. That pour has been a paper, a test, a hope, a clip. Routine pours are the unpaid invoice. One pour is a dare. A campaign of pours is a trucking company. Chapter 10 is the trucking company. Here, notice that a landing architecture that *requires* the pour cannot honestly print a month before the pour has manners.

**The last kilometer.** No air to bite. Engines that must throttle or stage or both. Dust that will sandblast the vehicle you need to leave in. A slope that was a pixel on a map and is a decision at ten meters. Abort, this close, is a different novel than abort on the way out from Earth.

**Leave.** The part the flag photographs skip. You must light again, from a well, with a vehicle that has sat in dust and cold and a night, and you must meet the cabin that will take you home, or be the cabin that will take you home. Apollo’s leave was a small, fierce, already-demonstrated trick by the time the public saw the flag. A new heavy lander’s leave is a new trick until it is not.

**Suits.** You must walk, bend, see, pick up, not cook, not freeze, not fill the bearings with glass dust, and get back in. A suit is a spacecraft you wear. Spacecraft slip. The glove is a workplace. The workplace has not yet done a shift on this dirt in this century.

**The rest of the house.** Power through a polar night or a polar “day” that is a long dusk. Navigation without a kindly GPS constellation, unless you brought one. A habitat that is the lander itself, or a can you sent ahead, or a tin that has not flown. Medical. Waste. The pH of something you are trying to grow, if you are arrogant enough to grow. Arrogance is allowed in a test kitchen. It is not a town charter.

---

“Attempt” is the word that keeps the temperature honest.

A program that says *we will land in year X* is printing a mood. A program that says *we will attempt a landing in this class of years, once these poles are paid* is printing an invoice. The second sentence can still be wrong. It cannot be as wrong as a downtown.

If the attempt happens and the crew walks and comes home, you have a flown sortie on dirt. Hot, the morning after, as a class of event that has rejoined the inventory. Still not a town. Apollo already taught this, and the century forgot it on purpose because forgetting is how you sell the next slide.

If the attempt happens and fails, you have a funeral or a miracle of abort, and a review, and a slip. The slip will be called a scandal by people who needed the downtown for a feeling, and a destiny by people who needed the failure for a feeling. Chapter 3 already refused both feelings as thermometers.

If the attempt has not happened when you read this, you are in the same kitchen this chapter was written in. The poles are still the poles. The year is still a mood.

---

Linh Pham designs suit gloves. She is invented. The dust is not. She has a box of lunar simulant that is not the Moon and is still rude to bearings. She can tell you, at a table, why a glove that works in a clean room fails after an hour of glass. She is the long pole that photographs badly. The poster wants a face in a gold visor. The visor is easy. The glove is the town, if there is ever a town: a person who can work. Work is not a flag. Work is a shift.

Mara’s airlock is a glove problem in a different gravity. She hits it. She thinks of Linh, whom she has never met, and of a year on a slide, which she has met too often. She logs the pH. The landing year, if it arrives, will not water the basil.

A worked morning, so “attempt” cannot hide inside a year.

The dirt has a night. Away from the poles, the Sun is gone for about two weeks, then rude for about two weeks. A polar peak can bargain for more light, and still has shadows, dust, and a crater that is a freezer. Power for those two weeks is batteries, a reactor, or a cable to a place that still sees the Sun. None of those is a downtown. All of them are mass you flew or mass you have not yet learned to make. A habitat that is the lander itself must survive that night with the crew inside, or the crew must leave before the night, which is a sortie with a curfew.

The leave has a price in speed. Getting off the lunar surface and up to a meeting place in the odd orbit the porch prefers is on the order of a couple of kilometers per second, paid in propellant, with an engine that has been sitting in glass. Apollo paid a smaller, already-practiced version and came home. A heavy lander that drank a campaign of tanker flights in Earth orbit has to have saved enough drink for this last lighting, or the flag is a headstone. Public arguments about that campaign run from several pours to more than ten, depending on the stack and the honesty of the slide. The number is warm. The noun is hot: more than one pour, done as a job, before a month is allowed to pretend it is a town.

Dust is the shift Linh already has in a box. Apollo suits came home sanded. Bearings, seals, visors, lungs if you are careless with the airlock. A one-hour photo is not an eight-hour glove. An eight-hour glove, repeated, with a spare glove on the shelf, is the beginning of work. Work repeated through a night, with a medic and a spare pump, is the beginning of an outpost. A school is later, and colder, and not on this ladder.

Medical, said plainly. A broken leg, a heart, a pregnancy, a tooth. On a sortie you stabilize and you leave. On an outpost you need a person who can do more than a checklist, and a way home that is not “the next architecture.” Evacuation from the Moon is days, if a ship is ready. Evacuation from Mars is a window measured in months. Chapter 11 keeps that window. Here, notice that a landable year which has not hired a medic and a ride home has hired a photograph.

Mara’s basil does not care which logo won the week. The pH will be wrong or it will not. A crew that walked and came home will have paid the poles once. Paying them once is the attempt. Paying them as a routine, with spares, is the outpost. The town is the routine plus children who do not need the next ship. The year on the slide skipped the last two nouns. That is why the year can be warm and the town stays cold.

Appendix A7 writes the long poles — refuel, last kilometer, leave, suits — as a list you can keep when the logo changes. Here, keep the ladder and the word *attempt*. A landable year is not a town.

## 8. Gateway and the Architecture Fights

A station in lunar orbit is a choice, not a law. Camps have floor plans that change.

Warm-to-cold: a winter-over. Months. Cargo from Earth. A Gateway or a surface tin that changes drawings because architecture is a fight, not a destiny. The fight is allowed. Treating the current drawing as a city charter is not.

![Figure 8. Two floor plans, same dirt: a can in a high odd orbit; a tin on the ground. A fight, not a law.](fig08.png)

Gateway, in the American ledger and its partners’ ledgers, is a small station in a near-rectilinear halo orbit — a path that is a compromise between seeing the Moon, seeing Earth, and not spending a fortune every time you want to go down or go home.

where NRHO is a high, lopsided lunar orbit that stays in view of Earth for long stretches and is not “low Moon” in the postcard sense.

A can in that path can be a staging porch: park, refuel, wait, send a lander down, catch a lander up, hide from a solar storm, run a science rack, let a crew sleep somewhere that is not the lander. It can also be a porch you paid for and then found you did not need, if the lander can go from Earth-side to dirt and back without the handshake. That sentence is the fight.

Surface-first people say: every kilogram in the halo can is a kilogram that is not a tin on the ice, not a rover, not a power cable. They say a porch is how you never arrive. Orbit-first people say: every crew that has no porch is a crew with one failure mode away from a very long wait, and a lander that is also a house is a house that must also be a rocket. Both are engineering sentences. Both can be true in different decades. Neither is a law of nature. A law of nature does not issue a contract amendment.

Architecture fights are how you know you are in a program and not a poster. A poster has one floor plan. A program has a graveyard of floor plans and a meeting that is late.

---

What a station is not.

It is not a town. The International Space Station is the existence proof: a magnificent, expensive, continuously staffed *outpost* in a well we already own, with a cargo cult from Earth that has never been optional. People have lived there for months. Children have not been raised there as a civilization. The umbilical is the point. Cut the cargo and the outpost becomes a problem, then a wreck. A lunar Gateway would be a smaller, farther, colder cousin of that outpost. Months, if it is lucky. Cargo from Earth. A science rack. A porch. Warm-to-cold as a sustained camp in the 2030s–2040s, because the fight can move a decade and the cargo still has to climb a well.

It is not required by physics. Physics requires a path, a Δv, a place to stand. Physics does not require a particular can in a particular halo. If a later architecture fires the porch and lands from a different path, the porch will have been a choice that was useful or not, like a kitchen island. Kitchen islands are not laws.

It is not a moral. People will try to make it one: *we must build the station or we are not a serious species*. Seriousness is a habit of invoices. It is not a floor plan.

---

Nandita Rao drafts the fight for a living. She is invented. She has a wall of versions. Version 3 had a habitat the size of a bus. Version 6 shrank it. Version 8 added a partner’s module that then slipped a year. Version 9 asked whether the landing needed the handshake. She does not hate any version. She hates the sentence “the architecture is settled.” Settled is what you say about a house you are no longer allowed to improve. Camps should not be settled. Camps should be honest about being camps.

Mara does not have a Gateway. She has a tin and a delay. On the delay, someone is still arguing about a porch she will not see. She logs the pH. The argument does not water the tray. The argument might, someday, change whether a person like her has a spare can to run to when the seal fails. That is a real stake. It is still not a city.

The existence proof is already flying, and it is not a city.

The station in low Earth orbit has been continuously staffed for a quarter century. Cargo ships climb to it on a schedule that is a trucking company, not a miracle. Crews stay months. The mass of food, water, oxygen, spare pumps, and experiments that must arrive or the outpost becomes a problem is measured in tonnes a year, not in a vibe. Children are not born there as a people. When a toilet fails, Earth sends a part. When a politics fails, the crew still needs the part. That is an outpost with an umbilical. A lunar porch would be the same sentence with a worse well: fewer tonnes, a longer delay, a solar storm with less air in the way, a partner module that can slip a year and leave a bolt pattern waiting.

Numbers, so the porch cannot become a downtown by adjective. A near-rectilinear halo is not low orbit. Going down from it and coming back up is a Δv you pay every shift, on the order of a couple of kilometers per second depending on the exact path — a real burn, not a hop. A surface tin pays a different bill: night, dust, and the leave. Neither bill is “a station,” which is a word people use when they want both bills to have been paid already. They have not.

What the existence proof actually eats.

A handful of people have lived above the air, continuously, since the year 2000. Call it seven, give or take a politics. They do not live on a vibe. They live on a recycle loop that still leaks, and on cargo ships that arrive with food, spare pumps, experiments, and the occasional toilet. Order of magnitude: tonnes a year for that handful, not a suitcase. Water is the item the posters forget, because the station recycles most of it and still has to be topped up when the loop sulks. Oxygen is a machine with a spare. Food is the thing you cannot yet print from a view of Earth. Cut the ships and you have months of rations, then a problem, then a wreck. That is an outpost that has already been paid for a generation. It is the most successful camp our species has ever flown. It has no children who grew up there as a people. It has no farm that would feed the next crew if the pad went quiet.

A lunar porch inherits the sentence and loses the kindnesses. The well is deeper. The storm shelter is a can with less air in the way of the Sun’s tantrum. The cargo ship is not a day’s climb; it is a campaign. A partner module that slips a year does not slip a paint color. It slips a bolt pattern, a power standard, a hatch that was supposed to meet another hatch. Nandita’s version 8 is that sentence. The fight about whether you need the porch at all is allowed to last a decade. A decade of drawings is not a decade of nights survived on the dirt.

Surface-first has its own tonne. A tin on the ice must carry the night — two weeks if you were foolish enough to sit on the equator, a stingy Sun if you sat on a polar ridge and still drew a shadow you cannot argue with. Power for that night is batteries you flew, a cable to a lit peak, or a reactor with a politics. Life support for a crew of four for a month is not a romance. It is on the order of a hundred kilograms of oxygen if you are only counting the breath, plus the machine, plus the water you will not want to ship twice, plus the food you cannot yet grow in a tray that has learned the dust. Four people, one month, one spare of every thing that can stick. That is already a cargo ship’s problem. It is an outpost if you can repeat it. It is a settlement only when the repeat can fail and the next generation still eats. Chapter 12 keeps that test. This chapter only refuses to let the porch skip it by being called a station.

Appendix A8 writes NRHO, the porch-versus-dirt fight, and why the low-Earth station is an outpost with an umbilical, not a template for a downtown. Here, keep the two floor plans. A fight, not a law.

## 9. Other Flags, Other Ledgers

China, ILRS, partners: real precursors; a 2030s station is still a camp on paper.

Warm as *intent and precursors*; cold as a city. Chang’e-class work is hot. A research station named like a destination is still a camp.

![Figure 9. Two ledgers, two flags, one gray dirt. Precursors on the left. A camp on paper on the right.](fig09.png)

The American ledger is not the only kitchen.

Chang’e-class landers and sample returns are flown facts, already billed in Chapter 5. Rovers on the familiar face and the far face. A relay satellite that made the far face less of a mute. A plan, with partners, for a camp they name the way you name a station: International Lunar Research Station, ILRS, on paper a 2030s presence at the south, after more precursors — a surveyor, a heavier lander, a cargo trick, a habit of showing up.

Intent is warm. Precursors that have already returned rock are hot. A multinational camp with a name is a drawing. Drawings are allowed. A drawing is not a downtown. A drawing of a research station is, at best, the middle noun: an outpost with an umbilical, weeks to months, cargo from Earth, a science rack, a flag that has company. Company is not a civilization. Company is how you split a cargo bill.

Other ledgers exist and will exist. India has landed and driven. Japan has dared a precision set-down. Europe writes modules and rides. Commercial trucks want a service. A later decade will have more logos on the dirt. More logos is a temperature about *access*. It is not a temperature about children who do not need a cargo cult.

---

Two ledgers do not make a city by addition.

People who like races will say the race *is* the settlement. A race is a reason a legislature pays. It is not a biosphere. Two outposts that cannot feed themselves are two outposts. They can even be useful to each other — a spare pump, a radio, a political reason not to be reckless — and still be camps. The cargo-cult test does not care how many flags are in the photograph. The test is: if the ships from Earth stop, do the babies still eat? Chapter 12 is the test. Here, notice that a second flag is often sold as the moment the first flag became a town. It is the moment the dirt got busier.

Look first, on a rock that never had a biosphere, still means: do not pretend the camp is a spare Earth, and do not treat the Earth you left as already written off because a rival’s slide is prettier this year. The basil does not care which language is on the packet.

Wei Ning is an invented payload scientist in a desert who opened a far-side box and did not sleep. The rock does not belong to a downtown. The rock belongs to a measurement. She will write a paper that makes polar ice a little hotter or a little colder as a tank farm. She will not vote for a skyline. She has seen too many skylines in the hall outside the lab.

On Mara’s delay, a slipped date arrives in a language she does not speak, then in one she does. The date is a camp on paper. She writes it under *Earth says*, next to the other Earth’s date, which also slipped. She logs the pH. Two ledgers. One tray. The dirt is the same dirt.

What “precursor” has to mean, so a press kit cannot wear the word.

A precursor lands, or orbits, or brings a sample home, and leaves a measurement. Chang’e-5 brought near-side rock home in 2020. Chang’e-6 brought far-side rock home in 2024. Chandrayaan-3 stood on a southern site in 2023 and drove. Those are hot. A surveyor that is still a drawing, a heavy cargo lander that is still a contract, a habitat that is still a name — ILRS, on paper a south-polar camp in the 2030s with partners — are warm as intent. The step from a sample capsule to a place where people sleep through a lunar night is not a step. It is the long poles of Chapter 7, paid again, in another language, with another weld.

Two programs do not average into a settlement. If one ledger lands a crew for a week and the other lands a rover that dies in a night, you have a sortie and a robot. Add them and you still have a sortie and a robot. The race, if there is a race, buys metal faster. Metal on a pad is not metal arranged into a kitchen that can miss a cargo ship. The cargo-cult test is still Chapter 12. A second flag does not grade the test. It only makes the photograph busier.

Look at the ice before you look at the name. Polar ice is the sentence that makes a south-polar camp more than a flag stand. If the ice is a film, or a lie of the radar, or locked in a crater a robot cannot work, the camp is a hotel with no well. If the ice is a deposit you can mine without spending the water on the mining, you have moved one pole of ISRU from a paper to a shift. You have not moved the babies. Wei Ning’s paper can heat or cool that one pole. It cannot print a school.

A partner camp still has to survive a night. The night does not translate.

Whoever lands, the Sun still leaves most sites for about two weeks. A south-polar name on a slide is a bet on peaks and crater rims where the bet is better and still not a lamp you can trust with your eyes closed. Power is the same invoice in every language: a reactor, a cable to a lit ridge, or a battery that is itself a cargo flight. Communications are the same invoice. The far side is a radio silence unless you brought a relay, which is why a relay satellite is a more honest “station” than a habitat drawing. A relay is a machine. A machine can be hot. It is not a school.

Ice, said as a tank and not as a mood. If a crater holds water ice in useful thickness, a miner still has to get there, cut it, haul it, and not spend three tonnes of spacecraft water to recover one tonne of ice. That ratio is the whole pole. Wei Ning’s paper can move the ratio from a rumor toward a number. A number is not a well until a shift has run it twice and a spare exists for the machine that broke. Two flags do not improve the ratio. They duplicate the argument.

What to keep when the partner list rots. The list will rot. A nation joins, a nation leaves, a company is bought, a date slips in a communiqué you have to read twice. The inventory that survives is boring: sample masses already on Earth, rovers that already drove, a far-side rock that already has a laboratory. Hot. Everything with a groundbreaking date in the 2030s is warm until the groundbreaking is a wheel on dirt. A city is cold in every language, because a city is babies and a missed cargo ship, and nobody’s ledger has paid that sentence.

Mara files the foreign date next to the domestic one and does not pick a winner. The tray does not have a nationality. The pH is the only communiqué that cannot slip without a consequence she can smell.

Appendix A9 writes Chang’e-class facts, ILRS as a paper camp, and other partners as a list that will rot and be replaced. Here, keep the two ledgers. Precursors are inventory. A name on a station is a wish that has started to buy metal. Metal is not a city.
