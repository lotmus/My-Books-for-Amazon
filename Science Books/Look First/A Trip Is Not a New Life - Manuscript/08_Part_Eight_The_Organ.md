# PART VIII — The Organ You Already Use

## 34. We Already Use the Organ

Then the sentence that will not die, because it is useful to people who sell keys: we only use ten percent of our brain.
False. Hot as a correction.
Imaging, lesions, the energy bill — the organ is on duty. Tissue that looks idle in one snapshot is still a staff with a shift you did not catch. You do not have a spare ninety percent in the cupboard, waiting for a password so you can meet “new challenges.” You have the organ you have, plus sleep, plus practice, plus tools, plus other people. That is already how we adjust. Plasticity is real and expensive. It is not a tank of unused mind.

Figure 8. A hatched share: the brain is about two percent of mass and about twenty percent of resting energy. Idle-looking is not off-duty.
The sentence is a zombie because it is flattering. It tells you that the person you already are is a draft. It tells you that a course, a pill, a headset, a founder’s password will open the real draft. It turns a limit into a treasure hunt. Treasure hunts sell. Limits do not.
Where did it come from? Not from a measurement you can cite and keep. It gathered like kitchen grease: a misread of early neurologists who noticed that large injuries sometimes left surprising function; a self-help habit of turning “we do not understand everything” into “ninety percent is waiting”; a cartoon of unused neurons sitting in the dark like unused guest soaps. A century-old moral sentence said we live below our limits. That is effort-talk. It is not a volumetric survey of cortex. The ten-percent figure is a mutation of effort-talk into a floor plan. The floor plan is wrong.
Start with the bill, because the bill does not flatter.
The brain is roughly two percent of a body’s mass and takes roughly twenty percent of the resting energy.
where the twenty percent is of resting metabolic rate — the quiet kitchen, not a sprint.
Twenty percent of the quiet kitchen is an outrageous line item for an organ that, in the ten-percent story, is mostly on unpaid leave. Evolution is a miser about watts. A miser does not keep a ninety-percent dark warehouse of tissue that costs like a furnace and does nothing. The wattage is not a vibe. It is glucose and oxygen, measurable in a PET scanner, a machine that tracks where the brain is spending fuel, measurable as a reason you get faint when blood sugar drops, measurable as a reason a small animal with a large brain had better have a good reason. Humans paid the reason: social prediction, language, hands that can bully chemistry, a childhood long enough to load the software. We did not pay for a decorative loaf of unused cortex.
The number is not “twenty percent of thought.” Thought is not a pie you can slice that way. The number is energy. Energy is what a cupboard of idle tissue would still owe. Idle tissue that owed this much would have been fired by the mesh — this book’s name for the sieve of natural selection, keeping some traits and dropping others — unless it was doing something. The something is the rest of this chapter.
Lesions are the rude teacher.
If ninety percent of the organ were spare, large injuries would be polite. They are not polite. A small clot in the left posterior frontal lobe can take speech and leave the rest of you watching. A strip of motor cortex can take a hand. A hippocampus — the structure that stamps a day into memory — on both sides can take the ability to mint a new Tuesday. A cut in the white-matter cables — the wiring, not the gray offices — can disconnect a function as surely as removing the office. Stroke maps, tumor maps, gunshot maps, the old wartime files: they are a cartography of on duty. The cartography is not complete. The brain is plastic, and Chapter 44 will not insult that fact. Plasticity is what a staff does when a colleague is gone: overtime, rerouting, a worse but livable shift. Overtime is not proof that the colleague was decorative. It is proof that the remaining staff were already working and can work differently at a cost.
People point at the opposite anecdote: a person missing a startling amount of tissue who can still talk, walk, joke. The anecdote is real and does not rescue the ten percent. Hydrocephalus — fluid pressure building up inside the skull — that thinned the cortex, the brain’s outer working layer, a hemispherectomy — surgery that removes or disconnects half the brain — in a child with a raging epilepsy, a slow tumor that stole a lobe while the staff moved: these are demonstrations that development and time can reassign jobs, and that some jobs were already shared. A child is not a cupboard. A child is a construction site. Construction sites can reroute. Adults reroute less, and pay more, and still do not reveal a hidden ninety.
The cerebellum — the hind loaf that looks, to a skimmer, like a bonus — contains a huge fraction of the neurons and is not a bonus. Damage it and you get a drunk walk, a drunk reach, a timing that will not keep a beat. The “extra” neurons were timing the body. They were not waiting for your podcast.
Imaging is the other teacher, and it is easy to lie with.
A functional MRI picture is not a lamp that turns a region on. It is a slow, indirect hint that blood flow changed, which is a hint that cells did more, which is a hint you must not treat as a photograph of a thought. The pictures that make magazines — a blob on a medial surface labeled love — are the worst ambassadors. The honest use is statistical and humble: when a person does this task, this set of regions changes its bill a little, compared with that other task. Almost the entire organ can be shown to change its bill under some contrast if you are patient and not picky. Sensory sheets, motor sheets, language networks, attention networks, a default-mode set that lights when you stop the assigned task and start being a person with a past: they are all expensive. The default-mode set is the one a ten-percent story wants to call idle. It is the opposite of idle. It is the staff that keeps a self, a gossip, a plan, a replay of the argument, a simulation of the orbiter’s next late message. When it is damaged, people do not become superheroes with free watts. They become unmoored.
Tissue that looks quiet in one snapshot is still a staff with a shift you did not catch. A voxel — one cube of a brain scan, the smallest unit a machine can report — that does not pass a threshold in a love-blob study is not a spare bedroom. It may be working in a way the contrast did not pay to see. It may be inhibitory. It may be waiting, which in nervous tissue is often a job — a ready pool, a tuned silence. Silence can be duty. A night watchman who is not shouting is not unemployed.
Single-neuron recording, where we have it (animals, and the rare human electrode for epilepsy), finds cells that care about astonishingly specific things and cells that care about many things, and very few cells that care about nothing and are just hanging around in case a founder needs them. Glia — the brain’s other big population of cells, alongside neurons — are not decoration either. They are metabolism, insulation, cleanup, immune mood. The ten-percent sentence sometimes mutates into “we only use ten percent of our neurons,” as if the other cells were packing peanuts. They are not packing peanuts.
Why the zombie survives.
Because unused potential is a kinder story than used limits. Because a school system that fails a child would rather say the child’s tank was never opened than say the organ was on duty and hungry, tired, untaught, or afraid. Because a marketplace of “unlock your mind” cannot sell a hearing aid and a nap as loudly as it can sell a secret switch. Because the phrase is short. False sentences that are short outcompete true paragraphs.
Hot as a correction means you do not get to dress the myth back up as a future. “We only use ten percent today; tomorrow we will use the rest” is the same sentence with a calendar glued on. There is no rest. There is practice, sleep, tools, other people, and a bill. Chapter 35 is the tools with temperatures. Chapter 44 is the expensive rerouting. This chapter is the refusal.
She does not have ninety percent of a mind in the cupboard. She has a morning, a radio delay, a basil plant, a joint that may take a capsule, and an organ that has been paying twenty percent since before she learned the names of the herbs. The organ is why she can name them. It is not waiting for a password to start.
About two percent of mass, about twenty percent of resting energy: that bill is not how a miser keeps a dark warehouse. Lesions teach the same lesson from the other side — lose a piece and a function fails; there was no spare loft labeled ninety percent. Quiet voxels are not off-duty cupboards. Idle-looking is not unlocked potential. You already use the organ. Tools and sleep and other people are how you meet new work, not a password to a tank.
Appendix A8 writes the energy fraction, the lesion logic, and why a quiet voxel is not a spare tank. Here, if you take one thing, take the bill. A miser does not keep a dark warehouse at those rates. You already use the organ.
Where the popular version goes wrong.
The slide promotes the noun and crops the receipt. Look first: ask what was measured, what still needs Earth or a body, what the checklist still leaves empty.
Rule: We already use the organ. The ten-percent sentence is false. Hot as a correction.

## 35. Cognitive Enhancement, Two Temperatures

Cognitive enhancement, two temperatures.
Education, eyeglasses, a hearing aid, a stimulant with a known invoice: warm-to-hot as tools. A chip that returns a lost function: a local fix again, with scars. Unlocking a hidden full brain: cold. Do not dress the myth up as a future.

Figure 9. A toolbox on the left — book, glasses, hearing aid, a scored pill. An empty tank marked 90 percent, crossed out, on the right.
A tool is a bargain you can name.
Eyeglasses are the cleanest example in the house. The organ was on duty. The lens in front of it was the wrong shape. A ground piece of glass returns a function the cortex already knew how to use. Nobody serious calls spectacles “unlocking the unused ninety.” They are a local optical fix. They have an invoice. They have side effects if you get the prescription wrong. They changed literacy, craft, and the age at which a person can still thread a needle or read a radio checklist. Hot as a class of win. Not helium.
Hearing aids sit in the same drawer and are more neglected. Hearing is not a luxury input. It is how a social primate keeps the room. When the input fades, the organ does not gain a spare tank. It spends overtime on guessing, withdraws from the conversation, and, in the ugly correlations, walks toward isolation and cognitive narrowing. A major dementia-prevention review has named uncorrected hearing loss the single largest modifiable risk factor it could find — modifiable meaning a cause you can actually do something about, unlike age or genes — ahead of every other habit on the list, worth on its own something like a twelfth of the population’s dementia risk, roughly one case in twelve; a later update spread the case across fourteen such factors and put roughly half of all dementia cases within reach of some combination of them. Aiding the ear is not a miracle for dementia. It is a tool that keeps a channel the brain was already using, and the invoice for skipping it is larger than vanity usually admits. The temperature is warm-to-hot as prevention of a preventable narrowing, not as a cure for every plaque. People skip the aid because of vanity and cost and fiddle. They do not skip it because the science said the ear was decorative.
Sleep is a tool that looks like nothing.
It is not nothing. It is clearance, consolidation, a reset of the day’s staff schedule, a metabolic and immune negotiation. Take it away and the organ does not reveal hidden capacity. It reveals a drunk. The last fifty years sold sleep as optional because the lights became cheap. That sale was a mismatch, and Chapter 36 will name the carrot that made the sale easy. Here, the temperature is hot as a requirement and warm as a protocol. How much, exactly, for this person, this age, this job: argued. That you cannot enhance a mind by permanently subtracting the night: not argued, except by people who are selling the subtraction.
Education is the slowest tool and the one that actually moved civilizations.
It is not a chip. It is other people, over years, installing compression tricks — reading, numbers, a trade, a way to notice that a claim has no invoice. It changes what a worldline — this book’s word for one person’s path through time — can do without changing the species. It has a brutal invoice: childhood hours, teachers who themselves had to be made, a state that decides who is in the room. It is hot as a lever. It is cold as a promise that a headset in a weekend will do the same job. The ten-percent story loves the headset because the headset can be boxed. A childhood cannot.
Stimulants are a warmer, messier drawer.
Caffeine is the world’s most popular cognitive enhancer and nobody puts it on a slide next to helium. It buys alertness with a known jitter, a known sleep theft if you take it late, a known habituation. Amphetamine-class drugs, in a person with a diagnosed attention disorder, can be the difference between a life that will vote for Tuesdays and a life that will not. In a person without that disorder, they can be a borrowed morning with an afternoon invoice: appetite, sleep, a heart rate, a temptation to raise the dose when the borrowed morning becomes the new floor. Warm as tools with trade-offs. Cold as a species upgrade. The honest kitchen labels the score. It does not pretend the score is free.
Other pharmacological “enhancers” — a wakefulness drug, a memory-ish candidate, a psychedelic with a therapy wrapped around it — live in the same temperature band when they live at all: small, messy effects, trial-shaped, not a cupboard opening. Many do not live. They live in talks. A talk is cold until a protocol can fail in public.
A chip that returns a lost function is Chapter 31’s logic in metal.
Cochlear implants are the existence proof: a microphone, a processor, an electrode string in the cochlea, the coiled inner-ear organ that turns sound into a nerve signal, a brain that already knew how to do hearing and will, over months, learn this new, cruder code. Scars, batteries, a range of outcomes, a culture argument about what a cure is. Local. A motor-cortex implant that lets a paralyzed person move a cursor or a robotic hand: local, scarred, astonishing, and not a spare-tank unlock. Deep-brain stimulators that quiet a tremor: local, and increasingly closed-loop, meaning the device senses the tremor starting and answers on its own instead of running a constant, unguided signal. The temperature is hot as restored channel in the cases that work, warm as how far this generalizes, cold as and then we upload the rest. Do not let a restored channel launder a hidden ninety.
What would a real enhancement look like, if we keep the word?
A tool that raises a function above the person’s own baseline in a domain they already had, with an invoice they can name, without pretending the organ was off. Caffeine in a tired surgeon is a small, real instance. A well-taught method for a calculation you used to fumble is a larger one. A future drug that safely lifts working memory by a slice would be news. It would still be a tool. It would still meet the many clocks. It would still meet the carrot. It would not be a different species.
The cold costume is specific.
It says: new challenges are coming — ten thousand years of them, or a Mars city, or a job that does not exist — and therefore we must unlock the unused mind. The “therefore” is a smuggle. New challenges are met the way old ones were: with the organ on duty, with other people, with tools, with time, with institutions that do not snap. Chapter 42 is the meeting. Chapter 44 is the expensive reroute. Chapter 39 is the refusal to let a calendar do physics. Here, notice that the costume needs the ten-percent lie the way a lockpick needs a lock. If the organ is already in use, the unlock is unemployed.
Look first before you overwrite a childhood because a slide said enhancement. A child’s brain is a construction site, not a tank. Stimulants, if they are the right tool, are a tool. A marketplace of “make your child 150 percent” is the carrot aimed at a parent. The parent has a wanting-loop too. The child cannot consent to a species project. They can consent, later, to glasses.
She uses checklists because memory is a staff, not a miracle. She sleeps in a bunk that smells faintly of basil and dust because the organ has a night shift. She does not wait for a chip to make the radio delay shorter. The delay is light. Light is late on purpose. A mind that is already on duty can still be a mind that uses a notebook.
A toolbox is not a confession of defeat. Writing that holds a day, checklists that catch a miss, hearing aids that return a room, scored pills that land on time, closed-loop stimulators that quiet a tremor, a cochlear code a brain must relearn for months: local restorations with scars and schedules. Cold is the empty tank labeled ninety percent, as if the label filled it. Warm is the trade-off you can name out loud. Hot is the false ten-percent sentence that dies every time it is printed and somehow returns in the next pitch deck, because a lockpick needs a lock. Keep the toolbox. Evict the loft. Take the tool if the Tuesday votes for it. Put the spare-tank sermon down before it hires the carrot.
Where the popular version goes wrong.
The slide promotes the noun and crops the receipt. Look first: ask what was measured, what still needs Earth or a body, what the checklist still leaves empty.
Rule: Cognitive tools have two temperatures. Restoration can be warm; unlocking a spare tank stays cold.

## 36. The Carrot Has No Off-Switch

The carrot has no off-switch.
Book 1 said it about a feast and a fake. It is still true if the feast is a long life. You do not need those pages. The loop can be taught in this kitchen. A Pleistocene wanting-loop in a body that lasts 120 years is not a wise king. It is a mismatch with more calendar. Look first before you call that an upgrade.

Figure 10. A loop: want, chase, stamp, want. No switch. One extra tick marked more calendar.
Food is not a sandwich. Food is a slope.
A body stays organized by running downhill on a difference: hot to cold, charged to spent, food-molecule to ash. The kitchen name for the ash is waste. Heat is the waste you cannot hide. A nervous system is a staff hired to keep the slopes coming — to find the fruit, the salt, the copy, the ally, the shade. The staff does not mail a pamphlet titled reproduce, then maintain. It mails a carrot. Those who found the act reinforcing left more descendants. Those who found it a chore, all else equal, left fewer. Pleasure is not the cosmic point.
where the point is: a copy that ships, and a body that lasts long enough to ship it.
Wanting is one juice. Liking is another.
Wanting is the seek loop: a cue tagged as worth chasing, a go, a narrowing of the room until the cue is got. Dopamine — a brain chemical that carries signals between neurons — in the popular mouth, is this juice, and the popular mouth is not entirely wrong and is too simple. Liking is the completion stamp: do that again. The stamp can be small. The seek can be huge. A person can want a thing they no longer like. A person can like a thing they barely chase. Addiction, in the ugly cases, is a seek loop that has uncoupled from the stamp and from the vote you would cast on a Tuesday. This is not a sermon about vice. It is staffing. The same house hired people who barely want the old feasts, or want a different menu. That is allowed. A lottery that must work on average will throw off quiet tickets.
The carrot has no off-switch labeled this is only a picture.
A gull will sit a giant fake egg in preference to its own. A fish will attack a slab of the right color more fiercely than a rival. That is a supernormal stimulus: the cue, turned up, outbids the thing. We are a primate with cheap infinite pictures, cheap infinite sugar, cheap infinite almost-wins. The factory sells the cue without the invoice — no second person who can leave, no pathogen, no child, a new face every thirty seconds; a cupboard of sweetness that would have been a week of hunting; a feed that never ends. Novelty re-opens the wanting. Industry industrialized the novelty. Hot as a mechanism. Not a verdict on a species, and not a proof that everyone bought a ticket. Plenty of people shrug. Plenty of people drown. Both are allowed by the same wiring.
If you have Book 1, Chapter 30 was this loop in the service of a shuffle and a fake. Courtesy: that is all. The rest of the argument, here, does not need a second origin or a solvent paper. It needs a long life.
A longer life is more calendar for the same loop.
That is the sentence the helium slide does not print.
Give a wanting-loop more Tuesdays and you have not given it wisdom. You have given it more cues. More years to chase a status that will not stamp. More years to open a cupboard. More years to refresh a feed. More years to sit a fake egg because the fake egg is larger than the real one and the off-switch was never hired. Some people will use the years for basil and a log. Some will not. The mesh did not select for a 120-year philosopher. It selected for a seeker who did not die before the copy shipped. The philosopher is a late, expensive hobby of a well-fed ape. The hobby is real. It is not a default setting that clocks in when the joint is fixed.
Look first.
A slide that says enhancement is a cue. It is designed — by people who also have a loop — to tag as worth chasing. The tag can be healthspan, which this book will defend as a product. The tag can be a different species by Friday, which this book will not. The difference is not a mood. It is whether the invoice can be named and whether the many clocks were consulted. A parent chasing an enhanced child is a loop aimed at a construction site. A founder chasing 1,000 years is a loop aimed at a fuse that Chapter 29 said is not on the counter. A reader chasing a spare ninety percent is a loop aimed at a cupboard Chapter 34 emptied.
Mismatch is the grown-up word.
A body built for scarcity meets a kitchen of cheap cues. A sleep system built for a dark night meets a cheap light. An attention system built for a leopard and a gossip meets a pocket that never stops gossiping. A status system built for a village meets a planet of strangers who can see you fail. None of this is new as a complaint. It is hot as a pattern, and the industry that builds the pocket has said so out loud: engineers have described tuning a feed’s reward on the same intermittent schedule a slot machine uses, because a payout that arrives unpredictably keeps the seek loop running longer than one that arrives on a clock. A device meant to be checked has, in recent surveys, been checked by its owner well over a hundred times a day. That is not a moral failing measured in willpower. It is a supernormal cue doing exactly the job supernormal cues have always done, built by people who read the same wiring diagram this chapter just read.
The new, ruder fact is what happens when medicine becomes good enough to add calendar without adding an off-switch. HIV became a condition and left people with decades the virus would have stolen — a hot win — and those decades are still lived with the same loops, the same cupboards, the same unkind feeds. The win was the decades. The sermon that the decades would automatically become a wise kingdom was cold.
What would an off-switch even be?
Not a lobotomy. Not a moral lecture. A tool, if it exists, would be something that lets wanting stay hired for the mornings you vote for and stay quieter for the fakes you do not. Sleep is a weak, real version: the night ends the day’s seek. A hearing aid is a weak, real version: the room comes back and the isolation-loop loosens. Education is a slow version: you learn to notice a cue that has no invoice. Therapy, for some, is a practice of uncoupling seek from stamp. None of these is a master switch. The cold version is a drug that deletes wanting. That is not enhancement. That is a different kind of chair.
A second origin might have hired a different carrot, or no carrot, or a committee instead of a skull. Courtesy: that zoo is not this book’s. This ape is. This ape’s loop is old, cheap to spoof, and not a king.
She wants the basil to live. That is a loop. She logs the pH anyway. That is a tool she uses on the loop: a measurement that can embarrass a sentiment. The capsule arrives with an optimistic label. The label is a cue. She takes the dose for the joint, which is a Tuesday she would vote for, and not for the species-change, which is a fake egg. The radio is late. Wanting will not make it early. Light does not care.
The honest upgrade, if you want the noun, is not a longer seek. It is more Tuesdays you would vote to repeat, with the seek still in the house, and a kitchen that knows the difference between a tool and a fake. Chapter 28 split the pots — lifespan in one, healthspan in the other. This chapter says why the pots get confused. The carrot will still be running at 80, and at 100, and at 122 if you are the tail. Plan the morning as if that were true. It is.
Mismatch with more calendar is the polite name for a Pleistocene wanting-loop living in a body the savannah never budgeted for. Wanting stamps; liking is another juice; the off-switch was never on the hiring plan. Sugar, feeds, slots, slides that say enhancement: same family, same mechanism, the cue turned up until it outbids the thing. Look first. A longer healthspan without loop hygiene is more chase dressed as wisdom. Time bought without attention paid is not a win you would vote to repeat.
