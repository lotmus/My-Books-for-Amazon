# PART IV — Who Stays

## 13. Who Stays

She hits the airlock and thinks, for a second, of the kitchen she left: a real one, with a clock that lied about Andromeda and told the truth about casseroles. Most of the species is still in that kitchen. They did not buy a ticket. They will not buy a ticket. A camp on a dead world is a staffing choice for a few thousand worldlines, maybe tens of thousands if the posters have a lucky decade. It is not a migration. It is a field trip that forgot to end.
So the question people ask — once we leave, will Earth recover for those who stayed? — has a rude first word. We. Most of us stay.

A ticket is not only money, though money is a filter with a mean streak. A ticket is a body that can take the g, a year that can take the radiation, a nation that will let you go, a program that has a seat, a window that is open, a family that can stand the delay. Most bodies, most years, most nations, most families: stay. That is not a moral. It is a headcount.
Who is “we,” when the slide says we must become a multiplanetary species?
Sometimes it means a company. Sometimes it means a flag. Sometimes it means a species that has been promoted, without a vote, to a passenger list it will never see. The promotion is the rude part. A person in a floodplain, a person in a heat wave, a person whose clinic closed, a person who will never be offered a physical for a stack, is being asked to cheer for a basket that will not hold them. They may still like the photograph. Liking a photograph is allowed. Being drafted into a we that leaves is not a measurement.
Mara’s sister, in the real kitchen, uses we the way the slide uses it. She means: our kind, our cleverness, our backup. She does not mean: I have a boarding time. Mara has a boarding time that is a window home, if the tin still holds. She does not have a species in her pocket.
Rohan’s mother lives in a city that floods in the ugly years. The flood is not a metaphor. She asked him, before he left, whether the camp would make the water recede. He told her the truth, which is Chapter 15’s title, and she nodded the way people nod when the truth is not the comfort they paid for. She stays. She is the recovery question. Not the basil. Not the slide. Her street.
A few thousand settlers, even a few tens of thousands, are a town on Earth and a rounding error in a species. They are a large outpost if they exist. They are not a departure of us. The recovery of the kitchen — air, ice, plastics, the dead — is a question about the people who still cook in it. Chapters 14 through 16 are that question. This chapter is the refusal to let the question put on a spacesuit and leave the room.
Look first. The world that already has a staff is this one. The staff is not a remnant. The staff is the species.
Headcount without a costume.
Launch cadence is a machine’s manners, not a migration’s. Even a lucky decade of pads and stacks does not become a species-move. Seats are scarce. Windows are scarce. Bodies that can take the g and the dose are scarce. Nations that will let you go are scarce. Families that can stand the delay are scarce. Multiply the scarcities and you get a village, maybe a campus, not a nation. The other eight billion — the number moves, the order of magnitude does not — still open a real oven. They are not a remnant waiting for the ambitious to finish packing. They are the staff. Calling them a remnant is how a slide promotes itself into a Noah story without building an ark that fits.
A ticket’s filters are rude on purpose, because physics and politics are rude. Money is one filter. Citizenship is another. Age, bone density, a medical board, a security clearance, a year of training that is also a year of not being on your sister’s coast — those are filters. Most people fail them without failing as people. The we on the slide does not mention the filters. The we wants a cheer. A cheer is allowed. A draft is not a measurement.
What a few thousand worldlines are, if they exist.
They are a large outpost. They are a town’s worth of names on a roster that still depends on a cargo cult. They are a news cycle. They are not us leaving. They are a staffing choice. Recovery of the kitchen — air, ice, plastics, the dead — is a question about the people who still cook in it, vote in it, flood in it, open a clinic in it, or watch the clinic close. Chapters 14 through 16 keep that question in the room. This chapter nails the door so the question cannot put on a spacesuit and claim the hallway.
Rohan’s mother asked whether the camp would make the water recede. The honest answer is no, and the honesty is not a sneer at the camp. The camp was never a pump for her street. Selling it as a pump is the costume. She stays. She is why the full Earth sits large in the figure and the camp is a tiny mark. The mark can matter to the people inside it. It does not get to rename the disk.
Mara’s sister still says we the way the slide says it. Mara has a window home, if the tin holds. She does not have a species in her pocket. On the delay, she hears a founder use we about a passenger list that will never include her sister. She does not vote. She writes most stay in the log, under hot, and then she logs the pH, because the tray is the staff she can actually bully.
Kinds of clocks for who stays.
There is the launch clock: how often a pad can throw a seat. There is the medical clock: who can take the dose for a season. There is the political clock: who is allowed to go. There is the family clock: who can stand the delay and the risk. Official years for camps will move. The headcount clock does not care. Eight billion is not a mood. It is a staff.
Most stay is the hot sentence under every poster.

The headcount, run honestly.
Since the first person left the atmosphere in 1961, a little under eight hundred people have flown to space, counting every flight by every nation, including the ones who went more than once. That is a number small enough to fit, with room to spare, in a single decent-sized theater. Of those, twenty-eight have gone as far as the Moon — twenty-four of them between 1969 and 1972, all men, all from one country, and four more this year, the first to make the trip in more than half a century, on the flyby Chapter 6 covered. Twelve have walked on the surface, all of them still from that first wave; nobody has landed since. The entire population of humans who have ever left low Earth orbit could be seated at a few dozen dinner tables.
Scale that against the number of humans alive at any point since 1961 — always several billion, now approaching eight — and the fraction who have left the planet at all is a number with several zeros after the decimal point before you hit a meaningful digit. This is not a criticism of the space program. It is an honest unit of measurement, and the book needs it before the next three chapters can mean anything: whatever “who stays” is asking about, it is asking about essentially everyone, on any clock this book can responsibly print.
What a ticket actually costs, beyond money.
Money is the filter people name first, and it is real: a seat has historically cost, depending on the era and the vehicle, anywhere from tens of millions of dollars for a government-run flight to, more recently, tens of millions for a commercial seat to orbit alone, before anyone has left for the Moon or beyond. But money is not the only filter, and the others do not show up on an invoice.
A body has to survive the trip. Crews selected for spaceflight have historically been screened for cardiovascular health, vision, and dozens of other criteria that quietly exclude most humans on Earth before age or opportunity even enter the picture. A launch subjects a body to several times its own weight for minutes at a time; a lunar or Martian transit subjects it to months of reduced or absent gravity, with measurable bone and muscle loss that some crew members do not fully recover from even after return; the radiation dose for a multi-year Mars round trip, by current estimates, approaches or exceeds career limits in a single mission. None of that is a filter you can buy past. It is a filter biology applies before the ticket agent does.
A nation has to let you go, which means a passport, a security clearance in some programs, a government’s willingness to spend political capital on your seat. A family has to survive the absence — months to years of it, with a radio delay instead of a phone call, and a real chance the traveler does not come home. Twenty percent of NASA’s Space Shuttle fleet was lost with all aboard, across a genuinely small number of missions. A program has to have a seat at all, on a stack with a truly modest number of seats per flight, on a cadence measured in years between crewed missions to anywhere past low orbit. Stack all those filters and the eight billion do not compete for a scarce resource. They are, almost all of them, not in the competition, because the competition was never open to them.
The rude fact, said plainly, because plainly is the only honest register for it.
“We” is a word that gets borrowed without asking. A founder who says “we are becoming a multiplanetary species” is not describing a plan that includes the reader. He is describing a plan that includes, at generous most-optimistic scale, a headcount in the thousands to tens of thousands over a matter of decades — a number Chapter 15 runs the emissions arithmetic on, and it is a rounding error against a species of eight billion. The “we” is doing rhetorical work the plan cannot do: it is recruiting the reader’s identity into a project the reader will, with near certainty, never physically join, so that the reader cheers for a basket that was never sized to hold them.
This is not a claim that the reader should resent the crew, or the program, or the founder. People are allowed to admire an achievement they will not personally participate in; most human achievements work exactly that way. It is a claim that admiration and participation are different postures, and the slide’s job is to blur them, because a species that believes it is collectively leaving will fund the leaving differently than a species that understands a few thousand of its members are going while the rest stay and pay the rocket bill either way.
Rohan’s mother, named for the first time with her own weight.
She lives in a delta city that floods on a schedule that used to be rare and is now merely irregular. She has asked her son, more than once, in messages that take the long way to reach him, whether the camp he works in will help her street. He does not lie to her. He tells her the camp will not move a single sandbag on her block, that the people who fund the camp are not the people who will fix the levee, and that the two problems are being solved, if they are solved at all, by entirely different budgets, wholly distinct engineers, wholly separate decades. She is not devastated by this. She already suspected it. She is, if anything, relieved to hear someone say it without a sales pitch attached.
She represents, in this book, the actual “we”: the several billion for whom the question of Mars is a spectator question, answered by a headline once a year, while the actual determinant of whether her street floods again is a storm drain budget, a levee committee, and a climate that Chapter 14 and 15 will measure in its own units. Conflating her fate with the fate of a Mars program is not a kindness to her. It is a way of not talking about the storm drain.
What this chapter is not saying, stated so it cannot be misread.
This is not an argument that spaceflight is a waste, or that the thousands who might go should not go, or that admiration for the achievement is naive. It is an argument that the achievement’s scale has to be reported honestly before the next question — will Earth recover if people leave — can be asked without absurdity. You cannot ask whether a rounding error’s departure heals a planet. You can only ask what the planet’s staff, who are and will remain almost everyone, are going to do about the planet. Chapter 14 does the arithmetic on that question properly. This chapter’s only job was to fix the headcount so the arithmetic has an honest input.
The selection process, since “who stays” starts with “who could even apply.”
A recent astronaut selection round, run by the American agency, drew a little over twelve thousand applications for a class that would eventually number roughly a dozen. That funnel — thousands to one, before any of the filters named below even engage — is itself a filter, and it deserves a moment’s attention, because the twelve thousand who applied were already a self-selected sliver: people with the specific combination of an advanced technical degree or equivalent flight experience, a body that had already passed an informal health bar just to imagine applying, and a life circumstance that allowed months of unpaid, unglamorous application paperwork in the first place. The eight billion never got near the twelve thousand. The twelve thousand never got near the dozen. Each stage of that funnel is a real filter, applied by genuine people, for reasons that are mostly defensible and none of which the slide mentions when it says we.
Other nations run smaller versions of the same funnel, with the same shape: thousands of applicants, filtered by education, by health, by a security or political vetting process specific to that country, down to a handful of finalists who then compete for a number of flight seats smaller still. The shape does not change because the nation does. A species that wants to send a person to space is, every time, choosing that person from a pool that was never the whole species to begin with, filtered by criteria most of the species never had the chance to meet.
What the corps actually looks like, demographically, and why it matters.
Astronaut corps around the world have, over the decades, skewed toward military test pilots and research scientists, two professions that are themselves filtered by education, by nationality, by economic opportunity, in ways that predate any space agency’s own selection criteria. Agencies have made real, public efforts in recent selection rounds to widen the pool — by nationality, by gender, by professional background — and the efforts have moved the numbers, modestly, in the direction they were aimed. The corps remains, on any honest count, a small and unrepresentative slice of the species that funds it. This is not an accusation. It is the same headcount arithmetic this chapter has been running from a different angle: the funnel was never going to produce a demographic mirror of eight billion people, because a funnel that narrow cannot mirror anything. It can only select.
Apollo’s share, revisited from the headcount side.
Chapter 9’s appendix gave the budget-share version of this comparison; here is the headcount version, because the two numbers, read together, tell a sharper story than either alone. At its peak, the Apollo program employed, directly and through its contractors, somewhere in the neighborhood of four hundred thousand people — engineers, technicians, seamstresses hand-stitching pressure suits, typists, machinists, a workforce large enough to function as a small nation’s worth of industrial employment, all aimed at putting a total of twenty-four people within reach of the Moon. Sit with the ratio: roughly seventeen thousand people employed on the ground for every one person who left Earth’s orbit. Spaceflight, even at its most successful, has never been a small operation relative to its headcount of travelers. It has only ever been small relative to the species funding it.
That ratio has not obviously improved with modern commercial efficiency. A modern heavy-lift program’s combined workforce — the prime contractor, the sub-tier suppliers this chapter’s own appendix already walked through, the ground crews, the mission controllers — still runs into the tens of thousands for a vehicle that, as of this kitchen, has carried nobody at all. The species has gotten better at building rockets. It has not gotten better at needing fewer people to do it. Every flown astronaut, on any nation’s ledger, still stands on top of a pyramid of ordinary labor that the poster never photographs, and that pyramid, not the astronaut at its peak, is where almost the entire species’s actual relationship to spaceflight lives.
Suborbital tourism is its own small, separate ledger. It deserves a different headcount.
Beyond the orbital and lunar programs this chapter has counted, two commercial ventures have, across the past several years, flown paying and invited passengers on brief suborbital hops — arcing above the internationally recognized edge of space for a few minutes of weightlessness before returning to the same runway or landing pad, without reaching orbital velocity at all. Several hundred people have now flown this way, a number that, added to the orbital tally given above, still leaves the total human population that has ever left the ground and come back changed by the experience under two thousand, against a species of eight billion.
It is worth keeping suborbital tourism in its own column rather than folding it into this chapter’s larger argument uncritically, because the physics is genuinely different: a suborbital hop requires a small fraction of the velocity, and therefore the propellant and the risk, that reaching orbit does, and the medical and financial filters, while still real, are measurably lower than an orbital or lunar seat’s. It is the closest thing currently flying to the low end of this chapter’s own ticket-filter ladder — still expensive, still medically screened, still nowhere near open to most of the species, but a genuine data point on how those filters loosen as the altitude and the ambition both come down. It is not evidence that space is opening to everyone. It is evidence that the bottom rung of a very tall ladder has, for the first time, a few hundred more people standing on it than it used to.
A filter this chapter has not yet named: language.
Every multinational crew this century has trained and operated in at least two languages, most often the two nations’ own tongues plus a working technical vocabulary neither crew member learned at home, because a checklist misread under stress in a second language is exactly the kind of small error that matters far more than the dramatic one, book-wide. A candidate who is medically flawless, well-trained, and fluent in only one language is, in most current multinational programs, not a candidate at all. This filter never appears on a poster and rarely appears in a press release. It is genuine, it is rude in the same way every other filter this chapter has named is rude, and it quietly removes a further share of an already tiny pool before any of the more dramatic filters even apply.
Earth has its own rehearsal rooms for isolation: analog missions.
Beyond the simulator time Chapter 6 described for a specific flight, several standing programs run multi-week or multi-month isolation studies on Earth — a sealed habitat in a remote desert built to mimic a Martian surface mission’s footprint, a purpose-built chamber where volunteer crews live under simulated mission constraints for periods stretching to a full year, all specifically to gather the psychological and interpersonal data that matters, this chapter argues, as much as any physical filter. Published results from these programs have documented real interpersonal strain, communication breakdowns, and the same kind of factional splits Chapter 12’s appendix found in the closed-ecosystem trial — evidence that the social engineering problem of a small, isolated crew is at least as stubborn as the life-support engineering problem, and considerably less funded.
This matters for the headcount argument this chapter has built from a different angle. The filters this chapter names — body, nation, family — are the filters that decide who gets considered. Analog missions are studying a filter this chapter has not yet named directly: which combinations of otherwise-qualified people can actually live together, for years, in a can, without the mission ending in the same factional collapse the closed-ecosystem trial suffered. It is, on the evidence gathered so far, a harder problem than the engineering, and one this book’s seven rows do not have a clean column for.

The pulse, worked as an actual number.
A gigatonne is a billion tonnes — roughly the mass of a large mountain, or about two hundred thousand fully loaded oil tankers, moved through the air in the form of a gas. Human activity currently adds somewhere in the range of thirty-five to forty gigatonnes of carbon dioxide to the atmosphere every year, mostly from burning coal, oil, and gas, with a smaller share from cement-making and land clearing. That is not a metaphor. It is a mass balance, measured by monitoring stations, cross-checked against fuel sales records, and it has been rising for two centuries with only a handful of brief dips — the largest in the modern record was a single-digit percentage drop during the worst year of a global pandemic shutdown, and it rebounded within a year once activity resumed.
Of each year’s pulse, roughly half is taken up fairly quickly by the ocean’s surface waters and by plant growth on land. The other half accumulates in the atmosphere, where it is joined by the portion of previous years’ pulses that has not yet been absorbed. The absorption is not instant and it is not complete: models built from ice-core records of past natural pulses — the ones released by ancient volcanic and methane events — suggest that after a large pulse of carbon, roughly a fifth to a quarter of it is still detectable in the atmosphere after a thousand years, because the slowest sinks, deep ocean circulation and rock weathering, work on a clock of centuries to tens of thousands of years, not decades. This is not a claim about doom. It is a claim about persistence, and persistence is the only property this chapter needs.
Ice, at the pace it actually moves.
The Greenland ice sheet holds enough water, if it all melted, to raise global sea level by a little over seven meters; the Antarctic ice sheet holds enough for around fifty-eight meters. Neither is going to vanish on a human lifetime’s clock under any scenario currently modeled. What is measured, by satellites that weigh the ice from orbit using tiny changes in gravity, is a net loss from both sheets that has accelerated over the past three decades, currently summing to several hundred billion tonnes of ice a year between the two of them — a number large enough to be one of the primary drivers of present-day sea level rise, and small enough, against the total mass of either sheet, that “melted” is the wrong verb. The correct verb is “shrinking,” on a clock that responds to today’s heat with next century’s water level, because ice sheets have thermal inertia the way a stockpot has thermal inertia: you can turn off the burner and the pot stays hot for a good while after.
Mountain glaciers, which are far smaller individually, respond faster — many have visibly retreated within living memory, some measurably within a single decade — and their loss matters enormously to the communities and rivers that depend on their meltwater on a seasonal clock, even though their total contribution to global sea level is smaller than the ice sheets’. Sea ice, which floats and adds no water to the ocean when it melts because it is already displacing its own weight, responds fastest of all — Arctic summer sea ice extent has declined by roughly forty percent since satellite records began in 1979 — and its disappearance matters for a different reason: it is a mirror, reflecting sunlight that a dark ocean would otherwise absorb, so losing it is not just a symptom of warming but a contributor to more of it.
Plastics, weighed rather than described.
Global plastic production now exceeds four hundred million tonnes a year, a figure that has roughly doubled every fifteen to twenty years since mass production began in the mid-twentieth century. Of the plastic ever made, only a small fraction — commonly estimated at under ten percent — has ever been recycled; a larger share has been incinerated or landfilled, and a share nobody can precisely measure has entered rivers, coastlines, and open ocean, where it does not decompose in any meaningful human timescale. It breaks physically into smaller pieces — microplastics, then nanoplastics — driven by sunlight and wave action, but the polymer itself, chemically, persists for centuries at minimum, by the estimates of the materials scientists who study its degradation. A plastic bottle in a landfill today will very likely outlast every reader of this book, and their grandchildren.
This is a drawer, not a gas. You cannot pull it back out of the ocean once it has dispersed into fragments smaller than a grain of sand, distributed through every ocean basin and, increasingly, found in human blood and organ tissue in small but measurable quantities whose long-term health effects are still being studied. Stopping production today — which nobody is proposing to do, and which is a policy question this book will not adjudicate — would only stop the drawer from filling further. It would not empty it.
Extinction, given the only unit that fits it.
The background rate of extinction, estimated from the fossil record over tens of millions of years, is on the order of one to five species per million species per year. Current extinction rates, as best biologists can estimate across well-studied groups, run tens to hundreds of times higher than that background rate — a range that has led much of the field to describe the present era as a sixth mass extinction, comparable in rate, though not yet in total toll, to the five previous mass extinctions recorded in the fossil record, the last of which ended the age of the non-avian dinosaurs.
A species, once its last individual dies, does not have a recovery clock, because recovery implies a return to a prior state and extinction is definitionally the state that cannot be returned from. A cloned individual from preserved genetic material — attempted, as of this kitchen, for a small number of recently extinct or critically endangered species with mixed and preliminary results — produces an individual, not a functioning population with its original ecological role restored, and even the most optimistic version of that technology does not apply to the vast majority of species that vanish without ever having their genome sequenced or preserved anywhere. This is the coldest pot in the kitchen. Every other pot in this chapter eventually cools, on some clock, however long. This one does not.
A tide-gauge tech, reading her own gauge.
Her instrument does not measure launches. It measures the height of the water against a fixed marker, several times a day, and has done so at her station for decades before her and, if funded, will do so for decades after. The trend line does not jump when a rocket lights and does not dip when a rocket program is cancelled. It responds to two things: how much ice has melted and how much the ocean itself has physically expanded as it warms, a process called thermal expansion that is, by several careful accountings, responsible for roughly a third to a half of observed sea level rise over the past several decades, a fact that surprises people who assume all sea level rise is melting ice.
She has explained this to visitors more times than she can count, and the conversation always arrives, eventually, at the same question: will it get better if people leave for Mars. She has stopped being annoyed by the question. She now simply points at the gauge and asks what a headcount in another gravity well would do to the number on the marker. The visitors, to their credit, usually work it out themselves before she has to say it. The marker does not know Mars exists.
Named events, so the clocks are not only abstractions.
The Larsen ice shelves, a chain of floating ice platforms along the Antarctic Peninsula, have collapsed in stages over the past three decades — Larsen A in 1995, Larsen B in a matter of weeks in 2002, an event so fast that scientists monitoring it by satellite described watching a structure that had held for roughly ten thousand years disintegrate on a human news cycle. A floating shelf’s collapse does not, by itself, raise sea level, because floating ice already displaces its own weight, the same physics Chapter 14’s main text already gave you for sea ice. What a shelf’s collapse does is remove the brake: the ice sheets behind these shelves, on land, had been held back by the shelf’s physical presence, and glaciologists watched the glaciers that used to feed Larsen B accelerate measurably within years of its breakup, because the plug that had been slowing them into the ocean was simply gone.
Coral reefs offer a different, faster clock. The Great Barrier Reef has experienced mass bleaching events — water too warm for too long, causing corals to expel the symbiotic algae that feed and color them — in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, and 2024, a frequency that has accelerated sharply within one professional lifetime; reef scientists who began their careers expecting to study bleaching as a rare, once-a-decade event have instead spent recent years studying it as a near-annual one. A reef can recover from a single bleaching event, over years, if the water cools in time and the coral was not too badly damaged. A reef bleached in consecutive years does not get the years it needs. This is a clock, running faster than the one most of this book runs on, and it is running on the same underlying heat.
What a named event buys the argument that a number alone cannot.
A statistic — several hundred billion tonnes of ice a year, forty percent less summer sea ice than 1979 — is true and also, for most readers, abstract in a way that is easy to set down. A named collapse, watched by name, dated, with a before-and-after satellite photograph that fits on one page, is harder to set down, because it has a shape a reader can hold. This chapter’s job was never to make the reader feel something for its own sake. It was to make the clock legible. A named ice shelf and a named reef, each with its own dated history, are the clock made legible. The dead do not care whether you remembered their names. The argument does.
Sea level, worked forward instead of only backward.
The scientific panel that assesses climate evidence for the world’s governments has, across its successive reports, offered a range of sea-level-rise projections tied explicitly to how much warming the century ahead actually produces: a lower-emissions pathway is associated with roughly a third to two-thirds of a meter of rise by 2100 relative to recent decades, a higher-emissions pathway with closer to a meter, and specific processes involving ice-sheet instability — mechanisms that are physically plausible and observed in the geological record but not yet confidently quantified — could push the higher end of that range meaningfully further, a possibility the panel’s own reports flag explicitly as a deep uncertainty rather than a settled number.
A meter of sea-level rise sounds, to most readers, like a survivable inconvenience: raise the seawall, move the furniture. The actual mechanism by which a meter matters is not a meter of water arriving at everyone’s door. It is a meter of baseline that turns what used to be a once-a-century storm surge into a once-a-decade event, and what used to be a once-a-decade flood into an annual one, for the low-lying, densely populated river deltas and coastal cities — Rohan’s mother’s city among the real-world class it represents — where a large share of the species actually lives. The range between the low and high pathway is not a rounding error. It is the difference between a manageable adaptation problem and a mass-displacement one, and which pathway the century takes is being decided now, by furnace and farm and forest policy, on a clock that does not care about a launch window.
Permafrost, the pot this chapter had not yet opened.
Beneath roughly a quarter of the land area of the Northern Hemisphere sits permafrost — ground that has remained frozen for at least two consecutive years, in many places for millennia, locking within it an estimated quantity of organic carbon, built up from dead plant matter that never fully decomposed in the cold, that some published estimates place at roughly twice the amount of carbon currently in Earth’s entire atmosphere. As permafrost thaws, that long-frozen organic matter becomes available to microbes for the first time in millennia, which decompose it and release both carbon dioxide and, in wetter, oxygen-poor conditions, methane — a molecule already named, earlier in this chapter, as a stronger blanket per unit mass than CO₂, though a shorter-lived one in the atmosphere.
This is a pot with a genuinely frightening property the others in this chapter mostly lack: it is a feedback loop rather than a simple response. Warming thaws permafrost; thawing permafrost releases greenhouse gas; released greenhouse gas causes more warming; more warming thaws more permafrost. The scale and speed of this feedback remain an active area of research, with published estimates for its total century-scale contribution to warming ranging widely, and the honest scientific position, as of this kitchen, is that the feedback is real, is already measurably underway in specific thawing regions, and is not yet confidently quantified at the global scale — precisely the kind of warm-not-hot claim this book has trained you, across thirteen chapters, to hold without either dismissing it or panicking about it. A pot with an unknown lid is still a pot. This one deserves watching more closely than most.
A number this chapter’s pots have not yet held: the insurance industry’s own accounting.
Reinsurance companies — firms that insure the insurers, pricing risk at a scale no single homeowner’s policy reflects — have, over the past two decades, revised upward, repeatedly and in published reports, their models for flood and storm-surge risk in exactly the coastal and delta regions this chapter’s own recovery clocks describe. This is not a political statement. It is an industry whose entire business model depends on pricing risk correctly, adjusting its prices because the historical data it used to rely on has stopped predicting the present. A market this literal about consequences, quietly raising premiums on Rohan’s mother’s street years before any government program admits the same trend out loud, is its own kind of clock — the coldest, most financially honest one this chapter has found.
Acidification, the pot this chapter’s stove had one more burner for.
Roughly a quarter to a third of the carbon dioxide humans add to the atmosphere each year is absorbed by the ocean, and that absorption was credited earlier as a mercy to the air pot — less staying airborne, a smaller pulse, a gentler needle. The same absorption is not a mercy to the ocean itself. Dissolved CO₂ reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, measurably lowering ocean pH — a shift of roughly a tenth of a point since industrialization began, which sounds small on the same logarithmic scale that made Mara’s tray-side pH readings matter so much, and represents, on that scale, a meaningful percentage increase in acidity, already measured, already affecting organisms — corals, shellfish, some plankton species — that build their skeletons and shells from a mineral form of calcium carbonate that dissolves more readily in more acidic water.
This is the ocean paying a bill the atmosphere pot’s own bookkeeping quietly moved off its own ledger. The two pots this chapter named for air and reef bleaching are related but not identical: bleaching is a heat problem, acidification is a chemistry problem, and a reef under both burdens at once is a reef fighting two clocks rather than one. A slide that claims Earth heals once the ambitious have gone would need to reverse a chemistry already measured in the ocean’s own pH meters, on a clock that has never once heard of a launch window.

The arithmetic, run with real numbers, once, carefully.
A resident of a wealthy nation — the kind of person statistically most likely to hold a spaceflight-adjacent job, let alone a seat on a rocket — accounts for somewhere in the range of eight to twenty tonnes of carbon dioxide a year through ordinary life: housing, transport, diet, the goods and services a modern economy delivers to their door. A resident of a lower-income nation accounts for a fraction of a tonne to a few tonnes. Global average sits around four to five tonnes per person per year, dragged down by the several billion people whose footprint is small and pulled up by the smaller number whose footprint is large.
Take ten thousand settlers — a genuinely optimistic figure for any Mars or Moon population within the next several decades, well beyond anything currently funded or scheduled — and assign them a footprint several times the wealthy-nation average, because early camp life runs on rockets, life support, and manufactured spare parts, none of which is a monastic existence. A generous figure might be fifty to a hundred tonnes per settler per year, camp-side, plus whatever fraction of the launch infrastructure’s own emissions you choose to allocate to them rather than to Earth’s ledger. Multiply it out and you get a number in the range of half a million to a million tonnes of CO₂ a year for the entire settlement. Compare that to global annual emissions of roughly thirty-seven billion tonnes, and the settlement’s share is on the order of one part in forty thousand to one part in seventy thousand — a rounding error so small that ordinary year-to-year noise in the global figure, driven by things like a mild winter or an industrial strike, regularly exceeds it several times over.
This is the whole claim, done honestly, with numbers a reader can check against public sources. The thermostat is not made of settlers. It is made of power plants, factories, vehicles, and land use, operated by the seven billion nine hundred ninety thousand people who did not go.
The swap, named so it cannot be missed twice.
The rhetorical move worth watching for goes like this: a speaker gestures at the abstract idea of humanity spreading to other worlds, invokes the real and serious problem of an overtaxed planet, and lets the listener’s mind quietly substitute “if people leave, the planet gets a break” for the true and much smaller claim, which is “if ten thousand people who were never going to significantly move the needle anyway relocate to a location that requires more resources per person, not fewer, Earth’s ledger is essentially unchanged.” The swap works because the two sentences share several words and opposite meanings, and the speaker is rarely required to say the second sentence out loud.
Collapse, treated honestly rather than as a thought experiment to score a point.
If global industrial activity actually stopped — not people leaving, but furnaces going cold, factories going dark, global trade halting — emissions would fall within months, and this is not speculation: it happened, briefly and partially, during the worst months of a global pandemic shutdown, when daily emissions dropped by roughly a sixth to a fifth at the peak, the sharpest sustained drop ever measured, and the annual total fell by a few percent for the year, still leaving atmospheric concentration higher at year’s end than it had started, because a slower pulse is still a pulse.
A genuine, sustained industrial collapse would go further than that brief dip, and it would also do something the pandemic dip revealed on a smaller scale: it would remove, along with the carbon dioxide, the aerosol pollution — soot and sulfate particles from the same smokestacks — that has been quietly reflecting some sunlight back to space and partially masking a portion of the warming those same industries caused. Researchers who study this effect estimate that aerosols are currently offsetting a meaningful fraction, plausibly in the range of a third to a half, of the warming that greenhouse gases alone would otherwise be producing. Remove the aerosols faster than you remove their warming cause — which is roughly what a sudden industrial stop would do, since aerosols wash out of the air in days to weeks while carbon dioxide lingers for centuries — and global temperature could, for a period of years to a decade or more, rise faster than it otherwise would have, even as emissions themselves have stopped. This is a real, published finding, not a rhetorical trick to defend industry. It is also not an argument against reducing emissions in an orderly way, which does not carry the same unmasking penalty, because an orderly reduction can be paired with reductions in the aerosol-heavy pollutants specifically, on a schedule that does not whipsaw the climate system.
The point of walking through this honestly is narrow and specific: it forecloses the lazy fantasy that any kind of societal collapse, industrial or otherwise, functions as a climate cleanup. It does not. It trades one kind of damage for another kind, on a different clock, with real people paying both bills.

A number this important deserves to survive a challenge. Here is the carbon footprint methodology behind it.
A “carbon footprint” is not one measurement. It is an accounting exercise, and different accountings draw the boundary in distinct places, which is why footprint figures for the same country can vary by a meaningful margin depending on the source. A territorial accounting counts emissions physically produced inside a country’s borders. A consumption-based accounting instead counts emissions embedded in everything that country’s residents buy, wherever in the world those goods were actually manufactured, which shifts a substantial share of a wealthy importing nation’s true footprint onto the exporting nations that did the manufacturing on its behalf. The two methods can disagree by twenty percent or more for a heavily trade-dependent economy. Neither method is a lie. Both are answering slightly separate questions, and a slide that quotes one number without saying which method produced it is not lying either, usually — it is simply not showing you the oven.
This chapter’s own settler-emissions arithmetic used a consumption-style estimate, deliberately, because a settler’s actual footprint is overwhelmingly a consumption question: almost nothing a camp consumes is locally produced, so almost the entire footprint is embedded in what gets shipped up the well. A territorial accounting, applied to a launch pad’s own physical location, would undercount a settlement’s true climate cost by attributing most of it to whichever nation’s factories built the parts. The consumption method is the honest one for this particular question, named here because a reader who checks this book’s numbers against a different source using a distinct method should know why the two numbers might not match to the decimal point, without either one being wrong.
The word “offset” gets used loosely. It deserves a harder look.
A carbon offset, in its intended design, is a credit representing one tonne of CO₂ either kept out of the atmosphere — a forest preserved that would otherwise have been logged — or actively removed from it, purchased by an emitter to counterbalance emissions it has not yet eliminated. The design is sound in principle. In practice, multiple independent investigations into major offset registries, across the past decade, have found that a large share of credited forest-preservation projects would very likely have preserved the same forest even without the offset payment — a problem researchers call additionality, meaning the credit claims a reduction that was never actually caused by the purchase — and that some projects’ claimed carbon storage has been substantially overestimated relative to what satellite and ground measurement later confirmed.
This chapter has already said, plainly, that rockets are not a carbon offset. It is worth being equally plain about the actual offset market: a great many real offsets, purchased in good faith by genuine companies wanting to do the honest thing, have turned out to buy considerably less atmospheric benefit than their certificates claimed. The lesson generalizes past space entirely, and this book will make it once, because it belongs here as much as anywhere: a market that sells the feeling of having solved a problem is not the same market as one that has solved it, and the gap between the two is exactly the kind of gap this whole book has spent sixteen chapters teaching a reader to find, on a slide, in a launch, or in a certificate framed on an office wall.
Three ledgers, since “global emissions” is not evenly distributed and the arithmetic changes depending on whose ledger you are reading.
A single nation currently accounts for roughly a third of global annual CO₂ emissions, having overtaken the historical leader in total annual output in the mid-2000s, though that same historical leader still holds, by a wide margin, the largest cumulative total emitted since industrialization began, because atmospheric CO₂ accumulates and a nation that industrialized a century earlier has had a century longer to add to the pile this chapter’s own pulse-and-tail arithmetic already described. A third major economy, industrializing later still, now accounts for a smaller but rapidly growing share, and per-person, its emissions remain a fraction of the two larger economies’ figures, a distinction that matters enormously in the international negotiations this chapter has not tried to adjudicate but cannot honestly ignore either.
None of these three ledgers has committed, as of this kitchen, to a Mars or lunar settlement program remotely close to the scale Part III describes. Say it plainly: the emissions arithmetic and the settlement arithmetic are, for now, almost entirely separate conversations, run by mostly separate institutions, funded from mostly separate budget lines. The settler-emissions rounding error calculated earlier in this chapter is not competing, dollar for dollar or tonne for tonne, with the three ledgers’ industrial emissions. It is a small number sitting next to a large one, and the real danger is not that the small number will grow to match the large one. It is that the small number’s mere existence will be used, rhetorically, to excuse inattention to the large one.
The two get blurred constantly. A last distinction: mitigation versus adaptation.
Mitigation means reducing the pulse itself — the furnaces this chapter has spent its pages on, burning less, differently, or not at all. Adaptation means living with the pulse already released — a seawall, a levee, a crop bred for a hotter season, the sandbags Rohan’s mother’s street has already needed more than once. Both are real, necessary, and entirely different budgets, run by mostly distinct institutions, on mostly separate clocks: mitigation pays off over the multi-decade tail this chapter’s pulse arithmetic already described, adaptation pays off immediately for the people who fund it. A settlement pitch that claims to be either is claiming something it cannot be. It reduces no furnace and builds no seawall. It is neither mitigation nor adaptation. It is, at most, a rounding error wearing one of their two costumes.
Additionality deserves a mechanism, not just a criticism. Here is how a satellite actually catches a felled forest.
The same orbital imagery this book’s earlier chapters have used for landing-site selection is, in a different application, the primary tool auditors now use to check offset-registry claims against reality: repeated satellite passes over a credited forest, compared frame to frame, flagging canopy loss a paper claim would otherwise have no independent way to catch. Several of the specific overestimation findings this chapter’s earlier material has already cited were made possible by exactly this technique — an offset project’s claimed preserved acreage checked, years later, against the actual satellite record, and found, in a meaningful share of cases, to have already lost canopy the credit had claimed to prevent.
The tool is a genuine improvement on the honor system it replaced, and it also belongs to the category of infrastructure this whole book keeps insisting is real and valuable: not a settlement, not a poster, a boring, unglamorous, repeatedly orbiting camera, doing the actual work of making a claim checkable. If this book has a favorite kind of hardware, it is this kind: the kind that exists specifically to keep a slide honest.

The industry, sized rather than gestured at.
A single heavy-launch vehicle burns, depending on its design and mission profile, somewhere between a few hundred and a few thousand tonnes of propellant to reach orbit. If that propellant is methane and oxygen, as several current and planned heavy vehicles use, the carbon dioxide released directly from combustion is a calculable number, in the range of a few hundred to a couple of thousand tonnes per launch — a figure worth comparing honestly to other things people measure in tonnes: a single large cargo ship crossing an ocean burns fuel producing carbon dioxide in a similar or larger range for one voyage; a coal power plant of ordinary size emits several million tonnes a year, meaning it produces in a single day what a heavy launch produces in one flight, many times over. At today’s launch cadences — even generously projecting forward a decade — the entire global launch industry’s direct emissions remain a rounding error against global industrial emissions, smaller than the annual output of a handful of individual coal plants.
That comparison is honest and it is also not the whole invoice, which is the warm claim this chapter exists to make. The warm claim is about scale, not about today’s cadence. If the industry of leaving grows toward the size that an actual multi-thousand-person settlement effort would require — not one heavy vehicle a month but dozens, not one pad but a coastline of them, not one propellant plant but a network of methane and oxygen production facilities running continuously, plus the mines, smelters, and factories on Earth that build all of it — then the comparison changes, because you are no longer comparing one flight to one coal plant. You are comparing an entire new heavy industry to the industries it would sit alongside. Nobody has built that industry yet. The chapter’s job is to notice, now, while it is still small, that the comparison will not stay flattering by default; it will stay flattering only if the industry that eventually gets built is engineered, deliberately, to be less carbon-intensive than the industries it resembles, which is a choice, not a law of physics.
Metals and cement, which do not photograph.
A launch pad is a slab of reinforced concrete and steel large enough to survive repeated ignitions of engines producing millions of pounds of thrust; the concrete alone for a single modern pad runs into tens of thousands of tonnes, and cement production is, on its own, responsible for roughly seven to eight percent of global carbon dioxide emissions worldwide, because the chemical process of making cement releases carbon dioxide directly from the limestone itself, independent of the energy used to heat it. A propellant plant sized to refuel a fleet of heavy vehicles needs steel tankage, cryogenic insulation, pumps, and power infrastructure on an industrial scale — the same category of manufacturing that builds refineries and chemical plants, with a similar carbon footprint per tonne of capacity, because the manufacturing processes are largely the same processes.
None of this is unique to space. It is unique in how rarely it gets mentioned in the same breath as a rocket launch, because the launch is the photogenic thirty seconds and the concrete pour, the steel mill run, and the years of mining that preceded it are the unphotogenic decade that made the thirty seconds possible. If a slide shows a rocket’s own plume as the entire carbon story of “going to Mars,” it has shown you one line of a much longer invoice and asked you to sign for the whole thing.
A phrase like “Earth is not our only option” or “this planet is not guaranteed” functions, whether or not the speaker intends it this way, as a permission slip: it tells a listener that urgency about the home planet’s problems can be traded for enthusiasm about an exit that does not, in fact, exist for the listener, and would not solve the listener’s problem even if it did. This book has not run that poll and will not pretend to cite one. The mechanism does not need a study to be plausible: a “backup planet,” like any other unproven future fix — a carbon-removal machine that does not exist yet, a fusion plant always a decade out — offers a listener permission to stop worrying about the boring fix that already exists, the storm drains and grid upgrades and building codes that actually move the needle on the tonnes in Chapter 14 and Chapter 15. Whether that permission measurably changes anyone’s actual vote or donation is an empirical question this book has not checked and marks, honestly, as unverified. This is not a claim that every person who admires a Mars program has been manipulated out of caring about the storm drain. It is a claim that the phrase does measurable work in a specific direction, and that work has a name: it is a vote, cast by the person who wrote the slide, spent on behalf of everyone who hears it and believes the planet has a backup.

Debris, the bill this chapter had not yet itemized.
Every launch that reaches orbit leaves something behind: a spent upper stage, a fairing half, a bolt that separated at the wrong moment, a flake of paint moving fast enough to punch through a hull. The count of tracked objects larger than a softball, in orbit around Earth right now, runs past forty thousand, and the count of untracked fragments too small to follow but large enough to kill a satellite or a spacewalker on impact is estimated in the hundreds of thousands to millions, because at orbital speeds — several kilometers a second — even a fleck of paint carries the kinetic energy of a rifle round. A single serious collision between two sizable objects has already, once, more than doubled the tracked debris count in the affected orbital band overnight, and the field that studies this problem has a name for the nightmare scenario, first described in a 1978 paper: a cascade, where debris from one collision causes further collisions, which produce more debris, which produce more collisions, until a whole orbital band becomes too dangerous to use for a generation.
An industry of leaving that scales toward city mass — many launches, many upper stages, many more objects in orbit — is not exempt from this bill just because its stated purpose is noble. Every additional launch is an additional roll of a die that gets rolled more often as cadence rises, and cadence rising is precisely the routine Chapters 10 through 12 spent hoping for. The honest sentence, matching this chapter’s own method: rockets are not a carbon offset, and a launch industry scaled toward settlement is also not a debris offset. It is a bill, itemized here so a later chapter does not have to discover it by surprise.

Orbital crowding is not an abstraction either. Here is a constellation, counted.
A single company’s satellite internet constellation had, by this kitchen, placed more active satellites in low Earth orbit than every other operator in the history of spaceflight combined — a number in the several thousands, climbing by hundreds more with every few weeks’ launch cadence, aimed eventually at a planned total running past ten thousand if every authorized phase is built. Other operators, from other nations and other companies, have announced competing constellations of comparable planned scale, aimed at the same orbital shells, for the same commercial reason: global broadband coverage is a genuinely valuable service, and the physics of providing it from low orbit actually requires thousands of satellites rather than a handful.
Astronomers have documented real interference from these constellations with ground-based observation — bright satellite trails crossing long-exposure images, a problem serious enough that the operator involved has committed to darkening treatments on newer satellites, with mixed and still-debated success. The debris question raised earlier compounds directly: a constellation of ten thousand satellites, each with an operational lifespan of a few years before replacement, is not ten thousand objects. Over a decade, it is tens of thousands of objects, launched, operated, and deorbited in sequence, each pass through that cycle a fresh roll of the same collision dice described above. None of this is a reason the constellations should not exist; the broadband they deliver is genuine and valuable to actual people, including some who will never otherwise touch a piece of space hardware in their lives. It is a reason the bill for an industry scaling toward routine, cheap, frequent launch was never going to stop at carbon. Orbit itself is a resource, and resources — the first page of this book already said so — are not infinite just because they are far away.
The bill of going has a literal address: a spaceport’s own backyard.
A launch site, wherever it is built, sits inside a real ecosystem, and the environmental review process most spacefaring nations require before a new pad is licensed has, in multiple documented cases, flagged genuine local costs: wetland habitat disturbed by construction and by the shockwave and heat of repeated launches, protected coastal bird species whose nesting behavior has measurably shifted near active pads, water systems used for sound suppression and flame diversion that draw on local aquifers or discharge into local waterways under permits that regulators and residents alike have, at various sites, publicly contested. None of this is unique to any one company or nation; it is a standing feature of building industrial infrastructure capable of producing the sound and heat of a controlled explosion, repeatedly, on a coastline chosen for a launch azimuth rather than for the wildlife that already lived there.
This is the most literal, least abstract version of the bill this chapter has spent its pages itemizing. A rocket’s plume is a number in the atmosphere, distant and diffuse. A spaceport’s water table, its wetland, its nesting shorebirds are a number in a specific place, measured by specific regulators, felt by specific residents who did not choose to live next to a launch pad and in some cases lived there before the pad existed. Look first, this book’s oldest rule, was never only about a dark sea or a dead world. It is also, at the smallest and least glamorous scale this book will describe, about the marsh grass fifty meters from the flame trench, and whether anyone counted it before the first countdown clock started.

A spaceport’s bill has a decibel line too. Sound, measured.
A returning booster’s landing burn and, where the vehicle uses one, its sonic boom on the way down are not silent events for the communities living near a launch site; real acoustic monitoring around active pads has recorded landing-related sonic booms loud enough to rattle windows and, in documented cases, prompt formal noise complaints from residents kilometers from the pad itself, a nuisance this chapter’s earlier environmental-review material has already flagged as part of the permit fight a site has to clear before flying at all. Regulators in multiple jurisdictions have, in response, placed limits on landing timing, trajectory, and cadence specifically to manage this complaint, treating a sonic boom the same way an airport treats a noise curfew: a genuine cost, imposed on people who did not choose to live near the infrastructure, priced in advance rather than discovered after the fact.
