A0 / A1. Delay, Dirt, and Why a Kitchen Is Not a Loaf
Hot / warm / cold as in the front matter. A claim can change temperature. A stack that has flown is hotter than a rendering. A slipped year is not, by itself, a con.
(1)  t = d / c
where t is one-way light-time, d is distance, c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
Moon: d ≈ 3.84 × 10⁸ m ⇒ t ≈ 1.28 s (about 1.3 s). Round trip ≈ 2.6 s. A conversation is possible if both ends are patient.
Mars: d runs from about 0.37 AU at a kind opposition to about 2.7 AU when the Sun is in the way of a straight radio path and the planets are far. One-way light-time is about 3–4 minutes at the kind end and about 21–24 minutes at the rude end. Round trip: eight to forty-some minutes. Treat talk as a retired verb.
A loaf — all times on one inventory — is a courtesy from another book. This appendix does not need it. Dirt and delay are local measurements.
Reading list, this note. A pH log runs 0–14; a slightly acidic nutrient film, on the order of 5.5–6.5, suits most leaf crops. Deviation of a full point is a fixable chemistry problem, not a crisis, if caught inside a day. Perchlorate salts appear in Martian regolith at roughly 0.5–1% by mass in the samples measured so far — a poison to unprotected biology and, with the right chemistry, a candidate oxygen source. Neither fact scales to a tank farm on its own.

A2. What Counts as Flown Hardware
Flown: a vehicle or payload that completed the named job in public, with a serial number and a result (including a failure that still arrived).
This-century lunar inventory, as classes (logos will rot):
•  Uncrewed crew-shaped loop and splashdown: Artemis I (2022). Empty seats on purpose.
•  Sample return, near side: Chang’e-5 (2020).
•  Sample return, far side: Chang’e-6 (2024).
•  Soft landing and rover, south polar region: Chandrayaan-3 (2023).
•  Precision landing with mixed posture: SLIM (2023–24).
•  Commercial landers: mixed stand / tip / quiet. Data, not a downtown.
•  Crewed lunar orbit or landing, this century: not flown as of this kitchen.
A rendering, a contract, a clean-room module, a hopped prototype: not flown as the job on the slide.
The five-card ledger. Rendering → Contract → Article → Test → Flight. A slide’s job is to make a rendering feel like a flight. A reader’s job is to ask which card, every time, before the year matters at all. An article that has passed one test (a static fire, a wet dress rehearsal, a cold-flow pour) is warmer than a rendering and colder than a flight; treat it as a dare, not a demonstration.
When a coupon gets overruled, sketched. The night before the January 1986 Shuttle launch, contractor engineers raised documented concerns about O-ring performance at forecast cold temperatures; a conference call reversed their initial no-launch recommendation under schedule pressure; a seal failed as predicted, 73 seconds after liftoff, killing seven. The Rogers Commission’s finding generalizes past that one program: a coupon that can only be logged, not stop a countdown, has honesty without authority. Sociologist Diane Vaughan’s later study of the same case coined normalization of deviance — a program treating a repeatedly-ignored warning as acceptable risk until the accumulated acceptances produce a failure nobody individually chose. Contrast with Artemis I’s shield below: the difference is not the engineers’ honesty in either case. It is whether the finding was allowed to stop anything.
Artemis I shield, as a worked coupon. The heat shield’s outer char layer separated in more locations and larger pieces than pre-flight models predicted, driven by trapped gas building pressure inside the ablative material during a skip-entry heating profile. No injury resulted; the cause was published; the fix (a different entry profile for the already-built shield, a redesigned material for later shields) was written down. That is the anatomy of an invoice, not a cover-up — the pattern A3 generalizes.

A3. Slip as Invoice: Historical Pattern
A slip is a moved official date for a named class of mission.
Pattern (not a morality play): Apollo slipped and then walked. Shuttle slipped, flew, then taught a tile-and-wish lesson. Constellation was cancelled as a program, not as a proof that the Moon is unreachable. Artemis-class crew dates have moved in public more than once in the 2020s. Commercial heavy-stack dates have moved in public more than once.
Read a slip as information when it names the long pole (shield, suit, pour, weld, partner can). Read it as fog when it names only “integration.” Fog is not automatically fraud. An empty oven with a roast on the slide is the fraud test.
Dates, for the record, knowing they will look quaint. Kennedy’s pledge: May 1961, target end of the 1960s, met July 1969 — an eight-year span that absorbed a twenty-month stand-down after the January 1967 pad fire. Shuttle: proposed 1969 for flight “within the decade,” first flight April 1981 — roughly a decade of slip against the loosest reading of the pledge. Constellation: announced January 2004, cancelled as a program October 2010 without a crewed flight. The crewed landing was announced as Artemis III with a 2024 target and restated for 2025, 2026, and 2027. By this kitchen the name had moved: the mission now called the rehearsal is an Earth-orbit test, and the landing attempt is a later flight. See A7. Do not read the old name as the current noun. A commercial Mars cargo-landing target named for “as early as” a window in the 2020s has passed two such windows without a hull committed to the trip.
The four-question test, formalized. (i) Does the slip name a part? (ii) Was a coupon retested? (iii) Did the rendering change with the date? (iv) Is the person who found the fault still permitted to speak? A slip passing all four is an invoice. A slip failing (iii) or (iv) is worth extra skepticism regardless of how it answers (i) and (ii).

A4. Three Days, 1.3 Seconds, One-Sixth g
Transit Earth–Moon at the speeds we actually use: on the order of three days, not a Hohmann season.
Surface gravity: gMoon ≈ 1.62 m s⁻² ≈ g/6.
Lunar Δv sketch (order of magnitude, not a flight plan):
(3)  LEO (low Earth orbit) → TLI (trans-lunar injection, the burn that leaves Earth orbit for the Moon) ~ 3.1–3.2 km s⁻¹; lunar orbit capture and landing add several more; Earth return is a heat-shield problem as much as a propellant problem.
Polar ice: permanently shadowed craters show hydrogen signatures consistent with water ice in useful-if-true amounts. Useful-if-true is not a tank farm. ISRU from that ice is a middle-noun program.
Ice, dated. LCROSS (2009) impacted Cabeus crater and its trailing spacecraft detected water vapor and ice grains in the ejecta plume, on the order of several percent water by mass at that one site on that one day. Orbital neutron and infrared mapping since has extended hydrogen signatures across many polar shadowed regions, with concentration estimates ranging from under one percent to several percent depending on site and depth — a range, not a single number, and not yet a survey a mining engineer could bid against.
Dose, dated. China’s Chang’e-4 lander carried a dosimeter to the far side starting January 2019. The published average dose equivalent on the unshielded surface was about 1,370 microsieverts a day (Zhang and colleagues, Science Advances, 2020). Multiplied by a year, that is about half a sievert, near career limits several agencies still use (order 600 mSv). The August 1972 solar particle event, between Apollo 16 and 17, is separately estimated to have delivered a dose in the range of several hundred to over a thousand mSv to an unshielded crew caught on the surface — potentially into acute-radiation-sickness territory. Shielding (regolith, water, a berm) is the standing answer; none has been built at habitat scale off Earth.

A5. Uncrewed Loops, Landers, Sample Returns
Loop ≠ landing ≠ return ≠ shift.
A loop tests stack, guidance, and Earth entry. A landing tests the last kilometer in a vacuum well. A return tests ascent, rendezvous or direct throw, and a second entry. A shift is a roster that repeats. This century has loops, landings, and returns. It has machine shifts beginning. It has not had a human winter-over on the Moon.
Tally, this kitchen. Sample mass returned, robotic, this century: Chang’e-5 ≈ 1.73 kg (2020, near side); Chang’e-6 ≈ 1.94 kg (2024, far side, first-ever). Apollo, crewed, last century: ≈ 382 kg across six landings. Luna program, robotic, last century: ≈ 0.3 kg across three returns. Commercial lander scorecard through this kitchen: one launch failure before lunar transit (propellant leak, Jan 2024); one landing that tipped and worked a week on its side (Feb 2024); one crater-tip failure (Mar 2025); one upright two-week success with eclipse imagery (Mar 2025); one crash on descent (a second attempt by a repeat operator). Stand, tip, quiet, crash — in roughly that mix, from three different companies, in under eighteen months.

A6. Artemis-II-Class: Sortie, Not Town
Class of mission: crew in a cabin on a cis-lunar loop — a path that stays within the Earth-Moon system rather than landing — home in days if the stack behaves. Warm as a this decade class. The exact month is a mood.
What it must demonstrate: life support with breath in it, crew operations, comms, entry with people. What it is not asked to demonstrate: landing, ISRU, a town, a child.
If the morning has already happened when you read this, you own a flown sortie. You still do not own a settlement.
Flown, this kitchen. Crew: Reid Wiseman (commander), Victor Glover (pilot), Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen (Canadian Space Agency). Launch: April 1, 2026. Closest lunar approach: April 6, ≈ 6,545 km. Splashdown: April 10, Pacific Ocean. Maximum distance from Earth: ≈ 406,771 km, a new crewed-flight record, surpassing the mark set by Apollo 13’s crew in 1970. Heat shield: Avcoat, flown on a modified, steeper entry after Artemis I’s 2022 uneven char loss. The first public look, in April, found no unusual conditions and far less char loss than the empty flight. By early September a safety-panel count put the loss at about nine sites, against more than a hundred on Artemis I. A fuller cut-up, including x-ray of the material, was still owed. The class is hot as of this kitchen. The next class, a crewed landing attempt, remains warm — see A7.
Life-support load, sketched. A crew of four exhales roughly 0.9–1.0 kg CO₂ per person per day (≈ 3.6–4.0 kg/day, crew total); cabin CO₂ partial pressure is typically held under about 0.7 kPa (roughly 5 mmHg) to avoid cognitive impairment, using regenerable scrubber beds. Potable water budget: order 2–4 L/person/day including hygiene margins on a short mission (no closed recycling loop required for a ten-day sortie the way ISS requires one for six-month stays). Free-return trajectory: a translunar path shaped so that, absent any further burn, the spacecraft swings around the Moon and re-enters Earth’s atmosphere. Apollo 13 had already left that path when the service module failed. A burn put the crew back onto a road that falls home. The kindness was reachable. It was not the road they were already on. Artemis-II-class missions are drawn to keep a version of the path from the start.

A7. HLS / Starship-Class Long Poles
Human Landing System class: a cabin that goes down and is supposed to come up.
Long poles:
1.  Orbital refueling if the architecture pours (cryogenic transfer, many times).
2.  Last kilometer: no air, dust, throttle, slope.
3.  Ascent / leave and meeting the home cabin.
4.  Suits as workplaces in glass dust.
5.  Power, abort, medical, polar night if that is the site.
Landing attempt in the 2030s: warm. “Attempt” is the honest noun.
Mission structure, revised in this kitchen’s year. In February 2026, the American ledger inserted an additional crewed mission between the flyby (A6) and the first landing attempt, citing lander-readiness and safety margin. The new middle mission — crew in Earth orbit only, no lunar transit — is built to test two competing lander designs’ systems without betting a crew on the last kilometer first. The landing attempt itself moved to the mission after that one, publicly targeted for 2028. Read this the way A3 teaches: a slip that names a part (lander readiness, an added test mission) is an invoice, not a scandal. The class — attempt, not town — has not changed. Only the numbering and the count of warm-up steps has.
Late in this same kitchen, 20 September 2026, the agency’s own note to its staff said the Earth-orbit rehearsal was about ninety days behind a summer-2027 best case. The two 2028 landing chances were the thing that pressure was eating; one of them might not survive the slip. The popular chapters keep “a landing attempt in the 2030s” on purpose. A printed 2028 is a weather report. The decade-class is the noun that is still true if the weather moves again. Starship-class refueling remains the unpaid clause under either year: one tank-to-tank transfer on a single stack is not a campaign of tankers.
Refueling, sketched. Public estimates for the number of tanker flights needed to fill a single HLS-class lander in orbit have ranged, across successive program statements, from roughly 10 to 20+, driven by boil-off losses during the wait between launches and by how much each tanker actually delivers versus its own reserve margin. As of this kitchen, an orbital propellant transfer between two vehicles of the relevant class had been demonstrated once, tank-to-tank on a single stack, not yet as a repeated multi-tanker campaign.
Suit-abrasion record. Apollo 17’s mission report documented glove and boot-cover wear, jammed zippers, and cabin dust contamination after roughly 22 hours of total surface EVA time across three days — the longest lunar surface exposure of the program. Modern polar-shift suit requirements, by comparison, are written against hundreds of hours of cumulative EVA across a multi-week or multi-month stay: several times the cycles, and about ten times if the stay runs for months. That is one order of magnitude, not two or three.

A8. NRHO, Stations, and Floor Plans
(NRHO) near-rectilinear halo orbit: a high, lopsided lunar path with long Earth view. A porch, not a law.
The station in Earth orbit is the existence proof of a continuously staffed outpost, and the proof that the supply ships never became optional. A cargo cult, in this book’s glossary, is a copy of the form without the supply. The station is the opposite lesson: the form has lasted because the supply did not stop. Gateway-class is a smaller, farther cousin on paper. Architecture fights (porch vs surface-first) can move a decade. Warm-to-cold as a 2030s–2040s winter-over.
Resolved, this kitchen. In March 2026 the halo-orbit station was paused in its existing design; its budget was redirected to a phased surface-base plan (order $20–30B over roughly a decade, 20+ robotic cargo landings in the plan’s first phase, crewed missions targeted at roughly twice a year from about 2028). Built modules were slated for repurposing rather than flight in their original role; partner agencies were left to decide, on their own schedules, what to do with hardware built for a station that may not fly as designed. Surface-first won this round of a fight that was never a law. It is not evidence that a settlement exists; it is evidence that a ledger can still fire a floor plan, the way A8’s chapter always said it could.
NRHO, sketched. Period ≈ 6.5–7 days; perilune (closest lunar approach) ≈ 3,000 km; apolune (farthest) ≈ 70,000 km; stable enough to require only modest stationkeeping burns over years, and shaped so a spacecraft in it is rarely eclipsed by the Moon and almost always has line-of-sight to Earth. The Δv cost of a full descent-and-ascent from this orbit to the lunar surface and back runs measurably higher than from a low circular lunar orbit — the toll surface-first advocates weigh against the orbit’s stationkeeping and visibility advantages.
ISS, sketched. Continuously crewed since November 2, 2000 (25+ years at this kitchen’s date); mass ≈ 420,000 kg; typical crew 6–7; resupply flights (US Cargo Dragon/Cygnus, Russian Progress) arrive several times a year, each carrying a few tonnes; onboard water recovery from urine, sweat, and cabin humidity now exceeds 90% by NASA’s own reporting, still leaving food, spare parts, and net water top-up as imports. Planned deorbit: a purpose-built tug is contracted to guide the station to a controlled reentry near the end of this decade.

A9. Chang’e-Class, ILRS, Partner Camps
Chang’e-5 / 6 sample returns: hot as flown. ILRS as a 2030s multinational research station: warm as intent and precursors; cold as a city. Other ledgers (India, Japan, Europe, commercial trucks) change access, not the cargo-cult test.
Two outposts are two outposts. Addition of flags is not closure of air, water, and calories.
ILRS, sketched. Announced jointly by China’s and Russia’s space agencies in 2021; partner list grown since to include additional signatory nations and organizations; published roadmap describes a “basic” robotic-and-crew-capable model near the south pole in the mid-2030s and an “extended” model with in-situ resource use and larger crew rotations in the following decade. As of this kitchen, hardware committed to the station itself (versus precursor landers and orbiters) remains at the design and agreement stage.
Other ledgers, sketched. India: Chandrayaan-3 landed August 2023 (Chandrayaan-2’s 2019 attempt crashed in its final descent); crewed low-orbit program (Gaganyaan) in ground and abort testing through this kitchen, with a stated crewed-lunar ambition around 2040. Japan: SLIM landed January 2024 within roughly 55 m of its target, on its nose, and survived two lunar nights; a pressurized crewed rover is in development with an American partner for later-decade use. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty (signed by all major spacefaring nations) prohibits national appropriation of celestial bodies by claim of sovereignty; two newer, non-binding principle sets (one US-led, one China/Russia-led) attempt to govern resource use and safety zones and remain, as of this kitchen, largely untested by any actual extraction dispute.

where a “quiet” flight is one that completed its stated mission without becoming a news event about a failure, and “reliable” payload excludes any mass lost to that flight’s own anomalies.
Reuse, sketched. A booster caught and reflown within hours to days has been demonstrated for the class of vehicle this chapter discusses; a heat-shield tile system requiring zero replacement between flights, on the relevant upper-stage class, had not been demonstrated as of this kitchen. A cargo planner’s insurable cadence — the number a contract can be written against — is conventionally built from a track record of dozens to hundreds of consecutive successful flights of the same configuration, the standard commercial and crewed-rated launch vehicles took years to accumulate.
The cold call, resolved this kitchen’s year. In February 2026 the vehicle’s own operator publicly retargeted the near-term program away from an uncrewed Mars attempt in the late-2026 window — the one a founder had named — and toward the Moon, citing lander economics and an unfinished refueling campaign; a substantive Mars settlement push was pushed out roughly five to seven years. Two subsequent flight tests that year, on a newer version of the vehicle, each reached orbital velocity and deployed payload, and each also lost the booster on descent to a landing or splashdown failure — data, in the vocabulary this chapter uses, not a verdict. The quiet column (A10’s cadence relation) still has few marks in it. The cold call on a founder’s Mars year was not this book’s guess. It was the arithmetic in this note.

(2)  Hohmann-class Earth–Mars: ~6–9 months. Synodic repeat: ~26 months (about 2.14 years).
Mars surface pressure: order 6 mbar (0.6 kPa), ~0.6% of Earth’s sea-level 1013 mbar. Parachutes are underemployed. EDL at house-mass is not rover-mass.
MOXIE-class: a box on a rover made grams-per-hour of O₂ from CO₂. Hot as a trick in miniature. Not a tank farm.
Light-time: see (1) and A1. Crewed Mars landing in the 2030s: warm-to-cold. City this decade: cold.
(4)  pMars ≈ 6 mbar ≈ 0.006 × pEarth(sea level)
(5)  MOXIE (2021–2023 operations aboard Perseverance): up to ≈ 12 g O₂/hour at peak, ≈ 122 g total across a full run, from atmospheric CO₂ at ~96% concentration. A crewed return-fuel ISRU plant, by published mission-architecture estimates, would need to produce oxygen and methane at a rate measured in tonnes, not grams, over a stay of many months — a scale-up on the order of 10⁴–10⁵ from the flown demonstration.
EDL record, by mass class. Seven US Mars landing attempts using parachute-plus-terminal-propulsion architectures have been made on rover/lander-class payloads (up to ≈ 1,000 kg), six successful; the most recent two (Curiosity 2012, Perseverance 2021) used a sky-crane maneuver at masses of ≈ 900 kg. No landing attempt has been made at house-mass (tens of tonnes); published engineering analyses note that a purely parachute-based system does not scale linearly past roughly one to a few tonnes of entry mass at Mars’s air density, requiring propulsive descent to carry an increasing share of the deceleration as mass grows.

Biosphere 2, sketched — the closest real trial the seven rows have had. Eight people sealed inside a 3-acre closed ecological facility in Arizona, 1991–1993, under Earth gravity and pressure, with a budget in the hundreds of millions and genuine ecological expertise on the design team. Air row: failed — oxygen fell unexpectedly (unmodeled soil-microbe respiration), requiring an emergency outside-oxygen injection partway through. Calories row: failed — food production fell short, crew on a calorie-restricted diet for much of the run. A second, shorter run performed better on both metrics, having learned from the first. Even under Earth’s own gravity, pressure, and free nitrogen supply, with genuine expertise and a two-year timeline, the loops did not close on the first honest attempt. Score any off-Earth settlement claim against this benchmark, not against a poster’s confidence.

A13. Headcount, Tickets, and Why “We” Is Rude
People ever in orbit: a village. People on the Moon: a last-century dinner party. Near-term Mars seasons, if a lucky decade arrives: a campus, not a nation. Species headcount: order 8 × 10⁹.
Launch cadence and tickets are not a species-move. “We left” is a grammar error unless the referent is a camp.
(10)  N(ever flown) / N(species) ≈ 780 / 8×10⁹ ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻⁷
a fraction with several zeros before the first significant digit; smaller than the population of a single city block against a nation.
(13)  Apollo-era ground workforce (direct + contractor, peak) ≈ 400,000 : 24 people who reached the Moon ≈ 17,000 : 1 — spaceflight’s headcount was never small relative to its ground crew, only relative to the species funding it. A modern heavy-lift program’s combined workforce still runs into the tens of thousands for a vehicle that, as of this kitchen, has carried nobody.
Filters beyond cost, sketched. Historic astronaut medical screening has selected against common cardiovascular and vision conditions well before financial or political filters apply. Launch acceleration: typically 3–4 g sustained for minutes. Bone density loss in microgravity: documented at roughly 1–1.5% per month for weight-bearing bone without vigorous countermeasure exercise, with incomplete recovery for some long-duration crew even after return. Career radiation dose limits (order 600 mSv, roughly age- and sex-adjusted by agency) are approached or exceeded, by published mission-architecture dose estimates, in a single multi-year Mars round trip using only current shielding. Space Shuttle: 135 missions, 2 total losses with all aboard (Challenger 1986, Columbia 2003) — a loss-of-crew rate near 1.5% per mission across the program’s history.

A14. Air, Ice, Plastics, Extinction
(6)  An extra CO₂ pulse: a large share taken up by ocean and land in decades to a few centuries; a tail in the deep ocean and rocks over millennia. Not a weekend reset to 1800.
Ice sheets: committed response on centuries–millennia. Mountain glaciers faster; sea ice faster still and a different invoice (albedo vs sea-level).
Plastics: persistent solids; fragmentation, not resignation. Production can stop; the drawer remains.
Extinction: one-way on any human clock. A freezer genome is a library, not a grandmother.
(15)  Permafrost organic carbon stock ≈ 2× current atmospheric carbon burden — locked, by definition, in ground frozen at least two consecutive years, in many places for millennia, under roughly a quarter of Northern Hemisphere land area. Thaw releases it to microbial decomposition as CO₂ and, in wetter conditions, methane, a genuine feedback loop (warming → thaw → release → more warming) whose century-scale magnitude remains an active, wide-ranging research estimate — real and already locally measurable, not yet confidently quantified at global scale. Warm, not yet hot; watch more closely than most pots in this note.
Numbers, this kitchen. Annual anthropogenic CO₂ emissions: ≈ 35–40 Gt/year, roughly half taken up within the same year by ocean and land sinks, the remainder accumulating; ice-core-informed models of past large carbon pulses suggest ≈ 20–25% of a pulse remains airborne after 1,000 years. Greenland ice sheet: sea-level-equivalent ≈ 7.4 m; current net loss ≈ 250–280 Gt/year (satellite gravimetry). Antarctic ice sheet: sea-level-equivalent ≈ 58 m; current net loss ≈ 150 Gt/year, concentrated in West Antarctica. Arctic summer sea-ice extent: down about 40% from the 1979 satellite-era baseline. Global plastic production: >400 Mt/year, nearly doubling every 15–20 years since mid-century; historically recycled share: under 10%. Current extinction rate estimates: tens to hundreds of times the fossil-record background rate of ≈ 1–5 species per million species-years.

A15. Settlers vs CO₂; Collapse vs Cleanup
(7)  Order-of-magnitude: 10⁴ settlers × ~10 t CO₂e/person-year ≈ 10⁵ t/year. Global fossil-and-industry emissions: order 3–4 × 10¹⁰ t/year. The camp is a rounding error and may have the wrong sign if it is a construction site.
The thermostat is industry and land, not headcount on a ship.
If industry collapsed: emissions fall fast; sulfate/soot parasol falls too; short-term unmasking of heat is a warm caution. Collapse is not a cleanup method.
Leaving as cleanup: cold.
(11)  Settlement share = 10⁴ settlers × (50–100 t CO₂e/settler-year, camp-loaded) / 3.7 × 10¹⁰ t global ≈ 1.4–2.7 × 10⁻⁵
roughly one part in 40,000 to 70,000 of global annual emissions — smaller than typical year-to-year measurement noise in the global total.
Collapse, sketched. COVID-19 lockdowns (2020) produced a peak daily emissions drop of ≈ 17% and a full-year drop of ≈ 5–7%, the sharpest sustained reduction on record, with atmospheric concentration still rising that year. Published aerosol-forcing estimates attribute roughly 30–50% of greenhouse-gas warming’s offset to co-emitted sulfate and soot particles, which wash out of the atmosphere in days to weeks versus centuries for CO₂ — the physical basis for the “unmasking” warning: a sudden stop removes the parasol faster than the underlying cause.

A16. Rockets, Metals, and the World with a Staff
A single launch is a small line on a global carbon ledger next to a coal fleet. A scaled industry of leaving (energy, mines, cement, pads) plus a write-off story (Earth is already lost) can worsen the bill for those who stay. Warm, not a law that spaceflight is a crime.
Rockets are not a carbon offset. Backup-world talk is not a climate policy.
(16)  Debris cascade (Kessler, 1978): collision → fragments → higher fragment density → more collisions → runaway fragment growth in an orbital shell, potentially rendering it unusable for a generation. Tracked objects larger than a softball currently orbiting Earth: >40,000. Untracked fragments large enough to disable a satellite on impact: estimated in the hundreds of thousands to millions, each carrying rifle-round kinetic energy at orbital velocity. A single serious collision has already, once, more than doubled the tracked-debris count in its affected shell overnight. An industry scaling toward routine (A10) is, without separate engineering discipline around deorbit and collision avoidance, also scaling this risk — a bill this note itemizes so a later reader does not discover it by surprise.
(12)  Per-launch CO₂(direct) ≈ 300–2,000 t, depending on propellant mass and mix, versus a mid-size coal plant’s ≈ 3–6 Mt/year (roughly one launch’s worth of CO₂ every few hours of plant operation).
Concrete and cement, sketched. A17. Pressure, Heat, and Why a Glove Is a Workplace
A suit is a spacecraft: pressure garment, cooling loop, radio, visor, bearings.
Operating pressure, US suit class: on the order of 30 kPa (about 4.3 psi), against about 101 kPa at sea level. Lower pressure keeps the joints bendable. It also forces a pure-oxygen mix and a pre-breathe so dissolved nitrogen does not come out of the blood as bubbles when the pressure drops. The trade is hot as a design fact. A shirtsleeve camp is a different machine.
Metabolic heat: order 100 W at rest, several hundred watts at work. Vacuum does not carry that heat on a breeze. A sublimator or a radiator has to be handed it. If the loop stops, the workplace stops, in minutes, not in a shift.
Apollo 17: about 22 hours of surface EVA across three days, and the mission report already logged glove wear, boot-cover wear, jammed closures, and cabin dust. A polar outpost written against hundreds of hours is several times the cycles, and about ten times if the stay runs for months — one order of magnitude, not two. The spare glove is a line item. The person whose job is the glove is a line item. Neither has been staffed on the Moon.

A18. The Fortnight, the Polar Bargain, Three Ways to Hold the Night
Equatorial lunar day: about 29.5 Earth days, noon to noon. Dark half: about 14.75 days. No air to store the day’s heat.
Surface temperatures away from the poles swing from above the boiling point of water in the sun to well below a kitchen freezer in the dark. The numbers move with soil and angle. The swing is the fact.
Three ways to hold the night, each a cargo: batteries sized for a fortnight of a habitat’s kilowatts (a phone is not this object); a reactor that does not care that the sky is black, and that is a politics as well as a mass; a cable from a lit ridge to a hollow, which is civil engineering in one-sixth g, in glass dust, in a suit that is itself A17’s spacecraft.
South-polar bargain: a high ridge can see the Sun for a large fraction of the month; a neighboring crater floor can have sat in shadow for geological time. Shadows still move. A drawing’s noon is not a drawing’s three o’clock, and three o’clock lasts for days. A sortie can leave before the night. An outpost pays it.

A19. The Ratio, the Cold, and Why a Deposit Is Not a Well
Permanently shadowed regions: hollows the Sun does not reach. Floors can sit at tens of kelvin. Cold enough for ice to persist is not the same sentence as a well you can bid.
LCROSS (2009), Cabeus: water in the plume at that one site, on the order of several percent by mass, one day. Orbital maps since then are a range, under one percent to several percent, by site and depth. A range is not a tank farm. A4 already said so. This note says the ratio.
The ratio: spacecraft, power, and losses spent, against water netted, and then again next month. Three tonnes of trouble for one tonne of water is a demonstration that lost money in a useful direction. Net gain, repeated, with a spare for the machine that broke, is the first time the word shift is honest. Babies need the shift to be boring.
Uses, if you get the water: drink after you clean it; air after you crack it; propellant if the engines drink the products; cooling; a jacket of shielding. Every use is a plant. The plant is mostly unflown. Look first means the second measurement before the harbor.

A20. Charge, Sharpness, and Why a Lasting Print Is Not a Charm
No wind, no rain. A boot print from the Apollo years is still a print. Gift to a geologist. Threat to a seal.
The grain is sharp because nothing has rounded it, and a little magnetic in its iron. Sunlight kicks electrons off the surface; dust levitates a little and follows fields onto suits, radiators, visors, and into a lazy airlock. Apollo crews smelled it in the cabin, spent-gunpowder, and coughed. A short stay can be a story. A year is a medical program and a maintenance program.
What it breaks: seals, bearings, radiators that must see black sky, solar arrays, suit joints, a precise instrument, a greenhouse tray if you track it in. Mitigation is a shift: brush in a porch, wait in the airlock, floors you can clean, bearings designed to be replaced, a rule that the glove comes off before the seedling. An hour of simulant is not a year.

A21. Drizzle versus Storm, and Why the Hat Is Mass
Two weathers. Galactic cosmic rays: a slow drizzle, including heavy nuclei that punch thin walls. You count days and come home before the count is a career. A solar particle event: a shove of protons on a scale of hours. Outside, without a shelter, that is a medical event.
Chang’e-4’s far-side dosimeter, 2019: about 1,370 microsieverts a day on the unshielded surface. Multiplied by 365 that is about 0.50 Sv a year, near career limits several agencies use (order 600 mSv). The August 1972 event, between Apollo 16 and 17, is estimated in the hundreds to over a thousand mSv for an unshielded surface crew. A4 carries the same two facts. This note is the hat.
Hat, order of magnitude, not a bid: on the order of a meter to a few meters of regolith turns a storm from a crisis toward a bad day and takes the edge off the drizzle. That hat is mass you move, or bricks you bake, or a tube you have surveyed. A lava tube is a warm hope until the roof at your site has been shown. Apollo’s stays were days. Months are a different dosimeter. A child is not an adult’s limit on a smaller body.

A22. Free Return, and Why a Later Abort Is a Burn
Free return: a translunar path that, with no further burn, swings the Moon and falls back toward Earth. Apollo 13 had already left a free return when the service module failed. A burn put the crew back on one. Artemis-II-class loops are drawn to keep a version of it. Splashdown can be the success because the architecture confessed, in advance, that not landing was allowed.
Once you have braked into orbit, or started down, home is a burn you must still be able to light: an engine that has waited, a rendezvous with a cabin that is moving, propellant you have not already spent. Apollo practiced it. A new heavy lander has not practiced it until it has.
A Mars transfer, weeks along, is not a U-turn. Turning around can cost more than finishing the fall. “We can always come back” is true inside a window of days and a tank budget. Outside that window it is a lullaby. The checklist is the burn, the drink left, and the day home is still the cheap path.

A23. Non-Appropriation, and the Resource Fight as Warm
Outer Space Treaty, 1967, Article II, in short: no nation may appropriate the Moon by claim of sovereignty. Signed by the spacefaring states. Hot as a legal sentence. You may go. You may use. You may not plant a fence and call the crater a province.
What a company may own if it extracts ice, and what a camp may do if it sits on a ridge for a decade, is warmer. Some national laws say extracted resources can be owned though the land cannot. Other readers of the same treaty call that a theft with a statute. Two non-binding principle sets, one US-led and one China/Russia-led, try to govern use and safety zones. A9 already said they are largely untested by an extraction dispute. This note says the same fight from the crater’s side.
The treaty does not contain this book’s three cards. It permits going and use. It forbids a national fence. It does not decide who owns extracted ice, and it does not found a school, a tax, or a child who is from the crater. A sortie fits inside what it permits. An outpost fits only as use, for as long as the partners keep the loops closed. A settlement — courts, a kitchen that can miss a ship — is not a clause from 1967.

A24. Program-Scale Money against a City and against the Furnaces
Orders, not a prettier coin. A heavy government launch in this era is often a billion-class event once stack, missing cadence, and the standing army of the pad are in the price. A program of a few crewed loops plus a landing attempt is tens of billions across a decade, the way large human-spaceflight programs have been tens of billions.
That is real money. It is the money of a few large infrastructure jobs on Earth. It is not the money of feeding eight billion people, and it is not the money of the furnaces Chapter 15 already named as the thermostat. Cancelling a loop does not move Nadia’s gauge. Changing furnaces, farms, and forests does. Spending the loop does not move the species. Chapter 13 counted the heads.

A25. Dose and Travel Time for a Crew That Does Not Sleep
At 0.01 c, Alpha Centauri is about four centuries. At 0.1 c, about forty years, before the problem of stopping, and before a grain of dust at that speed is an energy rumor rather than dust. Chemical rockets are horses beside either number. A14’s table is the clock arithmetic if flesh tries the fast path. Flesh does not like forty years in a can. Generation ships and sleeping flesh are stories biochemistry has not signed.

A machine has parity, redundancy, and a spare, not marrow. Slowing at the far end is a second fortune. A reactor that still knows its job after a century of quiet is the warm object. We do not yet own a control system that must not go mad in the quiet. We own the beginnings of a pattern engine. Those are not the same object. The chapter is an outlook, not a purchase order.

A26. Genome Size, Synthesis, and the Protection Categories
A human genome: on the order of 3 billion base pairs, a novella once the repeats are compressed. A biosphere is a literature: rock-eaters, light-eaters, seeds that remember winter, the messy extra of cultures. Sequence without a kitchen — enzymes, solvents, a sterility that survives a century of rays — is a book in a language no one left standing can cook. The wet lab is mass. A flag is light. The Δv invoices the difference.
Planetary protection, dull on purpose: icy moons with oceans sit in the categories where a sneeze is a policy. If the sea is taken, the library stays shut. If the sea is empty, opening it is a first day you do not get to un-teach. Directed panspermia (Crick and Orgel, among others) is cold as a detection in our own rocks and warm as a project we might do, and therefore a project we can refuse. A serial number in the genome has been looked for. What turned up was mostly junk that had been misread.
A printer that can make a cell can make a plague. Sterility is filters and assays, not a mood. Look first. Seed later. The greenhouse on a dead world whose exam was graded empty enough to farm is the small case. A dark sea is the ungraded case. Do not mix the folders.

A28. Life Expectancy, HALE, and the Supercentenarian Tail
(17)  Life expectancy at birth is a period summary of death rates, not a promise to a baby. HALE (healthy life expectancy) subtracts years lived with disability, weighted.
Rich-country LE: women often low-to-mid 80s; men a little lower (Japan, Switzerland, Spain, Australia, Italy, Nordics as a band). United States: high 70s in recent ledgers after a dip — a rich-country warning, not a law of biology.
Longest documented human life: ~122 years. Tail, not a mean, not a protocol. Supercentenarians (≥110) are a validation sport, not a clinic.
Maximum observed lifespan has not sprinted the way averages did. Averages moved largely by not losing the young.
Modest further healthy years this century in rich-country medicine: warm. Everyone shares them: cold.
A number sold as your real age. Methylation-based “biological age” clocks (Horvath’s original clock, then PhenoAge, GrimAge, DunedinPACE) predict real outcomes — disease, mortality — in large research cohorts: hot as correlates, in that setting, on blood samples, in the cohort that validated them. A 2026 reliability study found that the two most-referenced clocks behind today’s commercial tests, GrimAgeV2 and DunedinPACE, have only moderate-to-good technical reproducibility and were among the least reliable across repeated draws from the same person (pooled intraclass correlation — a 0-to-1 score for how well repeat tests on the same person agree with each other — of 0.4–0.7, none reaching “excellent”), and that a single meal, a stressful week, air pollution, or a trip to altitude can knock one reading into “poor” reliability on its own (Sehgal et al., Aging Cell, 2026). Most direct-to-consumer sellers do not publish which algorithm they run, and a saliva sample carries none of the blood-based validation that makes the research-grade clocks predictive at all. A number that moves with what you had for lunch is not HALE with better branding.

A30. Naked Mole Rats, Greenland Sharks, and the Limits of “No Aging”
(26)  hazard(age) ≈ constant past maturity, for the naked mole rat past its own reproductive maturity — “negligible senescence” (Ruby, Smith, and Buffenstein, eLife, 2018): mortality hazard does not rise with age out to about 25× the age of maturity, unlike every other mammal studied to date. Maximum verified lifespan >37 years; breeding females retain fertility into a third decade; body composition stable from about 2 to 24 years.
Mechanism, on the record: very-high-molecular-mass hyaluronan (more than 5× the human or mouse form, up to about 2.5 MDa), conferring early-contact growth inhibition and near-total cancer resistance (Tian et al., Nature, 2013). A transgenic mouse overexpressing the naked-mole-rat gene for making it (nmrHas2) showed higher tissue hyaluronan, lower spontaneous and induced cancer, reduced inflammation, and extended lifespan and healthspan (Zhang et al., “Increased hyaluronan by naked mole-rat Has2 improves healthspan in mice,” Nature 621, 196–205, 2023).
Greenland shark: eye-lens radiocarbon dating of 28 female sharks put minimum lifespan at 272 years; the largest specimen (502 cm) was estimated at 392 ± 120 years (range 272–512); maturity at roughly 150 years (Nielsen et al., Science, 2016). The longest-lived vertebrate confirmed to date.
Turritopsis dohrnii: reverts from medusa to polyp via transdifferentiation after damage or starvation; theoretically unlimited cycling at the colony/lineage level; individual medusae still die of predation and disease.
Jonathan (Aldabrachelys gigantea hololissa), Saint Helena: hatched circa 1832, about 193 years old as of 2026, the oldest verified living land animal; shows ordinary age-related decline (cataract blindness, lost sense of smell), not negligible senescence.
None of the four is a transferable off switch. Only the mole-rat mechanism has so far been moved into a different species, and there it bought healthspan and cancer resistance, not arrested aging.

A31. CRISPR-Class Edits: This Gene, This Tissue, This Invoice

A32. Four-Factor and Three-Factor Reprogramming: Papers, Dates, and the Teratoma Bill
Full four-factor reprogramming (a four-protein cocktail identified in 2006, sufficient to return a somatic cell fully to a pluripotent state, and the basis of the entire induced-stem-cell field): run continuously in vivo in mice, it produced teratomas from multiple organs and circulating iPS cells with totipotency-like features (Abad et al., “Reprogramming in vivo produces teratomas and iPS cells with totipotency features,” Nature 502, 340–345, 2013).
Cyclic full-factor dosing: short pulses (2 days on, 5 days off) of all four proteins in a mouse model of a severe progeroid genetic defect — not ordinary aging — improved several aging hallmarks and extended median lifespan by 33% and maximum lifespan by 18%, with no teratomas reported at that schedule (Ocampo et al., “In Vivo Amelioration of Age-Associated Hallmarks by Partial Reprogramming,” Cell 167, 1719–1733, 2016).
Three-factor local reprogramming (omitting the growth-associated fourth protein): in mouse retinal ganglion cells, restored a younger DNA-methylation pattern and transcriptome, promoted axon regeneration, and reversed vision loss in a glaucoma model and in naturally aged mice; required the demethylases TET1/TET2 (Lu et al., “Reprogramming to recover youthful epigenetic information and restore vision,” Nature 588, 124–129, 2020).
First human trial: an FDA-cleared investigational new drug application for a three-factor reprogramming gene therapy delivered by intravitreal injection (an injection into the eye itself), targeting two optic neuropathies, cleared 28 January 2026 — the first cellular-reprogramming therapy cleared for human testing. Phase 1 only; safety, not efficacy; one organ, one route.
Temperatures: hot as a mouse-tissue and mechanistic result; warm as an active, well-funded research program; cold as a whole-body or general anti-aging claim.

A40. Proper Time, Time Dilation, and the Gravity of Clocks
(24)  τ = ∫ √(1 − v²/c²) dt along a path. A clock reads its own proper time τ, the spacetime length of its path. Between two fixed events the unaccelerated path has the largest τ (maximal aging, in the sense of Taylor and Wheeler). Every bent path has less. The twin trip has no paradox: only one path bends, and acceleration is absolute, because an accelerometer reads it.
(25)  Δτ/τ (the fractional change in a clock’s rate) ≈ ΔΦ/c² in a weak field, with ΔΦ (the change in gravitational potential) ≈ g h (gravitational acceleration times height) near a surface. For h = 1 km this is 1.1 × 10⁻¹³, about 9.4 ns per day, about 0.27 ms over 80 years. In 2010 optical clocks resolved a height difference of 33 cm.
Every process on a path, chemical or atomic, reads the same τ. No experiment inside a closed ship shows the ship’s own rate. That is why the traveler lives no extra ticks. The method of drawing paths on a page and reading clocks off their lengths follows Epstein’s Relativity Visualized; the drawings here are original. The maximal-aging statement and its bookkeeping are in Taylor and Wheeler.

A41. Torpor, Therapeutic Hypothermia, and What Slowing Metabolism Buys
Spaceflight proposals: torpor-based Mars-transit habitat concepts have been studied on paper, plus NASA-funded animal and human physiology research (including the Studying Torpor in Animals for Space-Health in Humans program), since the mid-2010s. Aim: reduce consumables and habitat mass for a 6–9 month transit, not extend the crew’s own lifespan. No crewed application is scheduled or imminent as of this writing.
Clinical evidence: early trials of post-cardiac-arrest therapeutic hypothermia (32–34°C for about 24 hours) showed improved neurological outcome versus normothermia (about 55% vs. 39% favorable outcome, for ventricular-fibrillation arrest). Later, larger trials narrowed the claim: a 2013 randomized trial found no significant difference between a 33°C and a 36°C target; a 2021 trial of 1,861 patients found no significant difference between hypothermia and simply preventing fever, especially for non-shockable rhythms. Current practice: targeted temperature management remains standard for select patients, with a narrower and more contested benefit than the original 2002 trial suggested.
Hibernator neuroscience: in torpid arctic ground squirrels, brain tau protein becomes hyperphosphorylated to a degree resembling advanced Alzheimer’s pathology, and dendritic connections retract; both fully reverse within hours of arousal, with a compensatory overshoot in synaptic density on rewarming. No human data exists on whether any part of this transfers; humans are not adapted hibernators.
Temperatures: hot as hibernator biology and as a narrow, injury-specific clinical benefit; warm as an open, actively funded research question for induced human states; cold as a scheduled human hibernation flight, or metabolic slowing as a general anti-aging tool.

Hot: institutions have stored real methods (vaccines, packets, a grid) and have also lost them in fires, purges, and fronts. Warm: whether a given ledger will still be staffed in fifty years. Cold: progress as a law that fires if we wait, and a germline “enhancement” sold as a product. Germline editing is a political and ethical front, not a SKU. Look first: do not overwrite a childhood because a slide said the year 12,000 would thank you.

A43. Trials, Harm, and Who Pays for the Local Fix
Trials are how a kitchen refuses a founder’s word; they include no as an outcome. Conditioning, cytokine storms, vessel risk: entry fees, not fine print.
Price-as-delivery: a low-millions local fix is a ministry-or-insurer object. Rich-country healthspan gains warm; universal share cold.

A44. Plasticity Is Real and Expensive
Sensitive periods — language, vision, some motor maps — are hot as developmental windows. They are cold as a license to treat a childhood as a construction site because adaptation sounded cheaper than a tool. Adult maps still change. Stroke recovery is the worked case: tissue reroutes, and the reroute is practice, sleep, a therapist, and months. It is overtime. It is not a hidden room that was waiting unlocked.
Practice thickens a map and eats mornings. London-taxi spatial memory and musical training are the expensive versions of the same fact: the map gets larger where the hours went, and the hours are gone. Tools and other people are part of the method. A checklist, a night nurse, a colleague who has seen the pump, a school that protects sleep. None of that is a spare ninety percent. Chapter 34 already emptied that cupboard.
Adjusting is not spare capacity. It is the staff you already had, spent, plus help you hired, plus a morning you will not get twice.

A45. A File Is Not a Worldline
A worldline is the path a body actually takes. Proper time, in A40, is the length of that path as the body’s own clock reads it. A file is a description of a state. It is not the path. Running the description, if anyone ever can, starts a new path. At best that is a second person. The first person remains in the meat, or is dead. Travel did not happen. Copying a map is not walking it.
Restorative chips — a stimulator, a cochlear implant, a local prosthesis — are tools with scars. Warm to hot as tools. They do not pour the person into a file.
Uploads, a forever-mind, and a required last computation are cold. The required last mind was a claim that the universe needs a final observer to finish the sum. The cosmology book already refused it. Leftover glow is the habit of keeping the refused claim in the room. This note does not reopen it. It only keeps the file from being sold as the walk.

A46. Inventory of Claims, by Temperature
Hot: moving clocks and low clocks disagree with clocks left behind (measured), so a fast traveler’s date moves and her tick count does not; lifespan ≠ healthspan; hands age; 10% brain sentence is false; sickle-cell-class local wins exist; last ~50 years saw real uneven gains; most near-term humans are not a different species because of a capsule; at least four species combine an ordinary body with negligible senescence, a centuries-long clock, or a damage-triggered developmental reset, and one of those mechanisms has already been moved into a different species; a cyclic four-factor and a local three-factor reprogramming schedule each measurably helped specific mouse tissue, and a first human safety trial (one organ) exists as of January 2026; hibernators fully reverse, in hours, a brain change that would read as severe disease in a human.
Warm: a settled world needs its own time standard, corrected to home; many aging clocks (map real, unified switch not on the counter); modest further healthy years this century in rich-country medicine if institutions hold; S-curves as a pattern; plasticity as expensive reroute; restorative devices; an mTOR-inhibitor and an AMPK-pathway drug as tested, unproven repurposed candidates; whether partial reprogramming or induced human torpor extends beyond their first narrow results.

Glossary
One list. The camp and the body share the book, so they share the words.

Sortie. Go, work, come home — or die on a schedule Earth still owns.
Outpost. A season with an umbilical.
Settlement. The umbilical can fail and the babies still eat.
ISRU. In-situ resource utilization. Make the kitchen there. Not a city charter.
Slip. A moved official date. Invoice, not destiny, not automatically fraud.
Window. An alignment you do not vote off.
Cargo-cult test. Seven loops — air, water, calories, power, medicine, spares, law — scored separately. Headcount is not a loop. Borrowed, narrowly, from a mid-twentieth-century anthropological term describing Pacific Island communities that mimicked the form of a wartime supply operation after it departed; this book uses only the later engineering sense — a system that copies a working system’s appearance without its actual supply — not the anthropology, which is a more complicated and more respectful conversation than a glossary entry can hold.
NRHO. Near-rectilinear halo orbit. A porch, not a law.
Avcoat. The ablative heat-shield material flown on Apollo and on the current crew capsule; it protects by charring and shedding material, on purpose, which is why char loss is data, not automatically a defect (A2, A6).
EDL. Entry, descent, landing — the seven-minutes-of-terror span (A11) during which a spacecraft is on its own, because light-time makes real-time control from Earth physically impossible.
Anchoring. A cognitive bias in which a first-guessed number, often an arbitrary round one, distorts every later estimate even after everyone involved knows the first number was arbitrary (A3). A useful lens for reading any year that ends in a zero.
Additionality. In carbon-offset accounting, whether a credited reduction would have happened anyway without the payment. A large share of audited forest-offset credits have failed this test (A15).
Normalization of deviance. A pattern in which a program that survives ignoring a warning sign once begins treating that sign as acceptable risk, repeatedly, until the accumulated acceptances produce a failure nobody individually chose (A2).
Kessler cascade. A chain reaction in which orbital debris from one collision causes further collisions, producing more debris, first described in a 1978 paper and increasingly cited as launch cadence rises (A16).
Methalox. Liquid methane and liquid oxygen, the propellant pair behind the reuse economics and the in-situ-fuel ambitions this book’s Part III keeps returning to (A10).
Free return. A translunar path that falls home with no further burn. A path already left is not this path until a burn puts you back on it (A22).
HALE. Healthy life expectancy: years with disability weighted out. Not the pulse on the wall (A28).
Hallmarks. A review’s list of aging mechanisms. A list, not a fuse (A29).
Helium. In this series, a cold product sold as already on the dock: a scheduled immortality, a downtown by Friday, a spare mind, a straight line to furniture nobody can name.
Hot, warm, cold. Established; a working account with real gaps; speculation sold as inventory.
Non-appropriation. The 1967 rule that no nation may claim the Moon as territory. Use is not title. The clause does not contain this book’s three nouns (A23).
Pre-breathe. The wait on a lower-pressure oxygen suit, so dissolved nitrogen does not come out of the blood as bubbles (A17).
Regolith. Broken rock and dust on a surface with no air to round it and no rain to wash the charge off (A20).
Sievert. A dose weighted for biological harm. A thousandth is a millisievert. The open-surface reading in this book, about 1,370 microsieverts a day, is about half a sievert a year if you multiply (A4, A21).
Healthspan. Years you would vote to repeat.
Local fix. This gene, this tissue, this bill.
S-curve. Slow, drunk, wall.
Carrot. A seek loop with no off-switch labeled picture only.
File. A map. Not a worldline.
Proper time. The time a clock reads along its own path. The only clock a body ever keeps.
Maximal aging. Between two events, the unpushed path reads the most; a bend reads less.
Negligible senescence. A hazard of death that stays flat past maturity. Not zero. Not immortal.
Partial reprogramming. A nudge back toward a cell’s younger markings, stopped short of the door marked identity lost.
Torpor. A real, reversible drop in body temperature and metabolic rate. A slower clock, not a paused one.
