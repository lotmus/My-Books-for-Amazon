## 20. A Horizon Is a Fact About Events

A black hole is not a cosmic drain with a personality. It is a one-way membrane in the loaf.

Pick an event — a flash of light, a last radio beep, the moment a clock’s hand ticks. From that event, some future events can still be reached by a signal that never exceeds light. Some cannot. The boundary between those two sets, when gravity has bent the loaf enough, can close on itself. Inside, every future of every event leads deeper. Outside, you can still choose. The closed boundary is the horizon. It is not made of a material. It is made of *facts about which events can influence which others*.

That sentence is the whole chapter, if you let it sit. A horizon is not a black wall, not a hungry mouth, not a place you could chip with a hammer. It is a sorting of the loaf. Chapter 3 already gave you the tool: from any event, a future light cone of things you can still affect and a past cone of things that can still affect you. In ordinary space those cones stay open. Near a sufficiently compact mass they tip and close. Cross the closed surface and Earth’s worldline is no longer in your future cone. You can still be in Earth’s past cone for a while — as a signal, as a photograph, as a last beep. The asymmetry is not a mood. It is geometry.

This is a different object from the *cosmic* event horizon of Chapter 26. That one is a fact about the whole loaf’s later stretch: galaxies whose light, emitted today, never arrives. This one is local. A star collapses. A membrane forms around a clump. Most of the universe does not notice. Do not blur the two just because both are called horizons. One is a weather of the expanding dough. One is a fact about a compact mass.

We knew the local kind was real before anyone made a picture of a shadow. In the 1960s and 1970s, X-ray telescopes found binaries in which a visible star whipped around an unseen compact partner too heavy to be a neutron star. Cygnus X-1 was the rude prototype: a blue giant, a dark companion of more than a few Suns, gas screaming in X-rays as it lost an argument with an orbit it could not leave. The case was circumstantial and then it was not. No other object that heavy and that small holds itself up. The membrane was inferred the way a well is inferred from a missing bucket and a splash you cannot retrieve. The later pictures did not invent the well. They stopped the argument about whether the splash was a mood.

Karl Schwarzschild, in 1916, found the first exact hole in Einstein’s equations while dying, more or less, on the Eastern Front. He was an artilleryman as well as a mathematician. Between trajectory tables he took Einstein’s new field equations, assumed a mass that did not spin and did not charge, and found a clean solution Einstein had not yet written down. He posted the result to Berlin. Einstein read it to the Prussian Academy. Schwarzschild was dead by May, of an illness the war had not invented but had not helped. The radius that now carries his name is

*r_s = 2GM / c²*

*where G is Newton’s constant, M is the mass, and c is the speed of light.*

The formula is not a decoration. It says how small you must squeeze a mass before the membrane forms. Double the mass, double the radius. The speed of light sits in the denominator as a square, which is why everyday objects have absurdly tiny horizons and stars have horizons you could, in a dark mood, walk across. Appendix A20 writes the line and notes that the horizon is a coordinate scare, not a curvature scare: invariants stay finite there. The singularity, later, is the place the theory actually breaks.

A fact about events can still have a radius. That is the useful shock. The sorting of cones happens at a place you can write in kilometers, or millimeters, once you know the mass. For a stellar hole of ten Suns the radius is about thirty kilometers — a city you could hide under a cloud. For Sagittarius A* it is about twelve million kilometers, a good fraction of Mercury’s reach. For M87* it is tens of billions of kilometers, a solar system of darkness. None of those numbers is a wall. Each is the distance, in Schwarzschild’s labels, at which “you can still send a letter home” stops being true. The labels misbehave at that surface; the facts do not. Light aimed outward from just inside never arrives. Light aimed outward from just outside can, if it is lucky and not circling the photon sphere forever.

For the Sun, that radius is about three kilometers. The Sun is not a black hole; it is much larger than its Schwarzschild radius, and proud of it. Squeeze the Sun into those three kilometers and the membrane forms. For Earth, the radius is about nine millimeters — a pea, not a city. For a mountain, it is smaller than a nucleus and we do not live in a cartoon. Nature does not squeeze mountains. Nature squeezes the cores of heavy stars. When the core can no longer hold, and the leftover is heavier than the two or three Suns a neutron star can bear, the membrane is not optional. It is what the equations do with that much mass in that little room.

For the object in the middle of the galaxy M87, the radius is comparable to a solar system. Six and a half billion Suns, packed so that the one-way surface is tens of billions of kilometers across. The Event Horizon Telescope did not photograph the membrane. It photographed the *shadow*: the silhouette of paths that could not escape, against a glowing ring of gas that still could. The picture was released in 2019. It is a radio mosaic from a planet-sized interferometer, not a snapshot from a tourist bus. In 2022 the same collaboration published the hole in our own cellar — Sagittarius A*, four million Suns, a horizon the size of a planetary orbit, flickering on the timescale of a coffee break. Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel had already timed the stars that whip around that cellar, and taken a Nobel for the timing. The shadow was the picture that matched the clocks.

![Figure 20. The ring and dark center of M87*, enlarged. The shadow of a one-way surface.](Figures/figs/fig20.png)

A woman at a console in 2019 — invented, as the How to Read promised; the ring is not — watched the press image load and said, to no one, “That’s a hole.” She was almost right. It is the *absence* of paths. Photons that grazed the photon sphere, a circle at one and a half Schwarzschild radii for a hole that does not spin, can loop and still crawl out to a telescope. Photons that aimed closer cannot. The dark patch is a census of failed escapes. The bright ring is a census of last chances. The membrane itself is not in the JPEG. It is a fact about events the JPEG is allowed to imply.

Hot claim: horizons of this kind exist. Stars orbit something compact and enormous in galactic centers. Mergers ring spacetime and LIGO hears the ring. The shadow is now a picture. On 14 September 2015 — a Monday, if you like kitchen calendars — a ripple passed through Hanford and Livingston, a few milliseconds apart, the way a fact about two colliding holes should. The event is called GW150914. Two holes, about thirty-six and twenty-nine Suns, finished a dance 1.3 billion light-years from this kitchen and became one hole of about sixty-two Suns. Three Suns’ worth of mass left as gravitational waves. For an instant the glow in those waves outshone, in raw power, every star we can name. The announcement came in February 2016. Since then the catalog has grown: more stellar holes, neutron-star pairs, a zoo of masses no optical telescope had weighed. The ringdown is the loaf settling after a slap. It is as close as we have come to hearing a horizon be a horizon.

Warm claim: inside the membrane, Einstein’s theory keeps going until a singularity, a place where the theory itself throws up its hands. The classical interior is a prediction of the same equations that got the shadow and the ringdown right. It is not a photograph. Cold claim: we know what replaces the singularity. We do not. Quantum gravity is a research program. Loop sketches try to bounce. String theory offers replacements — fuzz, a tangle of extra dimensions, a horizon that is not quite a horizon. **String theory cannot currently be tested.** A replacement that cannot be weighed is an idea for ideas, in Barrow’s honest sense, not a second picture to hang beside Figure 20.

The kitchen mistake is to think of the hole as an object you could poke. The useful thought is the membrane of events.

Imagine a last radio beep. A probe, invented, falls toward a stellar hole while a ship waits far out. The probe’s beacon is a metronome. At first the ship hears it on schedule, Doppler and all. Then the ticks stretch. Each beep arrives redder, then redder still, as if someone were pulling the wavelength the way Chapter 8 pulls galaxy light. The probe, by its own clock, crosses the membrane in finite proper time and keeps falling. The ship never quite receives a last tick. The signal fades, redshifts, and is lost in the noise. Earth can still include the probe in *its* past, for a while, as a redder and redder last beep. The probe’s future cone no longer includes Earth. That asymmetry is the whole joke and the whole tragedy. It is also why a hole is not a door.

A woman on the ship — invented; the redshift is not — sits with headphones and a log. She marks the frequency of each beep. 1.4 gigahertz. Then 1.2. Then a smear she has to argue with the noise about. She is not watching a drain swallow a box. She is watching a sequence of events lose the right to be heard. The probe’s last second, on the probe’s clock, still happens. It is a crumb in the loaf. It is not a crumb the ship is allowed to share as a contemporaneous tick. “Now,” which was already in trouble in the kitchen, has no job left at the membrane. There is the probe’s proper time, finite and doomed. There is the ship’s proper time, in which the last beep is an ever-redder rumor. There is no third clock that declares them simultaneous.

A drain in a kitchen sink is a poor metaphor and a useful one. Water goes down. You do not get the water back by staring at the hole. But a drain is a material, with a rim you can touch, and a plumber can reverse it with a snake. A horizon has no rim you can chip. There is no snake. The “down” is a fact about cones. Cross it and you have not entered a basement. You have left the set of events that can still send a letter home.

Barrow liked to shock readers with a number that fits in a teaspoon. Earth’s horizon is nine millimeters. The entire planet, squeezed to a pea, would wear a membrane you could hide under a thumb. We do not do that. Gravity, given a few dozen Suns in a collapsing core, does. The shock is not the cartoon. The shock is that the same formula that makes a pea out of Earth makes a solar-system-sized shadow out of M87*, and that both numbers are now, in different ways, measured.

So: a horizon is a fact about events. It is not a personality, not a drain, not a door. The next chapter parks a clock beside one and watches “now” come apart in a more intimate way. Chapter 24 will take the door away for good. Here, keep the beep. If you remember nothing else, remember that the probe’s last second is not a second the ship is allowed to share.

## 21. An Hour Beside the Edge

Clocks run slow when they sit deep in a gravitational well. This is not a rumor from a story. It is why a clock on a rear truck bed, driven up a mountain and back, comes home younger by a number you can write on a grant application. It is why GPS, without corrections, would slide your map into a river.

The first clean tower measurement has a name and a height. In 1959 and 1960, Robert Pound and Glen Rebka sent iron-57 gamma rays up and down a 22.5-meter shaft in Harvard’s Jefferson Laboratory. Einstein’s 1915 theory said the photons that climbed should arrive tired — a gravitational redshift of a couple of parts in 10¹⁵. The experiment was a nightmare of vibration and recoil, fixed with a trick of moving the source so the Doppler wobble could find the gravitational shift. They found it. The well is not a metaphor. It is a measurable tax on light.

In October 1971, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating put cesium clocks on commercial airliners and flew them around the world, eastward and then westward, against twins that stayed at the United States Naval Observatory. Special relativity said speed would slow the fliers. General relativity said altitude would hurry them. East and west swapped the balance because the Earth is already spinning under the planes. The clocks disagreed when they met again, by hundreds of nanoseconds, in the direction the two effects predicted. The kitchen clock on the wall, the one that pretends to be the time of the cosmos, lost a small, public argument on a 747.

GPS lives on that argument. The satellites orbit at about 20,200 kilometers. Their clocks, left uncorrected, would run fast by roughly 45 microseconds a day from the weaker gravity and slow by about 7 microseconds a day from their speed. The net, about 38 microseconds a day, is 10 kilometers of map error if you ignore it. The phone in your pocket is a daily demonstration that a well changes a clock. You do not need a black hole to believe Chapter 5. You need a constellation and a ditch you would rather not drive into.

What “slow” means, if we are being careful, is not that the lower clock is broken. It is that its worldline is shorter between two meetings. Proper time is mileage. A path that sits deeper in a well is a shorter path, the way a trail that cuts through a valley can be shorter than a trail that stays on the ridge, except here the “shorter” is time itself. Pound and Rebka measured the tax on light. Hafele and Keating measured the tax on clocks that flew and clocks that stayed. GPS spends the tax every second so your map does not drown. The black-hole chapter is the same tax, made rude. Nothing new is invented at the horizon except the size of the bill.

![Figure 21. Two large clocks, one lower. The lower face is marked slow.](Figures/figs/fig21.png)

Near a horizon the same fact becomes rude. A clock hovering just outside, fighting with rockets to stay put, ticks slowly compared with a clock far away. An hour there can be years elsewhere. The numbers in posters — an hour for seven years — require a hole that is both enormous and spinning almost as fast as a hole is allowed to spin. Spin helps. A spinning hole, a Kerr hole, drags space with it and permits stable orbits closer in, where the well is deeper and the tides, for a given mass, are gentler. A supermassive hole can have an hour-beside-the-edge without spaghettifying the visitor. A stellar-mass hole would turn the visitor into a rumor of atoms before the hour was interesting.

Say the poster line out loud, then put a temperature on it. Warm claim: if you have a hole of millions or billions of Suns, spinning so close to the extremal limit that the leftover wiggle is a rounding error, and you park a station in the last orbits the equations still call stable, then an hour of proper time on the station can be years on a distant ship. Generic Kerr hole, generic orbit: the dilation is real and milder. Hours can be days. Days can be weeks. The seven-year postcard is a near-extremal special, not a brochure for every hole in the catalog. Appendix A21 writes the static factor *√(1 − r_s/r)* and notes how close to extremal spin the poster ratio really needs. Allowed is not the same as typical. Typical is not a holiday.

The innermost stable circular orbit — ISCO, in the jargon — is the last racetrack. For a hole that does not spin, it sits at three Schwarzschild radii. Matter in a disk can orbit outside that circle. Inside, it plunges. A Kerr hole, spinning, drags the loaf. Prograde orbits — going with the spin — can sit closer. In the extremal limit the last stable track crowds the horizon itself. That is why spin is not a flourish. It is how you get a station into a well deep enough for the poster, without asking the station to hover on rockets that would have to burn like a star. Even then the station is not comfortable. It is parked on a knife-edge the equations permit and astrophysics may not often build.

Frame-dragging is the grown-up name for the drag. A spinning mass pulls inertial frames around with it, the way a spoon pulled through honey pulls the honey. Lense and Thirring wrote the weak-field version a century ago; Gravity Probe B later measured a tiny twist of gyroscopes in Earth orbit and found the honey, faintly. Near a Kerr hole the honey is not faint. Orbits lean. Light leans. The last stable circle for a stone going with the spin can sit far closer than the last stable circle for a stone going against it. Accretion disks, the glowing junk that makes X-ray binaries and the EHT ring possible, live on those tracks. The woman in the station, if she exists at all, lives on one too. She is not hovering by brute force. She is falling sideways forever, on a rail the spin still allows. The rail is not a gift. Miss it inward and she plunges. Miss it outward and she is no longer in the poster well.

Tides are the other half of the honesty. The stretch across a body scales, near the membrane, as the inverse square of the hole’s mass. A ten-Sun hole, the sort LIGO hears, would pull your head from your feet before you had a philosophical thought about cones. Sagittarius A*, four million Suns, is gentler at its horizon; you could cross the membrane and still be a person for a while, which is not a kindness, only a delay. M87*, billions of Suns, has tides at the horizon that a human body would barely notice. The doom is the same. The itinerary of the doom is not. Do not confuse “the tides will not shred you at the door” with “the door is a place to live.”

Imagine a woman in a small station, parked in that close orbit, tending a rack of algae because someone still has to eat.

The station is the size of a bus that never learned to be a home. The algae live in clear sleeves along one wall, a green smear that bubbles when the pumps are honest. She checks the pH with a strip that stains her fingers. She eats a paste that was sunlight last month, then a smear, then a meal if you are not picky. The water tastes like the filter. The filter tastes like the last filter. There is a window, because the designer was sentimental, and the window shows a smear of lensed sky, a ring of light that is not for her. She does not go outside. Outside is a well.

Morning, by her watch, is a ritual with dirt on it. She bleeds a sleeve that has gone brown, dumps the dead green into a recycler that will pretend to be soil, and seeds a fresh rack because the oxygen budget is a number, not a vibe. The pumps click. A bubble sticks and she flicks the sleeve with a fingernail. This is the work. This is why she is here in the thought experiment: so the geometry has a person who still has to eat, not a tourist with a camera. The ship that sent her is a rumor with a call sign. The hole is not a view. The hole is a well she is parked in.

Her radio to a ship farther out is not just delayed by distance. It is delayed by *time*. She keys the mike and says “good morning” on a day her own log still calls Tuesday. The carrier crawls up the well. The ship hears it on a Thursday she will never have. The reply, when it comes, is already a conversation from a calendar she is not on. She is not time-traveling. She is accumulating less proper time between two meetings. Proper time is the mileage of her worldline, the number her watch is allowed to count. The ship’s worldline, higher on the same well, is longer between the same two reunions. The loaf did not rewind. Two worldlines of different lengths met again.

She keeps two logs because one log would lie. The station log is her proper time: sleep, pH, a paste that was algae, a Tuesday that belongs to her. The ship log, when the radio bothers to be kind, is a scatter of dates that skip. A maintenance window she was told would be “next week” arrives in her headphones as a report of a job already done. A birthday on the ship is a recording. She can answer the recording. She cannot attend the cake. “Now” is not a conference call. It is two mileages, and hers is the shorter.

She logs the algae. She sleeps. She wakes and the ship has had more hours than she has. If she stays a year by her count, the crew that sent her has had a different year — longer, grayer, full of Thursdays that were never hers. When she climbs back out, if the rockets and the orbits allow the climb, the ship’s crew has more gray in it than her mirror. She cannot take the extra years back. She can only have fewer of her own. This is the twin effect with a well instead of a rocket, and it is the only kind of “time travel to the future” relativity hands out for free. It is one-way in the human sense.

Do not book a ticket. This is not a resort and not a clinic. The station stinks of wet plastic. The radio is a stack of monologues, the way the greenhouse in Chapter 25 is a stack of monologues. The dilation that makes an hour into years is a near-extremal poster, not a civic plan. A person is a path, Chapter 5 said. A path that sits in a well is a shorter path. Shorter is not younger in the advertisement sense. Shorter is fewer of her seconds, spent on algae and a sky that does not belong to her. The book will not sell you a life beside a hole. It will sell you the geometry, and then it will send you back to the kitchen clock that was already lying.

Appendix A21 writes the dilation factor and the innermost stable orbit. Here, keep the two clocks. Keep the woman only long enough to feel that “now” was already in trouble in the kitchen. Beside the edge it is in ruins. She says good morning. Thursday answers. The casserole, if anyone still cares about casseroles, was done on a day she will read about and will not have lived.

## 22. The Hole That Runs Backward

If you take a black hole and run the film of spacetime backward, you get a white hole: a membrane you can leave but never enter. Light can come out. Nothing from the outside can go in. In the full, idealized diagram of a non-spinning hole — the Kruskal diagram, which looks like a secret handshake and is, in the appendix, four rooms — there is a black hole region, a white hole region, and two exteriors that meet only at a throat that pinches.

The kitchen version is ruder and clearer. A drain you cannot leave is a black hole. A fountain you cannot enter is a white hole. Same plumbing laws. Opposite manners. You may stand in the yard and watch the spray. You may not climb the spray and hide in the nozzle. You may lose a spoon down the sink. You may not fetch the spoon by asking the drain to run in reverse. The laws of the water do not forbid a film played backward. The objects in a real kitchen do not offer you that film. Eggs smash. They do not unsmash. Horizons form. They do not, around us, unform into fountains.

Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates are the honest map of Schwarzschild’s solution once you stop letting a bad choice of labels pretend the horizon is the end of the world. Schwarzschild’s original labels — the ones that give you *r_s* as a clean number — blow up at the membrane the way a bad map blows up at the poles. Nothing physical explodes there. The labels do. Kruskal and Szekeres, independently, found labels that stay calm across the surface. The map has four rooms. One room is our exterior: the space far from the hole, the place LIGO lives, the place the woman in Chapter 21 might climb back to. Opposite it, on the diagram, is another exterior, a mirror region no probe of ours has a road into. Above is the black-hole interior, the room whose every future leads deeper. Below is the white-hole interior, the room whose every past comes from a membrane you cannot go in through. The four rooms meet at a throat — an Einstein–Rosen bridge, a pinch in the loaf. The pinch lasts, in proper time, no time at all that a traveler could use. Try to cross from one exterior to the other and the throat slams. The diagram is a maximal mathematical extension. It is not a floor plan of something nature built and left unlocked.

Walk the rooms once, slowly, because this is the place posters get drunk. Our exterior is ordinary spacetime with a hole in it, if “ordinary” still means anything. Light cones tip as you approach the membrane but still include a way out, until they don’t. Cross into the black room and every future cone points at the singularity. There is no “turn around.” There is no second knob. The white room is the time-reverse: every past cone comes from a singularity, and the membrane is a past horizon you can leave but never enter. The second exterior is a mathematical twin. It is not Andromeda. It is not a parallel kitchen. It is what the equations write when you refuse to cut the diagram in half. Nature, when it makes a hole from a collapsing star, does not appear to build the white room or the twin exterior. Collapse writes two rooms at most: an outside and a black inside. The other two are what you get if you insist on the whole, eternal, vacuum solution — a hole that has always been there, will always be there, and came with a fountain attached. Stars do not make that object. Stars make a membrane in one direction, in finite time, from matter that used to shine.

![Figure 22. Four big labeled rooms: two exteriors, a black region, a white region.](Figures/figs/fig22.png)

No one has ever seen a white hole. They are allowed as mathematics and unwelcome as astrophysics. They are unstable the way a pencil on its point is unstable. A speck of dust, a photon, a rumor of mass falling toward the past horizon ruins the fountain. Any stuff that tries to enter from the exterior cannot; that is the definition. Any stuff that is already in the white room must leave, and once it has left the fountain is no longer the clean vacuum solution you drew. The universe, as far as we can tell, makes membranes in one direction: collapse, inward, one-way in. It does not spray matter out of ready-made white fountains.

A woman at a kitchen sink — invented; the spoon is optional — watches the drain and then, through the window, a cheap yard fountain that the bulb has not yet burned out. The drain takes water that will not climb back. The fountain throws water she cannot stuff back into the nozzle. Same water. Same gravity. Opposite boundary conditions. She can imagine a film in which the fountain runs in reverse and becomes a drain, and a film in which the drain becomes a fountain. She cannot make either film happen by wishing. The laws she learned in school do not pick a direction. The kitchen does. The kitchen started with an ice cube in a tray and an egg in a shell and a yesterday that was tidier than today. That is the arrow, sitting in the initial mess, not in the plumbing.

Hot claim: black-hole horizons exist and we have weighed them, pictured their shadows, and heard them ring. Warm claim: the time-reverse solution sits in the same notebook and deserves a diagram. Cold claim: there is a white hole in the sky, or at the beginning of time, waiting to be photographed. There is not, on present evidence. There is a picture we can draw.

People like to say the Big Bang was a white hole. The analogy has a poetic muscle and a scientific limp. A white hole is a local object in a larger spacetime. The bang, as Chapter 7 insisted, was not a bomb in a warehouse. It was everywhere. You would not have stood outside a fountain and watched the universe come out. You would have been inside the soup, and so would every other direction. A hole is a place. The bang was a time — a hotter, smaller-spaced version of everywhere. You can draw pictures in which a bang looks, mathematically, like a time-reverse of a collapse. You can also draw pictures in which it does not. Oppenheimer and Snyder, in 1939, wrote the collapse of a ball of dust and got a black hole. Run that solution backward and you get an explosion out of a white membrane into a pre-existing space. That is not the Friedmann loaf. The Friedmann loaf has no outside, no pre-existing dark, no nozzle. The leftover glow is not a spray from a nozzle. It is a baby picture of a fog that filled the loaf.

Keep the nouns on their leashes. A white hole, if one existed, would have a mass, a location, a sky around it. The bang had a temperature, a leftover helium fraction, a glow in every direction. Those are different courtrooms. Mixing them is how a reader ends up asking what the universe exploded *into*, and then answering “a white hole,” and then feeling clever. The feeling is not a fossil.

Conformal cyclic cosmology — CCC, a cold idea — tries to glue a remote, empty future to a next bang with a conformal trick. When the universe is old and thin and almost empty of mass, the remaining massless stuff does not care about a scale. Photons, in a world with no massive clocks left, do not know how big the loaf is. Maybe that future can be rewritten as a beginning. Maybe bang follows bang, not by bouncing through a crunch, but by a rescaling that turns a dead, cold loaf into a new hot one. Its advocates have looked for rings in the leftover glow, scars of previous aeons. The claims are disputed. The idea is allowed to sit on the table. It is not a white hole in the sky. It is a maybe about the shape of the whole loaf. Call it cold and let it sit. Barrow would have walked you to the edge of the idea and then refused to sell it as a fossil. The fossils we have — the glow, the helium, the stretch — do not require a fountain and do not require a previous aeon. They require a hot, dense, expanding everywhere. Chapter 7 already held that courtroom. This chapter will not reopen it with a prettier noun.

A man in a kitchen — invented; the egg is real enough — cracks breakfast into a pan. The film of that event, run backward, is a lawful film in Newton’s mechanics and in Einstein’s, and in the quantum amplitudes that assign the bits of shell a chance. The backward film does not happen. The egg does not climb back into its shell. The reason is not that the membrane of a hole has a preference. The reason is the boundary: a low-entropy yesterday, a kitchen that started orderly, a universe that started hot and smooth enough to leave us a glow. Time-symmetric laws do not have to make time-asymmetric *objects*. Maxwell’s equations do not care which way the light goes, and still a kitchen does not un-bake a casserole. Einstein’s equations do not care which way the membrane faces, and still the sky makes drains, not fountains. Same smash. Same refusal of a fountain. The arrow is in the boundary, in the low-entropy past, not in the membrane’s manners.

Carroll’s work on the arrow, which this book already owes, sits here without being hired as a sermon. If the fundamental rules look the same with the tape reversed, the direction we call time is a fact about the loaf’s edges, not about a little arrow painted on each crumb. A white hole is what you get when you take that tape-reversal literally at a horizon. A smashed egg is what you get when you refuse to. Both lessons belong in the same kitchen. The first is a diagram. The second is breakfast.

Appendix A22 writes the four rooms and the pinch. Here, keep the fountain and the drain. If someone offers you a white hole as an exit, or as a birth, ask whether they have a photograph or a diagram. A diagram is allowed. A photograph would change the temperature of the claim. We have photographs of shadows and ripples. We have no photograph of a spray that cannot be entered. Until we do, the hole that runs backward is a teacher, not a destination. It teaches that the equations are more generous than the sky, and that the sky’s stinginess is information. Collapse happens. Uncollapse, as a local fountain, does not appear to. That is a fact about our loaf, and it is already stranger than a two-way pipe.

One more kitchen caution, because the four rooms look like a floor plan and floor plans make people walk. You cannot enter the white room from our exterior. You cannot use the pinch as a hallway to the twin exterior. You cannot treat the bang as the white room with the labels scraped off. The diagram is a map of a vacuum eternity nobody has built. The sky’s holes are made, in time, from stars, and they come with one membrane and one direction. If a later chapter offers you a handle, it will say so and it will be Chapter 33. This chapter offered you a fountain that cannot be entered and a drain that cannot be left. That is enough plumbing for one sitting.

## 23. The Skin and the Leak

Jacob Bekenstein asked a rude question in the early 1970s: if you throw an encyclopedia into a black hole, where did the information go? If it vanishes, physics has lost its memory. If it stays, it has to stay somewhere that still counts.

The year was 1972, then 1973, papers that made respectable people itchy. An encyclopedia is a useful kitchen object. It has a finite number of sentences. You can, in principle, count the bits. Drop it down a well and the well gets a little heavier. The horizon grows by a tiny area. If ordinary thermodynamics still applies — if you are not allowed to hide entropy by throwing it into a hole — then the hole itself must gain at least as much unread-ness as the encyclopedia lost to the outside world. Bekenstein argued that the hole’s entropy — its hidden information, its unread library — scales with the *area* of the horizon, not the volume inside. Hawking first thought the idea was wrong, and said so with the confidence of a man who had already proved that horizons only grow. Then, in 1974 and 1975, he found that the same calculation that fixed the coefficient also made the hole glow. A glowing hole has a temperature. A temperature and an energy give you an entropy. The coefficient came out four. The entropy is

*S = kA / 4ℓ_P²*

*where A is the horizon area, k is Boltzmann’s constant, and ℓ_P is the Planck length, a ridiculously small ruler made from G, ħ, and c.*

The Planck length is about 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ meters. A square of that on a side is not a shelf. It is a pixel of the smallest bookkeeping the theory we have can write. One bit per few square Planck lengths on the skin. A solar-mass hole has a horizon area of a few thousand square kilometers and an entropy so large the number looks like a prank: on the order of 10⁷⁷ *k*, a library that would embarrass every warehouse on Earth and still fit, in the accounting, on a sphere thirty kilometers across. The inside, however deep it seems, does not get extra shelf space. This is the holographic idea in its first, stubborn form: the inventory of a region can be written on its boundary.

Hawking had already shown, classically, that the area of a horizon does not shrink. That area theorem is why he resisted Bekenstein: if area only grows, and if entropy is not area, then throwing the encyclopedia in looks like a way to cheat the second law. Once entropy *is* area, the theorem becomes a second law for holes. The encyclopedia’s unread sentences are not deleted from the universe’s books. They are charged to a lid that got a little wider. The glow, later, is the hole’s way of paying the bill in reverse — area shrinking, very slowly, as heat leaves. Classically the lid only grows. Quantum mechanically the lid can leak. Both statements can be true in their seasons.

Kitchen version, before the ambition starts. You throw an encyclopedia into a well. You want to know whether the universe still knows the encyclopedia. You do not get to climb in and check the basement. You get to watch the lid. The lid gets a little wider. The unread library, if it is still in the account, is written on the cover, not stacked in a volume you could walk. Count the lid. Do not count a warehouse that no letter can leave. The shock is Barrow’s kind of shock: the biggest object you can name — a billion-Sun hole — still files its secrets on a surface, at a density so fierce the numbers look like jokes until you remember that the jokes are the theory.

![Figure 23. A sphere. Hash marks on the skin only. The words: count the area.](Figures/figs/fig23.png)

People then got ambitious. Maybe the whole universe is a hologram. Maybe a gravity theory in a volume is secretly a quantum theory on a wall. In one special, negatively curved kind of space, this has been made precise enough to write papers that get jobs. Juan Maldacena’s AdS/CFT duality, from 1997, is the precise form: a gravitational theory in an Anti-de Sitter box, a space that caves in the way a saddle caves, matches a quantum field theory on the wall of the box. Calculations that look like black-hole thermodynamics on the inside look like ordinary quantum bookkeeping on the wall. That is a triumph of a model. That space is not our accelerating, leftover-glow space. We live with a shove, Chapter 18’s shove, that looks like a positive cosmological constant, a de Sitter mood, not an Anti-de Sitter box. A duality that works in a box we do not inhabit is not a photograph of the kitchen. It is a constraint on how ambitious you are allowed to be when you invent hiding places for bits. The leap from “black holes count on their skin” to “your kitchen is a projection” is a leap. Warm-to-cold. Useful as a constraint: you do not get to invent an infinitely roomy crunch-computer (Chapter 19) without saying where the bits sit. Tipler needed a loophole in a bound like this. The bound is not impressed.

Holography does not cancel events. It relocates the bookkeeping. The loaf may be a projection of something thinner. The events you care about — a last beep, a first helium nucleus, a woman saying good morning to a Thursday she will not have — still happen. They may have fewer hiding places than a volume would suggest. That is a change in the furniture of the account. It is not a vanishing. It is not a promise that a lost encyclopedia can be checked out later like a library book. It is a rule about how much unread stuff a region is allowed to hold.

The glow is a slow leak from the membrane.

In the kitchen version: near the horizon, the vacuum’s jitters get sorted. One part of a jitter falls in. The other part escapes. From far away, the hole is radiating, very slightly, as if it had a temperature. The formula, if you want the shape of it, is *T_H = ħ c³ / (8π G M k)*: colder when heavier, hotter when lighter, Planck’s constant in the numerator so that a purely classical hole would not glow at all. Hawking’s temperature for a solar-mass hole is about 10⁻⁷ kelvin — ten millionths of a degree above absolute zero, a hundred million times colder than the leftover glow in your kitchen. Small holes are hotter. A hole the mass of a mountain would sizzle. A hole the mass of an asteroid would be a brief, rude oven. A hole the mass of a star is colder than the leftover glow and, today, *gains* weight from the sky faster than it leaks. Only in the unimaginably far future, after the leftover glow has thinned, would stellar holes begin to lose.

The leftover glow is 2.725 kelvin. A solar-mass hole is a hundred million times colder than that. The sky, for now, is a heat bath the hole sits in, and the bath wins. This is why “black holes evaporate” is a true sentence about a later season of the loaf and a false poster about Tuesday. Tuesday, the hole in Cygnus X-1 is getting slightly heavier from the microwave fog and from whatever gas it can steal. The leak is real in the derivation. The leak is not winning.

If nothing interrupts, the leak eventually takes all the mass. The evaporation time for a solar-mass hole is about 10⁶⁷ years — a one with sixty-seven zeros, a number that makes the 13.8 billion years of the stretch look like a blink in a blink. Barrow would have put that integer on a blackboard and let it sit until someone laughed. The laugh is allowed. The integer is the theory. The hole shrinks, heats, shrinks faster, and ends in a last, small violence. Whether the encyclopedia comes back in the last violence, or came out slowly in the glow, or is written on a remnant, is the information problem. It is unfinished.

The fight has a shape, even unfinished. Hawking’s first glow looked thermal, which is a polite word for “the radiation does not remember the encyclopedia’s sentences, only the hole’s mass, spin, and charge.” If the hole then vanishes, the sentences have nowhere to live. That is a quarrel with quantum mechanics, which prefers to keep its books unitary: information is shuffled, not shredded. Proposed peace treaties have names. Complementarity says the outside and the inside are two accounts of one book, and you are not allowed both at once. Firewalls say the membrane might be a violent place after all, a contradiction dressed as a heat bath. Islands in the recent math say the entropy of the glow can fall late in the evaporation, as if the sentences had been hiding in the radiation all along and only became countable later. None of these is a photograph. All of them are warmer than a shrug and colder than a settlement. It is not unfinished in a way that lets you use a hole as a filing cabinet for resurrection. A soul is not an encyclopedia. An encyclopedia is not a person. Neither one is stored in a basement you can open later by being patient, or pious, or good at grants.

Hawking radiation has not been seen from an astrophysical hole. It is a derived glow, as firm as a derivation can be without a photograph. Laboratory analogs — sound horizons in fluids, tricks with lasers, a Bose–Einstein condensate with a sonic membrane — have made related noises. Bill Unruh taught the field to listen for a sound that cannot climb out of a moving fluid: if the fluid runs faster than sound, there is a surface sound cannot recross, and the math of that surface is cousin to the math of Chapter 20. Jeff Steinhauer and others have built such membranes in the lab and reported a glow with the right family resemblance. They are encouraging and not the same as weighing a photon from Cygnus X-1. An analog horizon is a teacher. It is not the leak from M87*. You can poke the fluid. You cannot poke M87*. Keep the temperatures straight. The derivation is warm-to-hot. The photograph is missing. The analog is a cousin.

For the travel chapters, the leak is a timekeeper. It says black holes are not eternal furniture. They are long-lived, then gone, on clocks that make civilizations look like sparks. If you want a library to last, do not drop it in. Put it under ice, or in a vault, or in a machine that can wait. The membrane is a fact about events. It is not a basement.

A woman — invented; the bound is not — stands at a real well in a yard and holds a battered encyclopedia, the kind with thumb-indexes and a broken spine. She could keep it. She could throw it. If she throws it, the well does not become a library. The lid, if the well were a hole, would remember in a way no visitor can read. That is the teaching. That is all the teaching. Do not promote the well to a filing cabinet. Do not promote the glow to a last judgment that hands the sentences back. Count the skin. Admit the leak. Leave the resurrection for books that are not this one.

## 24. Not a Door

The last mistake is the door.

A door has two sides you can use. A horizon has two sides you cannot trade. A kitchen door has two knobs. You go out for the mail. You come back for the casserole. The loaf of the house does not mind. You can stand in the yard and still be in the kitchen’s future, and stand in the kitchen and still be in the yard’s future. That is what two knobs *are*: two rooms that keep each other in their cones. A one-way membrane is not that door. Fall in and you do not come out “somewhere else” in this universe with a story to tell. There is no second knob. There is no mail. There is a sorting of events, and you have joined the set that cannot write home.

The flattering picture is always the same. The hole is a gate. The gate leads to a tunnel. The tunnel leads to another kitchen, or another century, or another you. The picture borrows its furniture from doors because doors are the one-way-looking objects we actually own, and because we have all, at least once, wanted a room that was not this one. Physics will not rent you that room at a horizon. The membrane does not open. It sorts. If you want a handle between two exteriors, that is a wormhole, and it is Chapter 33’s problem, and it is not this object. If you want a future in this universe with a story to tell, stay outside.

In the classical theory you come to a singularity, a ripped place in the loaf, and the theory stops. The geodesics — the straightest paths the geometry allows, the paths free clocks and free stones follow — do not come out. A geodesic that crosses *r_s* reaches *r = 0* in a proper time you can write on a napkin: tens of microseconds for a stellar hole, hours for Sagittarius A*, the better part of a day for a giant like M87*. Those are not transit times to another station. They are the lengths of worldlines that end. They reach, in that finite proper time, a boundary the equations cannot extend through. That is what “singularity” means when it is being honest. It is not a glowing point with a brand name. It is geodesic incompleteness: the paths stop, and the theory that was telling you how they lean has nothing left to say. Appendix A24 writes the line. Here it is enough to refuse the hallway. A path that ends is not a path that arrives in another kitchen.

You can try to be clever. You can fire rockets inside. You can aim “sideways.” Inside a Schwarzschild hole there is no sideways that leads out. The radial direction has become timelike: “out” is as unavailable as “yesterday.” That sentence is the whole joke, dressed as a coordinate fact. The membrane is not a wall you bounced off. It is a date you cannot un-reach. The geodesics do not come out because “out” is no longer a direction in their future cones. Chapter 20’s last beep, heard from the ship, is the outside’s view of a crossing that, inside, is already a one-way season.

The singularity theorems, and Hawking’s cosmological versions, say that if gravity pulls in the ordinary way and energy behaves, collapse and bang-like expansions have boundaries the geodesics cannot extend through. Penrose’s 1965 result is the local one: a trapped surface — a sphere from which light is already being dragged inward, outgoing as well as incoming — plus ordinary energy conditions, and the loaf is incomplete. You do not need to assume a pretty symmetry. You need a trapped surface and a rule that energy is not too exotic, and the paths cannot be extended forever. That is why a collapsing star is not a special cartoon. It is a case of a general sentence. Hawking applied the same muscle to the early universe: if the past is a trapped place in the right sense, the bang, too, is incomplete as a geodesic story. Inflation and dark energy violate some of those energy assumptions, which is why the theorems are not a courtroom for “the universe began” in every model. A burst that repels, a shove that stretches, can dodge a hypothesis the 1960s treated as polite. They are still a courtroom for “you may not treat the inside of a hole as a hallway.” A stellar collapse still makes a trapped surface. Ordinary matter still obeys the energy conditions the theorems asked for. A trapped surface is not a doorframe. It is a proof that the paths do not return.

Hot claim: if you fall into a hole of the kind we have pictured and heard, classical general relativity does not give you an exit in this universe. Warm claim: a quantum theory of gravity will replace the ripped place with something that does not infinite-curve. Cold claim: that something is a door, a bounce you can steer, a baby cosmos with a forwarding address. The warm claim is a research program. The cold claim is a hope wearing a lab coat. This book will not rent the coat.

In speculative theory you might come to a bounce, a baby universe, a tangle of extra dimensions. Those are cold. They are also, even if true, not a transit system. A baby universe you cannot call is not a destination. A bounce that pinches off a new loaf, if such a pinch exists, is a maybe about the interior, not a ticket window. You do not steer it. You do not mail a letter through it. You do not retire there. The new loaf, in the sketches that allow one, is not “somewhere else” on a map you already own. It is a separate region of events, pinched off, causally unavailable to the ship that watched the last beep. Calling that a trip is like calling death a change of address because the nouns still exist in a dictionary.

Extra dimensions offered by string theory come with the standing rule: **the framework cannot currently be tested.** A hallway drawn in an untestable framework is a drawing. It is not a road. Loop-quantum sketches that replace the ripped place with a bounce are a different drawing, also unphotographed, also not a subway. This book will let the drawings sit. It will not sell tickets. A cold interior is allowed. A transit system is a different claim, and it fails even if the cold interior is true, because availability is the whole of what “destination” means. If you cannot call, you did not arrive. If no one can call you, you are not a station.

Wormholes, in Chapter 33, are a different object: a handle, not a membrane. People blur them because both are “holes” and both are associated with Einstein. Do not blur them. One is common and one-way. The other is unphotographed and probably expensive beyond engineering. A wormhole, if nature or a civilization ever held one open, would be a throat between two exteriors you could, in the optimistic diagrams, enter and leave. You would see two mouths, two skies, two knobs, if the metaphor will sit still that long. It needs exotic stress, a violation of the energy conditions the singularity theorems were using as police — a stuff that antigravitates in the throat so the pinch does not slam. The Kruskal throat of Chapter 22 is not that object. That throat pinches in finite proper time and cannot be traversed. The Morris–Thorne kind of handle is a different drawing, built to stay open, and it pays for the staying with matter no kitchen has ever weighed. It is a maybe about a handle on the block. A black hole is not that handle. A black hole is the membrane we have already pictured and heard. Confusing them is how a shadow in a ring becomes, in a careless paragraph, a subway. This book will not be careless. Chapter 33 can spend its own pages on the handle. This page is the refusal.

A short inventory, so the blur has nowhere to hide. Horizon: a fact about events, one-way, photographed as a shadow, heard as a ringdown. White hole: the time-reverse, undrawn in the sky, unstable. Kruskal throat: a pinch, not a tunnel you can walk. Wormhole: a proposed handle, two-way if it exists, not this chapter, not in the EHT image. Baby universe: a cold pinch-off, not a destination. Singularity: where the geodesics stop, not a station. If a paragraph uses two of those nouns as if they were one noun, the paragraph is a poster. Put it down.

![Figure 24. A bright host galaxy, high contrast. A real hole lives here. It is still not a door.](Figures/figs/fig24.png)

Look at Figure 24 and practice the sentence. A real hole lives in a real galaxy. Stars orbit. Gas glows. A shadow, if we had the baseline, would sit in the middle the way Figure 20 sits in M87. None of that is a door. The host is not a terminal. The dark is not an arrival hall. The honest nouns are still the ones Chapter 20 paid for: a fact about events, a last beep, a leak so slow it makes civilizations look like sparks.

A woman in a kitchen — the same invented kitchen, a different mistake — puts her hand on both knobs of the back door and thinks of a well. The door opens inward and outward. The well, if it were a horizon, would not. She can fetch the mail. She cannot fetch the probe. The difference is not poetry. It is which events remain in which cones. Chapter 2 said an event is a happening, not a thing. A door is a thing we built to keep two rooms in conversation. A horizon is a happening-boundary we did not build. Promoting the second to the first is the last mistake because it is the most flattering. It lets you keep the casserole and the adventure. Physics will let you keep the casserole. It will not let you keep the adventure as a round trip.

She opens the door, because that is what knobs are for, and looks at the yard. The fountain from Chapter 22, if this is the same invented house, is still throwing water she cannot climb. The sink is still a drain she cannot reverse. The well, if there were a well, would still be a lid you count and do not enter. Three kitchen objects, three lessons, none of them a gate to another cosmos. She closes the door. Both knobs still work. That is the privilege of a house. It is not the privilege of a hole.

So: no transit. No filing cabinet for souls. No steered crunch. No baby-universe bus. A shadow in a ring, a slow leak, a one-way fact about events. That is the honest hole. It is enough. It is already stranger than a door.

The part that began with Schwarzschild’s letter from the front ends here, on a galaxy that does not owe us an exit. The next part will try to go somewhere anyway — a greenhouse, a delay, a launch window — without once treating a membrane as a knob. If you wanted the hole to be a door, you wanted a different loaf. This one sorts events and does not give them back. Take the casserole out when your clock says so. Leave the well alone.
