# PART III — A Burst, Then Seeds

## 12. Three Puzzles the Hot Bang Cannot Solve

The leftover glow and the leftover helium make the hot, expanding past look inevitable. They do not make it comfortable. Three puzzles sit in the model like bills on a table, and the three-minute workshop does not pay them.

The first is the *horizon problem*. Look at the microwave sky in two opposite directions. The temperatures match to a part in a hundred thousand. For two patches to agree that tightly, they usually need to have talked — to have exchanged light, mixed, come to terms. In the simple hot bang, those opposite patches have never been in each other’s past light cones. They are like two strangers who have never met and have never had a friend in common, wearing the same shirt to the same size. You can call it a coincidence. Physicists get hives.

The kitchen version is two thermometers.

Tape one to the wall above the stove. Tape the other to the wall by the back door. They agree to a part in 10⁵. In an ordinary kitchen that is not a miracle. Air mixed. Light bounced. The fan ran. The two walls have been in conversational range for hours. Now shrink the time the kitchen has existed until light from the stove has not yet had a chance to reach the door. The thermometers still agree. That is the horizon problem, moved off the sky and onto tile.

At 380,000 years, when the fog lifted and the leftover glow set out, a causal horizon — the farthest a light signal could have traveled since the simple bang began — was a small patch on the sky we photograph now. A couple of degrees. A thumbnail at arm’s length. The whole dome of the microwave sky is not a couple of degrees. Opposite patches sit a hundred thumbnails apart, and then some. The causal horizon at last scattering is much smaller than the sky. In the simple bang, the patches labeled A and B in Figure 12 have never been in each other’s past light cones. They did not mix. They did not come to terms. They still match, to one part in 10⁵, as if agreement were free.

You can try to rescue the simple bang by saying the universe started already even, on scales that had never talked. That is not a solution. That is a shrug wearing a lab coat. It pushes the evenness into the initial conditions and then looks innocent. Physics is supposed to *make* evenness, or at least to inherit it from a time when talking was possible. The simple bang, run backward from the leftover glow, does not give you that time. The leftover glow is a photograph of a single age, the entire sky at 380,000 years. The photograph is too even for the age.

Agreement, in physics, is not a fashion. Two patches of plasma come to the same temperature because photons ricochet between them until the hot spots have given their extra to the cold spots. That is thermal equilibrium, the same “we have mixed thoroughly” signature that makes the leftover glow a blackbody. Mixing needs a path. A path needs time. In the simple bang the time from a hot start to last scattering is 380,000 years, which sounds generous until you remember that the last-scattering surface we see is not 380,000 light-years across. Expansion has been stretching the rulers. The region that could have mixed by then subtends about a degree or two on today’s sky. Opposite patches — 180 degrees of argument — contain thousands of those little mixed rooms, none of which had a hallway to the others.

One part in 10⁵ is not a casual “pretty even.” It is the measured amplitude of the remaining freckles after the dipole of our own motion is subtracted. The average temperatures of opposite hemispheres match far tighter than a kitchen thermometer. Chapter 3’s light cones, applied at 380,000 years, do not overlap for A and B. Figure 12 is that non-overlap, drawn as a circle because the sky is a circle when you have only two dimensions of paper. The simple bang has no conversation that can buy the match. It has only a hope that the start was already a match. Hopes are not mechanisms.

The second is the *flatness problem*. The universe’s geometry — whether parallel lines stay parallel on the largest scales — is astonishingly close to “flat,” which is another way of saying the density is astonishingly close to a special critical value. In the simple bang, that closeness is unstable. A little too much density in the first second and the universe would have recollapsed before helium was done. A little too little and it would have become so empty, so fast, that galaxies would never have found each other. We live on a knife-edge that the equations keep trying to throw us off. Either someone balanced a pebble, or something *drove* the pebble to the middle of the blade.

Barrow liked a pebble on a blade. The blade is the critical density: the exact budget of stuff that makes *Ω = 1*, the universe in which the large-scale geometry does not curl into a ball or splay into a saddle. A little above, and gravity wins on a short clock. A little below, and the stretch wins so fast that clumps never find each other. We measure, today, a universe so close to that blade that the leftover glow, the later bombs of Chapter 18, and the frozen sound-scale in the galaxies all vote *flat* to within a percent or better. That is a fact about now. The insult is a fact about then.

In a decelerating bang — radiation, then matter, no burst — the quantity |Ω − 1| is driven *away* from zero as time runs forward. Closeness is unstable. Run the same equations toward the past and the required closeness blows up. By the time the clock reads one second, the pebble has to sit in the middle of the blade to something like one part in 10¹⁶. Not one part in a hundred. Not one part in a million. A 1-second pebble that would shame a watchmaker. Bump it, and you do not get a slightly different kitchen. You get a recollapse before helium, or a desert that never makes a galaxy.

Nobody balanced it with fingers. Either the initial conditions were an unpublished miracle, or something drove the pebble to the middle and held it there long enough that later physics would look inevitable. Inflation, in the next chapter, is the candidate drive. Here, notice only the bill: the simple bang does not explain the pebble. It assumes the pebble, and then spends 13.8 billion years trying to kick it off.

One second is not a mystic number. It is the door of Weinberg’s workshop: neutrinos have just decoupled, the neutron-to-proton ratio is freezing, the nuclear minutes are about to start. If the pebble is already wrong at that door, the workshop never opens, or opens onto a warehouse with no walls. The required closeness at the Planck time is worse — 10⁻⁶⁰, a number Barrow would have put on a chalkboard to watch a room go quiet. We do not need the Planck time to feel the insult. The 1-second pebble is rude enough. A pebble is not a mechanism either. It is a way of saying the tuning has a clock reading, and the clock reading is uncomfortably early.

The third is the *relic problem*. Grand unified theories — warm-to-cold ideas about how the forces were once one force — tend to produce leftover monsters: magnetic monopoles, heavy stable particles, knots in fields. The simple bang would have left the universe ankle-deep in them. We do not trip over monopoles on the way to the mailbox. Something diluted the relics, or they were never made in our past, or the grand theories are wrong. The first option is the one inflation was built to offer.

A magnetic monopole, in this context, is not a bar magnet you can put on the fridge. It is a knot in a grand-unified field, a particle that would be a north without a south, heavy, stable, and — if the simple bang passed through the grand-unified temperature — as common as the horizon would allow. One per causal patch, roughly, and then they sit there, too massive to decay, too stubborn to hide. They would outweigh the atoms. They would wreck the leftover helium’s accounting. They would be underfoot.

They are not underfoot. That is a fact you can take to the mailbox. So either the grand theories are wrong about the knots, or the knots were never cooked in our past, or something diluted them by a ridiculous factor — 10²⁵ in entropy, says the appendix, if you want a number to flinch at. Inflation was built, in part, as a dilution machine. Guth was thinking about monopoles when he started. The even sky and the pebble came along as bonuses. The bonuses are now the famous posters. The monopoles are the quiet third bill.

![Figure 12. A circle for the last-scattering sky. Two opposite patches, A and B. A short mark showing they never met in the simple bang.](Figures/figs/fig12.png)

Imagine the kitchen table at the end of Weinberg’s workshop.

Three envelopes were already paid. The leftover glow exists. The leftover helium exists. The stretch exists. Those are receipts. You can put them in a drawer. Then three more envelopes sit where the casserole should be, unpaid, the kind of bills that do not go away because you cooked a good dinner. One says HORIZON. One says FLATNESS. One says RELICS. The three-minute workshop did not write checks for them. It was not that kind of workshop. It reconstructed a soup. It did not explain why the soup’s bowl was so round, so even, and so empty of monsters.

Weinberg paid the nuclear minutes. He paid them with the same nuclear physics we still use in stars and in laboratories, and the helium still sits in the account. These three remain. They are not evidence that the workshop was a fiction. They are evidence that the workshop started with a table already set, and nobody at the table could say who set it.

None of these puzzles says the hot bang is false. They say it is incomplete as a story of *initial conditions*. Weinberg could reconstruct the nuclear minutes without knowing why the sky was so even. Guth, in 1980, asked whether a brief, violent stretch *before* those minutes could set the table so the minutes would look inevitable.

Guth was not, in 1980, handing the community a finished preface. He was a particle theorist staring at monopoles, asking whether a false-vacuum stretch could dilute them, and noticing that the same stretch would also even the sky and drive the pebble. The paper is a question with a mechanism attached, not a verdict. Old inflation, the first mechanism, will fail to exit. That failure is Chapter 13’s opening debt. The question survives the debt. The three bills are still on the table either way.

That question is the opening of Part III. It is not yet the solution. Guth’s first try will have a graceful-exit problem, a mess about how to stop, which Chapter 13 will have to clean. The sky will later be photographed in polarization by people who were sometimes too eager. Those fights belong to the next pages. Here the honest inventory is three unpaid bills, a paid nuclear dinner, and a physicist asking whether a burst could be the missing payment.

That is a different kind of move. The three minutes are a reconstruction. Inflation is a proposed preface. Prefaces can be wrong even when the book is right. Keep the temperatures straight. The puzzles are hot. The leading solution is warm. The details of the preface — what field, what potential, what first 10⁻³⁶ second — are still being written in pencil.

## 13. Inflation, Without the Hype

Here is inflation without the poster.

Suppose, in a sliver of time so short that “sliver” is already too romantic, a patch of the universe was filled with a field that behaved like a temporary cosmological constant: a stuff whose tension was so odd that gravity, locally, *repelled*. The patch doubled in size, and doubled again, and again, sixty or more times. Distances that had been smaller than a proton became larger than a grapefruit, then larger than a solar system, then larger than the part of the universe we can see. Then the field decayed. Its energy dumped into particles. The dump is called reheating, which is a calm word for setting a universe on fire. After the dump, you have a hot bang: soup, light, the three minutes, the leftover glow.

The kitchen is a pot on a high flame.

For a sliver the pot does not simmer. It *boils over*: the volume of steam is not a polite trickle, it is a room-filling insult, doubling and doubling. Then the whole violent pot dumps into a larger pot of soup. The dump is reheating. After the dump you have the hot bang. The boil-over is not the soup. The soup is what you can reconstruct. The boil-over is the proposed preface that makes the soup’s evenness less of a miracle.

The count that matters is *e-folds*. Each e-fold stretches every distance by a factor of *e*, about 2.7, which is almost a tripling if you are being fussy and a doubling if you are teaching. About 50 to 60 e-folds are what the three puzzles ask for: enough stretch that the whole sky we photograph was once inside one talking neighborhood, enough that a curved surface looks flat, enough that one relic per pre-burst patch becomes one relic per observable universe, or fewer. Sixty of them take a proton-scale patch and throw it past everything we can see. The clock for the whole insult is of order 10⁻³² seconds. That number is a round order, not a kitchen timer. It depends on how hard the field was shoving. It is short the way a lightning strike is short, except lightning is leisurely.

Slow-roll is how the pot is allowed to boil that long without instantly dumping. If the field sprinted down its hill, the boil-over would be over before it had stretched enough, or it would dump in a mess of bubbles. Slow-roll is a hill so flat the field creeps. Hubble friction acts like a damper. Sixty e-folds of almost-constant shove, then a dump. The field that does this job is called the inflaton because physics names things by their chores. We have not met it in a detector. It is unnamed in the only sense that matters: no mass, no charge, no track in a tank.

Reheating deserves a slower look, because the dump is where the preface becomes the book we already trust.

While the field is slow-rolling, its energy is locked in the odd tension: vacuum-like, smooth, shoving. When the field reaches the bottom of the hill it oscillates. Oscillation is particles, or at least the right to become particles. Those particles collide, thermalize, and you have a radiation bath — the hot bang, at last, with a temperature that depends on how the dump was done. The reheating temperature is model-dependent. The appendix will not apologize for that. What is not model-dependent is the order of operations: burst, dump, soup, three minutes, leftover glow. You do not get to skip the dump and still claim the helium. The helium is soup-work. The burst is table-setting.

Fifty to sixty e-folds is a window, not a sacred count. Too few, and a corner of today’s sky was never inside the talking neighborhood; the horizon bill stays unpaid. Too many is allowed by the local job description and starts to grow the rumor of Chapter 15: extra stretch somewhere else, extra rooms, a pot that does not stop. The local sky asks for about sixty. It does not ask for infinity. Do not let infinity borrow sixty’s paycheck.

Picture two kitchens, two years apart. In the first, 1980, a pot is boiling so violently that bubbles of “done” soup nucleate in a superheated sea and never quite fill the pot; the walls between bubbles are a mess. That is old inflation, Guth’s first try, the graceful-exit problem as a cooking failure. In the second, 1982, the flame is high but the pot is a wide, almost-flat pan. The whole surface comes up to temperature together and then, when the field finds the lip, dumps as one. That is slow-roll. Linde’s pan and Albrecht–Steinhardt’s pan were independent. Physics sometimes invents the same dish twice in one year when the bill is loud enough.

Why bother? Because the three puzzles go pale.

The opposite patches of sky *did* talk. They talked when they were neighbors, before the burst pulled them out of conversational range. The burst then stretched their agreement across the whole sky. The horizon problem becomes a memory of an old neighborhood.

The knife-edge of flatness gets driven toward the middle. Stretch a curved surface enough and any small patch looks flat. A basketball, to an ant the size of an atom, is a plane. Inflation is the move that makes us the ant.

The relics get diluted. One monopole per pre-burst patch becomes one monopole per observable universe, or fewer. We stop tripping.

That is the job description. It is not yet a proof. A preface that pays three bills is a good preface. It can still be the wrong preface, or the right job done by a field we have not named.

Repulsive, here, is not a personality. Einstein’s gravity, for ordinary stuff, pulls. For a stuff whose pressure is negative enough — a cosmological-constant-like tension — the same equations shove. The burst is that shove, brief and huge. It is not anti-gravity in a comic-book sense. It is the Friedmann equation with a budget that has the wrong sign for anyone who grew up on apples. Then the budget changes, because the field rolls and dumps, and apples make sense again. Figure 13 is that change of budget: a steep rise labeled burst, then a gentler slope labeled after. The after is the hot bang we already rebuilt. The rise is the proposed reason the after looks so unsurprising.

![Figure 13. One curve: a steep rise labeled burst, then a gentler slope labeled after.](Figures/figs/fig13.png)

Alan Guth’s first version had a graceful-exit problem: the burst did not know how to stop without making a mess. Andrei Linde, and independently Albrecht and Steinhardt, found a smoother hill for the field to roll down — *slow-roll*. Linde later suggested that the starting values of the field could be chaotic, different in different places, some patches inflating a lot, some not. That idea will grow a maybe-multiverse in Chapter 15. Do not let it jump the line. The *local* burst is the working theory. The froth of other bursts is a rumor the local burst can tell if you let it drink.

The graceful-exit problem, said without kindness, is this. Guth’s 1980 boil-over happened in pockets. True-vacuum bubbles nucleated in a false-vacuum sea and did not fill space without leaving walls and wreckage. The burst that was supposed to set the table set a junkyard instead. In 1982, Linde, and independently Albrecht and Steinhardt, flattened the hill. The whole patch rolls slowly together. New inflation, slow-roll. That is the working picture, the one whose job description still fits the leftover glow. Old inflation is a historical kindness we owe Guth for asking the question. It is not the pot we cook with.

What inflation has not done: name the field. We have no particle in a detector called the inflaton. We have a shape that works and a sky that looks like the shape predicted. That is more than a mood and less than a conviction. Appendix A13 writes the e-folds and the slow-roll parameters. Here it is enough to know that “inflation” is not a brand. It is a job description: a brief, repulsive stretch that sets the table for the hot bang we can actually reconstruct.

A humility from 2014 belongs on this page, because poster-inflation hates humility and the sky does not.

A telescope at the South Pole, BICEP2, looked for swirls in the leftover glow — B-modes — that a burst would have printed with gravitational waves. In 2014 the team announced a detection. The world did a lap. Warm claim, briefly worn as hot. Then dust in our own galaxy, polarized dust, sat down in the same seat as the signal. A 2015 joint analysis with Planck was the kill. The swirl was not the burst. The swirl was grit in the kitchen, the kind that lives in the Milky Way and polarizes starlight for a living. Humility is not a personality trait in a press office. It is what the sky did when someone claimed to have photographed the first 10⁻³² second.

The kill is worth teaching because it is how a warm claim dies correctly.

B-modes are a swirl pattern in the polarization of the leftover glow. Inflationary gravitational waves would write one kind of swirl, a primordial kind, whose amplitude is a number called *r*, the tensor-to-scalar ratio. Dust writes another kind, because dust grains in our galaxy align with magnetic fields and scatter starlight with a handedness of their own. BICEP2 looked at a clean-looking patch of southern sky at 150 GHz and saw swirls. Planck, looking at many frequencies, could tell dust from glow by color. Dust is redder in a specific way. The joint analysis showed the color of dust. *r* was not measured. An upper limit remained. Upper limits are not nothing. They are a fence the hill has to live behind. They are also not a detection. The poster that said first direct evidence had to be taken down. Taking it down is part of the science. Leaving it up would have been a different book.

What survived the dust is quieter and better. Planck measured the tilt of the primordial spectrum: *n_s* ≈ 0.96, a little redder than a perfectly scale-invariant 1. Slow-roll likes that song. A hill that is not perfectly flat, a field that creeps, a spectrum that leans a little toward larger scales: that is the shape, not the photograph of a particle. A tilt is a clue. Clues are allowed to be warm. They are not allowed to be a victory lap.

String theory sometimes offers inflatons the way a catalog offers furniture. The catalog is large. **It cannot currently be tested.** We will not pay the book’s rent with a catalog.

A catalog is tempting because the job description is wide. Many hills slow-roll. Many fields, in many theories, can pretend to be a temporary cosmological constant for 10⁻³² seconds and then dump. String theory’s catalog is famous for being large. Large is not the same as found. A furniture catalog does not furnish a kitchen. Until a particular hill is killed or confirmed by a number — a value of *r*, a feature in *P(k)* the leftover glow does not have, a leftover particle in a tank — we have a job, not a hire. The unnamed inflaton is not a failure of imagination. It is a failure of detection. Failures of detection are allowed to sit on the table beside the soup. They are not allowed to be called dinner.

Keep the temperatures straight on this chapter too. Hot: the three puzzles are real, and a brief stretch of the sort described would pale them. Warm: slow-roll inflation, 50 to 60 e-folds, a dump into soup, a tilt near 0.96, is the working preface. Cold: we have named the field, bagged it in a tank, and photographed its gravitational waves. We have not. The pot boiled over. We are eating the soup. The name of the cook is still a blank line.

## 14. How a Quantum Twitch Became a Galaxy

If inflation had stretched the universe perfectly smooth, there would be nothing to look at. No galaxies, no stars, no kitchens. Perfection is sterile. The universe is saved by a twitch.

Quantum fields cannot sit still. Even in their quietest state they jitter. During the burst, those jitters — differences in the field from place to place, at the smallest scale the quantum rules allow — get stretched from subatomic to cosmic the way a note written on a balloon becomes unreadable when you inflate it, except here the note becomes a *continent*. A region that jittered a little dense stays a little dense. After reheating, gravity, which has all the time in the world, starts calling in the debt. The dense patches pull. The thin patches get thinner. After a few hundred million years, the dense patches are making the first stars. After a few billion, they are making galaxies that look, cropped and gray, like Figure 14.

The kitchen mistake is to think of a twitch as a metaphor for “something small.” It is not a metaphor. An electron in a wire does not have a definite path. The same family of rule that forbids that path forbids a quantum field from sitting at one exact value in every place. Inflation is not a new kind of uncertainty. It is an amplifier. It takes the jitter the quantum rules already insist on and stretches it until the jitter is the size of a future cluster. Gravity is the sculptor. Gravity does not invent the pattern. Gravity spends 13.8 billion years pulling on a pattern that was already written, faintly, in a field that could not sit still.

This is Mukhanov’s territory, and Guth’s, and a workshop of people in 1982 who realized that inflation does not just solve puzzles. It *makes the seeds*. The leftover glow’s freckles are those seeds, photographed at 380,000 years, before gravity had done much sculpting. The pattern has a sound: almost the same power at every scale, a little redder on large scales, a spectrum written *P(k)* in the appendix and “almost scale-invariant” in conversation. Planck measured the tilt. The tilt matches slow-roll inflation’s favorite song. That is the warm claim at its warmest.

The 1982 workshop — a room of chalk, not a cathedral — is worth a pause because this is the moment the burst stopped being only a bill-payer. Viatcheslav Mukhanov, with G. V. Chibisov a year earlier, and then a crowd that included Hawking, Starobinsky, Guth, Turner, Bardeen, Steinhardt, had the same rude thought: if a field is jittering while it stretches, the jitter *is* a spectrum of density. After the dump into soup, that spectrum is a set of slightly overdense and underdense patches. The leftover glow is the photograph of those patches as temperature. Galaxies are the same patches, later, as matter. One twitch. Two exhibits.

The numbers are small on purpose. The amplitude of the primordial spectrum, *A_s*, is about 2 × 10⁻⁹. Two parts in a billion. That is how loud the twitch was, as a fractional difference in density, when it was stretched beyond the causal horizon and frozen. The tilt is *n_s* ≈ 0.965, Planck’s digit, a hair redder than 1. If *n_s* were exactly 1, every scale would have been given the same fractional power. Slow-roll does not quite do that. The hill is not perfectly flat, so large scales, which exit the horizon earlier, get a little more to work with. The leftover glow’s freckles sing that song. The song is not a galaxy. It is the score gravity will play.

*P(k)* is a recipe for how much power lives at each spatial scale. Scale-invariant means the fractional jitter looks the same if you zoom in or out, like a coastline that is wrinkly at every magnification. Almost scale-invariant means the coastline is a little calmer when you zoom into small bays, a little wilder when you back up to the whole shore. That is the red tilt. *A_s* ~ 2 × 10⁻⁹ sets how wrinkly, overall, the coastline was. Gravity turns wrinkle into continent. The leftover glow is the coastline at 380,000 years, still a coastline, not yet a continent. Figure 14 is a continent.

During the burst, a jitter of a given size is stretched until it is larger than the causal horizon — it *exits*. Outside the horizon it freezes: the patch cannot talk to its neighbors, so the overdensity stays an overdensity. After the burst, the horizon grows again, and the jitter *re-enters*. Gravity can then start sculpting. Different scales exit at different times during the 50 to 60 e-folds, which is why the spectrum is a story about a hill, not a single stamp. Large scales exit earlier. If the hill is slowly changing, large scales get a slightly different amplitude. That slight difference is *n_s* − 1. Planck’s 0.965 is a sentence about the hill’s slope, written in the leftover glow.

Mukhanov’s name is on the variable that lets you quantize the twitch in an expanding background — a combination of field jitter and metric jitter that the appendix will write as if it were a friend. You do not need the friend to understand the claim. You need this: the same quantum rule that smears an electron’s path smears the unnamed field’s value. The burst is a loudspeaker. Gravity is a chisel. The 1982 workshop is the moment a room of people heard the loudspeaker and realized they were also looking at a sculpture garden.

![Figure 14. One nearby spiral, disk filling the frame. A quantum twitch, stretched and pulled for thirteen billion years.](Figures/figs/fig14.png)

A detail that should not be allowed to stay abstract: the twitch is not a metaphor for “something small.” It is the same kind of uncertainty that keeps an electron from having a definite path in a wire. Inflation is the amplifier. Gravity is the sculptor. You are a later exhibit.

The timeline of exhibits, kept at the right temperature:

Hot: the leftover glow has freckles whose statistics we have measured. Their amplitude and tilt are numbers, not adjectives.

Warm: those freckles are the stretched jitter, and gravity grew them. The first stars, in this working picture, light up around 100 to 200 million years after the dump — a range, not a birthday. JWST photographs later exhibits: galaxies already assembled, already chemically rude, already sitting on the web that Chapter 16 will name. Those photographs are not a laboratory of the twitch. They are the museum downstairs from the laboratory.

The first stars are a warm chapter in a hot book. Theory says the first lumps that can cool and collapse do so around 100 to 200 million years after the dump, in minihalos that would look, if you could see them, like failed galaxies: a few hundred thousand or a few million solar masses of extra pull, a handful of massive, short-lived, metal-free stars. We have not photographed those stars as individuals. We have photographed, with JWST, galaxies that are already later exhibits — assembled, chemically polluted, sitting at redshifts that still make the stretch look like a time machine. Those galaxies are not the twitch. They are the twitch after gravity has had a few hundred million years to work and after the first stars have already lived and died and thrown carbon into the kitchen. Do not let a sharp infrared image pose as a laboratory of quantum jitter. It is a museum label that says LATER.

Gravity as sculptor is not a metaphor you can retire. Overdense patches pull. Underdense patches get emptied. On small scales the extra pull of Chapter 17 helps the lumps hold together against the stretch. On large scales the stretch eventually wins, and the web freezes. In between, for a few billion years, the sculptor is busy. A spiral disk is a late, local luxury: gas that cooled, spun, and settled in a halo that the twitch had already chosen. The choice was a nick of size 10⁻⁵ in the leftover glow. The luxury took the rest of cosmic time.

Cold: we have watched a twitch become a spiral in a laboratory. We have not. We have watched the intermediate photograph — the leftover glow at 380,000 years — and done the math that grows a 2 × 10⁻⁹ jitter into a disk like Figure 14. That is allowed to be enough, if we say so.

Chaos belongs here as mechanism. A jitter of one part in a hundred thousand is nothing in a kitchen and everything in a cosmos. Change the jitter’s pattern and you get a different web of galaxies — still a web, different rooms. That is not destiny. It is sensitivity. Chapter 16 will give chaos its own chair. Here, notice that the most important structures you will ever see began as noise.

Do not let the spiral in Figure 14 pose as a time-lapse of a quantum event. It is a nearby galaxy, light a few tens of millions of years old, a late sculpture. The twitch that seeded the line of lumps it grew from is not in the frame. The twitch is in the statistics of the leftover glow, and in the fact that a universe this empty still managed to make a disk this pretty. Gravity had time. Time is the sculptor’s only luxury. The quantum rule supplied the chisel’s first nick.

Between the freckles and the first stars there is a long, dark kitchen.

Last scattering is 380,000 years. The first stars, warm, are 100 to 200 million years. In between, the universe is not empty of stuff. It is empty of light worth a name. Neutral hydrogen, leftover helium, the extra pull gathering in the dark, the stretch still going. Lumps that were 10⁻⁵ too dense in the glow become, slowly, lumps that can cool. Cooling is the unglamorous step. Gravity pulls. Gas that can radiate away its heat can keep falling. Gas that cannot stays puffy and fails. The first stars are the first successful coolers, not the first lumps. JWST’s later exhibits have already inherited metals from coolers that died. That inheritance is why a sharp galaxy at high redshift is not a photograph of the nick. It is a photograph of a kitchen after several meals.

You cannot, in a laboratory, stretch a quantum jitter until it is a spiral and then wait out the sculptor. The amplifier is a burst we cannot turn on. The sculptor’s hours are cosmological. What we can do is measure the intermediate photograph, write *P(k)* with *A_s* ~ 2 × 10⁻⁹ and *n_s* ≈ 0.965, and check that gravity, given that score, grows a web that looks like the surveys of Chapter 16 and a disk that looks like Figure 14. That check is the warm claim at its most responsible. It is not a time-lapse. It is a courtroom with two exhibits and a lot of math between them.

Hot claim: the leftover glow has a pattern of freckles whose statistics we have measured. Warm claim: those freckles are stretched quantum jitters, grown by gravity into galaxies. Cold claim: we have watched a twitch become a spiral in a laboratory. We have not. We have watched the intermediate photograph and done the math. That is allowed to be enough, if we say so.

## 15. Bubbles That May Never Stop

Once you have a field that can burst, you can ask whether the bursting ever takes a holiday.

In many versions of slow-roll, the field reaches the bottom of its hill in some places and keeps rolling, or keeps jittering, in others. The places that keep going keep stretching. The places that stop become hot bangs — pocket universes, bubbles, whatever noun you can stand. Our observable sky would be inside one stopped place. Outside, the bursting might still be hiring.

This is eternal inflation, Linde’s and Vilenkin’s child, Guth’s grown rumor. It is a live hypothesis. It is not a photograph.

The kitchen is a pot that keeps boiling in the next room.

You live in the room where the pot already dumped into soup. The soup is the leftover glow, the leftover helium, the stretch, the galaxies. In the next room — a room whose door is not a door, because the stretch between rooms is not a hallway you can walk — the original pot may still be boiling over. New dumps, new soups, new leftover glows that are not ours. You cannot go there. A bubble is not a destination. It is not a planet with a pad. It is a volume that reheated, or a volume that has not, separated from you by a stretch that does not sell tickets. Chapter 25 will talk about greenhouses on worlds you can, in principle, point at. This chapter is not that chapter. Bubbles are a maybe about the loaf, not a travel brochure.

A destination has a worldline you can aim at. A bubble that nucleated elsewhere, behind a stretch that has been doubling for longer than our soup has existed, does not. Light from that bubble does not arrive. A ship does not arrive. The word “neighbor” in the cold claim at the end of this chapter is a courtesy and a trap. Neighbors, on Earth, share a street. These would share only a mathematics.

In many slow-roll models the quantum jitter in the field, per Hubble time, can be larger than the classical roll down the hill. Where the jitter kicks the field *up* the hill, that patch stretches more and the hill remains. Where it kicks down, that patch dumps into soup and becomes a bubble. The stretching patches make more volume than the dumping patches. Volume wins. The boil-over in the next room does not stop because the next room keeps being remade. That is eternal inflation as a mechanism, not as a mood. Linde’s picture is often this self-reproduction on a hill. Vilenkin’s picture is often bubbles nucleating in a false-vacuum sea. The nouns differ. The next-room pot is the same insult: the bursting may not take a holiday.

If the underlying physics has many ways to stop — many valleys for the field to fall into, many sets of effective constants — then different bubbles can freeze out with different laws. That is the expensive copy machine of Chapter 39, and it is where the anthropic filter of Chapter 41 gets its only real job. Weinberg’s argument that the cosmological constant should not be much larger than what still allows galaxies *needs* an ensemble. Eternal inflation plus a landscape is the ensemble people reach for.

Do not write the filter here. Chapter 41 will have to say, carefully, what an ensemble is allowed to explain and what it is only allowed to excuse. Here it is enough to see why Weinberg’s Λ is lonely without company. A vacuum energy — a hum of energy that empty space carries even with nothing in it — as small as the one we weigh is a joke if it is a unique, first-principles output of a theory. It is less of a joke if many vacua are tried and we can only find ourselves in one that still makes galaxies. That sentence is a pointer, not a proof. The pointer needs two things this chapter can name and cannot certify: a bursting that never quite stops, and a menu of valleys. The bursting is eternal inflation, Linde and Vilenkin, a live maybe. The menu, in its most advertised form, is a landscape inherited from string theory.

Weinberg, in 1987, asked how large a cosmological constant could be before galaxies fail to form. The answer is: not much larger than what we weigh. That is an upper bound from the existence of galaxies, not a calculation of the vacuum from first principles. An upper bound of that kind is idle unless many values are tried. One universe, one Λ, and Weinberg’s bound is a coincidence we live inside. Many bubbles, many Λs, and the bound becomes a filter: we could not have found ourselves in the bubbles that shoved too hard, too soon. Chapter 41 is the filter chapter. This chapter only points at the drawer the filter would need. Do not open the drawer and call it a house. Do not write the filter’s sentences here. Point, and leave the pointing visible.

The landscape, in its most advertised form, comes from string theory. **String theory cannot currently be tested.** A landscape of valleys inherited from an untestable framework is not a measurement. It is a maybe stacked on a maybe. This book will let the stack sit on the table. It will not serve it for dinner.

![Figure 15. Three large circles in a spreading field. A picture of a possibility.](Figures/figs/fig15.png)

Figure 15 is a picture of a possibility. It is not a map. Maps have distances you can travel. These circles do not.

If you find yourself packing a bag for a bubble, put the bag down. Chapter 6 already took away a single now. Chapter 3 already made light late on purpose. A bubble that is not in your past light cone is not late mail. It is elsewhere, in the technical sense, and then some: the stretch between dumped soups can put them out of every future cone you will ever occupy. That is why bubbles are not destinations. Destinations, even cruel ones, sit on a worldline you can still draw. These do not.

There is a further rumor: that the bursting has no beginning, that bubble has always begotten bubble. The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin result says otherwise for a wide class of stretching spacetimes: if the average expansion is positive, the spacetime is past-incomplete. Eternal inflation, even if it has no end, still faces a boundary in the past. The bubbles do not give you a free forever-behind-you. They give you a messier loaf.

Past-incomplete, in the kitchen, means this. Follow a worldline backward through a spacetime that has been stretching, on average, and you run out of geodesic — the straightest path the geometry allows, the trail a free stone or a free clock actually follows — before you run out of curiosity. You do not get a quiet eternity of boiling behind you. You get a boundary, a place where the description stops and a different description — quantum gravity, a bounce, a sentence we do not own — would have to take over. BGV is not a proof that the loaf had a kitchen-clock beginning. It is a proof that *this* kind of forever-behind-you is not for free. The next-room pot does not pay the first-word bill. Chapter 44 will sit with that bill. Here: eternal inflation is not a machine for manufacturing a past without an edge.

Measure problem, in one sentence: if there are infinitely many bubbles and infinitely many observers, the word “typical” breaks. You cannot say “most universes are like this” until you say how you count. No one has a count that everyone else has to accept. Until they do, “the multiverse predicts X” is a hope in a lab coat.

Volume-weighted counting rewards the patches that stretched longest. Observer-weighted counting tries to ask where a typical observer sits. Pocket-weighted counting treats each dumped soup as one. They disagree. They disagree about whether we are typical, whether the cosmological constant we weigh is typical, whether anything is typical. An ensemble without a measure is a drawer of photographs with no rule for which one is “the” photograph. Chapter 41 will need a rule if it wants the filter to be more than a mood. This chapter’s job is to admit that the drawer is a rumor, and that the rumor is not a destination.

Infinity is the rude guest. If the next-room pot never stops, there are infinitely many dumped soups. Infinitely many observers. Infinitely many values of anything you care to count. A ratio of infinities is not a prediction until you say which infinity sits in the denominator. Different measures are different denominators. They are not a minor bookkeeping quarrel. They change what the rumor is allowed to claim. Until one of them is forced on us by a principle we already trust, “the multiverse predicts X” should be read as “some ways of counting would like X.” That is a thinner sentence. It is the honest one.

Past-incompleteness and the measure problem are not the same bill. BGV is about the loaf having an edge behind you. The measure is about not knowing how to average the rooms in front of you, or beside you, if the boiling never stops. You can have an edge in the past and an uncountable froth in the future. That combination is allowed. It is not a comfort. It is a messier loaf, as the earlier paragraph said, and messier is not the same as mapped.

Warm claim: inflation *can* be eternal in many models. Cold claim: it is, and we live in a bubble with neighbors we will never meet. The sky we have does not require the neighbors. It permits them. Permission is not a postcard.

Keep the next-room pot on a back burner, not on the dining table. The local burst of Chapter 13 is the working preface to the soup we can taste. The pot that may still be boiling elsewhere is a maybe that the local burst can tell if you let it drink. Letting it drink is allowed. Paying rent with the drink is not.

The next-room pot is also a humility about nouns.

People hear “bubble” and picture a place, a film of soap, a destination with an inside you could visit if you had a better ship. The picture is a liar in three ways. First, the wall of a nucleated bubble, in the versions that have walls, is not a surface you approach. It is a stretch you cannot catch. Second, other dumped soups need not share our leftover glow, our helium fraction, our shove. Different valleys, different effective constants: that is the landscape rumor, and the rumor is untestable while it is borrowed from string theory. Third, even a bubble that dumped with laws like ours is not “next door.” Next door is a kitchen word. These rooms do not share a hall.

Guth’s grown rumor, Linde’s child, Vilenkin’s child: the bursting as a process that, once started, makes more of itself. That is the live hypothesis. It may be false. Slow-roll can be arranged, in some hills, so that the classical roll always beats the jitter and the pot dumps everywhere at once. Those hills pay Chapter 13’s bills without hiring Chapter 15. The sky we have does not yet force the hiring. It permits it. Permission, again, is not a postcard. Figure 15 remains a picture of a possibility. Frame it as a possibility and the chapter has done its job. Frame it as a map of destinations and the chapter has failed.

## 16. Chaos Is an Engine

Chaos, in physics, is not a mood and not a mess. It is a precise kind of sensitivity: two starts that differ by a breath end in rooms that do not recognize each other. Weather is chaotic. A pendulum swinging over a magnet can be chaotic. The approach to a final crunch, if the universe had been headed for one, would have been chaotic — Barrow proved things in that vicinity, and Belinski, Khalatnikov, and Lifshitz gave the mixmaster a name. A recollapsing cosmos would not politely reverse the film. It would scramble.

The kitchen is a dropped tray of yeast.

Same flour. Same water. Same yeast. The tray hits the floor and the grains go everywhere. Two bakers, starting from two almost identical spills, do not bake the same loaf. One kitchen gets a dense crumb. The other gets holes like a sponge. The ingredients did not change. The sensitivity did the work. Lyapunov, if you want the surname on the sensitivity, is the person who taught mathematics to measure how fast nearby starts stop being nearby. A Lyapunov time is a clock for forgetting. Weather forgets in days. A mixmaster crunch would forget in a scramble of axes. A quantum twitch, stretched and then pulled by gravity for 13.8 billion years, forgets the subatomic room and remembers only a web.

Take two trays. Spill them as identically as a human can. Watch. In a Lyapunov system the difference that was a grain of yeast becomes, after enough doublings of error, a different city of holes in the crumb. The number that measures the doubling is a Lyapunov exponent; its inverse is a time. Weather’s time is short, which is why the kitchen radio stops pretending on day ten. The stretch’s time, for the average expansion, is long, which is why Friedmann still works as a forecast. The mixmaster’s time, near a crunch, is short in the only way that matters: too short to steer. Nearby starts, unrecognizable rooms: that is the whole definition, with a surname attached so nobody can pretend chaos meant “messy feelings.”

At the beginning, chaos is an engine.

The quantum twitch of Chapter 14 is sensitivity as a gift. Stretch it, and you get a web. The cosmic web — filaments of galaxies around voids like holes in a sponge — is what gravity does with a nearly scale-invariant jitter after thirteen billion years. It is not a design. It is what you get when pull is allowed to run on noise.

Same engine, two temperatures. At the start, sensitivity is how a nick of size 10⁻⁵ becomes a sponge you can survey. At a hypothetical crunch, sensitivity is how a last decimal becomes a mixer nobody holds. The mathematics is cousin to itself. The moral is not. One gift. One theft. Do not let a poster collapse them into “chaos means we cannot know anything.” We can know the leftover glow. We can know the web’s family. We cannot know, from a grain of yeast, which hole in which loaf. That is the precise kind of not-knowing. It is enough.

The web is not a drawing. It is a survey. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the Two-degree-Field survey mapped enough galaxies that the sponge became a fact you could crop and print. Figure 16 is that crop: filaments as dark on light, voids as the light, a later exhibit of the same jitter that freckled the leftover glow. Two nearby starts in the primordial noise — two almost identical spills of yeast — would not grow the same rooms. They would still grow a web. Still a sponge. Different holes. That is chaos as an engine: the family of outcomes is stable, the particular kitchen is not.

A survey is a census, not an anecdote. 2dF, from Australia, and SDSS, from a telescope in New Mexico, redshifted enough galaxies that the sponge stopped being a theorist’s sketch. Filaments, walls, voids: the same family of pattern the leftover glow’s freckles asked for, grown. You can still argue about the second digit of how much extra pull sits in the filaments. You cannot argue that the web is a mood. Figure 16 is a measurement, cropped so a page can hold it. The yeast on the floor is the same measurement, earlier, before gravity baked. Same ingredients. Different loaves, if you spilled twice. One loaf, because we spilled once.

![Figure 16. A cropped survey map of galaxies, thresholded so the filaments read as dark on light. A web grown from twitches.](Figures/figs/fig16.png)

Barrow’s habit, useful here, was to walk you up to a large number and then refuse to call the number a destiny. The web is a large fact. It is not a plan. Mixmaster is a large fact about a kind of ending we do not appear to be headed for. It is not a control panel.

The mixmaster, named for a kitchen mixer because the axes of a collapsing universe would stretch and squash in a chaotic rotation, is Belinski, Khalatnikov, and Lifshitz: BKL. Near a spacelike singularity — a place, Chapter 24's proper subject, where the loaf tears and the equations stop describing what happens next — the generic behavior in general relativity is not a polite, spherical crunch. It is an oscillation of shapes, sensitive to the last decimal, a Lyapunov nightmare. Barrow proved things in that vicinity that matter for anyone who wants a last moment to be a steering wheel. Nearby starts become unrecognizable rooms. A civilization of perfect computers would not find a handle. The geometry would melt the handle.

Picture a collapsing room whose walls do not approach you together. One axis crunches while another stretches, then they trade, then they trade again, the sequence sensitive to the last decimal of the starting shape. That is Mixmaster, Bianchi IX in the jargon, a kitchen mixer beating a dough that is the geometry itself. BKL argued this is generic: not a special, spherical collapse, but the thing Einstein’s theory wants to do when a spacelike singularity is approached. Barrow took that generic chaos seriously enough to ask what a final intelligent observer could still control. The answer, in the vicinity of those proofs, is: almost nothing, unless you add extra structure the known laws do not hand you. Ideas for ideas can add the structure. Data have not.

At the end, if there were a crunch, the same family of sensitivity would be a theft. Tiny differences near a final singularity would be amplified until no one, not even a civilization of perfect computers, could steer. That fact will matter when someone tries to sell you a required Omega Point: a last moment in which mind takes the controls and computes forever. Chaos at the crunch is not a control panel. It is a warning that control panels melt.

Chapter 19 will finish that sale’s refusal on other grounds: the shove is winning, the bombs of 1998 are not a crunch track. Even in a universe that *did* crunch, BKL plus Barrow would still be waiting at the far end with a scrambled mixer. Forever-mind fails twice. Once because the data do not crunch. Once because a crunch, if you had one, would not be a computer. Do not let the Omega Point borrow this chapter as a mood. It is a mechanism. The mechanism is unkind to steering.

In the middle — which is where we live — chaos is why forecasts have horizons. We can reconstruct the three minutes because the soup was mixed and the laws were simple. We cannot forecast a galaxy’s weather in year 10¹². We can forecast the stretch. The stretch, with dark energy in the budget, looks dull and huge. Dull and huge is a kind of mercy. The web freezes in place as the shove between filaments wins. Galaxies go dark on their own clocks. The engine that built them idles.

Weather versus stretch is the distinction worth taking home. Weather is Lyapunov’s child: a breath here, a different storm next week. The stretch is not that kind of forecast. The Friedmann equations, fed a budget of pull and shove, do not forget their nearby starts on a weather clock. They tell you the dough will keep rising, and, with the shove in the account, rising faster. You can be wrong about the second digit of the Hubble number and still be right that the voids will get ruder. You cannot be a little wrong about a hurricane’s seed and still be right about next Tuesday. Do not confuse the two clocks. One is why you pack an umbrella. The other is why the cosmic web is a fossil in progress.

A kitchen radio that promised you Tuesday’s rain from Monday’s steam would be lying by Wednesday if the Lyapunov time had already run out. A cosmology that promised you the leftover glow from the three minutes is not lying: the soup was mixed, the laws were simple, the clock was short and hot. A cosmology that promised you the weather inside a particular spiral in year 10¹² would be the radio. The stretch forecast is closer to the soup than to the rain. Dull and huge, as the earlier paragraph said. Mercy, if you like mercy. A fossil, if you like fossils. The web we map with SDSS and 2dF is the baking caught mid-loaf. The shove will finish the bake by stretching the tins apart until no new filaments form. That end of the engine is Chapter 18’s personality change. This chapter’s personality is the engine itself.

Barrow would have called the Omega Point an idea for ideas and then, if he were in a kind mood, walked you to the mixmaster and let the mixer do the unkindness. Chapter 19 will do the data. Here the mixer is enough: crunch chaos is a second lock on that door. Nearby starts, unrecognizable rooms. No steering wheel survives the last decimals.

Keep chaos in the book as a tool, not a vibe. It made the rooms. It will not let anyone own the ending. It is the opposite of a watchmaker and the opposite of a destiny. It is what a twitch can do when the universe gives it time.

The dropped tray is still on the floor. Same ingredients. Different loaves. You live in one of them. The leftover glow is the photograph of the spill before the baking. The survey map is the photograph after. In between, gravity did what gravity does with a nick of size 10⁻⁵ and a lot of idle hours. That is an engine. It does not owe you a particular loaf. It owes you the family: webs, not destiny; sensitivity, not mood; a mixer at the far end that nobody steers.

If you want a last kitchen instruction: pick up the tray. Notice that picking it up does not unspill the yeast. The web is already baked into the surveys. The leftover glow is already on the antenna. Chaos, as an engine, has already done the morning’s work. What it will not do, tonight or in a crunch, is hand you a recipe that steers the last decimal. Barrow’s pebble, Guth’s unpaid bills, Mukhanov’s twitch, Linde’s next-room pot, and this dropped tray are one family of honesties. The universe is sensitive. It is not a mood. It is how rooms get made, and why nobody owns the ending.
