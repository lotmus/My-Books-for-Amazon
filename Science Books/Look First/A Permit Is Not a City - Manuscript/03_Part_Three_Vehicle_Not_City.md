# PART III — Vehicle, Not City

---

## 10. Starship as a Vehicle, Not a City

A ship that is large and, one day, cheap per tonne is a *vehicle*. If Starship-class flight becomes routine — cadence, refuel, a hull that does not make the news every time it lights — the mass you can throw changes. That is warm. Routine is the unpaid invoice.

A vehicle does not print air. A vehicle does not print a law. A vehicle does not print a childhood that can survive the week the cargo does not arrive.

![Figure 10. A large hull on a pad, daylight, no skyline. The label: truck. Routine is an empty box beside it.](fig10.png)

The eye wants to fuse. A hull the size of a building looks like a building. A building looks like a block. A block looks like a town. The fusion is how a founder’s year becomes a downtown in a single still. Refuse it. Size is a mass budget. A mass budget is a wonderful thing if it is real. It is not a biosphere.

What “cheap per tonne” would actually change, if the unpaid invoice is paid:

You could throw tanks, not thimbles. You could throw a tin that is a room, not a closet. You could throw spare pumps, spare suits, a pile of dirt-moving machines, a nuclear kettle, a greenhouse kit that is not a metaphor. You could, in a lucky decade, throw the first cargo of a camp that is trying to become the middle noun. That is a real increment. It is why this chapter exists instead of a sneer. A species that can throw mass like a drunk trucking company is a different species of project than a species that throws a few tonnes a year and calls each one a jewel.

What it would not change:

The six millibars of excuse on Mars. The last kilometer. The twenty-minute argument. The twenty-six-month sulk if you miss the window. The ice that must be mined, cracked, stored, and not lost to a leak. The dust. The dosimeter. The pH. The fact that a baby is a life-support system with opinions. The fact that a town is a web of invoices — food, law, medicine, boredom, spare parts — that a hull cannot print by being large.

---

Routine is the word that has to sit still.

where routine means: lights, flies, lands or is caught, is filled again, does this on a calendar that a cargo planner can bet a camp on, and does not make the news every time.

One loud flight is a dare. A dozen loud flights are a test campaign. A hundred quiet flights are the beginning of a truck. We have, in this kitchen, a vehicle class that has lit, flown, broken, taught, and sometimes come back in ways that make a clip. We do not have, as of this writing, the quiet hundred. By the time you read this, the count may be kinder. The temperature of *routine* should still be earned, not declared. Declaring routine is how a slide cashes an invoice it has not paid.

Refueling is the other unpaid line. A heavy lander or a Mars stack that needs to be filled in orbit is a stack that needs a tanker company, not a tanker occasion. Transferring cryogenic propellant in zero-g without making a fountain or a bomb is a sport. The sport has papers and tests. A camp that depends on the sport cannot print a month that the sport has not yet survived.

Reliability is the third line. A trucking company that loses one hull in twenty is a headline. A trucking company that loses one in twenty when the hull is also a house is a funeral program. The number that would make a planner sleep is ruder than the number that makes a clip.

---

Dex Ortega writes cadence boards. He is invented. His board has a column for *hoped* and a column for *flown* and a column for *still on the news*. He is tired of people pointing at the hull and saying *city*. He likes the hull. He likes it the way a person likes a truck that might, if it learns manners, move a house. He will not live in the truck and call the parking lot a town.

Uncrewed Starship on Mars *this decade* because a founder said the year: **cold** as a schedule. The vehicle is real and unfinished. Mars EDL, life support, and ISRU are not posters you can cash. A lucky window might take a loud hull on a long fall and try to hit a thin air. That sentence is allowed as a dare. It is not a town. It is not even an outpost. It is a vehicle, thrown, if it is thrown.

Mara hears the clip on the delay — a catch, a light, a cheer in a room she will never sit in. She is glad for Dex, whom she has not met. She logs the pH. The hull did not print the water. The ice did, yesterday, because she and Rohan bullied it.

What a planner actually bets on.

A cargo planner for an outpost does not bet on a clip. She bets on a rate: tonnes per window, windows per decade, losses per hundred lights, days to turn a hull around, whether the tanker company is a company or a dare. If any of those numbers is a wish, the outpost’s kitchen is a wish. ISS planners bet on a cargo cult that has already flown for a generation. They still lose sleep. A lunar or Martian planner who is asked to bet on a hull that still makes the news is being asked to staff a religion.

Mass is not only up-mass. It is down-mass if you want samples, and it is *left-mass* if you want a tin to stay. Left-mass is the unphotogenic increment a cheap stack would actually buy: a spare, a pile of dirt, a second pump, a kettle. Left-mass is how an outpost stops being a jewelry box. It is still not how a settlement starts. Settlement is closed loops, not a fatter umbilical. A fatter umbilical is a better outpost. Say so. Do not say city.

Dex’s board has started a column called *quiet*. Quiet means the flight was not a headline. He has very few marks in it. He wants more. He will not trade the marks for a skyline. The skyline can wait until the column is boring.

Appendix A10 writes mass, cadence, and why “cheap per tonne” is a conditional with three unpaid clauses. Here, keep the truck and the empty box. The box is labeled *routine*. It is still empty until a planner can bet a camp on it.

## 11. Mars Rhetoric vs Mars Engineering

Mars is where the posters go to breed.

Rhetoric is a decade, a founder, a child in a dome, a species that has become a two-world insurance policy. Engineering is a thin air, a fast arrival, a kitchen you have not built, a radio that is not a conversation, a window that is a fact about two orbits.

![Figure 11. Four large nouns: window, EDL, ISRU, delay. No skyline. The thin air is a bar at the bottom.](fig11.png)

**Windows.** A cheap path from Earth to Mars is a Hohmann-class fall: you leave when the planets are about to be kind, you coast most of a season, you arrive.

where a Hohmann-class transfer is on the order of six to nine months, and the kind alignment — the synodic clock — repeats about every twenty-six months.

Miss the window and you wait on the clock, not on a mood. There is no “we will leave on Tuesday because the slide is lonely.” There is a season you can buy, then a long sulk. A camp that needs a resupply and misses a window is a camp that has just learned what an umbilical is. Twenty-six months is not a weather delay. It is a second calendar you do not get to vote off.

**EDL.** Entry, descent, landing. You arrive at a world with an atmosphere that is a rumor by Earth’s standards — about six millibars at the surface, a percent of a percent of what your lungs grew up in — and you arrive fast, because interplanetary falls are fast. You must spend that speed on a heat shield, a parachute that has almost nothing to bite, engines that must finish the job, a crane, a belly flop, a trick that has not yet been demonstrated at the mass of a house. Rovers have done a version of this at the mass of a car, with a decade of nerves and a sky crane that still looks like a dare. A hull the size of a building is not a car. The thin air does not become thicker because the hull is famous.

where six millibars is the order of the surface pressure — a kitchen you cannot breathe, a parachute you can barely hire, a sky that is a lie the color of butterscotch.

**ISRU.** In-situ resource utilization: melt the ice, crack the carbon dioxide, print a spare, make the oxygen you will need to leave, make the methane if your engines drink that pair. MOXIE-class boxes have already, on a rover, made a breath of oxygen from the air that is mostly CO₂. That is hot as a *trick in miniature*. It is not a tank farm. A tank farm is power, mass, a mine, a leak policy, a winter, a spare, a person who logs the numbers when the box sulks. Papers and prototypes exist. A city does not.

**Delay.** Light-time one way is about four minutes at a kind opposition and about twenty at the rude ends.

where the round trip is eight to forty-some minutes, and a conversation is a word you should retire.

You send. You wait. You receive a reply to a problem that has already become a different problem. Surgery, a software panic, a seal, a fight: you own them. Earth owns an archive of advice that arrives late. Mara already lives in this sentence. The posters do not.

---

Crewed Mars *landing* in the 2030s: warm-to-cold. Allowed by hopium and by a lucky engineering decade. Not selected by present demonstrated pieces. The pieces we have photographed working are: rovers, a breath of oxygen, orbits, landings at car-mass, a vehicle class that is not yet routine, a species that has not yet walked on the Moon in this century. A lucky decade could stack those into a first foot. A first foot is a sortie. It is not a town.

Uncrewed hardware *might* be thrown in a lucky window this decade. Cold as a schedule if the reason is a founder’s year. Allowed as a dare if the vehicle earns the window and the EDL at that mass is not a wish. Still not a town.

A city this decade: cold. Children without a cargo cult: cold. “Settlement” as a product you can buy by Friday: cold.

---

The rhetoric has a few favorite lies that are not quite lies.

*Backup world.* A backup is a copy that can run the job if the first copy dies. Mars, in the near term, cannot run the job. It cannot feed the species. It cannot take the leftover eight billion. It cannot even take a town without Earth in the inbox. Calling it a backup is how you stop washing the kitchen. Chapter 15 is the funeral of that sentence.

*If we can go, we should, because the species must not keep its eggs in one basket.* Eggs and baskets are a metaphor that photographs well. A basket that cannot hold the eggs is not a basket. A few thousand worldlines on a dead world are a field trip. They are not an insurance policy for a species.

*The engineering is just details.* Details is what EDL is. Details is what a six-millibar parachute is. Details is what a twenty-six-month miss is. Rhetoric calls them details when it wants a year. Engineering calls them the job.

---

Pavel Grun writes EDL sims. He is invented. His sims have killed more crews than a war, on paper, and saved none, on dirt, because the dirt has not yet been asked at this mass. He is not a pessimist. He is a person who has seen the thin air win. He would like the thin air to lose, once, in public, at a mass that matters. He will not call the loss a city.

Mara already knows the delay. She knows the window because her ride home is a window. She logs the pH while a voice on Earth, twenty minutes ago, says a year that is a landing gear. She does not vote. The dirt does not vote. The orbits do not vote. The orbits are the only adults in the room.

Dust on Mars is not lunar glass and is still a personality. It is finer, magnetic in its moods, a thing that coats solar panels and lungs and the last kilometer of a landing that wanted to be tidy. A season-long dust event can turn a power budget into a dare. Opportunity — a rover, a car-mass, a saint of a machine — met a sky that would not clear. A house-mass camp meets the same sky with more to lose.

Power is the quiet long pole the posters draw as a rectangle of gold foil. Gold foil is a rover trick. A tin that wants to melt ice, crack CO₂, run a greenhouse lie, and keep a person from freezing through a dust week wants a nuclear kettle or a farm of panels with a spare and a broom. The kettle has a politics. The farm has a dust. Neither is a skyline.

Pavel will not put a kettle on a slide that also has a child. He will put the kettle on a slide that has a dosimeter and a mass. That is the engineering. The rhetoric will sneak the child back in the hall.

Appendix A11 writes the synodic clock, the Hohmann band, the six millibars, MOXIE-class oxygen, and light-time as a table that is allowed to sit still. Here, keep the four nouns. Window. EDL. ISRU. Delay. No skyline.

## 12. Three Nouns: Sortie, Outpost, Settlement

Three nouns. Keep them unfused.

**Sortie** — you go, you work, you come home, or you die on a schedule Earth still owns.

**Outpost** — you stay a season with an umbilical.

**Settlement** — the umbilical can fail and the babies still eat.

![Figure 12. Three large words in a column, unfused. Sortie. Outpost. Settlement. A line through any pair that tries to marry.](fig12.png)

This is the meeting-room chapter. The rest of the book has been refusing the marriage in pieces. Here the refusal gets a table and a test.

A sortie is Apollo. A sortie is an Artemis-II-class loop. A sortie is a landing attempt that walks and leaves. A sortie can be heroic, expensive, world-changing as a photograph, and still be a week or a month. The schedule Earth owns is the point. If Earth goes silent, the sortie is already a problem; if Earth stays loud, the sortie was never a civilization.

An outpost is a winter-over, a Gateway season, an ISS-class tin, a polar camp that eats cargo and returns science and rotates crews. The umbilical is the point. Cargo arrives, or the outpost becomes a dare. People can live months. People have lived months in Antarctica, on a station over Earth, in a tin that smelled like a locker. Months are not children. Months are not a farm that closes the loop. Months are the middle noun, and the middle noun is already a magnificent achievement if it is real. It is still not the third.

A settlement is the noun the posters steal. The test is rude and clean. If the ships stop — a war, a budget, a plague, a missed window that becomes a missed decade — do the babies still eat? Not the adults on rations. The babies. A settlement has a closed-enough kitchen: air, water, calories, spare parts, medicine, law, a culture that can teach a child without a download from a dying Earth. No such kitchen exists on the Moon or Mars. No such kitchen is scheduled as a product this decade. Cold.

ISRU is the kitchen word for the middle noun trying to become the third: melt the ice, crack the CO₂, print a spare. Papers and prototypes exist. A city does not.

---

How the fusion happens in a room.

Someone shows a sortie photograph — a flag, a face, a loop — and says *we are a spacefaring civilization*. Civilization is a third-noun word. A photograph is a first-noun object.

Someone shows an outpost drawing — a can, a solar farm, a rover — and says *the city begins here*. Begins is a slide word. An outpost begins an outpost.

Someone shows a hull and says *a thousand people*. A thousand people is a cruise ship if the umbilical is fat, and a famine if it is not. Headcount is not the test. The test is the babies and the cut cable.

Someone says *of course there will be cargo at first*. At first is how the third noun is always promised and never billed. A settlement can have trade. Trade is not an umbilical that, if cut, ends the children. Earth’s cities trade. Earth’s cities also grow food in a biosphere that was not shipped last Tuesday. That biosphere is the unpaid character in every Mars downtown.

---

The cargo-cult test, written so it can sit still.

where a cargo cult, in this book, is a camp whose kitchen is a religion of ships — the ships must come, the calendar of ships is the calendar of life, and the children eat the ships.

Antarctica is an outpost. It has a cargo cult and is honest about it. No one calls McMurdo a spare Earth.

ISS is an outpost. Cut the Progress, the Dragon, the Cygnus, and you have months of rations and then a problem.

A lunar polar camp in the 2030s–2040s, if it exists, will be an outpost. Warm-to-cold as a schedule. Honest if it says so.

A Mars town that cannot close air, water, and calories is an outpost or a sortie with a longer sulk. Calling it a settlement is inventory fraud.

---

Elena Voss, from Chapter 2, puts the three nouns on the wall of the review and will not let the slide use two in one sentence. She is invented. The wall is a kindness. People who fuse the nouns are not always liars. They are often in a hurry. A hurry is how a flyby becomes a school district. The wall is the school.

Mara is the honest picture of how soon: not never, not downtown, a stuck drawer on a dead world, waiting on a window. She is an outpost of one tin, or two, with an umbilical that is a radio and a ship that has not yet left. She hits the airlock. The basil does not care which logo paid for the LED. She logs the pH. She does not vote for the slide. The universe still does not have an opinion. The dirt does.

A meeting, written so you can steal it.

Elena puts three cards on the table. SORTIE. OUTPOST. SETTLEMENT. She asks the room to point at the hardware on the slide and pick one card. If two fingers twitch, she waits. If someone says “it’s a journey,” she asks them to put the journey down and pick a card. Journeys are how nouns get married in the dark.

The room hates this for twenty minutes and then starts to tell the truth. The loop is a sortie. The polar tin with a cargo calendar is an outpost. The dome with the child is a wish. The hull is a vehicle that might feed the middle noun if routine is paid. Nobody is fired. The slide gets a rewrite. The rewrite is the book’s product: not a cancelled Moon, a refused marriage.

If you run a review and you cannot steal that meeting, you are still in the poster business.
