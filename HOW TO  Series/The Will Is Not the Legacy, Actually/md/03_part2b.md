## Chapter 4. Titles, Deeds, and Joint Names

### Case 6: The transfer-on-death deed and the other two children

**The situation.** The father was a retired machinist in Riverside, a widower for six years, with a paid-off three-bedroom house, a modest savings account, and three adult children. The eldest son lived fifteen minutes away and came by most weekends to fix things and eat the father's chili. The daughter lived in Seattle and called every Sunday. The younger son lived in Phoenix, had three children of his own, and visited twice a year.

The father's will, signed with a lawyer when his wife was still alive and updated after her death, left everything in equal shares to the three children. It was a good will. It was also, as it turned out, mostly irrelevant.

At a barbecue, a neighbor told the father about a document that would let his house skip probate entirely. "You just sign a deed naming who gets it, record it, and when you go, it passes automatically. No court, no lawyers, no fees." The father liked the sound of no lawyers. He found the statutory form, filled it in, signed it in front of two neighbors as witnesses and a notary at the shipping store, and recorded it with the county a week later. He named the eldest son as beneficiary, because the eldest son was nearby and practical and would "take care of things." The father told the eldest son he trusted him to share the house with his brother and sister. He did not tell the daughter or the younger son about the deed at all, because it seemed like a technicality.

The father died four years later. The house was worth about 620,000 dollars. The savings account held about 30,000.

**What the family assumed.** The daughter and the younger son assumed the will divided everything equally, because they had seen it and it did. The eldest son assumed he would sell the house and share the money, because that was what his father had asked. The father had assumed that a deed and a will were two ways of doing the same thing.

**What actually happened, and why.** California allows a revocable transfer-on-death deed (Probate Code §§ 5600 and following). It is a useful tool. While you are alive, it changes nothing: you own the house, you can sell it, mortgage it, or revoke the deed. At your death, the house passes to the beneficiary named on the deed, outside probate. Crucially, it passes under the deed, not under the will. The will divides only what the will controls.

So the house became the eldest son's. Not "the eldest son's to share," just the eldest son's. The will divided the savings account, 10,000 dollars each, minus expenses.

The eldest son did intend to share. He said so at the funeral. Then, about four months later, his wife filed for divorce. The house he had inherited was legally his separate property, because in California an inheritance received by one spouse is generally that spouse's separate property. But the divorce raised questions anyway. The eldest son and his wife had spent weekends and money repairing his father's house in the last years. She wondered whether that gave her a claim. His lawyer advised him not to sign, transfer, or promise anything until the divorce was settled. "I was going to give my sister a third" is a moral position. In a divorce negotiation it is not a legal category.

The divorce took two years. By the time it was settled, the house had been sold to pay the eldest son's legal fees and the settlement, and what was left was not enough to give his siblings a third each. He gave them what he could. The daughter accepted it graciously. The younger son did not. The three of them are polite at weddings.

**What would have prevented it.** The father's goal had two parts: avoid probate on the house, and treat his children equally. One document could have done both.

- **A TOD deed naming all three children** in equal shares. California's form allows multiple beneficiaries. The house would have passed to the three of them as co-owners, outside probate, matching the will.
- **A revocable living trust** holding the house, with the three children as equal beneficiaries and one of them (the eldest son, if the father wished) as successor trustee to manage the sale. The trustee has a legal duty to the beneficiaries, so "take care of things" becomes an obligation instead of a hope.

Either option costs little more than what the father did. The difference is that both make the deed and the will say the same thing.

A note on the statute itself: the California law that authorizes these deeds is currently set to be repealed on 1 January 2032 unless the legislature extends it. Deeds signed before that date remain valid. If you rely on one, check its status with your lawyer every few years.

**What the form itself warns you about.** California prints the TOD deed form in the statute (Probate Code § 5642), and the form is unusually frank. It must be signed before a notary, and, for deeds signed since 2022, also in front of two witnesses who are present at the same time. It must be recorded with the county within 60 days of being notarized, or it has no effect. Beneficiaries must be named by full name; "my children" is not allowed. If a beneficiary dies before you, their share goes to the surviving beneficiaries, not to the dead beneficiary's own children (§ 5652). And if you own the house as a joint tenant or as community property with right of survivorship and die first, the deed is void: the house goes to the co-owner. The form tells you, in capitals, to provide only the information it asks for and to add no instructions of your own. The father's deed did everything the form required. It simply said something different from his will.

**The German version.** Germany has no transfer-on-death deed. Ownership of land passes at death only through inheritance, by will or by statute, and a lifetime transfer of land requires a notarized contract (BGB § 311b) and an entry in the land register. That makes the father's particular mistake impossible in Germany, but its cousin is common: a parent transfers the house to one child during life, by notarized gift, "so the others don't have to bother," and assumes the child will share. The other children may later have claims against the estate or, in some cases, against the child under the compulsory share rules, which add back gifts made within the last ten years (§ 2325). The moral is the same. If you want equality, put equality in the document.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 6).

**What to do.**

- Before you sign a transfer-on-death deed, lay it beside your will and check that the two name the same people in the same shares.
- Remember that a TOD deed names no alternates: if a beneficiary dies before you, their share goes to the others, not to their children. If that matters, use a trust instead.
- If you want one child to manage the sale, name all the children as beneficiaries, or use a trust with that child as trustee, rather than relying on a promise to share.
- If you rely on a TOD deed, check its legal status every few years: the current California law is due to expire in 2032 unless extended.

### Case 7: The son added to the title

The mother was a retired nurse in Los Angeles, seventy-four, living in the two-bedroom condominium she had bought after her divorce. Her only son was forty-five, recently remarried, and running a small landscaping business with more ambition than capital. She wanted him to have the condominium one day without probate, which she had heard was slow and expensive.

The idea came from the title company, while she was refinancing. A friendly employee, who was not a lawyer and said so, mentioned that many people simply add their child to the title as a joint tenant. "When you pass, it goes to him automatically. Simple." She signed a new deed adding her son as a joint tenant with right of survivorship and thought of his name on it as a sort of forwarding address for after her death. So, if he thought about it at all, did he.

Joint tenancy is not a forwarding address. It is co-ownership, and it begins on the day the deed is recorded. The mother had made her son the present owner of an undivided half of her home. "Right of survivorship" means that when one joint tenant dies, the survivor owns the whole. It also means that while both are alive, both own it. Over the next four years, every consequence of that arrived in her lifetime, one at a time.

The first was a lawsuit. Three years after the deed, one of the son's employees, driving a company truck, caused a serious accident. The injured driver sued the son and his business, and the business's insurance was not enough. The son's half of the condominium was his property, and the injured driver's lawyers noticed it. When they won a judgment, they recorded it, which created a lien against his interest. It did not force a sale while the mother lived there, but it sat on the title like a stone in a shoe.

The second was a signature. A year later the mother's health declined, and she wanted to sell and move into an assisted living community. To sell, she needed her son's signature, because he was an owner. Under pressure from the lawsuit and a difficult patch in his new marriage, he did not answer his phone for three weeks. When he finally signed, the lien had to be cleared at closing, and part of the price of her home went to her son's creditor instead of to her care.

The third was the tax, and it came last because it was invisible until the accountant did the arithmetic. In the United States, property inherited at death usually receives a new tax basis equal to its value at the date of death, so an heir who sells soon afterwards owes little or no capital gains tax. Property given away during life generally keeps the giver's original basis for the part given. By adding her son, the mother had given him half the condominium with her old purchase price attached. Had he inherited the whole at her death, he would have received a basis close to its value for all of it. The accountant put the difference in tax on the eventual sale at several tens of thousands of dollars, and there may also have been a gift tax return to file for the year she added him, because the gift exceeded the annual exclusion.

The mother's goal had been modest: her son inherits, no probate. Two tools do exactly that without giving him anything while she is alive. A revocable transfer-on-death deed (Chapter 13) leaves her full ownership and control; her son has no interest until her death, his creditors cannot reach what he does not own, and he receives the property with a new basis. A revocable living trust (Chapter 19) does the same and adds a successor trustee who can step in if she becomes incapacitated. And for the practical problem of selling later, a durable power of attorney (Chapter 20) would have let a trusted agent sign for her.

In Germany, putting a child on the land register as co-owner of the family home is a gift of a share of the land; it must be notarized (BGB § 311b) and registered. It can be a sound plan, because each parent can give each child up to 400,000 euros every ten years free of tax (ErbStG §§ 14 and 16), and using the allowance early can save tax at death. But the same consequences follow: the child is an owner now, and the parent can no longer sell or mortgage alone. A German notary will usually suggest protective clauses: a lifelong right for the parent to live in the house (*Wohnrecht*) or keep its income (*Nießbrauch*), and a right to take the share back if the child dies first, becomes insolvent, or tries to sell. Those clauses are the difference between a planned gift and an accident.

!> **The takeaway.** Joint tenancy is not a probate shortcut that happens to involve ownership. It is ownership that happens to avoid probate, and everything that comes with owning something comes with it: the co-owner's creditors, their divorce, their signature, and the giver's old tax basis.

**What to do.**

- Before you add anyone to the title of your home, ask your tax adviser to compare the basis your heir would get by gift with the basis they would get by inheritance.
- Ask whether a transfer-on-death deed or a revocable trust would do the same job without giving away ownership now.
- If your child runs a business or has debts, assume their creditors could reach any share you put in their name.
- If you have already added a child, do not sign a new deed to undo it until a lawyer and a tax adviser have looked at it together; the undoing is itself a gift, with its own reporting.

### Case 8: Tenants in common, and a share nobody planned for

*A conversation on the porch of a cabin in the Sierra Nevada, six weeks after a funeral. Three siblings had spent their childhood summers here: a wooden house with a sagging porch, a lake ten minutes' walk away, and a smell of pine and mothballs each of them could summon from memory. When their mother died, her will left the cabin to the three of them equally. They took title as tenants in common, one third each, and agreed over a bottle of wine on this porch that it would stay in the family forever. Now the brother, the one who loved it most, who fixed the roof and kept the logbook, has died of a stroke at fifty. He had no will. He had always meant to make one "once the divorce is sorted." He and his wife had been separated for two years; neither had filed. He also had a son from an earlier relationship, now nineteen. The two sisters are on the porch with the logbook and a letter from a probate lawyer.*

**The younger sister:** So his third comes back to us. That's how it works with family property, isn't it?

**The elder sister:** That's what I thought. The letter says no. Tenants in common have no survivorship. His share is part of his estate, like his car.

**The younger sister:** He didn't have a will. So it goes to his son.

**The elder sister:** Half of it does. The other half goes to his wife.

**The younger sister:** They were separated. She hasn't been up here since the wedding.

**The elder sister:** Separated isn't divorced. The lawyer quotes the statute. When someone dies without a will and leaves a spouse and one child, the spouse gets half of the separate property and the child gets the other half. The cabin share was his separate property, because he inherited it from Mom. But she's still his wife.

**The younger sister:** So we own the cabin with a woman neither of us has spoken to in eight years, and a nineteen-year-old.

**The elder sister:** Who has already asked whether he can bring friends up for the long weekend. With speakers.

**The younger sister:** What does she want?

**The elder sister:** To sell. Her lawyer says any co-owner can ask a court to partition the property. For a cabin, that means a sale.

**The younger sister:** Can we stop her?

**The elder sister:** We can buy her out. There's a newer California law for exactly this, for people who own as tenants in common without a written agreement. If she asks the court for a sale, we get the right to buy her share at the value the court sets. But we have to elect within forty-five days of the court's notice, and then actually find the money.

**The younger sister:** We don't have that kind of money.

**The elder sister:** We could borrow against our houses.

It took a year. In the end the sisters did borrow against their own homes, and bought out the widow at a price that both of them still think was too high. The nephew kept his sixth. The logbook has a new handwriting in it.

Everything the elder sister read from the letter was correct. Tenancy in common has no right of survivorship: each co-owner's share passes through that person's estate, by will or, without one, under intestacy rules. With a surviving spouse and one child, California gives the spouse half of separate property and the child the other half (Probate Code § 6401(c)); a separated spouse still counts as a spouse for inheritance. Any co-owner may ask a court to partition the property (Code of Civil Procedure § 872.210). Since 2023, the Partition of Real Property Act also gives the remaining co-owners a right to buy out the departing owner at the court-determined value. They must elect within 45 days of notice, and the price reflects the departing owner's share (§§ 874.311, 874.317). That right is useful only if the remaining owners can raise the money in time.

**What would have prevented it.** "It stays in the family forever" is a sentence with no legal content. It does not say what happens when one sibling dies, divorces, needs money, or wants out, or who pays for the roof. Two documents would have said it.

- **A co-ownership agreement** signed when they took title, giving the surviving siblings the right to buy a deceased sibling's share at a price set by appraisal, paid over a few years, with rules for use, costs, and repairs, and a procedure for anyone who wants out.
- **A will from each sibling,** saying where each share goes. A lawyer drafting the brother's would have asked about his marriage and pointed out that, until the divorce was final, his wife was still his heir.

Holding the cabin through a family company or a trust, with rules about who may own shares, is the heavier version of the same idea. For a modest cabin, an agreement and three wills would have been enough.

**In Germany,** the trouble would have started a generation earlier. When the mother died, the three siblings would have become a community of heirs (*Erbengemeinschaft*), co-owning the whole estate. Any co-heir can generally demand that the community be wound up at any time (BGB § 2042), and if they cannot agree how to divide a house, the default is a forced sale by partition auction (*Teilungsversteigerung*, § 753). A will leaving the cabin to one child, with the others compensated in money, or a binding agreement among the siblings, is the usual cure.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 8).

**What to do.**

- Look up how your co-owned property is titled. The deed will say joint tenancy or tenancy in common; if it says neither, ask.
- If you hold as tenants in common, sign a co-ownership agreement with a buy-out price formula, a payment period, and rules for costs and use.
- Make a will that says where your share goes.
- If you are separated but not divorced, update your will and beneficiary forms now; for inheritance purposes, your spouse is still your spouse.

### Case 9: Half of it was never hers to give

**The situation.** The wife and the husband had been married for thirty years and lived in a Spanish-style house in Pasadena that they bought in the third year of their marriage with two salaries and a mortgage that felt enormous at the time. It was in both their names. Neither of them could have told you exactly how the deed described their ownership.

It was a second marriage for both. The husband had two children from his first marriage; they were teenagers when the wife met him and adults now with children of their own. The wife had no children, but she had a niece, her late sister's daughter, whom she had more or less raised after her sister died young.

One evening, over a bottle of wine, they talked about what would happen "after." The husband said his children would be taken care of by him. The wife said she wanted the niece to have something. They agreed it was all very fair. Each of them later made a will on their own, at different times, with different lawyers. The husband's will left his estate to his two children. The wife's will left "my house and everything else I own" to the niece.

The wife died first, at sixty-eight.

**What the family assumed.** The niece assumed she had inherited the house, because the will said so. The husband assumed he would go on living in it, because it was his home. His two children assumed that whatever happened, their father's house would come to them.

**What actually happened, and why.** California is a community property state. Generally, property either spouse acquires during the marriage, other than by gift or inheritance, belongs to both of them equally as community property. At death, each spouse can leave only their own half of the community property by will. The other half already belongs to the surviving spouse (Probate Code § 100). The wife's will could not give away the husband's half of the house. It could only give away her half.

So the niece became the co-owner of one half of the house, with the husband, a man she had met perhaps a dozen times and who now lived in what was partly her property. She could, as a co-owner, have asked a court to partition the property, which for a single house would almost certainly have meant a forced sale. She was not a cruel person and did not want to throw the husband out of his home. She also had a mortgage of her own, a child starting university, and half of a house in Pasadena that produced no income. The husband could not afford to buy her out.

They made an uneasy arrangement: the husband would stay, pay the taxes and repairs, and the house would be sold when he died or moved. The husband died fourteen months later. His half went, under his will, to his two children. The house was now owned half by the niece and half by two people who regarded her as the woman who had tried to take their father's home. The sale took eleven months and two lawyers, and the conversation at the closing was conducted entirely through those lawyers.

**What would have prevented it.** The wife and the husband had a plan. They simply never wrote it down in one place. What they wanted was something like this: whoever dies first, the survivor keeps living in the house; after both are gone, the niece gets the wife's half and the husband's children get the husband's half. That is a perfectly reasonable plan, and there are well-worn ways to carry it out.

- **A joint revocable trust**, signed by both, holding the house. It could say that after the first death the survivor may live in the house for life, with the trustee paying expenses, and that after the second death the house is sold and the proceeds divided as agreed. Because both signed the trust, the survivor could not quietly change the deceased spouse's half of the plan afterwards, if the trust was drafted to prevent it.
- **Matching wills**, drafted by the same lawyer at the same time, with life-estate provisions or a testamentary trust for the survivor. Cheaper, but they need probate.
- **A conversation about how title is held.** California also allows spouses to hold property as "community property with right of survivorship" (Civil Code § 682.1), which passes the whole to the survivor without probate. That would have given the husband the whole house and left the niece nothing, which was not what the wife wanted, but at least it would have been a deliberate choice rather than a surprise.

**The German version.** Germany does not use community property by default. Most married couples live under the community of accrued gains, in which each spouse keeps their own property during the marriage and the gains are balanced at the end. A house in both names is usually simple co-ownership, each spouse owning a half. The wife's half would then pass under her will, just as in California, and the result could be very similar. That is why German couples in second marriages so often make a joint will or a notarized inheritance contract, and why German notaries ask, early and bluntly, "and what about the children from the first marriage?"

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 9).

**What to do.**

- List which of your assets are community and which are separate, and how each is titled. In California, your will controls only your half of community property.
- If either of you has children from an earlier relationship, see one lawyer together and sign one coordinated plan. Tact about seeing lawyers separately is exactly what produced the wife's will; separate advice is fine as well, but the plan for the house you share should be read by both of you in the same room.
- Decide in writing where the survivor will live, for how long, and who pays for the house in the meantime.
- If the survivor is not to be free to change the plan for the first spouse's half, make sure the documents say so.

### Case 10: The house given away eight years before

*The house is still given eight years before the father's death. What changes is who gets invited to the notary's table.*

**As it happened.** The father was a retired carpenter in a village near Regensburg, a widower with two children. His son, a quiet man who worked for the local water authority, lived in the granny flat behind the house and, when the father's heart began to fail, became his carer. For six years he did the shopping, drove his father to appointments, managed the medications, and sat up with him on bad nights. The daughter lived in Hamburg, an architect with two children and a demanding job. She visited at Christmas and on her father's birthday, and called every Sunday.

Eight years before he died, while he still felt reasonably well, the father went to the notary and gave the son the house by notarial deed. "My son has earned it," he told his friends, "and the rest will go to my daughter." The rest was a savings account of about 60,000 euros. The house was worth about 480,000. Two years later he wrote a will by hand naming the son as sole heir, "to keep things simple," on the theory that the son would hand his sister the savings.

He died at eighty-three. The son assumed the house was his beyond question; he had been on the land register for eight years. The daughter, who had always known about the house and never complained, assumed she would receive the savings.

German law saw it differently. A child excluded by a will, as naming the son sole heir excluded the daughter, has a compulsory share, the *Pflichtteil*: a money claim against the heir worth half of what the child would have received without a will (BGB § 2303). With two children and no surviving spouse, her statutory share would have been one half, so her compulsory share was one quarter.

A quarter of 60,000 euros is 15,000. But a parent cannot empty an estate by giving things away before death. Gifts made in the ten years before the death are added back for the calculation (§ 2325), shrinking by a tenth for each full year that has passed. The house had been given eight years earlier, so two tenths of its value, about 96,000 euros, was added back. (For gifts to a spouse, the clock does not start until the marriage ends; that exception did not apply. Nor had the father kept a usufruct, a *Nießbrauch*, over the house. Had he done so, German courts would have treated the gift as not yet made for this purpose, the clock would never have started, and the full value would have counted. A right to live in only part of a house usually does not stop the clock, but that depends on the facts.) The daughter's claim was a quarter of 156,000: about 39,000 euros, payable in cash by the son.

He did not have 39,000 euros. He had a house and a modest salary. German law can take a child's long-term care into account when children divide an estate (§ 2057a). That can also affect a sibling's compulsory share (§ 2316). But lawyers and courts disagree about whether, and how much, the rule applied when the son was already the sole heir. They also disagree about what six years of nights are worth in money. Each sibling hired one. It took a year and a half. At the end, the daughter told her brother that she would have signed anything their father asked her to sign, if only he had asked.

**As it could have gone.** Same father, same house, same notary, same day eight years before. This time the notary asks the question notaries are there to ask: "And your daughter? What does she receive, and does she know?"

The father invites both children to a second appointment. At the notary's table the daughter hears, for the first time in her father's words, what her brother does every night. The notary explains the proposed arrangement in language she understands and urges her to take independent advice and time to consider it. The father makes clear that she can refuse without losing his affection, contact, or any help she depends on. She chooses to sign a notarized waiver of her compulsory share, in return for the savings, left to her by name in a new will, and her father's workshop tools for her son, who wants to be a carpenter. German law allows a relative to renounce a compulsory share by notarized contract with the person whose estate it is (§§ 2346 and 2348), during that person's lifetime. The deed records that the gift is partly in return for the son's care, which may count differently from a pure gift. The father also takes out a small life insurance policy for the son, so that if any claim does arise there will be cash to pay it. If the daughter had refused, the father would have needed to plan for that choice rather than press her to waive her rights.

Eight years later the father dies. The son keeps the house. The daughter receives the savings and the tools. The siblings sit together at the funeral, and nobody hires anyone.

**The difference, line by line.**

- **What the father wanted:** the same in both versions.
- **What the daughter was asked:** nothing, or the one question that mattered, at a table, with her father present.
- **What the son owed:** about 39,000 euros and a year and a half of lawyers, or nothing.
- **The clock:** had the father lived ten full years after the gift, it would have dropped out of the calculation entirely. That is a reason to make planned gifts early, not a plan in itself.

**The California version.** California has no compulsory share for children. A California parent can disinherit an adult child entirely, as long as the will makes the intention clear. That freedom is real, and it is also why left-out children in California more often challenge wills on other grounds, such as capacity or undue influence (Case 16). Freedom to disinherit is not freedom from family disputes.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 10).

**What to do.**

- If you live in Germany and plan a large gift to one child, ask the notary to calculate the compulsory share consequences before you sign.
- A waiver of rights must be a genuine choice. Give the person time, an accessible explanation, and the chance to get independent advice; make clear that refusing will not cost them care, housing, contact, or affection. Ask the notary about other plans if they decline.
- If ongoing care contributions may affect a gift or estate plan, discuss them with the people involved where possible. With the caregiver's agreement, record dates, hours (including overnight care), tasks, duration, expenses, and effects on paid work. Include contributions by family, friends, partners, or others. Record any separate agreement about wages, reimbursement, loans, or gifts in writing. A log can help explain contributions; it does not itself promise payment or a larger inheritance, and no caregiver is obliged to keep one.
- Make sure the child who keeps the house will have cash to pay a sibling's claim, for example through life insurance.

### Case 11: The house for the son who pays the mortgage

**The situation.** The mother was seventy-three, a widow in a Central Valley town, living in the three-bedroom house she and her late husband had bought thirty years earlier. There was still a mortgage on it, from a refinance in her sixties. Four years ago, she needed cash for a new roof and medical bills. She signed a home equity investment agreement: a company paid her a lump sum in exchange for a share of the home's value when the agreement was settled. Settlement was due when she sold the house, died, or reached the end of the agreement's term. No monthly payments were due. The agreement had been recorded with the county, a detail she had barely noticed at the time.

She had two adult children. Her son had moved back into the house two years ago, after his own divorce, and since then he had paid the mortgage every month from his salary. Her daughter lived two hours away, with a family and a house of her own.

The mother wanted the house to go to her son. She had read about California's transfer-on-death deed and liked the idea, until she saw the word "revocable." Her son had paid the mortgage for two years. "I don't want him to feel I could change my mind and take it away," she said. A friend sent her a long answer from an online chat assistant. It recommended a life estate deed instead, "which can't be undone." The answer made five claims. It said the deed had to be recorded within 60 days, that the son could not witness it but the daughter could, and that it would cost "under a hundred dollars." It also said the deed was safe from Medicaid's five-year look-back. It promised that after her death the son would "simply sign an assumption agreement" with the lender and home equity company.

**What the family assumed.** The mother assumed that an irrevocable deed would protect her son and cost her nothing, because she would still live in the house. The son assumed that two years of mortgage payments gave him some kind of claim to the house already. The daughter assumed that whatever her mother decided would be fair, but she would have liked to be told.

**What actually happened, and why.** The mother took the printed chat answer to a title officer to ask about recording, and then, on the title officer's suggestion, to an estate planning lawyer. Most of what the chat had said was either wrong or incomplete.

**In short, the lawyer's answers.** The technical detail behind each point, with the statutes, is in [Appendix M](#Appendix M. Case 11, the Technical Notes).

- **The transfer-on-death deed.** California has only a revocable form; there is no irrevocable version. It must be signed before two witnesses present at the same time and a notary, and recorded within 60 days of the notary's date, or it has no effect. The son should not witness: if a beneficiary witnesses, the law presumes undue influence and he would have to disprove it. The daughter could legally witness a deed that gives her nothing, but neutral witnesses are stronger. ([Details](#The transfer-on-death deed, in detail).)
- **The life estate deed.** It would give the son a present interest she could not take back without his signature, and expose that interest to his creditors, a divorce, and his own early death. It would also mean a federal gift tax return, though the house would still be in her estate at death and so get a new tax basis. Property tax would follow the parent-child rules either way: the low assessment carries over only if the son makes the house his main home, and only up to a cap. Medi-Cal, if she ever needed nursing care, has its own look-back rules for gifts. ([Details](#The life estate deed, in detail).)
- **The mortgage.** Federal law stops a lender from calling in the loan on these family transfers when the child lives in the house. No "assumption" is needed for the son to keep it. ([Details](#The mortgage and the due-on-sale clause).)
- **The home equity investment.** No deed makes it go away. Her death would be a settlement event in any case, and a life estate deed recorded now might itself be a transfer needing the company's consent. There would be no "assumption agreement" for the son to sign; there would be a bill. ([Details](#The home equity investment, in detail).)

**What the mother actually did.** With the lawyer, she separated the two things she wanted: the son's security, and her own control.

- **For the son's security,** she signed a short written agreement with him. It recorded his mortgage payments and said whether they counted as rent, a loan, or an advance on his inheritance. It also explained what would happen if she sold the house or changed her plan: he would be repaid what he had put in, with or without interest, as they agreed. A signed writing is one way to prove a contract to make a will or devise under California law (§ 21700). The agreement turned "trust me" into a document.
- **For the house itself,** she chose a revocable living trust rather than a bare deed. She named herself as trustee and her son as successor trustee and beneficiary of the house. The trust could say what the deed form could not: that the son must settle the home equity agreement using the house or his own funds, how his payments were credited, and what the daughter would receive. Before anything was recorded, the lawyer got written answers from the home equity company about consent and from the mortgage servicer about the federal trust-transfer exception.
- **For her daughter,** she wrote a letter, and then had the conversation. The daughter would receive the savings and a cash gift from the trust; the house would go to her brother, who had paid for it. The daughter said she was relieved to have been asked.

A transfer-on-death deed, signed with two neutral witnesses and recorded within 60 days, would also have worked and cost less. Its revocability was the reason she hesitated. The written agreement answered that concern better than an irrevocable deed could. Adding the son to the title as a joint tenant was a third option. The same federal exception would have protected the mortgage, and adding a child as joint tenant is not, by itself, a change in ownership for property tax purposes while the parent remains a joint tenant. But it would have been a present gift of half the house. That could require a gift tax return once the gift exceeded the annual exclusion (19,000 dollars per recipient in 2026). The son would receive her tax basis for that half, his creditors and any divorce could affect the property, and the transfer would very likely require the home equity company's consent.

!> **What the chat answer got wrong, in five lines.** The 60-day recording deadline runs from the notary's acknowledgment, not from signing.
!>
!> A beneficiary-witness does not void the deed; it shifts the burden of proving there was no undue influence onto the beneficiary. The daughter was not barred from witnessing.
!>
!> "Under a hundred dollars" left out the recording fees, the advice, and the home equity investor's share of the house, which no deed reduces.
!>
!> California's Medi-Cal transfer review is being phased back in. Which months are reviewed depends on when long-term care is requested; the full 30-month review begins on 1 July 2028. Do not assume a gift is safe without current specialist advice. (Appendix M.)
!>
!> No "assumption agreement" makes a home equity investment disappear. Federal law keeps the lender from calling in the mortgage if the son lives there; the investor still has to be paid.

**Before you sign anything,** work through the [checklist in Appendix M](#Questions to ask before you sign a deed for one child). It covers the witnesses, the mortgage, any recorded agreement, the child's payments, property tax, nursing-care timing, gift tax, and the other children.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 11).

**What to do.**

- Separate the two goals: give the child security with a signed written agreement; keep control of the house with a revocable transfer-on-death deed or a living trust ([Chapter 13](#Chapter 13. Payable-on-Death and Transfer-on-Death), [Chapter 19](#Chapter 19. Trusts)).
- Before recording any deed, read every agreement recorded against the house and ask the mortgage servicer and any home equity investor, in writing, what the deed will trigger.
- Use two witnesses who receive nothing, and record within 60 days of notarization.
- Do not add a child to the title for inheritance purposes without advice ([Chapter 14](#Chapter 14. Joint Ownership)).
- Tell the other children, and record the plan on your Family List.
