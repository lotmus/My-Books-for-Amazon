# Part Nine: The Receiver's Half

*Educational guide, not legal advice. If you have just lost someone, you do not need to read all of this today. Read the first section, do what it says, and come back later. Appendix B is the same material as a printable checklist.*

## Chapter 37. The First Ninety Days

This chapter is written for the person who was named: the executor, the heir, the archive-keeper, or simply the family member who ended up holding the folder. Give it to them in advance, if you are the planner. If you are the receiver, start here.

![Figure 5. The first ninety days after a death, at a glance. The numbered steps below give the detail.](src/diagrams/fig3_first_ninety_days.png)

### Before you act: confirm your authority

Being named in a will does not by itself prove to a bank or other institution that you can act for the estate. In California, the executor must be appointed by the court; the court's Letters are proof of that appointment. Do not withdraw or distribute estate assets just because you are named. In Germany, the inheritance passes to the heirs at death; a named *Testamentsvollstrecker* has a separate role, which begins when they accept the office by declaration to the probate court (BGB § 2202). Confirm who is authorized to handle each matter and what proof the institution requires. Take prompt steps to protect people, pets, and property, and get local advice before any time-sensitive action you are unsure about.

### The first days

**Do not rush.** Very little has to be done in the first forty-eight hours, and almost nothing has to be decided. The exceptions are below.

1. **The death certificate.** In California, a doctor or coroner certifies the death and the funeral home usually files the death certificate and can order certified copies for you. In Germany, the death must be registered with the local registry office (*Standesamt*), usually by the funeral home, which can obtain copies of the death certificate (*Sterbeurkunde*). Order several certified copies. Banks, insurers, pension offices, and courts each want their own, and some keep them.
2. **Wishes about the body.** Check the Family List and any directive for wishes about organ donation, burial or cremation, and religious rites. Organ donation decisions are time-critical.
3. **Secure the home, the car, and pets.** Lock up. Collect valuables in plain view. Feed the animals, the same day. Arrange for mail to be collected or forwarded.
4. **Tell the people who need to know.** Close family, the employer if relevant, and the people named on the first page of the Family List.
5. **Find the documents.** Will, trust, powers of attorney (which end at death), the Family List, insurance policies. Do not write on the original will. Do not throw anything away.

### The first two weeks

6. **The will goes to the court.** In California, whoever has the original will must deliver it to the clerk of the superior court in the county where the estate will be administered. The deadline is thirty days after learning of the death (Probate Code § 8200), unless a probate petition has already been filed. In Germany, anyone holding a will not already in official custody must deliver it to the probate court without delay (BGB § 2259); wills in official custody are found through the Central Register of Wills, which is notified of deaths. The German court opens the will and notifies the people concerned.
7. **The funeral.** Pay attention to cost; in many families, the funeral is the first large expense and is paid in a rush. The estate can usually reimburse reasonable funeral costs later, so keep the receipts.

   If you are arranging it, begin with the person's written wishes and the worksheet in Appendix C. Ask the funeral home which decisions are time-sensitive, what can wait, and for an itemized written estimate. Ask which charges come from the funeral home and which may be billed separately by a cemetery, crematory, or other provider. Compare options that fit the person's wishes and the family's budget; a meaningful gathering does not require every available service or product. Ask about faith and cultural practices, language, accessibility, and lower-impact disposition options available locally. If wishes are unclear, let the family decide together where practical; no single kind of service is the measure of how much someone was loved.

   If you choose burial, say what you want for the grave after the funeral: a marker or no marker, flowers or plantings, upkeep, and whether visits matter to you. You may want your family to honor your legacy in ways that carry it forward, rather than feel obliged to tend a grave. Say so directly: "Let the grave be. If you want to honor me, share a story, pass on something I taught you, or keep one of my useful projects going if it still helps people." Flowers and visits can still be welcome; make clear that they are choices, not duties. Keep any instruction flexible enough to fit cemetery rules, costs, and what your family can manage.

   **United States.** Under the FTC Funeral Rule, a provider must give an itemized General Price List to people who inquire in person and provide accurate price information by phone. Consumers may select only the goods and services they want, except for items required by law and the non-declinable basic services fee. Ask for the current price list and a written statement of the selections and charges. Rules elsewhere differ; ask what is required by local law and what is the provider's policy.

8. **The lawyer or notary.** If there is a lawyer named on the Family List, call them. Ask whether probate (California) or a certificate of inheritance (Germany) will be needed, and what it will cost.
9. **Benefits and pensions.** Notify Social Security (United States) or the statutory pension insurer (Germany), and any employer or private pension. Payments made after the death may have to be returned; survivors may be entitled to new payments that must be applied for.

### In Germany: the six-week clock

German heirs inherit debts as well as assets (BGB § 1967). An heir who does not want to inherit, for example because the estate may be overindebted, must disclaim within six weeks (§ 1944). The period generally starts when the heir learns of the inheritance and the reason for it; for an heir named in a will, it does not start before the court announces the will. It is six months if the deceased's last residence was only abroad or the heir is abroad when the period begins. The form matters: a casual letter or email is not enough. The declaration must be made before the probate court for the record or in a publicly certified form, usually through a notary. Contact the probate court or a German inheritance lawyer immediately to confirm the deadline, required appointment, and formalities. Doing nothing means accepting.

A disclaimer passes the inheritance to whoever would be next in line, often the heir's own children, who may then need to decide for themselves and may face their own deadline. Get advice about any minor children before filing. Talk to the probate court or a lawyer before you act.

Where there is more than one German heir, they generally form an *Erbengemeinschaft*: the estate is held jointly, and one heir does not automatically have authority to distribute or deal with every asset alone (BGB § 2032). A will may appoint a *Testamentsvollstrecker* with authority over the estate. Ask the court, notary, or institution what proof is needed. An *Erbschein* is not required in every case; a notarized will or inheritance contract may prove succession for some purposes, though institutions can have different requirements.

### In California: probate or not

10. **Work out what passes outside probate.** Beneficiary designations, joint tenancy, POD and TOD assets, and trust assets usually pass directly, depending on title and the governing documents. California has simplified procedures for some qualifying property. For deaths on or after 1 April 2025, the general small-estate limit is 208,850 dollars, but it applies to the value of property counted under the statute, after applicable exclusions—not simply to all personal property or to the estate's headline total. A separate court petition may be available for certain primary residences valued up to 750,000 dollars. Each procedure has its own eligibility rules, forms, waiting periods, and exclusions; check the current California Courts self-help instructions or ask a probate lawyer before relying on one (Chapter 17).
11. **If formal probate is needed,** treat this as a map, not a complete filing guide. The proposed personal representative files a petition; interested people must receive notice, and a hearing is held. If the court appoints the representative, it issues Letters. Administration then commonly includes identifying and valuing assets, filing an inventory and appraisal, notifying creditors, addressing taxes and claims, and seeking approval for final accounting and distribution. Local court forms and procedures matter; use the California Courts' probate self-help guide and the county probate court's self-help services. Creditor claims generally must be filed by the later of four months after Letters are first issued or sixty days after notice is mailed to the creditor (Probate Code § 9100).
12. **If there is a trust,** the successor trustee takes over trust assets without court supervision, and must notify beneficiaries and heirs as the law requires. Ask a lawyer what notices are due and when.

### The first ninety days

13. **Make an inventory.** Use the Family List as your starting point. List every asset and debt, with values at the date of death. Keep statements.
14. **Keep estate money separate.** In California, open an estate account once the court has appointed the personal representative and issued Letters. In Germany, ask the bank what proof it requires and remember that co-heirs generally act together unless a *Testamentsvollstrecker* or other authorized representative can act. An *Erbschein* is one form of proof, not an automatic requirement in every case. Do not mix estate money with your own.
15. **Notify institutions.** Banks, insurers, brokers, the tax authorities, credit card companies, utilities. Ask each what they need. Many have a bereavement team; ask for it by name.
16. **Do not distribute too early.** Debts, taxes, and expenses come first. An executor who pays out the inheritance and then finds a tax bill may be personally liable for it.
17. **Taxes.** The deceased's final income tax return is still due. Estates may have their own income tax filings. Few estates owe United States estate tax; the basic exclusion amount is 15 million dollars per person for 2026. Still, the executor of a married person's estate may want to file a federal estate tax return to preserve the unused exclusion for the surviving spouse. Ask a tax adviser. In Germany, each heir may owe inheritance tax above their personal allowance (Chapter 15). An acquisition subject to inheritance tax must generally be reported in writing to the tax office within three months of learning of it (§ 30 ErbStG), subject to exceptions, for example where the court that opened the will has already informed the tax office. Take advice.
18. **Cancel what should be canceled,** carefully. Not the cloud storage holding the photos and recordings until everything has been downloaded (step 21).

### Family pressure

Someone will want the watch now. Someone will ask when the money is coming. Someone will say, "Mum always said I could have the piano." Three rules help:

- **Nothing leaves the house until the will has been read and the inventory done**, except what the will specifically gives and the executor agrees to release.
- This is a safeguard against premature distribution, not a reason to leave a person, pet, or home unsafe. Address urgent care, safety, perishables, or property at risk of damage; record what you moved or disposed of, why, and where it went. Check before discarding property of value.
- **Write things down.** Requests, promises, decisions, contacts, payments, and what you moved. Use the running log at the end of Appendix B; keep receipts and supporting messages with the estate records.
- **When in doubt, wait.** Very few things get worse with a few weeks' delay. Relationships get worse with a hasty decision.

### When to hire the lawyer

Hire a lawyer in California or see a notary in Germany if the estate includes real estate or a business, or assets in more than one country or state. Get advice as well if someone is challenging the will, the estate may be insolvent, a beneficiary is a minor or disabled, or you are unsure what you are allowed to do. The cost of early advice is almost always smaller than the cost of a mistake.

### The meaning archive

This is the collection of recordings, letters, photographs, and creative work the person left. Follow the permission sheet, and handle it at a pace the receiver can manage.

19. **Open the recordings and letters only as the instructions say.** If a letter and the will disagree about property, follow the will, title, or beneficiary designation that legally governs the asset, not the letter (Chapter 31). Keep the letter anyway; it may explain.
20. **Respect the permissions.** Share within what the permission sheet (Appendix H) allows. When in doubt, ask the other people named, or wait.
21. **Back up before you reorganize.** Copy every recording, photo, and document to two places before you rename, sort, or delete anything. Grief and accidental deletion go together.

### If you are the grandchild receiving it

You may love your grandfather and still choose a life he would not have chosen. His hopes are not a test you have to pass, and your choices are not a measure of how much you loved him. Take comfort from what fits; question, adapt, or set aside advice that does not. You do not owe him a particular career, faith, marriage, family, or achievement.

You do not have to watch every recording now. Choose a time that feels manageable, invite someone you trust to sit with you, pause when you need to, or come back later. You may keep a message private and decline to carry on a book, channel, or project. Follow the permission sheet; preserving one copy for yourself does not require you to publish it or become its caretaker. Ask the archive-keeper, creator steward, or the relevant backup to handle the rest.

If you want to remember him in your own way, start small: write down one phrase he used, label one photograph, or record one story you remember doing together. Ask another relative for a story you have not heard. You do not need to make a complete archive or remember everything correctly. The legal tasks in this chapter may have deadlines; listening to messages and making a family memory project can wait.

**Elsewhere?** See [Chapter 40](#Probate and estate administration, elsewhere) for local probate steps.

## Chapter 38. Digital Access From the Other Side of the Lock

### What you can do with the right preparation

If the person who died followed Part Six:

- Use the password manager's emergency access to open the vault.
- Use the device passcodes to open the phone and the computer.
- Request data through any legacy-contact setting they turned on.
- Download the photos, recordings, and documents.
- Close, memorialize, or keep accounts as their instructions say.

### What you can do without it

If there was no preparation:

- **Ask each provider.** Most major services have a process for deceased users. They typically ask for a death certificate and proof of your authority (letters of administration in California, a certificate of inheritance in Germany). Some will close the account; some will release data; some will do neither without a court order.
- **California.** The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (Probate Code §§ 870 and following) gives a personal representative a framework for requesting access. Content of communications must be disclosed if the deceased consented or a court directs it (§ 876); otherwise, the provider may only have to provide a catalog (§ 877). A direction the user gave through the provider's own online tool takes priority over the will (§ 873).
- **Germany.** The Federal Court of Justice held that a social-network account passes to the heirs, and that they are entitled to access it and its contents (BGH, 12 July 2018, III ZR 183/17). Heirs can rely on that when dealing with providers, although providers' processes vary.
- **Devices.** A provider process or court order may allow access to some account data, but it cannot guarantee that a locked, encrypted device can be opened. Without the passcode, data stored only on the device may be lost, although copies may exist in the cloud.

### What to export

Before closing anything, export:

- Photos and videos, at full resolution.
- Recordings and the index (Part Seven).
- Email folders that matter, especially anything with financial or legal correspondence.
- Contacts (you will need them for notifications).
- Documents and notes.
- Calendar entries (they reveal subscriptions, appointments, and obligations).

### What not to do

- Do not post on the deceased's social media as if you were them.
- Do not log into accounts in ways the provider's terms forbid, if you can avoid it; use the official deceased-user process. Logging in with a found password can be against the terms of service and, in some situations, the law.
- Do not delete anything until it has been exported and backed up.
- Do not share private messages beyond what the deceased permitted.

### Cryptocurrency and other bearer assets

If the deceased held cryptocurrency in self-custody, the will names who receives it, but access depends on the keys. Look for sealed instructions (Case 25). Be very careful: anyone who offers to "recover" crypto for an upfront fee is far more likely to be a fraudster than a rescuer.
