## Chapter 11. Signing It So It Counts: California and Germany

A will can be clear, fair, and useless if it is signed wrong. This chapter sets out the formal rules in the book's two home jurisdictions. If you live anywhere else, treat it as a picture of the kind of rules to ask about, not as the rules themselves.

### California: the three doors

California offers three main ways to make a valid individual will.

**The witnessed will.** Under Probate Code § 6110, the will must be in writing and signed by the testator (or by someone else in the testator's name, in the testator's presence and at the testator's direction). At least two people must sign as witnesses during the testator's lifetime. Both must be present at the same time, must witness either the signing or the testator's acknowledgment of the signature or of the will, and must understand that the document is the testator's will. Most lawyer-drafted wills use this door.

If the witnessing went wrong, § 6110(c)(2) offers a narrow rescue: the will is treated as properly witnessed if the person offering it proves by *clear and convincing evidence* that the testator intended it to be their will when they signed it. That is a high standard, argued in court, paid for by the estate. Do not plan to use it.

**The holographic will.** Under § 6111, a will that does not meet the witnessing rules is still valid if the signature and the material provisions are in the testator's own handwriting. No witnesses are required. If it is undated and that creates doubt about which of two wills came later, or whether the testator had capacity at the time, the undated will can fail to that extent. So date it. A statement of intent may appear on a commercially printed form, but the gifts must be handwritten.

**The California statutory will.** Probate Code § 6240 contains a fill-in will form, published free by the California courts. You fill in the blanks without adding or crossing out words, then sign in front of two adult witnesses, who sign in your presence and each other's. The form's own instructions say that notarization alone is not sufficient. It is straightforward for uncomplicated estates. It is not designed for blended families, businesses, or complicated trusts.

**Who may witness.** Any person generally competent to be a witness. A gift to a witness does not void the will (§ 6112), but unless there are two other disinterested witnesses, it creates a presumption that the witness obtained the gift by undue influence or similar wrongdoing, which the witness must then disprove. Use two people who receive nothing.

**Notaries.** A California will does not need a notary. Notarization does not replace witnesses. (Many other California estate documents, such as the statutory power of attorney and transfer-on-death deeds, do require notarization. Different documents, different rules.)

**Capacity.** A person must be at least eighteen and of sound mind (Probate Code § 6100). Roughly, you need to understand what a will is, what you own in broad terms, and who your family is. You do not need a perfect memory.

**After death.** Whoever has custody of the original must deliver it to the clerk of the superior court within thirty days of learning of the death (§ 8200), unless a probate petition has already been filed. Keep the original safe, tell your executor where it is, and give your lawyer a copy.

### Signing the statutory will in ten minutes

The California statutory will (Case 13) is designed to be completed without a lawyer:

1. Download the current form from the California courts' website and read its instructions all the way through, once, before writing anything.
2. Fill in the choices in ink. The form offers set options; if you need something it does not offer, that is a sign you need a lawyer.
3. Find two adults who receive nothing under the will, and sit with them at the same table at the same time.
4. Tell them, "This is my will," and sign while they watch.
5. Each witness signs in your presence and in each other's presence.
6. Put the original somewhere safe, write its location on your Family List, and tell your executor.

Ten minutes, two neighbors, one pen. It is the most underused free legal document in the state.

### Germany: two ordinary forms, and a few extraordinary ones

The German Civil Code (BGB) allows a will in ordinary form in only two ways (§ 2231).

**The notarial will (öffentliches Testament).** You declare your last will to a notary, who records it, or you hand the notary a document stating that it contains your last will (§ 2232). The document need not be in your handwriting; it can be typed, and it can be handed over open or sealed. The notary advises on the content, checks identity and capacity, and arranges for the will to go into official custody at the local court and be registered with the Central Register of Wills (*Zentrales Testamentsregister*). The notary charges a fee based on the value of the estate, set by statute.

Two practical advantages are worth knowing. First, a notarial will is reliably found. Second, it may save the heirs from applying for a certificate of inheritance (*Erbschein*). Under the Land Registry Code (*Grundbuchordnung*, § 35), a notarial will plus the court's record of its opening is usually enough to update the land register. A registry may still ask for a certificate if it has doubts. Banks often accept the will, too. Because a certificate of inheritance costs a fee based on the estate's value, the notarial will can pay for itself.

**The handwritten will (eigenhändiges Testament).** You write the entire text yourself, by hand, and sign it (§ 2247). The law says you *should* state the date and place, and that the signature *should* include first name and surname; if those are missing, the will may still be valid, but an undated will can fail if the date matters to its validity. A typed will signed by hand is not valid (Case 12). A person who is a minor or cannot read writing cannot make a handwritten will (§ 2247(4)); minors over sixteen may make a will before a notary (§ 2233).

You can keep a handwritten will at home, but you can also ask the local court to take it into special official custody (§ 2248). It is then registered in the Central Register of Wills, which the Federal Chamber of Notaries says charges a registration fee of 12.50 euros if collected through a notary and 15.50 euros if it bills you directly. Privately kept wills cannot be registered at all. Anyone who holds a will not in official custody must deliver it to the probate court without delay after learning of the death (§ 2259).

**The joint will (gemeinschaftliches Testament).** Only spouses (and, under the Civil Partnership Act, registered partners) can make a joint will (§ 2265). It can be handwritten by one spouse and signed by both, or made before a notary. The most common type, the *Berliner Testament*, has the spouses name each other as sole heirs and the children as heirs of the survivor (§ 2269). Provisions that depend on each other become binding: while both are alive, one spouse can revoke them only by notarized declaration to the other, and after the first death the survivor generally loses the right to revoke them unless they disclaim what they inherited (§§ 2270, 2271). Decide before signing whether the survivor should be free to change the final heirs, and write that freedom into the will if so.

**The inheritance contract (Erbvertrag).** A contract with another person about succession, which binds you in a way a will does not. It must be made before a notary with both parties present (§ 2276). Unmarried couples who want the binding effect of a joint will use it.

**Emergency wills.** If death is feared before a notary can be reached, a will can be made before the mayor and two witnesses (§ 2249). In near danger of death, or when cut off by extraordinary circumstances, it may instead be declared orally before three witnesses, who must make a written record (§ 2250). These emergency wills lapse three months after they are made if the testator is still alive (§ 2252). They are rare, and none of them is a video.

### Writing a German handwritten will that works

If you choose the handwritten route (Case 12), a few habits make the will stronger without making it longer.

- Use a pen, not a pencil, and plain paper. Write slowly enough that a stranger can read every word.
- Begin with a heading such as *Mein Testament* or "My Will," so nobody can mistake it for a draft or a letter.
- Name people fully, with dates of birth if you can, and describe property clearly enough to identify it: "my flat at [address]" rather than "the flat."
- Say who should inherit (the *Erbe*), not only who gets particular things. German law draws a sharp line between appointing an heir, who steps into your whole legal position, and leaving a particular item to someone (a *Vermächtnis*), who only has a claim against the heirs. Muddling the two is one of the commonest causes of disputes about German home-made wills.
- Write the place and date and sign with your first and last name at the end, below the last line of text. Anything added below the signature may not count.
- If you change your mind, write a new will rather than crossing things out.

### German wills use different building blocks

A Californian will gives property to people. A German will chiefly appoints *heirs* (*Erben*), who step into the deceased's shoes and take the estate as a whole, debts included, in shares. Leaving someone a single object or a sum of money usually creates a *legacy* (*Vermächtnis*): the beneficiary does not become an heir but has a claim against the heirs to hand the item over (§ 2174). The Civil Code says that if a will gives someone the whole estate or a fraction of it, that person is an heir even if not called one. If it gives only individual items, the person is, in case of doubt, not an heir—even if the will calls them one (§ 2087).

This matters more than it sounds. A German will that says "my house to my daughter, my savings to my son" may be read in either of two ways, depending on the values involved. It could make both children heirs in proportion to those values, with an instruction for dividing the property (*Teilungsanordnung*, § 2048). Or it could make one child the sole heir and the other a legatee. The readings have different consequences for debts, compulsory shares, and who applies for the certificate of inheritance. A German notary or lawyer would write, "I appoint my daughter and my son as my heirs in equal shares," then deal with the house separately. Write it that way, too.

### A short handwritten will in German, with a gloss

Here is a fictional example, kept deliberately simple, to show the shape. Square brackets mark where the real text would give names, dates, and places; in a real handwritten will, every word, including those details, is written out by hand. It is not a template for your situation.

> *Testament*
>
> *Ich, [Vorname Nachname], geboren am [Datum] in [Ort], setze hiermit meine beiden Kinder, [Vorname Nachname] und [Vorname Nachname], zu gleichen Teilen als meine Erben ein. Ersatzerben sind jeweils deren Abkömmlinge.*
>
> *Meine Nichte [Vorname Nachname] erhält als Vermächtnis meinen Ring mit dem blauen Stein.*
>
> *Alle früheren Verfügungen von Todes wegen widerrufe ich.*
>
> *Passau, den 10. Oktober 2026*
>
> *[Vorname Nachname, als Unterschrift]*

Gloss: The testator names her two children as heirs in equal shares, with their descendants as substitute heirs; leaves her niece a ring as a legacy; revokes earlier wills; and dates, places, and signs with first name and surname. All of it in her own handwriting, on paper, in ink.

### Official custody in practice

In Germany, you can take your handwritten will to the local court (*Amtsgericht*) of your choice, usually where you live. The court seals it in an envelope, records it in the Central Register of Wills, and gives you a deposit receipt. You may retrieve a handwritten will at any time; retrieving it does not revoke it. A notarial will is different: taking it out of official custody revokes it (BGB § 2256), a rule that surprises people. To change a will in custody, the simplest course is to write a new one and deposit it as well. A later will overrides an earlier one to the extent they conflict. Keep the deposit receipt (*Hinterlegungsschein*) in your binder so your family knows which court has the will. The court returns a will only to the testator, and a joint will only to both spouses. That helps prevent a well-meaning relative from "tidying up." One Family List entry gives your family the practical detail: "Original will: in official custody at the local court in Passau; receipt in binder, section 1."

### What neither system accepts

Neither California nor Germany accepts, as a will:

- A video or audio recording, however clear.
- A text message, email, or note on a phone.
- A typed document signed by hand without witnesses (California) or without a notary (Germany).
- A document someone else wrote that you only signed, unless it was properly witnessed (California) or handed to a notary (Germany).

Chapter 31 comes back to the first item, because it is where Part Seven's video legacy and the law meet, and where good intentions most often turn into a family argument.

### Where the original goes

- **Typical custody.** California: Lawyer's office, home safe, or bank box the executor can open; Germany: Official custody at the local court (notarial wills automatically).
- **Central registration.** California: No state-run will register; Germany: Central Register of Wills (Bundesnotarkammer) for wills in official custody.
- **Duty after death.** California: Deliver to superior court clerk within 30 days (§ 8200); Germany: Deliver to probate court without delay (§ 2259).
- **What heirs usually need.** California: Letters from the probate court, or a summary procedure; Germany: Certificate of inheritance, or notarial will plus opening record.
