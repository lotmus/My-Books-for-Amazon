# PART II — The Past We Can Rebuild

## 7. The Explosion That Happened Everywhere

People hear “Big Bang” and picture a grenade in a dark warehouse: a bright point, a blast wave, shrapnel flying into empty rooms. That picture is wrong in every way that matters, and it is the reason smart readers keep asking a question that has no answer: *what did it explode into?*

It did not explode into anything. It was not a bomb in space. It was space, getting roomier, everywhere at once.

If you could have been present at a minute old — you could not have; there was no solid ground, no shadow, no place to stand — you would not have seen a fireball receding toward a horizon. You would have been *inside* the fireball, and so would every other direction. The glow would have been the walls, the floor, the air, the idea of “away.” Density would have been falling because the distances between nearby bits of stuff were stretching, not because the stuff was fleeing a scene.

A baker’s analogy earns its keep here. Imagine raisin bread dough rising in a warm bowl. Every raisin sees every other raisin recede. There is no special raisin in the middle of the dough. The “middle” is a habit we picked up from grenades. In a universe that is, on the largest scales, the same in every direction and at every place — the Cosmological Principle, a working rule with a lot of data behind it — every galaxy is allowed to feel like the center of the stretch. That feeling is a symmetry, not a privilege.

![Figure 7. A grid of dots grows. Every dot sees the others recede. There is no center to stand outside of.](Figures/figs/fig07.png)

Read the figure as a rule, not a cartoon. If every dot recedes from every other dot, you cannot stand outside the grid and point at the blast. There is no outside. The grown-up name for that rule is FLRW: Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker, four people whose initials stand for a spacetime that is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. Homogeneous means no special place. Isotropic means no special direction. Put them together and you are not allowed a center, and you are not allowed an edge you could walk to. An edge would be a special place. A center would be a special place. The equations refuse both.

The single number that does the work is a scale factor, written *a(t)*.

*where a(t) is the relative size of unbound distances at time t, set to 1 today by habit, smaller in the past, growing if the raisins are receding.*

Distances between unbound things scale with *a*. Wavelengths of free light scale with *a*. Ordinary matter thins as *1/a³*, because the same stuff is poured into a larger volume. Radiation thins faster, as *1/a⁴*, because each photon is also getting tired. That bookkeeping is the whole of the simple expanding universe as a calculator. Appendix A7 writes the metric. Here it is enough to stop looking for a warehouse.

The equations that describe this are named for Alexander Friedmann, a young mathematician who, in the early 1920s, took Einstein’s gravity seriously enough to let the universe change size. Einstein had tried to freeze it with a fudge factor. Friedmann showed the freeze was optional. Georges Lemaître, a priest and physicist, said out loud what the math was hinting: the universe has a history. Edwin Hubble, with a telescope and a lot of patient plates of spiral nebulae, found that the farther a galaxy is, the faster it recedes. The grenade picture rushed back in through the newspapers. It has been misleading well-meaning people ever since.

The dates are not decoration. In 1922 Friedmann published expanding — and recollapsing — solutions of Einstein’s equations. Einstein’s first reply was that the paper contained a mistake. The mistake was Einstein’s. He had already, in 1917, added a term — the cosmological constant, written Λ — to keep the universe static, because a static universe was the respectable prejudice of the room. When the freeze proved optional, and then when the sky refused to sit still, Einstein regretted the term. Later retellings call it his biggest blunder. Whether he used that kitchen word, or only suffered the fact, is a historian’s fight. The physics fight is simpler: he had forced the loaf not to rise, and the loaf was rising.

Lemaître, in 1927, did the unfashionable thing of putting numbers on the stretch and saying, in print, that the universe had a history with a hotter, denser past — a primeval atom, in his phrase. Hubble, in 1929, plotted velocities against distances for a handful of galaxies and got a line. The line was noisy. The distances were too small by a factor of several. The slope was therefore too steep. The *existence* of the slope was the news. Chapter 8 will fight over the second digit of that slope. This chapter only needs the insult to the grenade: recession is a function of distance, not of a privileged warehouse.

Hot claim: the universe is expanding, and it was hotter and denser in the past. That is not a mood. It is the only account that has survived the leftover glow in Chapter 9 and the leftover helium in Chapter 10.

Warm claim: the simple expanding model — same in every direction, no special place — is a superb average. Under the average there is a foam of clusters and voids, which we will meet when quantum twitches get their chapter.

Cold claim, which this book will not smuggle in as fact: that the bang was a “beginning of everything” in the kitchen-clock sense, with a waiting room labeled BEFORE. The loaf may have a boundary. “Before” may not be a word that applies. Chapter 45 will sit with that until it stops itching.

Was there really a bang?

There was really a *hot, dense, expanding everywhere*. That is not a brand. It is two fossils and a stretch: the leftover glow, the leftover helium, the raisins receding. If you want the words “Big Bang” to mean a grenade, then no. If you want them to mean the past we can rebuild — a universe that was once a mixed fog, did nuclear work in minutes, and has been getting roomier since — then yes. The name is a nickname. The nickname stuck. The grenade did not.

It was not an exploding black hole. A black hole is a local one-way membrane in a larger loaf, made when a clump loses an argument with gravity. A white hole is that film run backward: a local fountain no one has seen. The bang was not local. There was no outside to explode into, no warehouse, no shrapnel crossing a pre-existing dark. Here: **a hole is a place. The bang was a time.** You can write math in which a collapse, time-reversed, looks like a bang. You cannot point at the leftover glow and say “that was a star that burst.” Stars come later. Holes come later. The fog comes first.

How old, if we are allowed a kitchen number? About 13.8 billion years (the Planck fit gives 13.79, uncertain by about twenty million years within the standard model[^21]), counted from the hot fog we can reconstruct — not counted from a waiting room labeled BEFORE. That is a hot-enough claim for this chapter: the leftover glow, the leftover helium, and the stretch-rate of now, run backward through the equations, meet at a finite age. The number will get a few more digits later. It will not turn into infinity.

Then why is the part we can see about 93 billion light-years across, if light has only had 13.8 billion years to travel? Because the dough kept rising while the light was in transit. A photon that has been flying for 13.8 billion years did not fly through a frozen warehouse. The places it left have been pulled farther apart the whole time. The farthest stuff whose light has had time to reach us sits, today, at a proper distance of about 46 billion light-years. Diameter, roughly 93 billion. That is not the universe’s edge. It is our archive’s edge. Chapter 26 will make the distinction official. Here: the loaf is larger than the slice we can still taste.

So what was it *from*?

Not from a kitchen nothing — not from an empty room with the lights off and a magician. Empty, in physics, is already a something: a vacuum with rules, jitters, a leftover shove, a geometry. Pair production makes a particle and its opposite from energy, and the energy had to be in the account. The early soup made particles and antiparticles by the truckload and then almost canceled them. We are the rounding error: about one leftover nucleon per billion photons, Chapter 10’s ratio, the reason atoms are a footnote. That leftover is a *warm* problem with a name, baryogenesis — why the cancellation was rude by one part in a billion. We have recipes (a bit of leftover difference between matter and antimatter, a bit of out-of-equilibrium cooking). We do not have the dish photographed. “Matter from nothing” is a poster. Matter from a slightly unfair soup is the grown-up line.

Andrei Sakharov, in 1967, wrote down what a kitchen would need to keep one extra ladle of matter when the soup tried to cancel. Three conditions, in ordinary language. First, the books must be allowed to go out of balance: the soup must be able to change the baryon count, to make a nucleon without making its twin. Second, the soup must be rude in a particular way. It must treat matter and antimatter a little differently, and it must treat left and right a little differently, which physicists write as C violation and CP violation — charge conjugation and its partner, the combined swap of particle-identity and mirror. Third, the cooking cannot be in equilibrium. A pot that has fully settled forgets its rudeness; the extra ladle gets undone on the way back. You need a moment when the reactions cannot keep up with the cooling — a slammed oven door, a stretch that outruns the chemistry.

Those three are warm. We have seen C and CP rudeness in laboratories on Earth, in kaons and B mesons, at the level of a small insult, not yet at the level of one leftover in a billion. We have not photographed the cosmic moment. The ratio is hot. The recipe that produced the ratio is a research program.

What about the soup’s own origin — the first slice of loaf?

Inflation, if you buy it, pushes the question back: a burst of stretch, then a reheating that *is* the hot fog. The burst still wants a field, a vacuum, a reason. Quantum-gravity sketches try to stop using “before”: Hartle and Hawking round the loaf off so time only becomes time when there is enough of it; Vilenkin tunnels from a technical nothing that is not a kitchen nothing. Those are cold, and they are allowed to be. The fossils do not reach that room. They reach the fog, the three minutes, the glow. **We do not owe you a waiting room labeled BEFORE in order to say the fog was real.**

For now, retire the grenade. Retire the bursting hole. Keep the dough. The past we can rebuild is not a point. It is a hotter, smaller-spaced version of everywhere, made of a soup that almost canceled and didn’t, and we do not yet own the first word of the recipe. We own the leftovers. That is already a courtroom.

## 8. Everything Is Stretching

In the 1910s, Vesto Slipher, working nights at a stubborn telescope in Arizona, noticed that the light from many spiral nebulae was shifted toward the red. Red, in the physics of waves, is the stretch. A siren falling away from you drops in pitch for the same family of reasons. Slipher did not yet know those nebulae were other galaxies. He had the stretch before he had the nouns.

The years were 1912 to 1917, more or less, a run of plates at Lowell Observatory. Slipher was measuring spectra: the fingerprints of atoms in light — hydrogen, calcium, the usual set of lines. In spiral nebulae those lines sat in the wrong place, shifted toward the red for most of them, toward the blue for a few. Receding, approaching. He did not yet have a distance ladder tall enough to turn the shifts into a law. He had a pile of redshifts and a puzzle. By 1917 he had two dozen spirals, and the pile was mostly red. The sky was already voting.

A law needs a ruler, not just a pile. The first honest cosmic ruler was a kind of star that brightens and dims on a schedule. Henrietta Leavitt, in 1912, working through plates of the Small Magellanic Cloud at Harvard, found that the longer the period, the brighter the star. Cepheids, named for the prototype in Cepheus. Once you have that relation, a Cepheid is a standard lamp: measure how fast it winks, infer how bright it truly is, compare with how faint it looks, and you have a distance. Hubble used those lamps to show that the spirals sat far outside the Milky Way. Then he used them again as rungs. Without Leavitt there is no Hubble law. There is only Slipher’s pile, glowing and unnamed.

Hubble and Milton Humason turned the stretch into a law. Plot distance against recession speed and you get a line, sloppy at first, cleaner every decade since:

*v = H_0 × d*

*where v is how fast a distant galaxy recedes, d is its distance, and H_0 — Hubble’s constant — is the slope, the stretch-rate of now.*

Humason had been a mule driver on the mountain before he became the person who could hold a faint galaxy on a slit for hours. The 1929 paper was a handful of points.[^22] The 1930s were Humason going fainter, the line getting longer, the slope refusing to go away. Hubble’s first number was about 500 kilometers per second per megaparsec, wildly high, because the distances were wildly short. The modern fight is not whether there is a slope. The modern fight is whether the slope is nearer 67 or nearer 73.

The number has been fought over for a century and is still being fought over a little. The fight is about the second digit, not about the existence of the line. Galaxies recede. Farther means faster. That is hot.

Say the digits out loud, because the argument is real and it is not a crack in the bang. One camp reads the leftover glow of Chapter 9 through the simple expanding model and gets about 67 kilometers per second per megaparsec.[^21] Another camp climbs Leavitt’s ladder in the nearby universe — Cepheids in hosts that also explode in a particular way — and gets about 73.[^23] Both error bars are small enough that the two numbers do not overlap. That is the Hubble tension.

> **Status as of October 2026: the Hubble tension**
>
> **Measured.** The early-universe route (the leftover glow plus the standard model) gives about 67.2 to 67.4 km/s/Mpc, uncertain by about 0.4 to 0.5.[^21] Baryon acoustic oscillations from DESI, calibrated with the helium-era physics of Chapter 10, give about 68.5 ± 0.6.[^24] The Cepheid–supernova ladder gives about 73.2 ± 0.9,[^23] and a 2026 combination of many local methods gives 73.5 ± 0.8, quoted as about seven standard deviations from the glow’s value.[^25]
>
> **Contested.** A ladder that uses a different kind of star (the tip of the red giant branch) lands near 70.4, with error bars wide enough to touch both camps.[^26] So the live question is whether one rung of the local ladder still hides a systematic error, or whether the simple model needs a new ingredient early or late.
>
> **What would settle it.** Independent distances that use no ladder at all: gravitational-wave “standard sirens,” time delays in lensed quasars, and more host galaxies measured by more than one method. None has yet reached the needed precision. It may mean a rung is still mis-cut. It may mean the simple model is missing a late-time ingredient. It does not mean the raisins have stopped receding. Appendix A8 keeps the algebra. Here: the first digit is a 6 or a 7. The existence of the stretch is not on the table.

The later ruler, the one that extends the ladder past where Cepheids fade, is a kind of exploding star: Type Ia, a white dwarf that goes thermonuclear with a peak brightness you can standardize. Chapter 18 will hire those explosions to show that the stretch is speeding up. Do not steal 1998 from that chapter. This chapter only needs the job description. Cepheids get you to nearby galaxies. Type Ia get you farther. Both are lamps. Both can be wrong in the second digit. Neither is optional if you want a number for *H_0* that is not just a slope on a chalkboard.

A megaparsec is a terrible kitchen length and a perfect cosmic one: about 3.26 million light-years, the distance at which the Hubble law starts to drown out the local shoving. Nearby, galaxies have peculiar velocities — they fall toward neighbors, swing in groups, approach like Andromeda, which is coming toward us at about 110 kilometers per second because the two galaxies are gravitationally bound. The Hubble law is not a traffic ticket issued to every smudge. It is the motion that remains after you leave the bound clump. Plot only the Local Group and you will not find a slope. Plot past a few tens of megaparsecs and the slope sits up. *H_0* in kilometers per second per megaparsec means so many extra kilometers per second of recession for each extra megaparsec of distance. At 70, a galaxy 100 megaparsecs away recedes at about 7,000 kilometers per second.

The redshift itself is not a guess. It is a shift of named lines. Hydrogen’s Lyman-α, the calcium H and K pair Slipher knew, the oxygen lines that light up star-forming gas: you know where they sit at rest, because you can measure them in a lamp. In a distant galaxy they sit redward by the same factor for every line. That common factor is *1 + z*. If the lines disagreed with one another, the stretch would be a fraud. They do not disagree. A single *z* slides the whole fingerprint. A speedometer reading of *v = cz* is only a translation for small *z*. At *z = 1* you are not allowed that translation. You are allowed the scale factor.

What is stretching is not the galaxies themselves, as if someone had put Andromeda on a rack. Bound things — people, planets, solar systems, galaxies — hold together. What stretches is the space between the unbound. Light traveling through that stretch gets its wavelength pulled longer. A photon that left a hydrogen atom as a sharp ultraviolet line arrives here as a tired red one. The amount of stretch is written *1 + z*, where *z* is the redshift. A galaxy at *z = 1* sent its light when the universe was half the scale it is today. A galaxy at *z = 6* is a teenager from when the first big galaxies were still assembling. The leftover glow of Chapter 9 sits at *z* about 1,100. We are looking at the universe when it was about a thousandth of its present size.

Put the scale factor back on the table, because the sentence deserves its own line:

*a = 1 / (1 + z)*

*where a is how large unbound distances were when the light left, compared with today, and z is how much the wavelength has been pulled.*

At *z = 1*, *a = 1/2*: half-size. At *z ≈ 1,100*, *a ≈ 1/1,100*: the leftover glow. Redshift is not a speedometer glued to a galaxy. It is a receipt for how much the dough rose while the photon was in the mail.

![Figure 8. A handful of distant galaxies, cropped so each smudge stays sharp. Almost every one is receding.](Figures/figs/fig08.png)

There is a cruelty hidden in the kindness of the deep field. The most interesting smudges are so far that their light has been in transit for most of the age of the universe. We will never send a reply that arrives while anything there still resembles what we photographed. the night sky is not a room. It is a history of rooms, stacked by how much they have been pulled since the light left.

The James Webb Space Telescope did not invent high redshift. It made it rude. There are galaxies at *z > 10*. That sentence is hot: spectroscopically confirmed objects from when the universe was a few hundred million years old, already shining as galaxies, not as a rumor of gas. The fight — and it is a real fight — is how mature they look. Some of them seem too massive, too dusty, too done for the time on the clock, if you feed certain models of how fast stars should have assembled. That fight is warm. It is a fight about the first factories, not about whether the dough rose. Early galaxies exist. How quickly they put on weight is a measurement in progress, not a loophole in Chapters 9 and 10.

There is a distinction the newspapers keep flattening. Light-travel time is how long the photon was in the mail. Proper distance now is where the sender sits in today’s dough. At *z = 1* the mail took about eight billion years; the sender now sits farther than eight billion light-years because the dough rose in transit. At the leftover glow the mail took 13.8 billion years and the sender’s current seat is about 46 billion light-years. This chapter only needs you not to treat *z* as a speed and not to treat lookback time as a ruler you can lay on the table without the scale factor.

One more detail, because it is beautiful and true. The Hubble law does not pick a center. If every raisin recedes from every other raisin in proportion to distance, then *your* raisin is allowed to write the same law. The slope is a property of the dough, not of a privileged kitchen.

A rough inverse of *H_0* is a Hubble time. If the slope is 70 kilometers per second per megaparsec, *1/H_0* is about 14 billion years. That is not the age, because the stretch-rate has not been constant — matter used to brake, the shove now speeds — but it is the same order as 13.8, which is why a slope could smell an age before the leftover glow gave us a clock. Hubble’s first number implied something like two billion years and was already in a fight with rocks. The second-digit fight of 67 versus 73 is a fight inside a number that finally agrees with the stones.

Appendix A8 writes the redshift into the scale factor and shows how *H_0* is a snapshot of a function *H(t)* that has not been constant. Dark energy, when we get there, is a statement about that function’s later life. The stretch is older than the speeding-up. The speeding-up is a plot twist, not the opening scene.

## 9. A Baby Picture in Microwaves

In 1964, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were trying to make a radio antenna in Holmdel, New Jersey, shut up.[^27] It hissed. The hiss came from every direction. They cleaned out the pigeons. They checked the wiring. The hiss stayed. A few miles and a few conversations away, a Princeton group was looking for exactly that hiss: the leftover heat of a once-hot universe, stretched by expansion into microwaves, cooled to a few degrees above absolute zero.

The Princeton group had names, and they had a theory, and they were a little late. Robert Dicke had built the kind of radiometer that can hear a sky this quiet. Jim Peebles had calculated that a hot, dense past should leave a glow at a few degrees Kelvin. Peter Roll and David Wilkinson were getting ready to look. In 1965 the Holmdel hiss and the Princeton calculation met, and the leftover glow stopped being a prediction. It became a measurement. Penzias and Wilson had not been hunting a bang. They had been hunting a clean antenna. The universe does not care what you were hunting.

They had found the baby picture.

It is not a photograph in the camera sense. You cannot walk outside and see it. You need a radio ear. But it is as real as the Sun, and in some ways ruder, because it is in your kitchen too. A small fraction of the snow on an old analog television was this glow, leaking into the living room, older than any continent.

Why microwaves, not visible light? Because the oven cooled. A blackbody’s peak wavelength stretches with *a*. At last scattering the fog was optical: 3,000 K, a dull red if you could have had eyes, which you could not. Stretch by a thousand and the peak sits near a millimeter, in the microwave, a frequency around 160 gigahertz on the FIRAS curve. That is why Penzias and Wilson heard a hiss on a radio horn and not a glow on a photographic plate. Alpher and Herman had predicted a leftover of a few degrees Kelvin in 1948. They did not have Holmdel. They had the right order of magnitude and a number that waited seventeen years for an antenna that would not shut up.

The temperature is 2.725 kelvin — two and three-quarters degrees above the coldest anything can be.[^28] The spectrum is a blackbody so clean it makes laboratory blackbodies look sloppy. A blackbody is the glow of something in thermal equilibrium, a “we have mixed thoroughly” signature. The early universe was mixed. Then it stretched, and the glow stretched with it, and the mix was frozen into the sky.

COBE, the Cosmic Background Explorer, carried the instrument that made that sentence unkind to rivals. FIRAS — a spectrophotometer built to argue with the sky — found a blackbody at 2.725 K so tight that the error bars sit inside a line on a plot. John Mather’s team had measured the temperature of the universe the way a baker measures an oven: not by mood, by a curve. If you want a hot claim with a decimal, take that one. The leftover glow is not “about three degrees.” It is 2.725, and it is a blackbody, and the departures from that curve are so small they had to be hunted on purpose.

A blackbody this clean is also a veto. Starlight bounced off dust, a fog of leftover starlight, a glow from nearby radio junk: those stories produce the wrong shape. They can make a hiss. They cannot make FIRAS. The curve is the courtroom. The pigeons were a reasonable first suspect. They were not a spectrum.

![Figure 9. The microwave sky as a gray oval. Warmer and cooler patches are labeled in words. This is a measurement, not a snapshot from a camera.](Figures/figs/fig09.png)

The freckles have a date. COBE’s DMR map, in 1992, showed that the sky is not perfectly bland: differences of about one part in 100,000, the first photograph of the seeds Chapter 14 will grow into galaxies. George Smoot’s team had not found continents. They had found the rumple in the dough. WMAP, through the 2000s, turned the rumple into a spectrum of sizes. Planck, with maps in 2013 and a final cosmology release in 2018, sharpened the weather chart until the peaks in that spectrum became a courtroom of their own: how much ordinary matter, how much extra pull, how fast the stretch, how flat the large-scale geometry. Those later maps are how Chapter 11 gets its percentages. This chapter only needs the baby: a blackbody with freckles, photographed on the whole sky.

Why 380,000 years? Before that, the universe was a plasma: nuclei and electrons, loose, swimming in light. Light could not travel far. It kept bouncing off free electrons, the way sunlight has trouble getting out of the middle of the Sun. When the universe cooled enough for electrons to lock onto nuclei and make the first hydrogen atoms, the fog lifted. Photons that last scattered then have been flying in straight-ish lines ever since. We photograph the surface of last scattering the way you photograph a cloud bank from an airplane: you do not see through it. You see the top.

Two nouns get mixed here, and they should not. *Recombination* is the chemistry: electrons locking onto nuclei, the universe becoming electrically neutral for the first time. (The name is a fossil; nothing had combined before. It was combination. The word stuck anyway.) *Last scattering* is the optics: the last time a typical photon bounced off a free electron and then ran. They happen at nearly the same age, because once the electrons are locked, the fog has nothing to bounce off. Nearly is not identical. A few electrons stay free. The last bounce is a little later than the locking, a surface with a thickness, not a pane of glass. Kitchen language: the soup sets, and then the last steam stops hitting the lid. We photograph the lid.

Before the fog lifted, a photon’s mean free path was a kitchen, not a cosmos: bounce, bounce, bounce, like light in milk. After, the mean free path became the size of the observable universe. That is the whole optical trick. We are not looking through milk. We are looking at the last skin of milk, stretched into an oval.

The temperature then was about 3,000 K — a red-hot fog, the color of a stove element, not a microwave. Stretch the wavelengths by *1 + z ≈ 1,100* and 3,000 K becomes 2.725 K. The number is not a coincidence. Temperature of free light scales with *1/a*. Cool the oven by stretching the kitchen.

One large pattern is not a freckle and should not be sold as one. The whole sky is a few thousandths of a degree warmer in one direction and cooler in the opposite direction: a dipole. It corresponds to a motion of about 370 kilometers per second toward Leo, the Sun’s peculiar velocity relative to the leftover glow. We are not at rest in the dough. We orbit the Galaxy; the Galaxy falls toward a crowd; the crowd has a velocity of its own. Maps like Figure 9 have that dipole subtracted, the way you subtract the motion of the train before you argue about the scenery. What remains is the baby picture, not our weather.

The oval is not raw sky. Our own Galaxy shines in microwaves too — dust, electrons spiraling in magnetic fields, a plane of junk across the middle. Planck’s maps are the sky after that junk has been modeled and taken off, a subtraction as necessary as wiping flour off a scale. What you are allowed to call cosmology is the remainder. WMAP, the Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe, carries David Wilkinson’s name because he was one of the people waiting in 1965 and one of the people who spent a career making the wait into a map. He died before the full harvest. The probe did not. The sky does not pause for obituaries.

Inside the freckles there is a sound, almost literally: the plasma, before last scattering, could ring. Overdense patches compressed, bounced, compressed again, a fluid with light as the spring. Those acoustic oscillations froze at 380,000 years into a preferred set of angular sizes on the sky — peaks in a power spectrum. Planck’s 2013 and 2018 papers are, in large part, a reading of those peaks.[^21] This chapter will not steal Chapter 14’s seeds or Chapter 11’s inventory. It will say that the baby picture is not a blur. It is a freeze-frame of a ringing fog, and the ring has been measured.

Those freckles are not decoration. They are the seeds of Chapter 14, written in temperature. A patch that was a little denser then is a little more likely to be a cluster of galaxies now. The baby picture is also a will.

The evenness of the picture is its own unpaid bill. Look at two patches on opposite sides of Figure 9. Their temperatures match to a part in a hundred thousand. In the simple bang of this part, those patches have never been in each other’s past light cones. They have not had time to talk, to mix, to agree. That is the horizon problem. It is a real problem. It is not a reason to throw out the leftover glow. It is a pointer: Part III exists because this sky is too even for a kitchen that never shared a pot. Do not solve it here. Notice that the baby picture both confirms the hot fog and hands you a puzzle the fog, by itself, does not pay.

Hot claim: the leftover glow exists, is a blackbody, is almost the same in every direction, and has freckles at the level of one part in a hundred thousand. Warm claim: those freckles are the fingerprint of slightly uneven density in a hot, expanding plasma. Cold claim: any story of the first 10⁻³² seconds that does not eventually produce this picture is entertainment.

When you look at Figure 9, you are not looking at a place. You are looking at a time. The oval is the entire sky. The entire sky is one age. That is what “the explosion happened everywhere” looks like when it has had 13.8 billion years to cool in public.

## 10. The First Three Minutes

Steven Weinberg gave this interval its fame, and he was right to. Between about a hundredth of a second and a few minutes, the universe did something we can reconstruct with the same nuclear physics we use in the Sun and in weapons labs. Before that, the story gets warmer and then cold. After that, for a long time, not much of nuclear interest happens. The three minutes are a workshop. We still own the tools.

He was not the first to guess that a hot past would cook light nuclei. In 1948 Ralph Alpher and George Gamow published a paper with Hans Bethe added, as a joke, as the middle author, so the names would read α, β, γ — alpha, beta, gamma.[^29] The αβγ paper argued that the early universe could build nuclei by capturing neutrons, and that a leftover glow should still be around. The capture-chain details were wrong in ways that later work had to repair: you cannot climb the whole periodic table in three minutes, because there are no stable lodgings at five or eight nucleons. The helium insight was the seed. The leftover-glow insight was the other seed. Weinberg’s book, decades later, is the workshop with the tools calibrated. Gamow’s joke is how the idea first got a table.

At a hundredth of a second, the temperature is about a hundred billion degrees. No atoms. No nuclei that last. A soup of light, electrons, positrons, neutrinos, and a few leftover nucleons — protons and neutrons — swimming in a glare that would unmake any chemistry you have a word for. The nucleons are outnumbered by photons about a billion to one. That ratio is still written in the sky. It is why the universe is so empty of atoms compared with light, and why we are a footnote in a photon’s memoir. The billion dead twins are the antimatter that did not get a chair. Food, when we get to it, is a later argument about leftovers. The first leftover is this: the soup was unfair, and the unfairness is why there is anyone to eat.

Write the temperature once, as a kitchen clock, so the later ticks have a place to hang.

*T ≈ 10¹¹ K at t ≈ 0.01 s*

*where T is the temperature of the soup and t is time since the hot fog we can reconstruct — not since a waiting room.*

At that glare, electrons and positrons are still being born in pairs from photons and annihilating back. Neutrinos still talk to the rest of the pot. The nucleons are a trace ingredient, a pinch of salt in a vat of light.

Neutrons are slightly heavier than protons. In equilibrium, there are fewer of them. As the soup cools, the reactions that turn protons into neutrons and back slow down. At about one second, neutrinos stop talking to the rest of the soup — they *decouple* — and the neutron-to-proton ratio freezes near one-to-six, then keeps dropping slowly because free neutrons decay. You have about ten minutes before most of the leftover neutrons would be gone. The universe does not give you ten minutes of workshop time. It gives you about three.

The free neutron’s lifetime is about fifteen minutes. That is why the workshop is in a hurry. Leave the nucleons sitting as a warm gas with no oven and the neutrons wander off into protons, electrons, and ghostly leftovers, and there is nothing left to glue into helium. Ten minutes would have wasted most of the inventory. Three minutes spends it. The clock is nuclear, not rhetorical. A kitchen timer on the counter is the same kind of object as this: a half-life with consequences for the recipe.

Decoupling, here, is not a mood. It is a race. The soup is thinning and cooling. The weak reactions that let neutrinos knock nucleons around get slower. When the reaction rate falls behind the stretch rate, the neutrinos leave the conversation, streaming as their own leftover — a cosmic neutrino background we have not photographed as a map, but whose presence is felt in the helium and in the leftover glow’s peaks. One second is the door slam. The neutron fraction at the slam, plus a little decay on the walk to minute three, is the inventory the workshop will be allowed to spend.

At roughly three minutes, the temperature has fallen to a billion degrees. Deuterium — a proton glued to a neutron — can at last stay stuck. Before that, the glare broke deuterium as fast as it formed. This is the deuterium bottleneck, a traffic jam with a clock. Once deuterium survives, helium-4 assembles with almost greedy efficiency. There are no stable nuclei with five or eight nucleons, so the assembly line stalls at helium. A sprinkle of lithium is a leftover rounding error. Almost every neutron that survived is now locked in helium.

*T ≈ 10⁹ K at t ≈ 3 min*

*where that billion-degree tick is the oven setting at which deuterium stops being smashed, and helium can be baked.*

If nearly every leftover neutron is locked in helium-4, and you started with about one neutron per seven nucleons after decay, the mass fraction of helium sits near one quarter.

*Y_p ≈ 0.25*

*where Y_p is the primordial mass fraction of helium-4, counted before stars had a chance to lie.*

Deuterium that escaped the greedy step survives as a sprinkle.

*D/H ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻⁵*

*where D/H is deuterium nuclei per hydrogen nucleus in uncooked gas, a thermostat for how crowded the nucleons were in the pot.*

![Figure 10. Four huge ticks on a single line: 1 second, 3 minutes, 380,000 years, now. Temperature lives under each tick.](Figures/figs/fig10.png)

Then the workshop closes. The soup is still too hot for atoms. It will be too hot for 380,000 years. But the nuclear work is done. The universe has committed, by weight, to about three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium, plus a dust of the rest. Stars will later lie about this. They will burn hydrogen into more helium and brag. The *first* helium is not their brag. It is a fossil.

Weinberg’s afterword, years later, had to make room for inflation and for freckles in the leftover glow. He did not have to take back the three minutes. The three minutes have only gotten sharper. That is what a hot claim feels like when you meet one in the wild.

If you could inventory the ordinary atoms in the universe — not the dark extra pull, not the dark extra shove, the atoms — you would find that about one-quarter of their mass is helium-4. That is an absurd amount of helium to blame on stars.

Stars are helium factories, yes. Our Sun is making helium in its core right now, at a stately, suburban pace. In ten billion years it will have converted a respectable fraction of its hydrogen. It will not have converted a quarter of the hydrogen in the *universe*. There has not been time, and there have not been enough stars. The first stars, which were massive and brief and sloppy, made a mess of carbon and oxygen and the iron in your blood. They did not flood intergalactic space with a quarter-by-mass of helium. Something else did.

Say that as a budget, not as an insult. Take every star that has ever shone in the observable loaf, give it a generous lifetime, let it burn at a generous efficiency, and you still cannot cook a quarter of all baryons into helium. Stars make *metals* — astronomers’ rude word for anything heavier than helium — in interesting amounts. They make extra helium locally, in cores and in winds. They do not set *Y_p*. The sky’s baseline helium is older than the first star. That is why the workshop is not optional, and why Alpher and Gamow, joke author included, were pointing at the right oven even when the recipe was still a mess.

The something else is the workshop, caught in the act.

Astronomers measure primordial helium in the least sophisticated gas they can find: dwarf galaxies that have made almost no stars, clouds that have not had a chance to lie. The number they keep finding sits near a quarter by mass, with a little scatter and a lot of argument about the second digit. Deuterium, which stars destroy more easily than they make, is a second fossil. Too much deuterium and the early workshop was too empty of nucleons. Too little and it was too crowded. The observed sprinkle of deuterium is a thermostat for the nuclear minutes. Lithium is the sulky cousin: the prediction and the measurement still do not quite shake hands. That fight is real and unfinished. It does not cancel the helium.

The practical kitchen for helium is an H II region: a nebula where hot stars have stripped electrons off hydrogen, so the gas glows in lines you can put a spectrometer on. Look in metal-poor dwarf irregulars — galaxies that have barely cooked a periodic table — and the helium lines sit near that quarter by mass. Look at deuterium in high-redshift clouds, uncooked by stars, and you get a few deuterium nuclei per hundred thousand hydrogen nuclei, about *2.5 × 10⁻⁵*. The photon-to-baryon ratio that makes those numbers come out is the same billion-to-one already sitting in the leftover glow: about *10⁹* photons per nucleon. Two fossils, one crowding number. Lithium-7 is the exception that still itches: the workshop predicts two to three times more than the oldest stars seem to hold, or the stars seem to hold two to three times less than the workshop baked. Factor two or three, not a factor of a hundred. Warm, unfinished, not a loophole.

The lithium fight has a name on the stellar side: the Spite plateau, a floor of lithium-7 in the oldest, most metal-poor stars, lower than the three-minute prediction by a factor of two or three. Either the stars ate some after they formed, or the workshop’s lithium-7 channel is still missing a correction, or both. People who work on stellar atmospheres and people who work on nuclear rates have been polite at each other for years. The helium does not care. The deuterium does not care. Lithium is allowed to be the sulky cousin and still sit at the table.

Here is the detail that makes the fossil personal. Every breath you take is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, cooked in stars that died before the Sun was a cloud. The helium in a party balloon is mostly not that. Some of it leaked from Earth’s crust, where uranium and thorium have been throwing helium nuclei like litter for eons. Some of the helium in the *cosmos*, the quarter that is not a party trick, has been helium since before there was a periodic table with more than two honest entries. You cannot hold that helium. You can know it.

Hot claim: the light-element abundances match a hot, expanding workshop[^30] with a known nucleon-to-photon ratio. Warm claim: the remaining lithium tension is a problem for astronomers and nuclear theorists, not a loophole for a cold beginning. Cold claim: any rival origin story that cannot produce this helium is not an origin story. It is a mood.

The leftover glow says the universe was once a mixed, hot fog. The leftover helium says the fog did nuclear work on a three-minute clock. Two fossils, two methods, one past. That is as close as cosmology gets to a courtroom.

## 11. Almost Nothing, Almost Everywhere

Smooth the universe. Take every star, every planet, every wandering rock, every atom in every lung, and spread them until the lumps are gone. You will have, on average, about one atom of ordinary matter in a space the size of a closet — a cubic meter, give or take the fights about the second digit. A good vacuum pump on Earth is proud to do worse.

Insist on the second digit and the closet is a little emptier than the sentence suggests: about 0.2 ordinary atoms per cubic meter, once you weigh the universe properly and give the atoms only their share. “About one” is the kitchen insult. 0.2 is the receipt. Either way, you are not living in the average. You are living in a lump.

That is the catalog’s first insult. We live in a freak clump. The clump is not the rule. The rule is the emptiness.

The second insult is that the atoms are not even the main clump. When astronomers weigh the universe the way you weigh a truck — by how hard it pulls, not by how shiny it is — the atoms are a minority. About five percent of the energy budget is the stuff of chemistry. About a quarter is an extra pull that does not shine. About two-thirds is an extra shove that makes the stretch speed up. Chapters 17 through 19 are the inventory of those insults. Here it is enough to feel the loneliness of the five percent.

Elena weighs a bag of flour on a kitchen scale and trusts the number because the bag pushes down. She does not trust the number by how white the flour looks. Shine is a terrible scale. A bag of powdered sugar and a bag of whole wheat can fool the eye; they cannot fool the spring. Cosmology’s spring is gravity. Rotation speeds in the outskirts of galaxies, the way clusters lens background galaxies, the peaks in the leftover glow: those are pulls. The shine — stars, nebulae, the kitchen table — is the five percent announcing itself with light. Weigh the loaf by pull and the flour is a minority ingredient. Chapter 17 is the extra pull. Chapter 18 is the extra shove. Notice, only, that Elena’s habit is the right one, and that the cosmos does not owe her a bag that matches the photograph.

The bookkeeping has a name. There is a critical density, *ρ_c*, the amount of stuff that would make the large-scale geometry sit on a knife-edge: neither curling closed toward a recollapse nor hinging open so fast that galaxies never find each other.

*ρ_c = 3H_0² / 8πG*

*where ρ_c is that critical density, H_0 is the stretch-rate of now, and G is Newton’s constant — the same G that weighs apples, planets, and Elena’s flour.*

What we actually have is counted in units of that knife-edge:

*Ω = ρ / ρ_c*

*where Ω is a density parameter, a fraction of critical, and the pieces of Ω are allowed to add.*

The leftover glow, plus the stretch, plus the way structure grew, currently split the bill about like this: *Ω_b ≈ 0.05* in ordinary atoms (baryons), *Ω_c ≈ 0.27* in extra pull that does not shine, *Ω_Λ ≈ 0.68* in extra shove. The sum sits near 1, which is why “flat” keeps surviving the maps. Those decimals will move in the fourth digit. They will not swap the story that chemistry is a rounding error with a press agent.

Put a kitchen number on the knife-edge, because “critical density” sounds like a mood and it is a mass. For a Hubble constant near 70 kilometers per second per megaparsec, *ρ_c* is about 9 × 10⁻²⁷ kilograms per cubic meter — a few proton-masses in each empty closet if all of it were atoms, which it is not. Only the baryon slice, the 0.05, gets to be atoms, and that is how you arrive at 0.2 per cubic meter. The rest of the pull does not sit on Elena’s scale as flour. The shove does not sit on it at all. “Flat” means the large-scale geometry is the one in which parallel lines stay parallel and the angles of a cosmic triangle add to 180 degrees. Planck’s maps like that answer. Part III will ask why the pebble sat on the blade. This chapter only weighs the bag.

Radiation is a rounding error *now*: the photon share, Ω_γ, is a few parts in ten thousand. Photons dominated the budget when the universe was young and the wavelengths were short; they lost the argument because each photon got tired as well as diluted. Neutrinos, decoupled at one second, are another small slice — a relic you feel in the helium and in the leftover glow’s peaks, not the extra pull of Chapter 17. The extra pull does not shine and does not, as far as the maps can tell, live in the periodic table. The extra shove does not dilute as matter does. Those two sentences are previews. They are not the chapters.

The 0.05 in Ω_b is not a guess from counting stars. Stars are a terrible census of atoms: most ordinary matter is not in stars. It is in hot gas, warm gas, a thin salt in clusters, a fog between galaxies. The leftover glow’s peaks and the three-minute deuterium thermostat agree on the baryon crowding to a level that would be vanity if they did not use different physics. That agreement is why 0.05 is hot enough to print. Counting kitchens will not get you there.

![Figure 11. A large cube. One black dot. A cubic meter of a smoothed cosmos.](Figures/figs/fig11.png)

Figure 11 is the most dishonest honest picture in the book. The cube is a meter on a side. The dot is an atom. If you drew the cube at the scale of Elena’s kitchen, the next atom of the average cosmos would not be in the house. It would not be on the block. You would need a walk that feels like a mistake. We do not take that walk, because we live in the freak, and the freak is full of dots that stuck. The figure is what you get when you refuse the freak and insist on the climate.

Barrow liked to shock readers with billions, and the billions are still shocking if you let them be specific. The observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across, not because it is 93 billion years old — it is 13.8 — but because the stretch has been pulling the farthest light’s starting points farther while the light was in transit. Inside that sphere there are a few hundred billion galaxies you could, in a generous mood, name. Each galaxy has, give or take, a hundred billion stars. Multiply and you get a number that looks like a lie: on the order of 10²² stars. Then remember the closet with one atom. The stars are the rare weather. The emptiness is the climate.

The 93-billion-light-year sphere is a census volume, not a wall. Ten-to-the-twenty-two is an order of magnitude, not a roll call: a hundred billion here, a hundred billion there, and the product is a 1 with twenty-two zeros and an argument about the first factor of ten. You may add a zero when the deep fields finish arguing about faint galaxies. You will not add emptiness to the stars and make the stars the rule. Figure 11 is the rule. The Milky Way is a rounding error that learned to shine.

Compare the densities so the closet stays honest. Air in Elena’s kitchen is about 2 × 10²⁵ molecules per cubic meter. The space between stars in the Galactic disk is already a vacuum by kitchen standards — maybe a million atoms per cubic meter in the better neighborhoods, far less in the halo. The smoothed cosmos is 0.2. Each step is a humiliation of the last. You cannot see 0.2 atoms per cubic meter. You can only weigh its consequences, or refuse to smooth, and live in the clump.

The clumps nest. A person is a clump in a kitchen. The kitchen is a clump on a planet. The planet is a clump around a star. The star is a clump in a galaxy. The galaxy is a clump in a group; our group is falling toward Virgo; Virgo sits in a larger web. Chapter 14 will grow the web from freckles. Here the point is only inventory: every noun you care about is a place where the average failed, gravity won, and the stretch was told to wait. The 10²² stars are 10²² local victories. They still occupy a negligible fraction of the 93-billion-light-year sphere.

Stars themselves are a later luxury. They are places where gravity won a local argument against the stretch, piled gas until the pile was hot enough to fuse, and then spent millions or billions of years turning hydrogen into the rest of the table. When the more massive ones die, they throw the table into space. Planets are the sediment of that throw. You are the sediment of the sediment, walking around asking where the sediment came from.

The catalog, then, is not a list of objects. It is a list of exceptions. A star is an exception to emptiness. A planet is an exception to the habit stars have of being unfriendly. A kitchen is an exception to the habit planets have of being dead. A book is an exception to the habit kitchens have of being used for casseroles.

The past we can rebuild explains how the exceptions got their raw material. It does not owe the exceptions a destiny. The leftover glow and the leftover helium will still be true if no one ever builds another kitchen. That is not a threat. It is the courtesy of a universe that does not need us in order to have had a first three minutes.
