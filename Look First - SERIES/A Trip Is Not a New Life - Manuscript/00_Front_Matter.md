## How to Read This Book

Every claim in these pages wears a temperature. The difference matters more than any date on a slide.

**Hot — established.** Measured, repeated, and used to predict new measurements. Light to the Moon takes about a second and a third. Light to Mars takes minutes. A Hohmann path between the two planets is a season, not a weekend. Most humans will stay on Earth for any leaving we can actually staff. Lifespan and healthspan are different pots. The sentence that we only use ten percent of the brain is false. A sickle-cell-class gene edit can correct a broken instruction in a lineage of cells that will keep the correction. The last half-century produced real, uneven gains: vaccines, a genome you can read, packets, cheaper compute.

**Warm — working account, real gaps.** A crew around the Moon this decade is a class of mission, not a month. A landing *attempt* in the 2030s is the honest noun for a cabin that still owes a demonstration of the ugly parts. Aging is many clocks, not one fuse. Modest further gains in healthy years this century, in rich-country medicine, if institutions hold. Progress that is more often an S-curve than a ray.

**Cold — speculation sold as inventory.** A city on the Moon or Mars this decade. Leaving as a cleanup. A body-wide rewind. A scheduled 150-year or thousand-year ape. A hidden ninety percent of mind. Last fifty years times two hundred as the furniture of the year 12,000. A required last mind at the end of time.

**Contested — serious people disagree, and evidence alone has not settled it.** Some questions are not waiting on a better instrument. They are waiting on an argument. What consciousness is, and whether a copy of a mind would be the same person, are contested. So are long-range economic scenarios — what a tonne to orbit will cost in 2050 — and social forecasts about who will want to live where, and under what law. On these, this book takes a side and says so. It gives the strongest rival view first. A contested tag is not a cold tag. Cold means the evidence is against a schedule. Contested means reasonable readers, holding the same evidence, still land in different places.

Helium, in this book, is shorthand for a hope held sincerely and sold too early: a scheduled immortality, a downtown by Friday, a spare mind. The people who hope for these things are often the reason the tools get built at all. A hearing aid is not helium. A vaccine is not helium. Those are tools, and tools have invoices. Helium is what a slide asks you to wait for instead of the tool.

A temperature can change. A stack that has flown is hotter than a rendering. A local gene fix that was a paper can become a clinic. A slipped date is not, by itself, a con, and it is not destiny. It is an invoice the slide did not want to show.

On one standing rule. **String theory, if it is named in this book, cannot currently be tested.** It will barely be named. Camps need dirt, delay, and dates. Bodies need clocks you can already count.

A few scenes are stories: a woman in a habitat on Mars, an airlock that sticks, basil under an LED, a radio that answers late, a meeting that tries to fuse three nouns. They are thought experiments with dirt on them. The numbers in them are real. The people are invented.

The main chapters keep the math in the sentence. Numbered relations live in the Appendix. Small raised numbers point to the Notes at the back, where each key factual claim has a source you can open; every note links back to the page you came from. A Claims Guide follows this section, and an Index of key ideas closes the book.

You do not need a physics degree or a medical degree. You need a willingness to let a downtown wait, and to let a miracle come apart into pots.

## This Book Stands Alone

This is Volume 2 of *Look First*. It is a complete book. You are not being assigned homework.

It used to be two later kitchens: a trip that is not a settlement, and a longer life that is not a new body. They are one book now, because they are one invoice. You do not get a new world by boarding, and you do not get a new body by waiting. The Earth you already staff does not get cleaner because someone has a bold plan elsewhere. The hands on the airlock still age after a useful dose.

A longer book already retired a cosmic “now” and treated elsewhere as a *permit*: *The Universe Has No Now*. You do not need it. The permit here is simpler. A world you can weigh. A delay you can time. A date that will slip. A morning you would vote to repeat.

Two chapters in the middle — a crew that does not sleep, and a library of Earth — used to sit in that cosmology book, because they were the far ticket. They sit here now, after the near ticket has been priced. Ice oceans, leftover glow, and a second dictionary stay in the other book.

You can stop here. The airlock will still stick tomorrow. Her hands will still age.

## Author’s Note

Most people meet leaving, and the future of the body, in the wrong order.

First comes a picture. Then a year. Then a city. Then a headline about a gene, a chart that goes up and to the right, and a sentence that says the other ninety percent of the mind is a tank. Earth is treated as a kitchen you can abandon. DNA is treated as a fountain. Time is treated as a fuse someone confident can name.

The people who make those pictures are not, mostly, fools or frauds. Many are engineers and doctors who know the invoices better than I do and have decided that a bold picture is how you get anyone to pay them. Sometimes they are right; a bold picture is part of how the Moon was reached the first time. This book argues with the order, not with the hope.

It runs a different sequence on purpose. It starts with dirt, delay, and dates, because those are what a program actually is. It keeps three nouns apart — *sortie*, *outpost*, *settlement* — because fusing them is how a flyby becomes a school district. It prices the Moon, the vehicle, Mars, the people who stay, and the classroom the renderings skip. Only then does it take up the far ticket: a crew that can wait, and a library you do not pour into a sea you have not tasted. The second half is the body on the same invoice. Lifespan is not healthspan. A gene fix is local. The organ is already in use. Ten thousand years is not a straight line. And on the question of whether a copy of you would be you, the book states the view it defends, gives the best rivals their due, and marks the whole question contested.

John D. Barrow’s short books are still the formal model: brief chapters, no swagger, a clean line between a measurement and an idea. Neighbors in this lane treated a camp as a bill, a biosphere as a clock, a gene as a local object, and a brain as an energy bill. They are used. They are not copied. Where a year will move, the year is written as a *kind of clock*. This kitchen is dated October 2026.

A book like this should succeed if a sequence of recognitions arrives and stays:

that a slipped date is an invoice, not a scandal;  
that the Moon is first because it is close, not because it is a spare Earth;  
that a landable year is not a town, and a large cheap stack is a vehicle;  
that sortie, outpost, and settlement are three different nouns;  
that most of us stay, and leaving is not a cleanup;  
that flesh will not take a century cruise, and a library is not a flag;  
that you look first and seed later;  
that years on a wall clock are not years you would vote to repeat;  
that a gene fix is this tissue, this invoice;  
that the ten-percent sentence is false;  
that the last fifty years were real and are not a method;  
that a copy is, at the least, a second runner, and whether it is you is an argument, not a product;  
and that the world which already has a staff, and the morning which is still worth logging, are the product.

That last recognition is not a counsel against going, and it is not a counsel of despair. Look first. Seed later. The basil still has to live. The joint, if you paid for the joint, is allowed to be the win.
