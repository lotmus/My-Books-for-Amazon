## Chapter 6. The Gap Before Death

Many estate plans assume a clean line: fully able, then dead. Real life can have a long, untidy middle: illness or injury may change what a person can manage. Someone may need physical or communication support while still making their own decisions; someone else may be unable to make particular decisions even with support. The four cases in this chapter happen in that middle. None of them is about a will. All of them are about the documents people mean to sign "later."

### Case 17: The springing power that would not spring

The mother was a retired accountant in San Jose, seventy-six, independent to the point of stubbornness and proud of never having missed a bill in her life. Her son lived nearby and was the obvious person to help if she ever needed it. Her daughter lived in Boston.

When her lawyer suggested a durable power of attorney naming the son as her agent, she hesitated. She trusted him, she said, but did not like the idea of his being able to sign for her while she was perfectly capable of signing for herself. "What if he sells my house while I'm on a cruise?" she asked, only half joking. The lawyer offered a solution: a "springing" power, effective only when two licensed physicians certified in writing that she could no longer manage her financial affairs. California permits exactly that: a power of attorney can take effect on a future event, and the principal can name the people who declare, in writing under penalty of perjury, that the event has happened (Probate Code § 4129). She liked it. It felt like a lock on the door.

Two years later she had a series of small strokes. On some days she was clear and sharp; on others she was confused about the date and could not follow a bank statement. The property tax instalment was due. The long-term care insurance premium was due. A contractor wanted paying for a repair to the roof. The son went to the bank with the power of attorney, and the bank, reasonably, asked for the two certifications the document required.

The neurologist was cautious. On the day of the appointment the mother was lucid, and the neurologist would not certify incapacity in a patient who had just discussed her own medication intelligently. The primary care doctor wanted a formal cognitive assessment first, and the waiting list was six weeks. It was eleven weeks before the son could act.

The property tax penalty was a few hundred dollars. The real loss was the long-term care policy, which lapsed. She had paid premiums for fifteen years so that it would pay for exactly the care she now needed, and for several months it paid nothing while the insurer reviewed a reinstatement that needed medical paperwork she was in no state to provide. The son paid the roofer from his own savings and was never sure whether he was allowed to pay himself back. On her clear days the mother was furious about the delay. On her unclear days she did not understand why her son was asking doctors to call her incompetent. That, more than the money, is what he remembers.

Her lock had been on the wrong door. Her fear was that her son might misuse the power. A springing power does not prevent misuse once it takes effect; it can delay an honest agent at the moment help is most needed. What protects against misuse is accountability, and a power of attorney can build that in: it can require the agent to send monthly statements to a second person, such as the daughter, and name her as successor agent. If the mother had wanted to keep practical control, she could have signed an immediately effective power and kept the original in her own files until it was needed. And if she had insisted on a trigger, she could have named her two children, rather than two doctors with professional reasons for caution, as the people who declare it.

California's Uniform Statutory Form Power of Attorney (§ 4401), signed and acknowledged before a notary, is widely recognized by banks. Many banks also have their own forms and may accept them faster. The time to ask is before you need it.

German advisers give the same warning about a *Vorsorgevollmacht* made conditional on future incapacity: banks and others cannot easily check whether the condition has happened. The usual German advice is to make the power effective immediately and control the risk through the choice of agent and an internal agreement about when it will be used.

!> **The takeaway.** A springing power protects you from the person you trust, at the moment you most need them. If you trust your agent, make the power effective now and add a second pair of eyes; if you do not trust them, choose someone else.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 17).

**What to do.**

- Read your power of attorney and find the sentence that says when it takes effect. If it springs, ask who decides and how fast they can.
- If you trust the agent, make the power effective now and require statements to be shared with a second person.
- Name a successor agent in case the first cannot act.
- Ask your bank and your long-term care insurer in writing whether they will accept your power.

### Case 18: No power at all

*The stroke still comes. This time, two signed forms give his partner authority before the ambulance is needed.*

**As it happened.** The man in this case was sixty-one, a self-employed electrician in Bakersfield with a van, two part-time employees, and a reputation for turning up when he said he would. He had lived for twelve years with his partner, a dental receptionist, in a house he owned. Both had been married before, and neither saw a reason to do it again. When she once mentioned a power of attorney, he said, "I'm sixty-one, I'm fine," and changed the subject.

On a Thursday in February he had a severe stroke at a job site.

He could not speak for himself and had named no one to speak for him. When a patient has no directive and no agent, hospitals turn to a surrogate, someone close to the patient who can decide in the patient's interest, and they look at who knows the patient's values. An unmarried partner of twelve years may well be accepted, but it is not guaranteed, and when relatives disagree, hospitals are cautious. His brother flew in from Arizona, having not seen him in years, and assumed that as next of kin he was in charge. He disagreed with the partner about almost everything: the rehabilitation facility, a feeding tube, whether to move him closer to Arizona. The ethics committee met twice. The woman who had shared his life for twelve years found herself pleading with strangers to listen to her.

The money was worse. Without a power of attorney, nobody could sign for him. His partner could not reach the business account to pay the employees or suppliers, cash clients' checks, deal with the van lease, or talk to the mortgage lender. The only route to authority was a conservatorship: a court proceeding with a petition, notice to relatives, a court investigator, a hearing, often a bond, then inventories, accountings, and ongoing supervision, all of it public. His brother filed a competing petition. The court appointed the partner in the end, after months, and the fees came out of the electrician's money. The employees left. He recovered partially after five months, walking with a cane and speaking slowly. The business did not recover at all. The court costs, the investigator, the bond, two sets of lawyers, and the lost business came to more than he earned in a year.

**As it could have gone.** Same man, same stroke. But on an ordinary Saturday the year before, at his partner's insistence, he had spent an afternoon at the kitchen table with two forms.

The first was California's free statutory advance health care directive. He completed it in about half an hour. He named his partner as healthcare agent and his sister as backup. In his own words, he added two wishes: he wanted rehabilitation if there was a real chance he could work again, and he did not want to be moved from home. Two qualified adult witnesses signed it; a notary could have done so instead. He gave copies to his partner and doctor and registered it with the Secretary of State's Advance Health Care Directive Registry. When the stroke came, the hospital called the person named on the form. His brother flew in and disagreed. The hospital showed him the directive. The ethics committee did not meet.

The second was a durable power of attorney for finances, effective immediately, naming his partner, with his sister as successor agent, and expressly covering his business accounts. A lawyer reviewed it because he owned a business and a house, and a notary acknowledged it. With it went a one-page business continuity note on the Family List: his key clients and suppliers, where the business banking was, and the name of another electrician who could finish open jobs. On the Monday after the stroke his partner paid the employees, phoned the clients, and arranged for the open jobs to be finished. There was no court, and no competing petition.

**The difference, line by line.**

- **Who the hospital asked:** a disputed surrogate, or the agent he named.
- **Who could pay the bills:** nobody, for months, then a court-appointed conservator; or his partner, on the Monday.
- **What it cost:** more than a year's earnings and his business; or an afternoon and a lawyer's review.
- **What his brother learned:** that the partner was "only" a partner; or, from the directive, whom his brother trusted.

The defaults are built around spouses, registered partners, parents, and children. In California, registered domestic partners have the same rights and duties as spouses under state law (Family Code § 297.5), including priority to be appointed conservator and inheritance without a will. Marrying or registering is a decision about a relationship, not about paperwork, and nobody should do it only to fill a gap two documents can fill. But if the person who knows you best is not on the default list, only paper puts them there. If he had died, his partner would also have inherited nothing without a will (as in Case 12).

**In Germany,** the gap is the same. Without a *Vorsorgevollmacht*, the court appoints a legal guardian (*Betreuer*) for an adult who cannot manage their affairs (BGB § 1814). The court must consider the person's wishes and close relationships, and a long-term partner is often appointed, but it takes time and the court supervises. The 2023 spouses' emergency right (Case 19) does not help unmarried partners at all.

**What to do.**

- Sign a durable power of attorney and an advance health care directive this month, whatever your age.
- If you are not married or registered, name your partner explicitly as agent in both, and say in the directive that you want them involved in your care.
- If you are self-employed, make sure the power covers your business accounts, and put a one-page business continuity note in your Family List.
- Tell any relative who might object, before anything happens, whom you have chosen.

### Case 19: The German spouse who could decide, but only for a while

**The situation.** The husband and the wife had been married for twenty-six years and lived in a terraced house in Nuremberg, which they owned jointly. The husband was an IT administrator and a keen weekend cyclist; the wife taught primary school. They had talked, vaguely, about signing powers of attorney for each other, but then they read in the newspaper that since 2023 spouses in Germany could represent each other automatically in a medical emergency. That seemed to settle it.

In April, the husband was hit by a car on a country road. He had a serious head injury and spent three weeks in an induced coma, followed by months of rehabilitation in which he could communicate only with difficulty.

**What the family assumed.** The wife assumed she could handle everything as his wife. Their adult daughter assumed the same.

**What actually happened, and why.** At first, the newspaper was right. Since 1 January 2023, BGB § 1358 has given a spouse a right to represent the other in health matters when the other is unconscious or too ill to handle them. The wife could consent to or refuse examinations and treatment, sign the hospital and rehabilitation contracts, and receive information from the doctors, who were released from confidentiality toward her for that purpose.

But the statute is deliberately narrow. A doctor must confirm in writing that the patient meets the conditions and that incapacity is the reason. The spouses must not be living apart. The right does not apply if the patient refused it or already appointed someone else. It covers health matters and closely related contracts, not money, a bank account, a house, or car insurance. It ends no later than six months after the doctor confirms incapacity.

The husband's rehabilitation took nine months. After six, the wife's statutory authority ended. Meanwhile, the husband's salary account was in his sole name. His share of the house could not be mortgaged or dealt with without him. The car insurance claim against the driver needed his signature or someone with authority to act for him.

The wife had to apply to the guardianship court for appointment as the husband's legal guardian (*Betreuerin*). The court heard the husband as far as possible, obtained a medical report, and appointed the wife, but with the supervision a guardianship brings: an inventory of the husband's assets, annual reports, and court approval for certain transactions involving the house. The wife described it later as being audited for loving her husband.

**What would have prevented it.** A *Vorsorgevollmacht* from each spouse naming the other, with their daughter as backup, covering both health and finances, plus a *Patientenverfügung* stating the husband's own treatment wishes.

German law requires a power of attorney to be in writing and to name certain serious medical decisions expressly if the agent is to make them (§ 1820). A handwritten power is valid, but banks often prefer their own form or a notarized one. For real estate, the land registry generally requires a notarized or officially certified power. You can register it with the Central Register of Lasting Powers of Attorney (*Zentrales Vorsorgeregister*), run by the Federal Chamber of Notaries, so a guardianship court can find it before appointing anyone. If a registered power names the spouse, the court will generally not appoint a guardian because the power makes one unnecessary. Registration does not make a power valid; it makes it findable, which, in a hospital corridor, is the same thing.

**What it cost.** Less money than in Case 18, because the German guardianship court appointed the wife quickly and the fees were modest. The cost was time and supervision. For a year the wife kept careful records of what she spent from the husband's account, filed reports, and waited for court approval before she could refinance the house when interest rates fell. The husband recovered enough to sign a *Vorsorgevollmacht* himself the following spring; the guardianship was lifted soon afterwards. Both of them now carry, in their wallets, a small card saying that a power of attorney exists and where it is.

Her experience shows why the 2023 spousal right is only an emergency bridge. It covers health decisions briefly, not the long-term management of money or property. For those, choose an agent in a power of attorney or rely on a court-appointed guardian.

**The California version.** California has no equivalent automatic spouse power for finances. Without a power of attorney, a spouse may need a conservatorship to manage the other spouse's separate property, and some transactions involving community property can also require court involvement when one spouse cannot sign. The cure is the same: sign the documents while you can.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 19).

**What to do.**

- If you are married in Germany, do not rely on the spouse's emergency right. Each of you should sign a *Vorsorgevollmacht* covering health and money.
- If you own property, have the powers notarized or officially certified so the land registry will accept them.
- Register the powers in the Central Register of Lasting Powers of Attorney.
- Carry a card in your wallet saying that a power exists and where the original is.

### Case 20: "No heroic measures"

*A family meeting in a small room off a hospital ward in Santa Barbara. The mother, eighty-four, a retired librarian, sharp-witted and allergic to fuss, broke her hip in her kitchen three days ago and has developed pneumonia. She is feverish and confused. Years ago, after watching a friend die slowly in intensive care, she filled in an advance directive naming her daughter as her healthcare agent. In the space for instructions she wrote one sentence: "I do not want heroic measures." She showed it to her daughter, said "You know what I mean," and put it in a drawer. Her son has flown in from Denver. He knew the directive existed. He had never read it. The hospital doctor has the directive in front of her.*

**The doctor:** Your mother's pneumonia is treatable. I'd like to start antibiotics today. I also need to know what you want if her breathing gets worse: whether we should put in a breathing tube and move her to intensive care.

**The son:** She said no heroic measures. That's in writing. I think she'd want to go home and be comfortable.

**The daughter:** Antibiotics aren't heroic. Heroic is a ventilator. Resuscitation.

**The son:** Who says? She's eighty-four with a broken hip.

**The doctor:** Can I ask what she meant by "heroic"? Medically, the phrase doesn't have a fixed meaning. For some people it means only resuscitation. For others it includes a feeding tube, or any stay in intensive care, even a short one with a good chance of recovery.

**The daughter:** She said I'd know what she meant.

**The doctor:** Do you?

*A long pause.*

**The daughter:** I think she meant she didn't want to be kept alive on machines with no hope. I don't think she meant she should die of pneumonia.

**The doctor:** You're her agent. The law asks you to decide according to her instructions and, where they're unclear, according to her best interest as you understand her values. I'll support whatever you decide on that basis.

**The daughter:** Start the antibiotics. No breathing tube.

The mother recovered from the pneumonia but not fully from the hip fracture, and spent her last eight months in a nursing home, which she had always said she dreaded. The son believed his sister had overridden their mother's wishes and said so, often: to the staff, to the ethics committee, to his own children. The daughter felt she had been guessing, and still does. Years later she said the hardest part was not the decision but the loneliness of it, and that she wished she had asked one question over lunch while she still could: "If you got pneumonia and couldn't tell us what you wanted, would you want antibiotics?"

Legally, the directive worked: it named an agent, and the doctors deferred to her. Practically, it failed, because a slogan feels complete to the person who writes it and hands the people left behind only the shorthand, not the picture.

**What would have prevented it.** A directive that described situations, not slogans:

- "If I have an infection and the doctors think I am likely to return to roughly my current condition, treat it, including with antibiotics."
- "If I am unlikely to recover the ability to recognize my family and communicate, I do not want to be kept alive by a ventilator or a feeding tube. Keep me comfortable."
- "I do not want resuscitation (CPR) if my heart stops."
- "If a cure is no longer the goal, I would rather be at home, with hospice care, than in a hospital."

California's statutory advance directive form has room for exactly these choices, and Appendix F helps you put your wishes into ordinary language first. For people who are seriously ill there is also a POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment), completed with a doctor: a medical order that emergency staff can follow immediately. Given the mother's serious illness and likely treatment decisions, her doctor might well have suggested one.

And the twenty minutes. The mother could have sat with both children, the directive on the table, and talked through a few questions: What makes a day worth living for me, and what would make it not? How do I feel about hospitals, and about dying at home? If I could not recognize you, would I want to be kept alive? Is there any treatment I would always want, or never want? Written down in her words, the answers would have been worth more than the form.

**In Germany,** courts have said this plainly. The Federal Court of Justice held that a general phrase such as "no life-prolonging measures" is not, on its own, specific enough to bind an agent as a *Patientenverfügung*. The directive must refer to particular treatments or situations (BGH, decision of 6 July 2016, XII ZB 61/16). German law defines an advance directive as written decisions about specific examinations, treatments, or interventions for a future situation in which the person cannot consent (BGB § 1827). If the directive does not fit the situation, the agent must work out the patient's treatment wishes or presumed wishes from earlier statements, beliefs, and values. In both countries, a vague document does not free the agent to decide as they like. It leaves them the harder job of reconstructing what the patient would have wanted.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 20).

**What to do.**

- Rewrite any one-line directive as situations and treatments: infections, ventilation, feeding tubes, resuscitation, place of care.
- Add one sentence about your values, such as whether being at home matters more to you than living longer.
- Go through it with your agent and with everyone else likely to be at the bedside, at the same time.
- If you are frail or seriously ill, ask your doctor about a POLST in California, or a detailed *Patientenverfügung* in Germany.

## Chapter 7. Families, Borders, and the Digital Locker

### Case 21: The Berliner Testament and the second marriage

In 1990, after a friend's sudden death, a Hamburg couple sat down at their kitchen table one evening and wrote a joint will. They had married in 1968 and raised a daughter and a son in a flat in Eimsbüttel that they had eventually bought. The wife wrote the will out by hand, as German law allows for a joint will between spouses, and both of them signed it. They followed a familiar pattern: each named the other as sole heir; after the survivor's death, their children would inherit in equal shares. Being careful people, they deposited it in official custody at the local court. Being people at a kitchen table, they did not ask what would happen if one of them married again.

She died in 2015, and he inherited everything, as planned. He was lonely for several years. Then, at a dance evening for retired people, he met a widow from Lübeck with a good laugh and strong opinions about football. They married in 2020, when he was seventy-seven and happier than he had been in a decade. He wanted to look after her, so he wrote a new handwritten will leaving her the flat they lived in, and the rest to his children. He told her the flat would be hers. He died in 2025.

The children assumed, if they thought about it at all, that the old joint will had been replaced. It had not, and in German law it could not be.

Only spouses and registered partners can make a joint will (BGB § 2265). In the classic form, each spouse names the other as heir and the children as final heirs after the second death. This is the *Berliner Testament*. Unless the will shows a different intent, the law generally treats the children as heirs of the surviving spouse to the combined estate (§ 2269). Some provisions are reciprocal (*wechselbezüglich*): each spouse made a provision in reliance on the other's. The law presumes that certain provisions are reciprocal (§ 2270). While both spouses are alive, either can revoke a reciprocal provision, but only through a formal, notarized declaration to the other. Once the first spouse dies, the survivor's right to revoke ends (§ 2271).

So his appointment of the children as final heirs became binding on the day his first wife died. His new will, as far as it gave the flat to his second wife, conflicted with that binding provision and was ineffective. The children inherited everything, including the flat. His widow, excluded from the estate, had a compulsory share claim against them, half of her statutory share (§ 2303): a money claim, not a right to stay in her home. The children, who liked her, let her stay for a year. Then they sold the flat.

It could have been worse. Had he tried to get around the binding will by giving her the flat during his lifetime, the children might have been able to reclaim it after his death. That remedy applies if the gift was meant to undermine their inheritance and he had no legitimate lifetime interest in making it. German courts apply BGB § 2287 to binding joint wills by analogy. There had also been a narrow way out after the first death, which he never knew about. A survivor may escape the binding provisions by disclaiming what the first spouse left them, generally within six weeks of learning of the inheritance (§ 1944). But that means giving up the inheritance. In the grief of 2015, he had no idea he would marry again.

The fix belonged in 1990, and it was a sentence long. A change clause (*Änderungsvorbehalt*) could have let the survivor alter the provisions for the final heirs, either entirely or within limits. For example: "The survivor may give a new spouse the right to live in our home, or up to a quarter of the estate." A remarriage clause (*Wiederverheiratungsklausel*), common in notarial joint wills, could have set out what the children receive if the survivor remarries. One protects the new spouse; the other protects the children. A couple can use both. German notaries routinely ask about remarriage when drafting a joint will. Even after 2015, he could have reached a notarized agreement with his children, perhaps giving his second wife a lifelong right to live in the flat in return for something. But that required their goodwill. He never asked.

None of this makes the *Berliner Testament* a bad choice. It is simple, it protects the surviving spouse, it postpones the children's inheritance until both parents have died, and its binding effect protects the children from a survivor who might be talked into changing everything. The trouble comes only when life after the first death turns out differently from what the couple imagined at the kitchen table, which, over twenty or thirty years, it often does.

California has no joint will of this type with automatic binding effect. Couples who want the survivor bound usually use a joint revocable trust that becomes irrevocable as to the first spouse's share at the first death, or a contract to make wills, and both need careful drafting. The question is the same in both countries.

!> **The takeaway.** Before you sign a joint will, answer one question in writing: after the first of us dies, may the survivor change the plan? A change clause, a remarriage clause, or a deliberate "no changes" costs a sentence. Silence decides it for you, and in Germany silence usually means "no."

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 21).

**What to do.**

- Before you sign a joint will in Germany, decide whether the survivor may change the final heirs, and write it into the will.
- Decide what should happen if the survivor remarries, and put that in the will too.
- If you are a survivor bound by a joint will, see a notary before you remarry or make any large gift.
- If you are marrying someone bound by an old joint will, ask to see it before you give up your own home.

### Case 22: Two passports, two homes, one estate

**The situation.** The engineer was born in Würzburg, studied in Munich, and at twenty-eight took a job in Silicon Valley that was supposed to last two years. It lasted thirty. He married a US citizen; they had a son and later divorced. He became a US citizen and kept his German one. He owned a house in Sunnyvale, which he put into a California revocable trust on his lawyer's advice, naming his son as beneficiary.

At sixty-two he retired to Bavaria. He bought a small flat in Bamberg, opened a German bank account, and started a quieter life. He met a widow, who became his partner. After a few years, without seeing a lawyer, he wrote a handwritten German will leaving "everything I own in Germany" to his partner, by name. The house in Sunnyvale, which he rented out, stayed in the California trust for the son.

He died in Bamberg at seventy.

**What the family assumed.** The son assumed the trust governed the California house, and that the German will had nothing to do with him. His partner assumed the German will governed the German property. Both thought the two plans sat side by side, like two passports in a drawer.

**What actually happened, and why.** For deaths since 17 August 2015, the European Succession Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 650/2012) applies in Germany and most EU states. Its default rule is that the law of the country where the deceased had their *habitual residence* at death governs the whole succession, wherever the assets are (Article 21). The engineer had lived in Bamberg for eight years. In this example, the circumstances of his life there made Germany his habitual residence. Citizenship and property in another country did not, by themselves, make that country's law govern the succession.

The regulation allows a person to choose instead the law of a country of which they are a national (Article 22), and a person with more than one nationality may choose any of them. As a US citizen, he could have chosen the law of a US state, but only if he did so expressly or clearly in a will. His handwritten will said nothing about choice of law.

Under German law, the son was the engineer's only child. The will made his partner heir to "everything I own in Germany." A German court would have to interpret what that meant for the rest of the estate. Did he mean his partner as sole heir and the California house as a separate gift to his son, or did he mean his partner to inherit only some things? Either way, the son had a child's compulsory-share claim: half of his statutory share. German law calculates that claim against the whole estate. Advisers would also need to consider whether the California house counted toward the claim. He could have removed it from his revocable trust at any time before he died, so the house might count as part of his estate or as a gift added back under the ten-year rule.

Meanwhile, in California, the trust did exactly what it said: the successor trustee transferred the house to the son, without probate, under California law, which knows nothing of German compulsory shares.

So the two systems pulled in different directions. His partner, as heir in Germany, faced a compulsory-share claim from the son that took the California house into account. The son, who had received the house in California, might be asked to account for it in Germany. German inheritance tax applies to worldwide estates when the deceased was resident there (ErbStG § 2). US federal estate tax can apply to American citizens wherever they live. A tax treaty between the two countries helps prevent the same estate from being taxed twice. There were translators, apostilles, a European Certificate of Succession for the German assets, and lawyers in two time zones. His partner and the son, who had met twice and got on well, spent two years being polite to each other through counsel.

**What would have prevented it.** One coordinated plan, made with advice in both countries.

- **A will with an express choice of law.** Depending on what he wanted, he could have considered choosing German law or the law of his nationality under the EU Succession Regulation. Because the United States has multiple state legal systems, Article 36 and US conflict rules matter in identifying which state law would apply; he should not assume that writing "California law" would settle that question. Even a valid foreign-law choice may not displace German compulsory-share rules in every case. Germany's highest civil court has held that applying a chosen foreign law that leaves a child with no fixed minimum share can violate German public policy when the case is closely connected with Germany. A cross-border succession lawyer would need to advise whether a choice suited him and would hold.
- **Documents that referred to each other.** The German will could have said, "My property in the United States passes under my revocable trust dated [date]; this will governs everything else." The trust could have referred to the German will.
- **A tax review.** Moving from California to Germany changes the tax picture entirely. A cross-border tax adviser should see the plan.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 22).

**The European Certificate of Succession.** One document did help. A European Certificate of Succession, issued by the German probate court, proves who the heirs are and what powers they have, and takes effect in every member state bound by the Succession Regulation without any further procedure (Article 69). It does not reach Denmark or Ireland, which are not bound by the regulation, and it has no automatic effect in the United States. For the German and other European assets it was the key that opened every bank. For the Sunnyvale side, the son's trust certificate did the same job in California. Two keys, two systems, one estate.

**What to do.**

- Within a year of moving to another country, have your whole plan reviewed by advisers who know both countries.
- If you have two nationalities, ask whether a choice of law in your will would protect what you want.
- Make each country's documents refer to the other's, so that no document claims more than it should.
- Ask a cross-border tax adviser how each country will tax your estate, before you die rather than after.

### Case 23: The phone nobody could open

*A conversation at a desk in a Pasadena flat, ten days after a funeral. The woman who lived here was a freelance graphic designer, forty-six, single, organized in her work and chaotic in her drawers. She died suddenly of a ruptured aneurysm. Her simple will left everything to her sister and named her as executor. The sister sits at the desk with the designer's phone and laptop. The designer's closest friend, who shared a studio with her for years, has come to help.*

**The sister:** I'm the executor. I've got the court papers. So I should be able to get into her accounts.

**The friend:** Have you tried the phone?

**The sister:** Four times. It says to wait an hour before I try again.

**The friend:** Stop. Some phones erase themselves after too many wrong guesses, or lock for good. Everything's on there: the client files, ten years of photographs, the invoices. And the children's book she was writing.

**The sister:** Then I'll ask the phone company. Or the manufacturer. I have a legal right.

**The friend:** She told me once that not even the manufacturer can open these. That's the point of the encryption.

**The sister:** What about her email? I can reset the password.

**The friend:** And the reset code goes where?

**The sister:** To her phone.

*Silence.*

**The friend:** Did she ever set up one of those legacy-contact things? Where you name someone who gets your data?

**The sister:** I don't think she knew they existed. Her passwords were "in her head, and in the phone somewhere." She used to say that as a joke.

**The friend:** Then let's work out what we can rebuild. I have every draft of the book she sent me for comments. Her clients have copies of her invoices. Half the family photos are on other people's phones.

Over the next months, the sister learned the shape of what the law can and cannot do. California's version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (Probate Code §§ 870 and following) gives an executor a framework for requesting a deceased user's digital assets, in an order of priority. First, any instruction the user gave through the provider's own online tool, such as a legacy-contact or inactive-account setting, governs, and overrides a contrary instruction in a will if the tool lets the user change it (§ 873). Next come instructions in a will, trust, or power of attorney. If neither exists, the provider's terms of service apply. The content of emails and messages must be disclosed to the executor only if the user consented or a court directs it (§ 876); otherwise the executor may get only a catalog, the who-sent-what-when without the content (§ 877).

The designer had set up no provider tool, and her will said nothing about digital assets. So the sister could ask, with court papers, for catalogs and some account data, and a few providers released data after a long request process. Others offered only to close the account. And no statute can decrypt a device. The phone and laptop became, in her words, "bricks with sentimental value." She recovered the invoices from clients, photographs from friends, and the book from the friend's inbox. Most of ten years was gone. Meanwhile, charges for software, cloud storage, and a domain name kept accruing until she found them line by line in months of card statements.

**What would have prevented it.** Most of it needs no lawyer and can be set up in an evening.

- **Legacy and inactive-account settings** on her email, cloud storage, and main social accounts, naming her sister. In California, these settings outrank the will.
- **A password manager with emergency access** for her sister, holding the passwords, the device passcodes, and the recovery codes for two-factor authentication. If she did not respond within a waiting period she chose, her sister would get in.
- **A sealed note** with the phone and laptop passcodes and the password manager's master password, kept with the Family List or with her lawyer.
- **A backup** of the business files to a second place her sister could reach without the phone.
- **One clause in the will** consenting to disclosure of her electronic communications to her executor, which under § 876 would have required providers to disclose content.
- **A list of recurring charges** in the Family List.

Part Six and Chapter 38 describe how to set this up without writing passwords on a sticky note.

**In Germany,** the law is in one respect more generous to heirs. In 2018, the Federal Court of Justice held that a social-network account passes to heirs like any other contract (judgment of 12 July 2018, III ZR 183/17). The heirs can therefore demand access to the full account, including private messages. Subscriptions pass to heirs, too, and they may cancel them. But even a German heir with a court judgment cannot unlock an encrypted phone without its passcode. The law can give your heirs the right to ask. Only you can give them the key.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 23).

**What to do.**

- Tonight, ask yourself one question: if I died before morning, could anyone unlock my phone and my laptop? If the answer is no, start there.
- Set up emergency access in a password manager for one trusted person, and store your device passcodes and two-factor recovery codes there.
- Add a clause to your will consenting to disclosure of your electronic communications to your executor.
- Put a list of your recurring charges in the Family List, so the subscriptions can be found and stopped.

### Case 24: The video will

At sixty, he married a widow who brought a grown daughter into the marriage. His wife died two years before his own diagnosis. So by the time a retired postal worker in Ventura was told, at seventy-three, that he had pancreatic cancer and a few months, the person who drove him to chemotherapy, cooked for him, and slept on his sofa on the bad nights was his stepdaughter. His two sons from his first marriage visited when they could. His will, made long before the second marriage, left everything to the sons.

As he grew weaker he wanted to change that. He was too tired to write and, he admitted, a little afraid of lawyers. One afternoon he asked his elder son to set up his phone on a stand. Looking into the lens, he spoke clearly for twenty minutes. He gave his full name and the date. He said this was his last will: the house to his stepdaughter, "who has been a daughter to me," and his savings divided between his two sons. He talked about his life, about his wife, about how proud he was of all three of them. Both sons were in the room, and both nodded. The stepdaughter cried. The video went to the cloud. He died five weeks later.

Everyone assumed the video was his will. It had his face, his voice, his name, and the date. His two sons had heard and agreed with every word. It was not a will.

California requires a will to be in writing. A formal will must be signed by the testator, or by someone else in his presence and at his direction, and witnessed by at least two people (Probate Code § 6110); a holographic will needs the signature and material provisions in his own handwriting (§ 6111). California courts can rescue a will that was not properly witnessed if there is clear and convincing evidence that the testator intended the writing as his will, but the rescue still needs a writing. A video is neither writing nor handwriting. Germany would have reached the same result: an ordinary will must be notarial or entirely handwritten and signed (BGB §§ 2231, 2247), and even the German emergency wills require a written record.

So the old will governed. Everything went to the two sons, and the stepdaughter, never adopted, received nothing. The sons who had nodded at every word found after the funeral that they remembered different things. The elder son thought their father had been confused by the medication. The younger son thought they should honor the video. The elder son's wife thought the stepdaughter had "got to him." In the end the younger son gave the stepdaughter part of his own share, and the elder son did not. The three of them, who had sat together in that room, do not speak.

Five weeks was enough. If he could still hold a pen, three sentences would have done: "This is my will. I give my house to my stepdaughter [full name]. I give everything else to my sons [full names] in equal shares." Signed and dated, those words would make a valid holographic will in California. Written entirely by hand and signed, they would also make a valid will in Germany. If he could not write, California law allows another person to sign a typed will at his direction and in his presence (§ 6110), before two witnesses who receive nothing. A hospice social worker or mobile notary service may be able to find witnesses quickly. Many lawyers will visit a home or hospital.

Then the video would have been exactly right, as a companion. Recorded after the signing, with the stepdaughter out of the room, it could have shown that he knew the date, his family, his property, and what he was doing. He could have explained why the woman who had nursed him should receive the house and expressed his love for all three children. That evidence could help answer a later challenge, though it would not guarantee an outcome. A lawyer may advise against recording if the person appears confused; a poor video can hurt a case as much as a good one can help. Chapters 29 and 31 explain how to record, store, and share one.

Germany does have a narrow emergency-will procedure involving three witnesses (BGB § 2250). It applies only when death seems so imminent that a notary cannot be reached, and the witnesses must record the declaration in writing. The will automatically lapses if the person survives three months (§ 2252). It is a lifeboat, not a ferry.

!> **The takeaway.** A video can show who you are. It cannot, on its own, decide who gets your house. Write three sentences by hand, or sign in front of two witnesses, and then press record.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 24).

**What to do.**

- If you are seriously ill and want to change your will, call a lawyer today and ask for a home or hospital visit.
- If you cannot wait, write three sentences by hand, sign them, and date them.
- Record your video after the will is signed, without the main beneficiary in the room, and say in it that a written will exists.
- If you want a stepchild to inherit, name them in that written will.

### Case 25: The seed phrase in the will

**The situation.** The man in this case was a software developer in Palo Alto, thirty-nine, married, with a two-year-old daughter. Over a decade he had built up a substantial holding of cryptocurrency, held in a self-custody wallet: no bank, no exchange, just a hardware device and a twenty-four-word recovery phrase that could restore access to the funds from anywhere in the world.

When he and his wife saw a lawyer about wills after their daughter was born, the developer was proud of his foresight. He brought a typed draft of the clause he wanted, which included the twenty-four words. "That way my wife will have them," he said. "And if anything happens to both of us, the executor will."

**What the family assumed.** The developer assumed a will was a private, secure document, like a letter in a safe.

**What actually happened, and why.** Luckily, this case ended well, because the lawyer read the draft.

A will is not private once it is used. In California, a will must be delivered to the court after death (Probate Code § 8200), and a will that goes through probate becomes part of a court file that is generally open to the public. Anyone who read the developer's will could, in principle, have used the recovery phrase to move the cryptocurrency before his executor did. Transactions of that kind are usually irreversible.

The will would also have passed through several hands before it was ever signed: the lawyer's assistant, the paralegal who formatted it, the two witnesses, the copy shop, the scanned file on the law firm's server. Any of them could have photographed the twenty-four words.

The lawyer removed the phrase from the draft and explained why. The developer's next idea was a sticky note in his desk drawer. The lawyer discouraged that too, gently.

**What would have prevented the risk.** A plan that separates two things: who should receive the cryptocurrency, and how to get to it.

- **The will (or trust) says who.** "I give all my digital assets, including cryptocurrency, to my wife [full name], or if she does not survive me, to the trustee of the trust for my daughter." No keys, no phrases.
- **A separate, sealed set of instructions says how.** It explains what the assets are, where the hardware device is, how the recovery phrase is stored, and step-by-step how to recover the wallet. It is stored securely: a safe deposit box, a fireproof safe at home, or split into parts held by different trusted people, depending on the amount and the family. A password manager's emergency access can hold some of the pieces.
- **A test.** While the developer was alive, he and his wife could test the instructions by recovering the wallet with a small amount, so that any gap in the explanation showed up while he was there to fix it.
- **A trustee who understands it.** If the daughter might inherit, the trustee must be able to handle the assets, or have authority to hire someone who can.

Chapter 24 describes the general pattern, and it is not limited to cryptocurrency: online banking passwords, PINs, safe combinations, and server passwords all work like keys, and none of them belongs in a will. Secrets go in secure storage; documents that may become public say only who gets what. The law treats the coins like a bank balance: in Germany everything the deceased owned passes to the heirs as a whole at the moment of death (BGB § 1922), and in California a digital asset is part of the estate the executor must collect. The difference is practical. A bank can be ordered to pay; a wallet without its key cannot.

**The German version.** A German will is opened by the probate court after death, and the people concerned are informed of its contents. It is not usually a public file in the American sense, but many people read it: heirs, court staff, sometimes banks, and anyone those people show it to. The same rule applies: no passwords, no PINs, no recovery phrases in the will.

**What a good access plan contains.** A sound plan for digital assets has four parts. An inventory: what you hold, where, and roughly how much. A location note: where the devices, backups, and recovery materials are kept. A method: step-by-step instructions, written for someone less technical than you, for recovering access. And a person: someone who knows the plan exists and can reach the recovery instructions when authorized, without having unilateral access to spend the assets while you are alive. Many people split recovery materials so that no single person or place holds everything; others use a professional custodian. The right choice depends on the amount, the family, and how comfortable the heirs are with the technology. Whatever the choice, the will should mention that digital assets exist and say who receives them, so that the executor knows to look.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 25).

**The family conversation.** His wife was not a developer. When the lawyer asked whether she would know what to do with a hardware wallet if the developer died, she laughed and said she did not know what one looked like. That was the most useful moment of the meeting. A plan for digital assets that only its author understands is not a plan. The developer spent a Sunday afternoon showing her the device, the storage, and the instructions, and rewrote the instructions twice until she could follow them on her own. He described it as the most humbling code review of his career.

**What to do.**

- Search your will drafts and any notes kept with them for passwords, PINs, or recovery phrases, and remove them.
- In the will, say who receives your digital assets. Keep how to reach them in a separate, sealed set of instructions.
- Test the instructions with the person who will use them, by recovering a small amount together.
- If a trustee may have to manage digital assets for a child, make sure the trust lets them hire expert help.
