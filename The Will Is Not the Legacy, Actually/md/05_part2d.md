## Chapter 6. The Gap Before Death

### Case 16: The springing power that would not spring

**The facts.** Carol, in San Jose, signed a financial power of attorney naming her son, Eric, as agent. Wary of giving him power too soon, she had it drafted to take effect only if two doctors certified in writing that she could no longer manage her affairs. Two years later she had a series of small strokes. Some days she was clear; some days she was not.

**The move.** A "springing" power, which feels safer.

**What governed.** The document governed, exactly as written. Eric needed two physicians' written certifications. Carol's neurologist was cautious about certifying incapacity in a patient whose condition fluctuated; the second doctor wanted a fresh assessment. Meanwhile, Carol's property tax bill came due, an insurance premium lapsed, and her bank, perfectly reasonably, would not let Eric act without the certifications. It took eleven weeks.

**What should have been signed.** An immediately effective durable power of attorney, if Carol trusted Eric, which she did, with a second agent named as backup. If she did not want Eric acting while she was well, the honest answer was to choose a different agent, not to make the trusted one jump through hoops at the worst moment. California law includes a Uniform Statutory Form Power of Attorney (Probate Code § 4401), signed and acknowledged before a notary, which banks are used to seeing.

**The lesson.** Springing powers sound safe and work slowly. The real protection is choosing the right agent.

### Case 17: No power at all

**The facts.** Robert, sixty-one, a self-employed electrician in Bakersfield, had a severe stroke. He was unmarried. His partner of twelve years, Denise, lived with him. He had no power of attorney and no healthcare directive.

**The move.** None. "I'm sixty-one, I'm fine."

**What governed.** Without a power of attorney, nobody had legal authority to handle Robert's bank accounts, his business, or his house. Denise could not pay his business suppliers or sign for his clients. For medical decisions, hospitals look for a surrogate when a patient has no directive, and an unmarried partner may or may not be accepted depending on the circumstances; Robert's brother, who lived in another state and had not seen Robert in years, flew in and disagreed with Denise about almost everything. For the money, Denise ultimately had to petition the court for a conservatorship, a public court process with filings, an investigation, court supervision, and fees. Robert recovered partially after five months. His business did not.

**What should have been signed.** A durable power of attorney for finances naming Denise, and an advance health care directive naming Denise as his healthcare agent. In California, an advance health care directive is valid if signed and either notarised or witnessed by two qualified adults, and it can be registered voluntarily with the California Secretary of State's registry. Cost of all this: an afternoon.

**The lesson.** Unmarried couples are invisible to many default rules. Paper makes them visible.

### Case 18: The German spouse who could decide, but only for a while

**The facts.** Martin and Petra, married and living in Nuremberg, had never signed a *Vorsorgevollmacht* (a lasting power of attorney) because they had read in the newspaper that since 2023 spouses could represent each other automatically. When Martin fell into a coma after a cycling accident, Petra did indeed make decisions about his treatment.

**The move.** Rely on the statutory spouse's emergency right.

**What governed.** Since 1 January 2023, BGB § 1358 gives a spouse a right to represent the other in health matters if the other is unconscious or too ill to handle them: consenting to or refusing examinations and treatment, signing hospital contracts, and similar. But the statute is narrow. It covers health matters, not money. It requires a doctor's written confirmation. It does not apply if the spouses live apart. And it ends, at the latest, six months after the time the doctor certifies. Martin's coma became a long rehabilitation. After six months, Petra's authority expired. She had never had authority over his bank accounts or his share of their house. She had to apply to the guardianship court for appointment as Martin's legal guardian (*Betreuerin*), with the court process and supervision that brings.

**What should have been signed.** A *Vorsorgevollmacht* from each spouse naming the other, covering health and finances, with a *Patientenverfügung* (advance directive) stating treatment wishes. German law requires a power of attorney to be in writing and to mention certain serious medical decisions expressly if the agent is to make them (§ 1820). Notarising it is not always required, but it is wise if the power must be used for real estate, and banks often prefer their own form or a notarised one. The power can be registered in the Central Register of Lasting Powers of Attorney (*Zentrales Vorsorgeregister*), also run by the Federal Chamber of Notaries, so that courts can find it.

**The lesson.** The spouse's emergency right is a bridge, not a road. Sign the real document.

### Case 19: "No heroic measures"

**The facts.** Edith, eighty-four, in Santa Barbara, had an advance directive that said only: "I do not want heroic measures." After a fall, she developed pneumonia. Her daughter, Joan, her agent, believed antibiotics were ordinary care. Her son, Tim, believed his mother had meant no hospital at all. The doctors asked who decided.

**The move.** A short, well-meaning sentence.

**What governed.** The directive named Joan as agent, so Joan had authority. But the instruction was vague, and Tim, who disagreed, made the hospital's ethics committee his second home for a week. Joan felt she was guessing, and still does.

**What should have been signed.** A directive that says, in plain words, what Edith wanted in specific situations: antibiotics for an infection if recovery to her present state was likely; no ventilator; no resuscitation; comfort care at home if a cure was not the goal. California's statutory form includes spaces for exactly this. Appendix F has a worksheet that translates your wishes into ordinary language before you sit down with the form. And a conversation, with both children in the room, so nobody has to interpret a sentence alone.

**The lesson.** Write what you mean, in situations, not slogans. Then say it out loud to the people who will have to carry it out.

## Chapter 7. Families, Borders, and the Digital Locker

### Case 20: The Berliner Testament and the second marriage

**The facts.** Hans and Ingrid, married in Hamburg, wrote a joint will (*gemeinschaftliches Testament*) in 1990 naming each other as sole heir, and their two children as heirs after the second death. Ingrid died in 2015. In 2020, Hans remarried. He wanted to leave something to his new wife, Monika, and made a new will leaving her his flat.

**The move.** Write a new will. It was his money now, wasn't it?

**What governed.** Only spouses (and registered partners) can make a joint will in Germany (BGB § 2265). The classic form, where each spouse names the other as heir and the children as final heirs, is called a *Berliner Testament*, and § 2269 says that in case of doubt the children are heirs of the surviving spouse for the whole combined estate. In joint wills, provisions made in reliance on each other's provisions can become binding once the first spouse dies, and the survivor generally cannot revoke them later unless the will reserved that right. Hans's will in Monika's favour conflicted with the binding provisions for the children and was, to that extent, ineffective. Monika received nothing from it.

**What should have been signed.** The 1990 will could have included an express clause allowing the survivor to change the final heirs, or to leave limited gifts to a new spouse. Couples who write a *Berliner Testament* should ask a notary specifically what happens if the survivor remarries, and decide it then.

**The lesson.** A joint will is a promise. Before you make it, decide whether the survivor should be free to change it.

### Case 21: Two passports, two homes, one estate

**The facts.** Stefan, a German and American dual citizen, spent his working life in California and retired to Bavaria, where he lived for the last eight years. He had a California revocable trust holding his California house and a German handwritten will for "everything in Germany." The two documents named different heirs for his residual property.

**The move.** One plan per country, written years apart.

**What governed.** For deaths since 17 August 2015, the European Succession Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 650/2012) applies in Germany. By default, the law of the country where the deceased had their *habitual residence* at death governs the whole succession (Article 21). Stefan's habitual residence was Germany, so German courts applied German law, including the compulsory share rules, to his estate as they saw it. The regulation also lets a person choose the law of a country of which they are a national (Article 22), including, for a dual national, either nationality. Stefan had not made such a choice. In California, his trust governed the house, because the trust owned it. The two systems did not agree on everything, and the German compulsory share claims of a child Stefan had largely left out were calculated on assets that included the California property.

**What should have been signed.** One coordinated plan made with advice in both countries, with an express choice of law in the will if that suited Stefan's goals, and documents that referred to each other rather than contradicting each other.

**The lesson.** If you live between countries, your plan has to be bilingual in more than one sense. Chapter 39 says more.

### Case 22: The phone nobody could open

**The facts.** Claire, a freelance designer in Pasadena, died suddenly at forty-six. Her will left everything to her sister, Nina. Claire's photos, client files, invoices, and passwords were all on her phone and her laptop, both encrypted, both locked. Her email used two-factor authentication with codes sent to the phone.

**The move.** None. "It's all in my phone."

**What governed.** Nina was the executor and could show the court's letters to service providers. California's version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (Probate Code §§ 870 and following) gives personal representatives a framework for requesting access to a deceased user's digital assets, and lets users give directions through a provider's own online tool, which overrides contrary instructions in a will if the tool lets the user modify their choice. But the law cannot decrypt a device. Without the passcodes, the phone and laptop were bricks with sentimental value. Some providers released account data after a lengthy request process; others offered only to close the account.

**What should have been signed.** Legacy-contact or inactive-account settings turned on in the major accounts Claire used, naming Nina. A password manager with emergency access for Nina. The device passcodes in the sealed part of the Family List. Part Six and Chapter 38 explain how to do this without writing passwords on a sticky note.

**The lesson.** The law can give your executor the right to ask. Only you can give them the key.

### Case 23: The video will

**The facts.** Raymond, in Ventura, was diagnosed with a terminal illness. Too tired to write, he recorded a twenty-minute video on his phone. Looking into the lens, he said clearly that this was his last will, that his house should go to his stepdaughter, Alicia, who had cared for him, and that his savings should be divided between his two biological sons. He dated it, said his full name, and saved it to the cloud. His sons were present. Everyone agreed it was what he wanted.

**The move.** Use the phone, because it was there and a pen felt like too much.

**What governed.** California requires a will to be in writing. A formal will must be signed and witnessed by two people (Probate Code § 6110); a holographic will needs the signature and material provisions in the testator's handwriting (§ 6111). A video is neither a writing nor handwriting. It was not a will. Raymond's earlier will, made before he married Alicia's mother, left everything to his two sons. Alicia, who had never been adopted by Raymond, received nothing under it. The sons, who had agreed with every word in the video while their father was alive, discovered after his death that they had different memories of what he had meant.

Germany would have reached the same result: a will in ordinary form must be either notarial or entirely handwritten and signed (BGB §§ 2231, 2247). Even the German emergency will before three witnesses (§ 2250) requires a written record. A video alone is not one of the permitted forms.

**What should have been signed.** A short handwritten will, even three sentences long, if Raymond could still hold a pen; otherwise a typed will signed (or signed for him at his direction and in his presence, which § 6110 permits) in front of two disinterested witnesses. The video would then have been a valuable companion: evidence of his wishes, his capacity, and his reasons. Chapter 31 explains how to use video alongside a will without mistaking it for one.

**The lesson.** Record your face for the people you love. Record your wishes on paper for the court.

### Case 24: The seed phrase in the will

**The facts.** Oliver, a software developer in Palo Alto, held a substantial amount of cryptocurrency in a self-custody wallet. Proud of his foresight, he wrote the twenty-four-word recovery phrase into his will, so his heirs would have it.

**The move.** Put the key where the heirs would find it.

**What governed.** A will that goes through probate in California is filed with the court and can become a public record. Anyone who reads Oliver's will could, in principle, take the cryptocurrency before his executor did. The will also passed through several hands (two witnesses, a paralegal, a copy shop) before it was signed. Luckily, Oliver's lawyer spotted the phrase in the draft and removed it. Oliver's next plan was a sticky note inside the desk drawer, which his lawyer also discouraged.

**What should have been signed.** A will that says who receives the cryptocurrency, plus a separate, sealed set of instructions for accessing it, stored securely and tested with a small amount while Oliver could still correct mistakes. Chapter 24 has the general pattern for keeping secrets out of documents that may become public.

**The lesson.** Wills can become public. Keys must not.
