# Part Ten: What This Book Cannot Know

*Educational guide, not legal advice.*

## Chapter 39. One Honest Chapter About Where You Live

This book has used California and Germany as its worked examples. It cannot know the rules where you live, and fifty thin state summaries would be worse than none, because they would be out of date before the ink dried and wrong in the details that matter. Instead, this chapter tells you what kinds of rules to ask about, so that you arrive at a lawyer's or notary's office knowing the right questions.

### The two big families of law

**Common-law systems** (most of the United States, England and Wales, Ireland, Canada outside Quebec, Australia, New Zealand, and others) generally give you wide freedom to leave property to whom you choose, use executors and probate courts to administer estates, and recognize trusts. Protection for spouses comes through rules such as community property, elective shares, or family-provision claims, which vary widely.

**Civil-law systems** (Germany, most of continental Europe, Latin America, Quebec, and others) generally have the estate pass directly to heirs, use notaries heavily, and protect close family through compulsory or "forced" shares. Germany's version, the *Pflichtteil*, gives disinherited descendants, parents, and spouses a money claim worth half their statutory share (BGB § 2303). Some other countries reserve a fixed portion of the estate itself for children, which a will cannot take away.

### Questions to ask, wherever you are

1. **What forms of will are valid here?** Witnessed, handwritten, notarial, statutory form, electronic? How many witnesses, and who may not witness?
2. **Do my children or spouse have rights I cannot override?** Forced shares, community property, elective shares, family provision?
3. **What happens if I die without a will?** Who inherits, in what shares? Does my partner inherit if we are not married?
4. **Is there probate, and what does it cost?** Is there a simplified procedure for small estates, and what is the current limit?
5. **What passes outside the will?** Beneficiary designations, joint accounts, transfer-on-death arrangements, trusts?
6. **What taxes apply at death or on gifts?** Estate tax (charged on the estate), inheritance tax (charged on the heir), gift tax, capital gains on inherited property? What are the current allowances?
7. **How do I appoint someone to act for me if I lose capacity?** Financial and healthcare powers: form, witnessing, notarization, registration?
8. **Is there a will register?** How do I make sure my will is found?
9. **What are the rules on digital assets?** Does the law give my executor access, and do platforms' own tools override my will?
10. **Do I have connections to another country?** List every citizenship, place of residence or domicile, country where property is located, and country where close heirs live. Which law may apply to each part of my plan?
11. **Will my family be able to use the handoff?** What languages do they read? Do key names match across documents? Which papers need a translation, and will it need to be certified or authenticated?

### If you live between countries

People with two passports, two homes, or heirs scattered across borders need a plan that works in every relevant place. Three things to know:

- **Within the EU,** the Succession Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 650/2012) applies to deaths on or after 17 August 2015. By default, the law of your habitual residence at death governs your whole estate (Article 21). You can instead choose the law of a country of which you are a national, including either of two nationalities (Article 22), by making that choice expressly in a will. If that country has multiple legal systems, Article 36 and its internal conflict rules determine which system applies; do not assume that naming a state or region is enough. The regulation also created a European Certificate of Succession, which heirs can use to prove their status in other EU member states.
- **The United States is not part of that system.** American states apply their own conflict-of-law rules, which often send real estate to the law of the place where it is located. A German court and a California court may each look at the same estate and apply different laws to different parts of it (Case 22).
- **Taxes do not coordinate automatically.** Germany taxes inheritances based on the residence of the deceased and of the heir, and the United States taxes the estates of its citizens wherever they live. A tax treaty may reduce double taxation; ask advisers in both countries.

Make a one-page map of the connections: citizenships, residence or domicile, property locations, where heirs live, and every will, trust, power, and beneficiary form already in place. Give the same map and copies of the relevant documents to each adviser, and ask them to coordinate rather than review one document in isolation. Ask which documents need translation, whether it must be certified or authenticated, and where the originals should be kept. Keep a plain-language translation for family beside the legal original; have a qualified local adviser confirm the legal document's effect in each place.

For important legal decisions, waivers, and instructions, use an independent qualified interpreter when needed. Do not rely only on a relative who benefits from the decision to interpret it. Ask family members what language and format they prefer; do not assume one person speaks for everyone or that a cultural practice is shared by all. A handoff should be understood by the people expected to use it.

### When the rules change

Thresholds and allowances change. California's small-estate limits are updated every three years. United States estate and gift tax figures are adjusted annually and sometimes rewritten by Congress. German inheritance tax allowances are a recurring subject of political debate and could change with any reform. Everything in this book was checked in October 2026. Check again before you rely on any figure.

### The sentence to take to the professional

"I have read a general book. Here is my Family List, my draft wishes, and my questions. Please tell me what is wrong with them for where I live."

A professional who hears that sentence will be delighted. You will have saved them an hour, and yourself a fee for it.

## Chapter 40. If You Live Elsewhere: Topic by Topic

California and Germany are this book's worked examples, not its limits. The underlying planning questions travel; the legal rules do not. This chapter takes the six topics where the rules differ most and gives, for each, a few general points for other US states, the United Kingdom (Great Britain—England, Scotland, and Wales—and Northern Ireland), Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. It is deliberately short and general. Laws change, and details depend on your state, province, territory, or (in the UK) which nation's legal system applies. Before you act, check the official guidance listed under "Other countries" in [Appendix N](#Appendix N. Sources), or ask a local professional.

### A practical handoff for any jurisdiction

1. Write down the exact place whose law may apply: state, province or territory, and (for the UK) nation. Include where you live, where each property is, and where the other people involved live.
2. Use the official court or government source for that place to check signing, probate, tax, healthcare, and deadline rules. The Appendix N sources are starting points; follow their links to the local authority that handles the specific issue.
3. Before signing or transferring anything, ask a local estate professional to review cross-border property, a blended family, a business, a trust, a minor or disabled beneficiary, or an unequal gift. Bring the will, beneficiary forms, deeds, and contracts together.

Do not treat a country-level summary as a form or a substitute for local advice. Even official guidance can cover only one part of the process: for example, a will guide may not answer tax, pension, property-title, or incapacity questions.

### Making a valid will, elsewhere

- **Other US states.** Each state sets its own formalities. A typed will signed before two adult witnesses is the common pattern, often with an optional notarized "self-proving" affidavit that makes probate easier. Some states also accept a will written entirely in your own hand without witnesses; many do not. Louisiana, with its civil-law tradition, has its own forms.
- **United Kingdom.** In England and Wales a will must be in writing and signed in the presence of two adult witnesses who are both present when you sign, and who then sign in your presence. Leave nothing to a witness or a witness's spouse or civil partner: the will stays valid, but that gift fails. Marriage or civil partnership generally cancels an earlier will, and divorce changes how it works, so review the will after either. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules.
- **Canada.** Wills and estates are provincial and territorial law. Quebec, with a civil-law system, recognizes a notarial will, a will in your own handwriting, and a will made before witnesses; a notarial will there takes effect without probate. The other provinces and territories each have their own wills statute.
- **Australia.** Each state and territory has its own succession law and formalities. A typed will signed before witnesses is the usual form; check your state's rules before relying on anything else.
- **New Zealand.** A will must be in writing and signed with at least two witnesses present together; each witness signs in the will-maker's presence. Check the current Wills Act requirements and get local advice before relying on a will made elsewhere.

### Family members you cannot leave out, elsewhere

- **Other US states.** Nine states are community property states: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. In most other states a surviving spouse can claim a share of the estate even against the will, often called an elective share; the size and the assets it reaches vary. Adult children generally have no fixed share.
- **United Kingdom.** England and Wales allow wide freedom, but certain family members and dependants can ask a court for reasonable financial provision from the estate (Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975). Scotland is different: a spouse, civil partner, and children have "legal rights" to part of the movable estate (money, investments, belongings, but not land or buildings) whatever the will says.
- **Canada.** Provinces allow certain dependants, and in some provinces adult children, to ask a court to vary a will that does not provide for them adequately. Who can claim, and on what grounds, differs by province.
- **Australia.** Every state and territory allows certain family members to apply to court for "family provision" from an estate. Who is eligible and the time limit vary; in New South Wales, for example, an application must generally be made within twelve months of the death.
- **New Zealand.** Eligible family members may seek provision under the Family Protection Act, and a person promised a reward for services may have a claim under the Law Reform (Testamentary Promises) Act. A will may therefore be challenged even when it was properly signed.

### What passes outside the will, elsewhere

The principle in [Chapter 12](#Chapter 12. Beneficiary Designations) holds everywhere: beneficiary designations and joint ownership usually beat the will. The details differ.

- **Other US states.** Payable-on-death bank accounts and transfer-on-death securities accounts are available across the country. Transfer-on-death deeds for real estate exist in a majority of states but not all; see the section that follows.
- **United Kingdom.** There is no transfer-on-death deed. Property owned as joint tenants passes to the surviving owner; property owned as tenants in common passes under each owner's will. Pension schemes usually ask you to say who should receive death benefits; ask your scheme how it treats that nomination.
- **Canada.** Most provinces let you name beneficiaries on life insurance and registered retirement and savings plans, so those pass outside the will; Quebec has its own rules. Jointly held property with a right of survivorship passes to the survivor, but adding an adult child to an account or title can raise questions about whether the child was meant to keep it.
- **Australia.** Superannuation does not automatically form part of your estate. Funds offer binding nominations, which the fund must follow (some lapse after three years unless renewed), and non-binding nominations, which only guide the trustee. Name your estate (your "legal personal representative") if you want super to follow your will.
- **New Zealand.** Check each account, insurance policy, and retirement scheme's rules for death benefits and nominations; do not assume the will controls them. Confirm with the provider whether a benefit goes to the estate or directly to a named person.

### Other US states: transfer-on-death deeds and enhanced life estate deeds

California's rules are not everyone's rules. According to the national commission that drafts uniform state laws, about thirty American jurisdictions, including California and the District of Columbia, now allow real estate to pass by a transfer-on-death deed, sometimes called a beneficiary deed, and more states consider it every year. Many of them use the commission's uniform act; others, such as California, Missouri, Ohio, and Arizona, have their own versions. The forms, witness rules, recording deadlines, and effects on creditors and public benefits differ from state to state.

A handful of states, mainly in the South, also use the enhanced life estate deed. It looks like a life estate deed, but the owner keeps the power to sell, mortgage, or give away the property during life without the remainder beneficiary's consent, so the beneficiary's interest vanishes if the owner changes course. Vermont has a statute for it, with a form. In Texas the state's Medicaid handbook recognizes these deeds and treats the whole property as the owner's resource. Michigan and Florida courts deal with them regularly. West Virginia, often listed among these states, has a transfer-on-death deed statute, but no official West Virginia source recognizing enhanced life estate deeds could be found when this book was checked, so treat any such claim with caution.

If you own property in another state, do not assume that California's form, or any form found online, works there. Check that state's own statutes, or ask a lawyer licensed there, before you sign anything. If you own property in several states, a living trust may be simpler than a different deed for each.

### Powers of attorney and healthcare, elsewhere

- **Other US states.** Every state has its own durable power of attorney and advance directive (living will and healthcare agent) rules, and many publish free statutory forms. A document made in one state is often honored in another, but signing a new one after a move is the safer course.
- **United Kingdom.** In England and Wales, a lasting power of attorney comes in two types: property and financial affairs, and health and welfare. Each must be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian before it can be used, and a health and welfare one only works when you lack capacity to decide for yourself. Registration takes weeks, so do it while you are well. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own systems.
- **Canada.** Powers of attorney and healthcare directives are provincial. Ontario, for example, uses a continuing power of attorney for property and a separate power of attorney for personal care, each signed before two witnesses.
- **Australia.** Enduring powers of attorney, guardianship appointments, and advance care directives are made under state and territory law, with different names and forms in each.
- **New Zealand.** An enduring power of attorney can cover property or personal care and welfare. These are separate appointments; use New Zealand forms and guidance, and make them while you have capacity.

### Taxes at death, elsewhere

- **Other US states.** The federal estate tax affects only very large estates: for deaths in 2026, those above a basic exclusion of 15 million dollars per person ([Article 9 of the sample will](#Article 9: Taxes and debts)). A minority of states levy their own estate or inheritance tax, sometimes on much smaller estates; check your state's revenue department.
- **United Kingdom.** Inheritance tax is normally not due if the estate is below the 325,000-pound threshold, or if everything above it goes to a spouse, civil partner, or charity. The standard rate on the part above the threshold is 40 percent. Gifts made in the years before death, and a home left to children or grandchildren, have their own rules.
- **Canada.** There is no separate inheritance tax. Instead, a person who dies is treated as having sold their capital property just before death, and any gain is reported on their final income tax return, unless the property passes to a spouse or common-law partner or another exception applies.
- **Australia.** There is no inheritance tax or death duty. Capital gains tax can apply when an heir later sells inherited assets, and superannuation death benefits have their own tax treatment, which depends on who receives them.
- **New Zealand.** There is no inheritance tax, but the deceased may have a final income-tax return and the estate may have tax filing obligations. Ask Inland Revenue or a local tax adviser about the estate's income and assets.

### Probate and estate administration, elsewhere

- **Other US states.** Every state has probate courts and a simplified small-estate procedure, with its own value limits. Some states have adopted simpler, less supervised probate; others remain closer to California's model.
- **United Kingdom.** In England and Wales the executor applies for a grant of probate (or letters of administration if there is no will). Scotland's equivalent is called confirmation.
- **Canada.** Executors in most provinces apply to court for probate (the name varies), and provinces charge their own probate fees or taxes. In Quebec a notarial will needs no probate.
- **Australia.** Probate is granted by the Supreme Court of each state or territory; small estates can sometimes be collected without it, depending on the institution and the state.
- **New Zealand.** The executor usually applies to the High Court for probate; an administrator applies for letters of administration when there is no executor able to act. A grant may not be required for an estate below the current small-estate threshold, so check the official guidance.

### If you are moving

Moving between any two of these places, or between US states, is one of the life events in [Appendix A](#Immediately after any of these events). Take your will, powers, and beneficiary list to a professional in the new place within the first year.

### What you leave is an invitation

A complete legacy does not require a perfect archive, a family that agrees, or a project finished on schedule. It needs a handoff people can use: clear legal documents, a current Family List, and someone who knows where to find them. If you want to leave more than instructions, offer a story, a skill from work, a recipe, a page of fiction, or a joke. Say whether your family may preserve it, adapt it, update it, or let it rest.

A will directs the legal transfer of property. The rest is an invitation. Give the people who come after you enough to carry what matters, and enough freedom to make their own lives with it.
