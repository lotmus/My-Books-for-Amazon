# Part Seven: Your Voice on Record

*Educational guide, not legal advice. Video and audio are wonderful ways to leave yourself behind. They are not wills, in California, in Germany, or anywhere this book knows about. Chapter 31 explains why, and how to make them work alongside the paper.*

## Chapter 26. Why Record at All

For almost all of human history, the dead were silent. You could keep their letters, if they wrote any, and their photographs, once photographs existed. Their voices went with them. A grandchild born a year after you died would know your face from a picture and your personality from stories, filtered through whoever told them, which is roughly like knowing a symphony from a description of it by someone who once heard it on the radio.

That has changed, and almost nobody has noticed. The phone in your pocket is a better film studio than anything a television station owned forty years ago. The webcam on your laptop records in high definition. Storage costs almost nothing. Anyone, at any age and in any state of health, can now leave hours of themselves on record: talking, laughing, explaining, apologizing, telling the story about the goat for the forty-first time, but this time with the timing intact.

Most people do not, for three reasons. They feel silly talking to a lens. They do not know what to say. And they assume there will be time later. This part deals with all three.

### What recordings do that paper cannot

- **They keep your voice.** Not just the sound of it but the rhythm, the hesitations, the way you say a particular name.
- **They keep your face doing things.** A photograph shows you at one moment. A video shows you thinking.
- **They keep your certainty or doubt.** On paper, "I think it was the right decision" is ambiguous. On video, the people who know you will know exactly how sure you were.
- **They keep things nobody would write down.** The recipe that depends on "about this much." How to hold a fishing rod. The bedtime song with your own wrong words.
- **They keep you available to people who have not met you yet.** Great-grandchildren. A son-in-law's future children. The child of a child who is now six.

### The two styles

This part covers two styles of recording, which you can mix freely.

The **diary style** is ongoing: short clips recorded over months or years, about ordinary days, thoughts, memories, and the news as you saw it. Over time the clips become a portrait. Nobody reads a diary for its plot. They read it to spend time with the writer.

The **testament style** is deliberate: messages recorded for a specific purpose, often for specific people, sometimes to be opened at a specific time. A message for a child's wedding. An explanation of a decision. A goodbye. A statement of your values. This style borrows the word "testament" from the old meaning, a statement of what you believe and want, not from the legal document. Chapter 31 is firm about that distinction.

### Three archives, and what each one did

The legal cases in Part Two show what goes wrong when paper is missing. Recordings fail and succeed in their own ways, and the ways are just as predictable. Three short examples, invented but typical:

**The grandmother who recorded too much, and indexed nothing.** For six years a widow in a farming town recorded a clip most Sunday mornings: the weather, the hens, the news, a memory of her own mother. When she died there were more than two hundred files on an old laptop, named by the camera, in no order. Her son opened the first one, heard her say good morning, and closed the laptop for a year. When he finally went through them, his daughter sat beside him for courage. They found the best clips by accident: one where she explains how she decided to marry, another where she sings the hymn from her own wedding, and one where she says, quite cheerfully, what she wants at her funeral. A one-page index would have let him find those three on the first evening. (Chapter 30 shows how.)

**The father who recorded one message, and made it count.** A man in his sixties, not a talker, recorded a single nine-minute video for each of his two children after a heart scare. He wrote five lines of notes first, sat by the kitchen window, said his name and the date and who the message was for, told one story about each child that they had never heard from his side, apologized for one specific thing, and stopped. He gave each child a drive with their message on it, sealed in an envelope, the same week, and lived another eleven years. His daughter says she has watched hers perhaps twenty times, and that she knows the exact place where his voice changes.

**The mother whose recordings were locked away.** A woman recorded beautiful messages for her grandchildren on her phone and kept them nowhere else. The phone was encrypted, and nobody knew the passcode. The messages exist, somewhere, and nobody will ever see them (Case 23 is the same story about a business).

The differences between the three are not talent or equipment. They are an index, a plan for each message, and a second copy in someone else's hands. The rest of this part is about getting those three things right.

## Chapter 27. How to Record: Phone, Webcam, Light, and Sound

You do not need special equipment. You need a device you already own, a quiet room, a window, and the willingness to look slightly foolish for twenty minutes. Here is how to make the recordings good enough that people will actually want to watch them.

![Figure 4. A simple video-diary setup, seen from above: face the light, prop the phone at eye level, sit about an arm's length away in a quiet room.](src/diagrams/fig5_video_diary_setup.png)

### The device

**A phone** is the best camera most people own. Use the rear camera if someone else is filming you, because it is usually better. If you are alone, use the front camera so you can see yourself, or the rear camera with the phone propped up and a test recording first.

**A laptop webcam** is convenient for diary-style clips, because you are already sitting there. The image is usually worse than a phone's, and the microphone usually worse still. It is fine for regular short clips. For an important testament-style message, use the phone.

**A tablet** sits between the two and is easy to prop up.

Whatever you use, check the settings once: record in a common, widely supported format at a sensible resolution. High definition (1080p) is plenty; it looks good and the files stay manageable. Ultra-high resolutions make huge files and add little to a person talking in a chair.

### Steady the camera

Do not hold the phone in your hand while talking to it. A propped phone is better than a wobbling one. A small, inexpensive phone tripod or a stack of books works. Put the lens at eye level or slightly above. A camera looking up your nose is unflattering and, worse, distracting. The stack of books does not need to be photogenic. It is working behind the scenes.

### Horizontal or vertical

Hold or prop the phone horizontally (landscape) for messages that will be watched on televisions and computers, which is how families tend to watch together. Vertical is fine for short diary clips meant for phones. Pick one style for a series, so the clips fit together.

### Light

Light is what makes amateur video look amateur. The easiest good light is a window.

- Sit facing a window, with the camera between you and the window, so daylight falls on your face.
- Do not sit with a window behind you. You will become a silhouette, which suits witness-protection interviews better than family messages.
- Avoid overhead lights alone; they make dark shadows under the eyes.
- If you record in the evening, put a lamp in front of you, slightly to one side, and turn off the overhead light.

### Sound

People will forgive a mediocre picture. They will not forgive bad sound.

- Choose a quiet room. Turn off the television, radio, dishwasher, and anything that hums.
- Rooms with soft furnishings (curtains, carpets, sofas, bookshelves) sound better than kitchens and bathrooms, which echo.
- Keep the device close: an arm's length or a bit more. The further away, the more the room sounds like a cathedral.
- An inexpensive clip-on microphone that plugs into the phone makes a dramatic difference for important messages. It is the single best upgrade.
- Do a ten-second test recording and listen with headphones before the real thing.

### Framing

Frame yourself from mid-chest up, with a little space above your head. Sit slightly off center if you like. Look at the lens, not at the screen, when speaking to someone directly; on a phone, the lens is the small dot near the top, which is not where your own face appears. Put a small sticker beside the lens if it helps you remember.

### Background

Tidy enough not to distract. Personal enough to be yours. A bookshelf, a garden, the workshop, the kitchen table where everything happened. In twenty years, the background will be part of the memory. Someone will say, "That's the old clock."

### Length

- Diary clips: two to ten minutes. Short enough that you will keep doing them.
- Testament-style messages to a person: three to fifteen minutes. A long message can be split into chapters.
- Story sessions: as long as the story, but break at natural pauses, one story per file.

### When someone else films

If a family member or friend films you, let them ask the questions (Chapter 28 has lists) and have them sit beside the camera, so you look at them and almost into the lens. This feels natural, and it is how most good documentary interviews are shot. Ask them to stay quiet while you answer; their "mm-hmm" will be on every clip.

### If you are ill or tired

- Record in short sessions, at your best time of day.
- Sitting up in bed with the phone on a stand is fine.
- Audio alone is better than nothing. If video is too much, use the voice recorder.
- Ask someone to handle the device so you only have to talk.
- Let someone else interview you, scan photographs, label names, or type while you tell the story. You can answer one question at a time, dictate a recipe, or record a few minutes and stop.
- Invite a relative to help make one small piece with you: a recipe card, a labeled photo album, a short how-to clip, or a story transcript. Sharing the work can make it easier and give you time together now.
- Do not wait for a "good day" that may not come. A tired, honest message is worth more than a perfect one never recorded.

### Starting each recording

Start every testament-style recording the same way. Say:

- your name,
- the date,
- where you are, and
- who the message is for.

"This is [your full name]. It's the tenth of October 2026, I'm at home in Sacramento, and this message is for my grandson [his name]." This takes ten seconds and saves your family hours of guessing later. It also matters if anyone ever asks when and where a recording was made.

### Your first recording, minute by minute

The hardest recording is the first one. Here is a plan for a first session of about twenty minutes, from setting up to stopping.

1. **Minutes 0 to 5: set up.** Sit facing the window. Prop the phone horizontally at eye level, about an arm's length away. Turn off the television and anything that hums. Put a glass of water within reach, out of the frame.
2. **Minutes 5 to 6: test.** Record ten seconds saying anything at all. Play it back with headphones. If you can hear the fridge, move. If your face is dark, turn toward the light.
3. **Minutes 6 to 7: the opening.** Start recording and say your name, the date, where you are, and who this is for. If it is for nobody in particular, say "for the family."
4. **Minutes 7 to 17: one prompt.** Choose one easy prompt from Chapter 28, such as "describe the house you grew up in, room by room," and answer it as if you were talking to one person sitting just behind the phone. Do not stop when you stumble. Stumbles are what you sound like.
5. **Minutes 17 to 18: the ending.** Say "That's all for today," or anything you would say at the end of a phone call. Then stop recording.
6. **Minutes 18 to 20: save it.** Copy the file off the phone, rename it (`2026-10-10 [your name] - first recording - the house I grew up in.mp4`), and put it in your recordings folder. Then tell one person that you have started.

You will probably dislike the result. Almost everyone dislikes the sound of their own voice. The people who love you will not.

### When something goes wrong

- **You freeze.** Look away from the lens and talk to the window for a minute, then look back. Or have someone sit beside the camera and ask the question aloud.
- **You cry.** Keep recording if you can. A pause and a breath, on a recording meant for your family, is not a mistake to be edited out.
- **The phone runs out of storage.** Shorter clips; one story per file; copy each one off the phone after the session.
- **The sound echoes.** Move to a room with curtains, carpet, or bookshelves, or hang a blanket behind the camera.
- **You look tired.** You probably are. Record at your best time of day, and record anyway.
- **You keep starting again.** Allow yourself one restart per session, then keep whatever comes.

## Chapter 28. What to Say: Prompts That Work

Facing a lens with no plan produces a minute of awkward silence followed by the weather. Prompts fix this. Pick one prompt per recording. Answer it as if talking to one person. Stop when you are done.

The weather can make a cameo; it does not need top billing.

The prompts below are grouped by purpose. Appendix I is a one-page prompt card you can print and keep beside the camera.

### Getting started (the easy ones)

- Where were you born, and what is the first thing you remember?
- Describe the house you grew up in, room by room.
- What did your parents do for a living, and what was a normal day like for them?
- What did you want to be when you were ten?
- What was your first job, and what did it pay?
- Tell the story you always tell at family gatherings. Tell it properly this time.

### Life and work

- How did you choose your work, or how did it choose you?
- Describe a normal working day in the job that defined you. What did you actually do between nine and five?
- Who taught you the thing you are best at, and what did they do that a book would not?
- What was your best day at work? Your worst?
- What decision would you make again, and what did you know at the time?
- What decision would you not make again? No lecture: just what you knew and what you missed.

### Family and love

- How did you meet the person you built a life with? Include the unromantic parts.
- What was it like when your first child was born?
- What do you remember about each of your children at the age they are now? (A message for a child to watch at forty, describing them at forty-ish through your eyes, is a remarkable thing to receive.)
- What do you want your grandchildren to know about their parents when they were small?
- Who in the family do you most resemble, and how?
- What ordinary ritual, song, meal, place, or joke do you hope they remember?
- Who cared for you when you needed help, and what did you learn from them?
- What place did you leave, arrive in, or make home? What do you remember about the first day?
- What is something you hope your grandchild chooses for themselves, even if it is different from what you chose?

### Beliefs and lessons

- What do you believe now that you did not believe at thirty, and what changed your mind?
- What have faith, doubt, prayer, ritual, or a religious community meant to you? What, if anything, do you hope the next generation will keep, question, or leave?
- What do you want a child or grandchild to know about money that is not a slogan?
- What do you think matters most in a friendship?
- What are you still unsure about?
- What do you hope is not repeated in the family, said without instructing anyone's life?

### Neighbors and the places we shared

- Who lived next door when you were young? What do you remember about them?
- Which neighbor welcomed you, helped you, or became a friend? What happened?
- What did the street, building, or block sound and feel like at an ordinary time of day?
- How did neighbors help one another? What small kindness stayed with you?
- Who has moved away or died that you hope the family remembers?
- What has changed in this neighborhood, and what do you hope remains?

When a story includes someone else's private life, ask before recording or sharing it. Chapter 32 explains consent and privacy.

### Testament-style messages to a named person

- Start with their name.
- Say what you love about them, specifically. Not "you're wonderful": "the way you argued with the referee when you were seven, and were right."
- A memory you share with them.
- Something you hope for them, without turning it into an order.
- If there is something to apologize for, name it. Specific apologies land; vague ones sound like a press release.
- If there is something you want them to know about a decision that affects them, explain the reason you actually had.
- End with something they will want to replay.

### Messages for an occasion

- A wedding: what you hope their marriage has, what you learned from yours.
- The birth of a child: what you wish you had known as a new parent.
- A graduation: what you wish someone had told you at that age.
- A hard time: a message for "a day when everything has gone wrong."
- A birthday you will not see: what you imagine them doing at that age.

### Knowledge and creative work

- How to make the family recipe, filmed in the kitchen, with the actual measurements ("about this much" is fine, as long as the camera sees how much).
- How the house works: the boiler, the fuse box, the leaking tap that you fix with a coin.
- How to do the thing only you know how to do: sharpen the knives, prune the roses, tune the guitar.
- If you write fiction: the story, character, or imagined world you hope your family will remember, and whether you want anyone to continue it.

### Weak and strong answers

The difference between a recording people treasure and one they watch once is specificity.

> **Weak:** "Family is the most important thing. Always look after each other."
>
> **Strong:** "In 1987, your uncle and I didn't speak for two years over a car. A car! When your grandmother was ill, we sat in the hospital corridor and he handed me a coffee without asking how I took it, and he got it right. That's when I knew the car didn't matter. If you two ever fall out, remember the coffee."

> **Weak:** "Work hard and you'll succeed."
>
> **Strong:** "I turned down the partnership in 1998 because your grandmother was sick. I've never been sure it was right. What I'm sure of is that I don't remember a single meeting from that year, and I remember every afternoon at the hospital."

Not every story needs to be about work or a major decision. A quiet ritual can carry just as much of a person:

> **Strong:** "On Sundays your grandmother played the same record while she made soup. She sang the wrong words to the second verse every time. I never corrected her. When you make that soup, sing it wrong too—or don't. I just want you to know where the song came from."

The strong versions have dates, places, objects, and a doubt. That is what makes them yours.

### How to get from the general to the specific

Most first answers are general, because general is safe. "My father was a hard worker." The art of a good recording, whether you are interviewing yourself or someone else, is the follow-up question that turns a general sentence into a scene. Four follow-ups do most of the work:

- **When?** "What year was that? How old were you?"
- **Where were you standing?** Place brings back detail: the room, the light, who else was there.
- **What did it look like, sound like, smell like?** The senses are where memory keeps its best material.
- **What happened next?** Stories end later than people think.

Try it on yourself. "My father was a hard worker" becomes, after four questions: "In the winter of 1962 my father left for the brickworks at five, before it was light. I know because I could hear him in the kitchen, cutting bread with the big knife, and the door clicked behind him. I was seven. I thought everyone's father left in the dark."

### A message, annotated

Here is a short testament-style message, about three minutes when spoken, from a grandfather to a grandson who is twelve now and will watch it, perhaps, at eighteen. It is fictional. The notes after it say why it works.

> *"This is [your full name]. It's the tenth of October 2026, I'm in the workshop, and this is for my grandson [his name], for when you're eighteen. If you're watching it earlier, that's fine too.*
>
> *You were seven when you asked me why I keep every screw in a jar. I told you it was because I'm cheap. That's partly true. The real reason is that my father kept them, in the same kind of jar, and when he died I found eleven jars in his shed, all sorted by size, and I sat on the floor and couldn't stop laughing, because he'd never once told me he did it. So now you know: it runs in the family. You're allowed to break the habit.*
>
> *The thing I want you to know at eighteen is this. When I was nineteen I left an apprenticeship I hated, and everybody told me I was throwing my life away. I wasn't. I was throwing that life away, which is different. If you ever have to do something like that, do it kindly, tell the people who'll be disappointed yourself, and then work twice as hard at the next thing. That's all the advice I have. The rest you'll work out better than I did.*
>
> *I'm proud of you. Not for anything in particular. Just for the way you hold a hammer, which is properly, because I showed you. Look after the jars."*

**Why it works.**

- **It opens with the four facts:** name, date, place, recipient, plus a line about when to watch it.
- **It starts with a shared memory,** in the grandson's own words from when he was seven, which tells him he was listened to.
- **It reveals one small thing he did not know** (the eleven jars), which is what makes a message feel like a gift rather than a speech.
- **It gives one piece of advice,** with the story that earned it, and then explicitly stops advising.
- **It releases him** ("you're allowed to break the habit").
- **It ends with something specific to replay:** the hammer, and the jars.

It says nothing about property. If the grandfather leaves the workshop tools to his grandson, that belongs in the will, and the message can mention that the will exists (Chapter 31).

### Things to leave out

- Instructions about property. They belong in the will (Chapter 31).
- Accusations against living people who cannot answer, unless you have thought very hard about who will watch and why.
- Medical details you would not want played at a family dinner.
- Passwords, account numbers, and anything a stranger could use.
