## Chapter 11. Signing It So It Counts: California and Germany

A will can be clear, fair, and useless if it is signed wrong. This chapter sets out the formal rules in the book's two home jurisdictions. If you live anywhere else, treat it as a picture of the kind of rules to ask about, not as the rules themselves.

### California: the three doors

California offers three main ways to make a valid individual will.

**The witnessed will.** Under Probate Code § 6110, the will must be in writing and signed by the testator (or by someone else in the testator's name, in the testator's presence and at the testator's direction). At least two people must sign as witnesses during the testator's lifetime. Both must be present at the same time, must witness either the signing or the testator's acknowledgment of the signature or of the will, and must understand that the document is the testator's will. Most lawyer-drafted wills use this door.

If the witnessing went wrong, § 6110(c)(2) offers a narrow rescue: the will is treated as properly witnessed if the person offering it proves by *clear and convincing evidence* that the testator intended it to be their will when they signed it. That is a high standard, argued in court, paid for by the estate. Do not plan to use it.

**The holographic will.** Under § 6111, a will that does not meet the witnessing rules is still valid if the signature and the material provisions are in the testator's own handwriting. No witnesses are required. If it is undated and that creates doubt about which of two wills came later, or whether the testator had capacity at the time, the undated will can fail to that extent. So date it. A statement of intent may appear on a commercially printed form, but the gifts must be handwritten.

**The California statutory will.** Probate Code § 6240 contains a fill-in will form, published free by the California courts. You fill in the blanks without adding or crossing out words, then sign in front of two adult witnesses, who sign in your presence and each other's. The form's own instructions say that notarisation alone is not sufficient. It is simple and suitable for simple estates. It is not designed for blended families, businesses, or complicated trusts.

**Who may witness.** Any person generally competent to be a witness. A gift to a witness does not void the will (§ 6112), but unless there are two other disinterested witnesses, it creates a presumption that the witness obtained the gift by undue influence or similar wrongdoing, which the witness must then disprove. Use two people who receive nothing.

**Notaries.** A California will does not need a notary. Notarisation does not replace witnesses. (Many other California estate documents, such as the statutory power of attorney and transfer-on-death deeds, do require notarisation. Different documents, different rules.)

**Capacity.** A person must be at least eighteen and of sound mind (Probate Code § 6100). Roughly, you need to understand what a will is, what you own in broad terms, and who your family is. You do not need a perfect memory.

**After death.** Whoever has custody of the original must deliver it to the clerk of the superior court within thirty days of learning of the death (§ 8200), unless a probate petition has already been filed. Keep the original safe, tell your executor where it is, and give your lawyer a copy.

### Germany: two ordinary forms, and a few extraordinary ones

The German Civil Code (BGB) allows a will in ordinary form in only two ways (§ 2231).

**The notarial will (öffentliches Testament).** You declare your last will to a notary, who records it, or you hand the notary a document stating that it contains your last will (§ 2232). The document need not be in your handwriting; it can be typed, and it can be handed over open or sealed. The notary advises on the content, checks identity and capacity, and arranges for the will to go into official custody at the local court and be registered with the Central Register of Wills (*Zentrales Testamentsregister*). The notary charges a fee based on the value of the estate, set by statute.

Two practical advantages are worth knowing. First, a notarial will is found reliably. Second, it can save the heirs from having to apply for a certificate of inheritance (*Erbschein*): under the Land Registry Code (*Grundbuchordnung*, § 35), a notarial will together with the court's record of its opening is usually sufficient proof of succession to change the land register, although the registry may still ask for a certificate if it has doubts. Banks often accept it too. Since a certificate of inheritance also costs a fee based on the estate's value, the notarial will can pay for itself.

**The handwritten will (eigenhändiges Testament).** You write the entire text yourself, by hand, and sign it (§ 2247). The law says you *should* state the date and place, and that the signature *should* include first name and surname; if those are missing, the will may still be valid, but an undated will can fail if the date matters to its validity. A typed will signed by hand is not valid (Case 11). A person who is a minor or cannot read writing cannot make a handwritten will (§ 2247(4)); minors over sixteen may make a will before a notary (§ 2233).

You can keep a handwritten will at home, but you can also ask the local court to take it into special official custody (§ 2248). It is then registered in the Central Register of Wills, which the Federal Chamber of Notaries says charges a registration fee of 12.50 euros if collected through a notary and 15.50 euros if it bills you directly. Privately kept wills cannot be registered at all. Anyone who holds a will not in official custody must deliver it to the probate court without delay after learning of the death (§ 2259).

**The joint will (gemeinschaftliches Testament).** Only spouses (and, under the Civil Partnership Act, registered partners) can make a joint will (§ 2265). It can be handwritten by one spouse and signed by both, or made before a notary. The most common type, the *Berliner Testament*, has the spouses name each other as sole heirs and the children as heirs of the survivor (§ 2269). Provisions that depend on each other become binding: while both are alive, one spouse can revoke them only by notarised declaration to the other, and after the first death the survivor generally loses the right to revoke them unless they disclaim what they inherited (§§ 2270, 2271). Decide before signing whether the survivor should be free to change the final heirs, and write that freedom into the will if so.

**The inheritance contract (Erbvertrag).** A contract with another person about succession, which binds you in a way a will does not. It must be made before a notary with both parties present (§ 2276). Unmarried couples who want the binding effect of a joint will use it.

**Emergency wills.** If death is feared before a notary can be reached, a will can be made before the mayor and two witnesses (§ 2249), or, in near danger of death or when cut off by extraordinary circumstances, by oral declaration before three witnesses, of which a written record must be made (§ 2250). These emergency wills lapse three months after they are made if the testator is still alive (§ 2252). They are rare, and none of them is a video.

### German wills use different building blocks

A Californian will gives property to people. A German will chiefly appoints *heirs* (*Erben*), who step into the deceased's shoes and take the estate as a whole, debts included, in shares. Leaving someone a single object or a sum of money usually creates a *legacy* (*Vermächtnis*): the beneficiary does not become an heir but has a claim against the heirs to hand the item over (§ 2174). The Civil Code says that if a will gives someone the whole estate or a fraction of it, that person is an heir even if not called one; if it gives only individual items, the person is in case of doubt not an heir, even if called one (§ 2087).

This matters more than it sounds. A German will that says "my house to my daughter, my savings to my son" may, depending on the values involved, be read as making both children heirs in proportion to the values, with an instruction on how to divide (*Teilungsanordnung*, § 2048), or as making one of them sole heir and the other a legatee. Each reading has different consequences for debts, for the compulsory share, and for who has to apply for the certificate of inheritance. A German notary or lawyer writes "I appoint my daughter and my son as my heirs in equal shares" and then deals with the house separately. Write it that way too.

### A short handwritten will in German, with a gloss

Here is a fictional example, kept deliberately simple, to show the shape. It is not a template for your situation.

> *Testament*
>
> *Ich, Maria Beispiel, geboren am 3. Mai 1950 in Passau, setze hiermit meine beiden Kinder, Anna Beispiel und Paul Beispiel, zu gleichen Teilen als meine Erben ein. Ersatzerben sind jeweils deren Abkömmlinge.*
>
> *Meine Nichte Lea Muster erhält als Vermächtnis meinen Ring mit dem blauen Stein.*
>
> *Alle früheren Verfügungen von Todes wegen widerrufe ich.*
>
> *Passau, den 10. Oktober 2026*
>
> *Maria Beispiel*

Gloss: Maria names her two children as heirs in equal shares, with their descendants as substitute heirs; leaves her niece a ring as a legacy; revokes earlier wills; and dates, places, and signs with first name and surname. All of it in her own handwriting, on paper, in ink.

### What neither system accepts

Neither California nor Germany accepts, as a will:

- A video or audio recording, however clear.
- A text message, email, or note on a phone.
- A typed document signed by hand without witnesses (California) or without a notary (Germany).
- A document someone else wrote that you only signed, unless it was properly witnessed (California) or handed to a notary (Germany).

Chapter 31 comes back to the first item, because it is where Part Seven's video legacy and the law meet, and where good intentions most often turn into a family argument.
