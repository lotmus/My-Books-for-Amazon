# How to Use This Book

This book takes up two jobs people often confuse: transferring what they own, and preserving what they think. A will does the first. It can do almost nothing for the second. Most people plan only for the first, if they plan at all. Then the stories, reasons, and half-finished explanations go into the ground with them, pushing up daisies instead of reaching the people who might have wanted to hear them.

The title is the argument. A will says who should receive what you own at death. In California, a court may carry it out through probate; in Germany, the estate usually passes directly to the heirs under the will or the law. A legacy is everything else: the account nobody knew about, the password nobody can find, the reason you left the job in 1998, the voice your grandchildren will never hear unless you press a red button on your phone this week. The will matters. It is simply not the whole thing, and in many families it is not even the biggest thing.

## Choose a route

You can read the book in order, or start with the part you need most:

- **Making a plan now:** Chapter 1, then Chapters 8–25; finish with Appendices A and C.
- **Leaving stories, skills, or recordings:** Chapters 26–36. If you want to record something today, start with Chapters 27 and 28.
- **Settling an estate:** Chapter 37 and Appendix B. Add Chapter 38 for digital accounts and Appendix L for a specific case.
- **One immediate problem:** Find the closest case in Part Two, then use Appendix L for the next steps. If you live outside California or Germany, use Chapter 40 as an orientation and take its questions to a local professional.

## How the book is laid out

Part One is the short version. If time is short, read Chapter 1 and do what it says. It is a sequence, not a philosophy.

Part Two is twenty-five worked cases. Some read like a case file, some like a short story, some like a conversation at a kitchen table, and some show the same family twice, as it went and as it could have gone. Each one shows which document or rule decided the outcome, what would have prevented it, and what to do, including what to do if it has already happened to you. Here the rules stop being abstract: you see what they mean for an actual family.

Part Three walks through a sample will clause by clause, then plans the same estate three different ways, and ends with the formalities that make a will valid or useless in California and in Germany.

Part Four covers the alternatives to a will, each with its failure modes: beneficiary forms, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death, joint title, gifts while alive, dying without a will, small-estate procedures, contracts that override everything, and trusts.

Part Five is about the documents that work while you are still alive: powers of attorney and healthcare directives.

Part Six is the Family List of Everything: the binder, physical or digital, that tells your people what exists, where it is, and whom to call.

Part Seven is about keeping your voice and know-how. A phone can record a story; a book or video can preserve a skill. This part shows how to leave messages and diaries, and how to pass on what work and hobbies taught you through how-to books, fiction, and other creative work your family can preserve, update, or continue. It covers what to say, how to keep the files and accounts accessible, and why none of it counts as a will.

Part Eight is the craft of leaving thoughts: the ethical will, letters, timed messages, turning voice memos into a short memoir, and handing over unfinished work.

Part Nine is the receiver's half: the first ninety days for the person you named, the digital-access problem from their side of the locked screen, and how to receive the meaning archive without turning love into an obligation.

Part Ten is one honest chapter on what this book cannot know about where you live.

The appendices are working pages: two printable to-do lists (one for you, one for your family after a death), an "In Case of Emergency" binder checklist, worksheets with filled examples, and a comparison of the California and German rules.

## The two homes of this book

Rules about wills differ by country and, in the United States, by state. Rather than try to cover every state and country, this book uses two worked examples: California and Germany, where I live. California uses probate courts, trusts, and witnesses. Germany uses notaries, compulsory shares, and inheritance courts that issue certificates. These systems show two distinct approaches and recurring ways inheritance plans work or fail. Treat them like worked textbook problems: the reasoning may travel, but the figures do not. If you live in another US state, England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland, Canada, Australia, or New Zealand, [Chapter 40](#Chapter 40. If You Live Elsewhere: Topic by Topic) gives a short guide to each key topic. The relevant chapters point you there, too.

Every legal figure in this book that names a jurisdiction was checked against an official source (the statute itself, a court's self-help pages, or a tax authority) in October 2026. The Sources appendix lists them. Thresholds change. California adjusts several of its small-estate figures every three years, and the next adjustment is due on 1 April 2028. Tax exemptions move with legislation. Check the current figure before you rely on it.

Where a point is general (true in most places, but not guaranteed anywhere in particular), I say so, and I do not attach a number to it.

## Not legal advice

This book is an educational guide. It is not legal, tax, financial, or medical advice, and reading it does not make you anyone's client. I am not a lawyer. I am an engineer who reads statutes the way other people read thrillers, which is to say with a pencil and a growing sense of dread. Have a qualified professional where you live review anything that moves property, names a guardian, or grants a power of attorney before you rely on it.

You will see a short version of this note once at the start of each part, not on every page. Repeating a warning on every page does not make it truer. It only teaches you to skip it.

## The names in this book

Every person in the cases and examples is invented. The families are composites built to show a rule at work, not to represent a nationality, family type, or community. Customs and beliefs vary within every jurisdiction. If a name matches someone you know, that is coincidence, and probably a sign that the name was too common to begin with.

## If this is about wanting to die

If any of this planning is about wanting to die rather than expecting to, please stop and talk to someone today. In the United States, you can call or text 988. In Germany, the Telefonseelsorge answers around the clock at 0800 111 0 111 and 0800 111 0 222. The paperwork can wait. You are not paperwork.
