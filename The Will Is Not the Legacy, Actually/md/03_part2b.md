## Chapter 4. Titles, Deeds, and Joint Names

### Case 6: The transfer-on-death deed and the other two children

**The facts.** Walter, a widower in Riverside, owned his house outright and had three children. His will left everything equally to the three. His eldest son, Paul, lived closest, and a neighbour told Walter about transfer-on-death deeds. Walter recorded one naming Paul as beneficiary, intending Paul to "take care of things" and share with his siblings.

**The move.** Avoid probate on the house, which was by far his largest asset.

**What governed.** California allows a revocable transfer-on-death deed (Probate Code §§ 5600 and following). Walter kept full ownership while alive; at his death the house passed to the beneficiary named on the deed, not to the people named in the will. Paul owned the house. Nothing obliged him to share it. The will divided the rest of the estate, which was a modest bank account, three ways. One child got a house; two got a few thousand dollars each.

Paul did intend to share. Then his own marriage ran into trouble, and the house he had inherited became a topic in his divorce negotiations, where "I was going to give my sister a third" is not a legal category.

**What should have been signed.** A TOD deed naming all three children as beneficiaries in equal shares, if Walter wanted the house to avoid probate. Or a revocable living trust holding the house, with equal shares for the three children. Either way, the deed and the will should have said the same thing.

A side note for California readers: the statute authorising these deeds is currently set to expire on 1 January 2032 unless the legislature extends it. A deed executed before that date remains valid. If you are relying on one, ask your lawyer about the current status.

**The lesson.** A deed is a will for one asset. If it disagrees with your will, the deed wins for that asset, and the will divides whatever is left.

### Case 7: The son added to the title

**The facts.** Gisela, a retired nurse in Los Angeles, added her son Mark to the title of her condominium as a joint tenant with right of survivorship. She wanted him to inherit it without probate, and a title-company employee said this was "the simple way."

**The move.** Joint tenancy, the do-it-yourself probate avoidance tool.

**What governed.** Joint tenancy worked exactly as designed: at Gisela's death, Mark became sole owner automatically. But it also worked while she was alive. Adding Mark was a present gift of half the property. When Mark was sued after a car accident, his half-interest in the condominium was an asset his creditors could pursue. When Gisela wanted to sell the condominium and move into assisted living, she needed Mark's signature, and Mark, by then going through a difficult patch, did not answer his phone for three weeks. And the gift had tax effects her accountant would have flagged: inherited property in the United States usually receives a new tax basis at death, but a lifetime gift generally carries over the giver's original basis for the part given away. Mark's eventual capital-gains bill on the half he received as a gift was larger than it needed to be.

**What should have been signed.** A transfer-on-death deed naming Mark, which passes the condominium at death without giving Mark any ownership during Gisela's lifetime. Or a revocable trust. Either avoids probate without handing half the home to someone else's creditors.

**The lesson.** Joint tenancy is not a probate tool that happens to involve ownership. It is ownership that happens to avoid probate.

### Case 8: Tenants in common, and a share nobody planned for

**The facts.** Three siblings, Anna, Ben, and Clara, inherited their parents' holiday cabin in the Sierra Nevada and took title as tenants in common, each owning one third. Ben died unexpectedly at fifty. He had no will. He was survived by his wife, from whom he was separated but not divorced, and a son from an earlier relationship.

**The move.** None. The siblings assumed the cabin "stays in the family" and that Ben's share would come back to the other two.

**What governed.** Tenancy in common has no right of survivorship. Ben's third was part of his estate. With no will, California's intestacy statute decided who received it. Ben's interest in the cabin was his separate property, because he had inherited it. Under Probate Code § 6401(c), with one child, a surviving spouse takes one half of the separate property, and the child takes the rest. So Ben's estranged wife and his son each received one sixth of the cabin. Anna and Clara now co-own a cabin with their brother's estranged widow, who would like to sell, and a nineteen-year-old nephew who would like to have parties there.

**What should have been signed.** A co-ownership agreement among the siblings, giving the others a right to buy out a deceased sibling's share at a fair price. And a will from each sibling saying what they wanted to happen to their share. Ben, if asked, would almost certainly have said "to my son," and separated-but-married spouses are exactly the kind of fact a lawyer wants to hear about.

**The lesson.** "Tenants in common" means "my share goes wherever my estate goes." If you do not decide where your estate goes, the statute will, and the statute has never been to the cabin.

### Case 9: Half of it was never hers to give

**The facts.** Patricia and George, married for thirty years in California, owned a house bought during the marriage with their salaries, held in both names as community property. George had a will leaving his estate to his two children from a first marriage. Patricia had a will leaving everything to her niece, because she and George had once discussed, over a bottle of wine, that his children would be taken care of by him and her niece by her. Patricia died first.

**The move.** Patricia's will purported to leave "my house" to her niece.

**What governed.** California is a community property state. Property acquired by a married person during the marriage is generally community property, and at death each spouse can dispose of only their own half. Patricia's will could give her niece Patricia's half-interest in the house, but not George's half. The niece became co-owner with George, a man she had met twice. When George died a year later, his half went to his children. The house ended up owned half by the niece and half by George's children, none of whom liked each other much.

**What should have been signed.** A coordinated plan, made together, ideally a joint revocable trust or matching wills that said what happens after the first death and after the second. If the plan was "each side of the family gets half eventually," the documents should have said so, including who lives in the house in between. That wine-fuelled conversation should have been carried to a lawyer's office.

**The lesson.** In California, a married person's will controls their half of the community property, not the whole. Plan as a couple, or at least plan in the same room.

### Case 10: The house given away eight years before

**The facts.** Friedrich, a widower near Regensburg, had two children, Jonas and Sophie. Jonas lived with him and cared for him through a long illness. Eight years before his death, Friedrich gave Jonas the family house by notarial deed, saying "the rest will go to Sophie." The rest was a savings account worth a fraction of the house. Friedrich's will named Jonas as sole heir, to "keep things simple."

**The move.** Give the house during life, leave the remainder by will, and assume a lifetime gift was beyond anyone's reach.

**What governed.** In Germany, close family members who are cut out of the estate by a will have a *Pflichtteil*, a compulsory share worth half the value of their statutory share (BGB § 2303). With two children and no surviving spouse, Sophie's statutory share would have been one half; her *Pflichtteil* was one quarter of the estate's value, as a money claim against Jonas. And because of § 2325, gifts made within ten years before death can be added back to the estate when calculating the *Pflichtteil*: a gift in the last year counts in full, and each earlier year counts one tenth less. The house, given eight years before death, still counted at two-tenths of its value. Sophie's claim was therefore larger than Friedrich had imagined, and payable in cash by Jonas, who had a house and not much cash.

**What should have been signed.** Advice from a notary on the *Pflichtteil* consequences of the gift, at the time of the gift. Options included making the gift earlier, structuring it with conditions, compensating Sophie in other ways, or a notarial waiver: German law lets a relative renounce their compulsory share by contract with the person who will leave the estate (§ 2346, notarised under § 2348), often in return for something of value now. Sophie might well have agreed, given Jonas's years of care, if anyone had asked her while Friedrich was alive.

**The lesson.** In Germany you cannot simply write a child out of the estate. You can reduce what they get, and you can agree with them in advance, but the conversation has to happen while you are there to have it.
