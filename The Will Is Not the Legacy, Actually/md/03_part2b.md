## Chapter 4. Titles, Deeds, and Joint Names

### Case 6: The transfer-on-death deed and the other two children

**The situation.** Walter was a retired machinist in Riverside, a widower for six years, with a paid-off three-bedroom house, a modest savings account, and three adult children. Paul, the eldest, lived fifteen minutes away and came by most weekends to fix things and eat Walter's chili. Susan lived in Seattle and called every Sunday. Mark lived in Phoenix, had three children of his own, and visited twice a year.

Walter's will, signed with a lawyer when his wife was still alive and updated after her death, left everything in equal shares to the three children. It was a good will. It was also, as it turned out, mostly irrelevant.

At a barbecue, a neighbour told Walter about a document that would let his house skip probate entirely. "You just sign a deed naming who gets it, record it, and when you go, it passes automatically. No court, no lawyers, no fees." Walter liked the sound of no lawyers. He found a form, filled it in, had it notarised at the shipping store, and recorded it with the county. He named Paul as beneficiary, because Paul was nearby and practical and would "take care of things." Walter told Paul he trusted him to share the house with his brother and sister. He did not tell Susan or Mark about the deed at all, because it seemed like a technicality.

Walter died four years later. The house was worth about 620,000 dollars. The savings account held about 30,000.

**What the family assumed.** Susan and Mark assumed the will divided everything equally, because they had seen it and it did. Paul assumed he would sell the house and share the money, because that was what his father had asked. Walter had assumed that a deed and a will were two ways of doing the same thing.

**What actually happened, and why.** California allows a revocable transfer-on-death deed (Probate Code §§ 5600 and following). It is a useful tool. While you are alive, it changes nothing: you own the house, you can sell it, mortgage it, or revoke the deed. At your death, the house passes to the beneficiary named on the deed, outside probate. Crucially, it passes under the deed, not under the will. The will divides only what the will controls.

So the house became Paul's. Not "Paul's to share," just Paul's. The will divided the savings account, 10,000 dollars each, minus expenses.

Paul did intend to share. He said so at the funeral. Then, about four months later, his wife filed for divorce. The house he had inherited was legally his separate property, because in California an inheritance received by one spouse is generally that spouse's separate property. But the divorce raised questions anyway: Paul and his wife had spent weekends and money repairing his father's house in the last years; she had ideas about whether that gave her a claim; and Paul's lawyer advised him not to sign anything, transfer anything, or promise anything to anyone until the divorce was settled. "I was going to give my sister a third" is a moral position. In a divorce negotiation it is not a legal category.

The divorce took two years. By the time it was settled, the house had been sold to pay Paul's legal fees and the settlement, and what was left was not enough to give his siblings a third each. He gave them what he could. Susan accepted it graciously. Mark did not. The three of them are polite at weddings.

**What would have prevented it.** Walter's goal had two parts: avoid probate on the house, and treat his children equally. One document could have done both.

- **A TOD deed naming all three children** in equal shares. California's form allows multiple beneficiaries. The house would have passed to the three of them as co-owners, outside probate, matching the will.
- **A revocable living trust** holding the house, with the three children as equal beneficiaries and one of them (Paul, if Walter wished) as successor trustee to manage the sale. The trustee has a legal duty to the beneficiaries, so "take care of things" becomes an obligation instead of a hope.

Either option costs little more than what Walter did. The difference is that both make the deed and the will say the same thing.

A note on the statute itself: the California law that authorises these deeds is currently set to be repealed on 1 January 2032 unless the legislature extends it. Deeds signed before that date remain valid. If you rely on one, check its status with your lawyer every few years.

**The plain-language version.** A transfer-on-death deed is a will for one house. If it says something different from your real will, it wins for that house. Most families never notice the difference until the house is the only thing that mattered.

**The German version.** Germany has no transfer-on-death deed. Ownership of land passes at death only through inheritance, by will or by statute, and a lifetime transfer of land requires a notarised contract (BGB § 311b) and an entry in the land register. That makes Walter's particular mistake impossible in Germany, but its cousin is common: a parent transfers the house to one child during life, by notarised gift, "so the others don't have to bother," and assumes the child will share. The other children may later have claims against the estate or, in some cases, against the child under the compulsory share rules, which add back gifts made within the last ten years (§ 2325). The moral is the same. If you want equality, put equality in the document.

**Questions to ask before you sign a deed.**

- Who will own this property the day after I die, and what document decides that?
- Does that match my will, my trust, and what I have told my family?
- What happens if the person I name dies before me? (California's form lets you name alternates. Use them.)
- What happens if the person I name is in debt, divorcing, or ill when I die?
- Who gets a copy, and who knows it exists?

**What to do.**

- Before you sign any deed, form, or designation, ask: does this match my will?
- If you want the house to avoid probate and be shared, name every intended beneficiary on the deed, or use a trust.
- Do not rely on one child to "share" with the others. Moral obligations do not survive divorces, creditors, or the child's own death.
- Tell all your children what you have signed. A family meeting with a copy of each document on the table costs an afternoon and saves a decade.

### Case 7: The son added to the title

**The situation.** Gisela was a retired nurse in Los Angeles, seventy-four, living in a two-bedroom condominium she had bought after her divorce. Her only son, Mark, was forty-five, recently remarried, and running a small landscaping business with more ambition than capital.

Gisela wanted Mark to inherit the condominium without going through probate, which she had heard was slow and expensive. At the title company, while refinancing, she asked a friendly employee how to do it. The employee, who was not a lawyer and said so, mentioned that many people simply add their child to the title as a joint tenant. "When you pass, it goes to him automatically. Simple." Gisela signed a new deed adding Mark as a joint tenant with right of survivorship.

Three years later, one of Mark's employees, driving a company truck, caused a serious accident. The injured driver sued Mark and his business. The business's insurance was not enough.

A year after that, Gisela's health declined, and she wanted to sell the condominium and move into an assisted living community. Mark, under pressure from the lawsuit and a difficult patch in his new marriage, did not answer his phone for three weeks.

**What the family assumed.** Gisela assumed she still owned her home, with Mark's name attached as a sort of forwarding address for after her death. Mark assumed the same, if he thought about it at all.

**What actually happened, and why.** Joint tenancy is not a forwarding address. It is co-ownership. When Gisela added Mark as a joint tenant, she made him a present owner of an undivided half of the property, today, not at her death. The "right of survivorship" means that when one joint tenant dies, the survivor owns the whole. It also means that, while both are alive, both own it.

That had three consequences, all of which arrived in Gisela's lifetime.

**Creditors.** Mark's half-interest was his property, and in principle it was an asset his creditors could pursue. The injured driver's lawyers noticed it. They recorded a lien against Mark's interest in the condominium. It did not force a sale while Gisela lived there, but it complicated every later step.

**Control.** To sell or refinance the condominium, Gisela needed Mark's signature, because he was an owner. For three weeks she could not get it. When he finally signed, the lien had to be dealt with at closing, which meant part of the sale price went to Mark's creditor instead of to Gisela's care.

**Tax.** In the United States, property inherited at death usually receives a new tax basis equal to its value at the date of death, so if the heir sells soon after, there is little or no capital gain. Property given away during life generally keeps the giver's original basis for the part given. By adding Mark, Gisela had given him half the condominium with her old basis attached. If he had inherited it all at her death instead, he would have received a basis close to its current value for the whole. Their accountant estimated the difference in tax on the eventual sale at several tens of thousands of dollars. There may also have been a gift tax return to file for the year she added him, since the gift exceeded the annual exclusion.

**What would have prevented it.** Gisela's goal was simple: Mark inherits; no probate. The tools that achieve that without giving Mark anything during her lifetime are:

- **A revocable transfer-on-death deed** naming Mark. Gisela keeps full ownership and control; Mark has no interest at all until her death; his creditors cannot reach what he does not own; and he receives the property with a new basis at her death.
- **A revocable living trust** holding the condominium, with Mark as beneficiary after her death. Same advantages, plus a successor trustee who can step in if Gisela becomes incapacitated.

For the practical problem of selling the property later, a durable power of attorney naming a trusted agent would have let someone sign on Gisela's behalf if she could not.

**The plain-language version.** Joint tenancy is not a probate shortcut that happens to involve ownership. It is ownership that happens to avoid probate. Everything that comes with owning something, creditors, divorces, signatures, taxes, comes with it.

**The German version.** In Germany, putting a child on the land register as co-owner of the family home is a gift of a share of the land, and it must be notarised (BGB § 311b) and registered. It can make sense, especially for inheritance tax, because each parent can give each child up to 400,000 euros every ten years free of tax (ErbStG §§ 14 and 16), and gifts that use up the allowance early can save tax at death. That is a planning reason, not a mistake. But the same consequences follow as in California: the child becomes an owner now, with all the exposure that brings, and the parent can no longer sell or mortgage the house alone. A German notary will usually suggest protective clauses: a lifelong right for the parent to live in the house (*Wohnrecht*) or to keep its income (*Nießbrauch*), and a right to take the share back if the child dies first, becomes insolvent, or tries to sell. Those clauses are the difference between a planned gift and an accident.

**Questions to ask before you add anyone to a title.**

- Am I giving away ownership today, or only arranging who receives it at my death?
- Could this person's creditors, spouse, or business partners reach the property?
- Will I still be able to sell, refinance, or move without their signature?
- What is the tax effect now, and what would it be if they inherited instead?
- If I change my mind, can I undo it, and at what cost?

**What to do.**

- Do not add a child to the title of your home "to avoid probate" without advice. In California, a TOD deed or a trust usually does the job better.
- If you have already done it, ask a lawyer whether and how to undo it. Undoing it may itself have tax and gift consequences, so do not simply sign another deed.
- If your child runs a business, has debts, or is in a fragile marriage, be doubly careful about putting assets in their name early.
- Ask your tax adviser about basis before you give property away. The tax on a gift can arrive years later, on someone else's return.

### Case 8: Tenants in common, and a share nobody planned for

**The situation.** Anna, Ben, and Clara grew up spending summers at their parents' cabin in the Sierra Nevada: a wooden house with a sagging porch, a lake ten minutes' walk away, and a smell of pine and mothballs that each of them could summon from memory. When their mother died, the will left the cabin to the three of them equally. They took title as tenants in common, each owning one third. They agreed, over a bottle of wine on the porch, that the cabin would "stay in the family" forever.

Ben was the one who loved it most. He came up every month, fixed the roof, cleared the gutters, and kept a logbook of who had used it. He married at forty-two; the marriage went badly, and by forty-eight he and his wife had separated, though neither had got round to filing for divorce. Ben also had a son, Tyler, from an earlier relationship, then nineteen.

Ben died suddenly at fifty, of a stroke, on a Tuesday. He had no will. He had always meant to make one "once the divorce is sorted."

**What the family assumed.** Anna and Clara assumed Ben's third would come back to them, because the cabin was the family's. Tyler assumed his father's share would be his.

**What actually happened, and why.** Tenancy in common has no right of survivorship. Each tenant in common owns a separate share that passes through their own estate when they die, by will or, without a will, under the intestacy rules. Ben's third was part of his estate.

Because Ben had inherited his share from his mother, it was his separate property, not community property shared with his wife. With no will, California's intestacy statute decided who received his separate property. Under Probate Code § 6401(c), when the deceased leaves a surviving spouse and one child, the spouse takes one half of the separate property, and the child takes the other half. Ben was separated but still married. In the eyes of the statute, a separated spouse is a spouse.

So Ben's estranged wife received half of his third: one sixth of the cabin. Tyler received the other sixth. Anna and Clara now co-owned the cabin with a woman neither had spoken to since the wedding, and with a nineteen-year-old nephew whose plans for the cabin involved friends, speakers, and long weekends.

Ben's widow wanted to sell her share, or have the cabin sold. In California, a co-owner who cannot agree with the others can ask a court to order a partition, which can mean a physical division (rarely practical for a cabin) or a sale with the proceeds divided. Anna and Clara did not want to sell. They did not have the money to buy her out at the price she wanted. The negotiation took a year and ended with the two sisters taking out a loan against their own homes.

**What would have prevented it.** Two documents, neither expensive.

- **A co-ownership agreement** among the three siblings, signed when they took title. It could have given the surviving siblings a right to buy a deceased sibling's share at a fair, agreed price (perhaps based on an appraisal), paid over a few years; set rules for use, costs, and repairs; and said what happens if one sibling wants out. Agreements like this are common for family property and are well worth a lawyer's time.
- **A will from Ben.** If Ben had wanted his share to go to Tyler, or back to his sisters, a will would have done it. A lawyer drafting it would have asked about his marriage, and might also have advised him to finish the divorce, or to consider how a separated spouse would be treated in the meantime.

The siblings could also have held the cabin through a family limited liability company or a trust, which can carry built-in rules about who may own shares. That is more complicated and more expensive, and for a modest cabin, a co-ownership agreement and three wills would have been enough.

**The plain-language version.** Tenants in common means "my share goes wherever my estate goes." If you do not decide where your estate goes, the statute decides, and the statute has never been to the cabin and does not know which sibling fixed the roof.

**The German version.** In Germany, the same story would begin earlier. When the mother died, the three siblings would have become a community of heirs (*Erbengemeinschaft*), co-owning the whole estate together. Any co-heir can generally demand that the community be wound up at any time (BGB § 2042), and if they cannot agree how to divide a house, the default route is a forced sale by auction, among themselves or to outsiders (the partition auction, *Teilungsversteigerung*, under § 753). Many German families end up there, because a house is the one asset that cannot be cut into thirds. A will that leaves the cabin to one child, with the others compensated in money, or a binding agreement among the siblings, is the usual cure.

**Why the porch agreement failed.** "It stays in the family forever" is a sentence with no legal content. It does not say what happens when one sibling dies, divorces, needs money, or wants out. It does not say who pays for the roof. Families who want to keep a shared property for a generation need rules, and the time to write them is while everyone still likes each other, on the porch, with the wine.

**Questions to ask.**

- How do we hold title, and what happens to each share at death?
- If one of us wants to sell, how is the price set, and can the others buy first?
- Who decides on repairs and who pays for them?
- What happens if one co-owner's share passes to a spouse or child we have never met?

**What to do.**

- If you co-own property with anyone other than a spouse, know how the title is held: joint tenancy (survivorship) or tenancy in common (no survivorship).
- If it is tenancy in common, sign a co-ownership agreement with a buy-out clause.
- Make a will that says where your share goes.
- If you are separated but not divorced, assume your spouse is still your spouse for inheritance purposes until a lawyer tells you otherwise, and plan accordingly.

### Case 9: Half of it was never hers to give

**The situation.** Patricia and George had been married for thirty years and lived in a Spanish-style house in Pasadena that they bought in the third year of their marriage with two salaries and a mortgage that felt enormous at the time. It was in both their names. Neither of them could have told you exactly how the deed described their ownership.

It was a second marriage for both. George had two children, Lisa and Andrew, from his first marriage; they were teenagers when Patricia met him and adults now with children of their own. Patricia had no children, but she had a niece, Hannah, her late sister's daughter, whom she had more or less raised after her sister died young.

One evening, over a bottle of wine, they talked about what would happen "after." George said his children would be taken care of by him. Patricia said she wanted Hannah to have something. They agreed it was all very fair. Each of them later made a will on their own, at different times, with different lawyers. George's will left his estate to Lisa and Andrew. Patricia's will left "my house and everything else I own" to Hannah.

Patricia died first, at sixty-eight.

**What the family assumed.** Hannah assumed she had inherited the house, because the will said so. George assumed he would go on living in it, because it was his home. Lisa and Andrew assumed that whatever happened, their father's house would come to them.

**What actually happened, and why.** California is a community property state. Generally, property either spouse acquires during the marriage, other than by gift or inheritance, belongs to both of them equally as community property. At death, each spouse can leave only their own half of the community property by will. The other half already belongs to the surviving spouse (Probate Code § 100). Patricia's will could not give away George's half of the house. It could only give away her half.

So Hannah became the co-owner of one half of the house, with George, a man she had met perhaps a dozen times and who now lived in what was partly her property. She could, as a co-owner, have asked a court to partition the property, which for a single house would almost certainly have meant a forced sale. She was not a cruel person and did not want to throw George out of his home. She also had a mortgage of her own, a child starting university, and half of a house in Pasadena that produced no income. George could not afford to buy her out.

They made an uneasy arrangement: George would stay, pay the taxes and repairs, and the house would be sold when he died or moved. George died fourteen months later. His half went, under his will, to Lisa and Andrew. The house was now owned half by Hannah and half by two people who regarded her as the woman who had tried to take their father's home. The sale took eleven months and two lawyers, and the conversation at the closing was conducted entirely through those lawyers.

**What would have prevented it.** Patricia and George had a plan. They simply never wrote it down in one place. What they wanted was something like this: whoever dies first, the survivor keeps living in the house; after both are gone, Hannah gets Patricia's half and George's children get George's half. That is a perfectly reasonable plan, and there are well-worn ways to carry it out.

- **A joint revocable trust**, signed by both, holding the house. It could say that after the first death the survivor may live in the house for life, with the trustee paying expenses, and that after the second death the house is sold and the proceeds divided as agreed. Because both signed the trust, the survivor could not quietly change the deceased spouse's half of the plan afterwards, if the trust was drafted to prevent it.
- **Matching wills**, drafted by the same lawyer at the same time, with life-estate provisions or a testamentary trust for the survivor. Cheaper, but they need probate.
- **A conversation about how title is held.** California also allows spouses to hold property as "community property with right of survivorship" (Civil Code § 682.1), which passes the whole to the survivor without probate. That would have given George the whole house and left Hannah nothing, which was not what Patricia wanted, but at least it would have been a deliberate choice rather than a surprise.

**The German version.** Germany does not use community property by default. Most married couples live under the community of accrued gains, in which each spouse keeps their own property during the marriage and the gains are balanced at the end. A house in both names is usually simple co-ownership, each spouse owning a half. Patricia's half would then pass under her will, just as in California, and the result could be very similar. That is why German couples in second marriages so often make a joint will or a notarised inheritance contract, and why German notaries ask, early and bluntly, "and what about the children from the first marriage?"

**Questions to ask as a couple.**

- Which of our assets are community, which are separate, and how is each one titled?
- If one of us dies, where does the survivor live, and on what money?
- After both of us die, who gets what, and do we both agree?
- Can the survivor change the plan for the first spouse's half? Do we want that?

**What to do.**

- In a community property state, remember that your will controls your half of the community property, not the whole.
- In a second marriage, plan as a couple, with one lawyer, in one room, at the same time.
- Decide explicitly where the surviving spouse will live and for how long.
- Put the plan in documents that bind both halves of the estate, not in a conversation over wine.

### Case 10: The house given away eight years before

**The situation.** Friedrich was a retired carpenter in a village near Regensburg, a widower with two children. His son Jonas, a quiet man who worked for the local water authority, had never moved far; he lived in the granny flat behind his father's house and, when Friedrich's heart began to fail, became his carer. For the last six years Jonas did the shopping, drove his father to appointments, managed the medications, and sat up with him on bad nights. His sister Sophie lived in Hamburg, a successful architect with two children and a demanding job. She visited at Christmas and for Friedrich's birthday and called every Sunday.

Eight years before he died, while he still felt reasonably well, Friedrich went to the notary and gave Jonas the house by notarial deed. He was proud of having done it. "Jonas has earned it," he told his friends, "and the rest will go to Sophie." The rest was a savings account worth about 60,000 euros. The house was worth about 480,000.

Two years later Friedrich made a handwritten will naming Jonas his sole heir, "to keep things simple," on the theory that the paperwork would be easier with one heir and Jonas would hand Sophie the savings.

Friedrich died at eighty-three.

**What the family assumed.** Jonas assumed the house was his, beyond question, since he had owned it for eight years and was on the land register. He assumed Sophie would receive the savings, as their father had said. Sophie, who had always known about the house and had never complained about it, assumed the same.

**What actually happened, and why.** In Germany, a testator cannot simply write a child out of the estate. A child who is excluded by a will (and naming Jonas sole heir excluded Sophie) has a compulsory share, the *Pflichtteil*: a money claim against the heir worth half of the share the child would have received without a will (BGB § 2303). With two children and no surviving spouse, Sophie's statutory share would have been one half. Her compulsory share was therefore one quarter of the value of the estate.

The estate itself was only the savings account, 60,000 euros. A quarter of that was 15,000. If that had been the whole story, Sophie would have had a modest claim, and Jonas would have handed her the savings anyway.

But German law does not let a parent empty the estate by giving things away shortly before death. Under § 2325, gifts made in the ten years before the death are added back to the estate for the purpose of calculating the compulsory share. The add-back shrinks over time: a gift in the last year counts in full, and for each year that passes, one tenth less counts. The house had been given eight years before Friedrich's death, so two tenths of its value, about 96,000 euros, was added back. (There is an important exception that did not apply here: for gifts to a spouse, the ten-year clock does not start running until the marriage ends.)

So Sophie's compulsory share was calculated on 60,000 plus 96,000: a quarter of 156,000, about 39,000 euros, payable in cash by Jonas.

Jonas did not have 39,000 euros. He had a house and a modest salary. There was a further twist that could have helped him: German law can take long-term care by a child into account when shares among the children are calculated (BGB § 2057a), and that can carry over into the calculation of a sibling's compulsory share (§ 2316). Whether and how much it applied when Jonas was already the sole heir, and what his years of care were worth in money, are exactly the questions on which lawyers and courts disagree. Jonas and Sophie each hired one. It took a year and a half to settle.

Sophie, who had never wanted to take anything from her brother, told him at the end that she would have signed anything their father asked her to sign, if only he had asked.

**What would have prevented it.** At the time of the gift, the notary could and should have discussed the compulsory share consequences with Friedrich. Several options were available.

- **A waiver of the compulsory share.** German law allows a relative to renounce their compulsory share by notarised contract with the person whose estate it is (§§ 2346 and 2348). Sophie could have waived her claim, perhaps in return for a payment or another asset, perhaps simply because she agreed that Jonas had earned the house. Such waivers are common in German family planning. They must be agreed during the parent's lifetime.
- **Making the gift earlier.** After ten full years, the gift drops out of the calculation entirely.
- **Writing the care into the documents.** The deed and the will could have recorded explicitly what Jonas's care was worth and that the gift was in part payment for it. A gift that is partly a payment for services may count differently from a pure gift.
- **Planning for liquidity.** If a compulsory share claim was going to arise anyway, Friedrich could have made sure there was cash to pay it: a life insurance policy for Jonas's benefit, for example.

**The plain-language version.** In Germany, close family members have a floor under their inheritance. You can lower what they get, but you cannot take it below the floor without their agreement. Gifts in the last ten years are counted back in, a little less each year. If you want to treat children unequally, even for very good reasons, the time to sort it out is while you are alive and everyone can sit at the same table.

**The California version.** California has no compulsory share for children. A California parent can disinherit an adult child entirely, as long as the will makes the intention clear. That freedom is real, and it is also why California wills are more often challenged on other grounds, such as lack of capacity or undue influence, by the children who were left out. Freedom to disinherit is not freedom from family disputes.

**What to do.**

- If you are in Germany and want to treat your children unequally, see a notary about compulsory shares before you give anything away.
- Ask the child who will receive less whether they would sign a waiver, and what would make it fair.
- Write down why you are doing what you are doing. Appendix G has a template for an unequal-gift letter.
- If one child is caring for you, record it: dates, hours, what was done. It may matter later, and it will matter to them now that you noticed.
