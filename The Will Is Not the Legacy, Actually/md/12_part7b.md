## Chapter 29. The Video Diary: Recording Over Months and Years

A single message is a letter. A diary is a relationship. If you have time, the diary style leaves something richer than any one message can: a person changing, noticing, joking, worrying, and getting on with life.

### Make it a habit, not a project

- **Pick a rhythm you can keep.** Once a week is sustainable for most people. Once a day is admirable and usually abandoned by February. Once a month is fine.
- **Pick a time and place.** Sunday morning, at the kitchen table, with coffee. The habit matters more than the content.
- **Keep it short.** Five minutes. If you have more to say, record a second clip.
- **Lower the bar.** The diary does not have to be interesting today. It has to exist. Over a year, the boring clips become the most precious ones, because they show what an ordinary week was like.

### What to talk about

- What happened this week, and what you thought about it.
- Something you noticed: a bird, a conversation, a smell that reminded you of something.
- The news, from your point of view. In thirty years, your family will be fascinated by what you thought of events that will by then be history.
- A memory that surfaced.
- A question you are thinking about.
- One prompt from Chapter 28, if nothing else comes.
- How you are feeling, if you want to say. If you are ill, the diary can hold the truth of it in a way that conversations sometimes cannot.

### Seasonal and annual recordings

Some families record a short message on each birthday, or at New Year, or on the first day of each school year. Over decades, these become extraordinary. A child who receives "you at five, six, seven..." through a parent's eyes has something no photograph album can match. If you start this for your children, film them too, and ask them questions. Their answers at seven are comedy gold at thirty.

### Interview sessions

The diary can include longer sessions in which a family member interviews you. Grandchildren are often the best interviewers: they ask the questions adults are too polite to ask. "Did you ever get in trouble with the police?" "What was the worst thing you ate in the war?" Let them.

## Chapter 30. Organising, Storing, and Keeping It Alive

A hundred recordings on a phone that nobody can open are not a legacy. They are a locked box. This chapter is about making sure the recordings outlive the device, the account, and you.

### Name every file

Rename each file when you copy it off the device, using a consistent pattern:

> `2026-10-10 Margaret - for Leo - wedding message.mp4`
>
> `2026-10-12 Margaret - diary - the plum tree.mp4`
>
> `2026-11-02 Margaret - story - how we bought the house.m4a`

The date first, in year-month-day order, so the files sort in time order on any computer. Then who, what, and for whom. Avoid special characters other than hyphens and spaces.

### Organise in folders

A simple structure:

- `Recordings/Diary/2026/`
- `Recordings/Messages/For Leo/`
- `Recordings/Messages/For Anna/`
- `Recordings/Messages/Occasions/` (weddings, births, graduations)
- `Recordings/Stories/`
- `Recordings/Practical/` (recipes, how the house works)
- `Recordings/Sealed/` (to be opened at a specific time; see below)

### Keep an index

A single document, `Recordings - index`, listing each file: date, title, who it is for, a one-line summary, and any instructions ("play at Leo's wedding," "open on Anna's 30th birthday," "private: Anna only"). This index is what your archive-keeper will use. Without it, they will have to watch everything to find anything, and many will stop after the first ten files because it is too painful.

### Choose formats that will still open

File formats age. A recording made in an obscure format may not play in thirty years. To give your recordings the best chance:

- **Video:** MP4 with standard video and audio settings is the most widely supported format today. Phones usually record in something compatible, but some use newer high-efficiency settings that older devices cannot play; if your phone offers a "most compatible" option, use it for recordings you intend to keep.
- **Audio:** M4A, MP3, or WAV. WAV is uncompressed and large but extremely durable; MP3 plays almost anywhere.
- **Documents:** PDF for the index and transcripts, with a plain text copy as well.
- **Photos:** JPEG for broad compatibility.

Every few years, check that the files still open on a current device. If a format is becoming obsolete, convert copies.

### Back up: three copies, two kinds, one away

A common rule of thumb for anything precious:

- **Three copies** of every recording.
- On at least **two different kinds** of storage (for example, a computer and an external drive, or a drive and a cloud service).
- With at least **one copy away from home**, so that a fire or flood does not take everything (Case 14 lost a will to a flooded cellar; recordings drown just as easily).

In practice, many people use: the original on the computer; a copy on an external drive kept at home; and a copy in a cloud storage account. Some also give a drive to a family member in another city.

Cloud storage is convenient but depends on someone continuing to pay for it and being able to get into the account after your death. A drive in a drawer depends on someone knowing it is there and on the drive still working. Each covers the other's weaknesses. Drives fail with age; replace or recopy them every few years.

### Keep it accessible after death

This is where most video legacies fail. The recordings exist; nobody can get to them.

1. **Name an archive-keeper.** One person who will hold the recordings, carry out your instructions, and share them as you wished. They may be your executor or someone else (Chapter 1, Day 1).
2. **Tell them where everything is.** Write it on the Family List (Part Six, section 14).
3. **Give them access.** Through the password manager's emergency access, a legacy-contact setting on the cloud service and the phone, or a drive they already hold. Chapter 24 explains how to pass on access without writing passwords in plain text.
4. **Turn on legacy settings.** Several major phone, photo, and cloud services offer a legacy-contact or inactive-account setting, which lets a person you name download your data after your death. Check the services you use, turn the setting on, and tell the person you named. Under California's digital-assets law, a direction given through such a tool can override a contrary instruction in your will (Probate Code § 873), so make the tool and the will agree.
5. **Keep the subscription paid.** Tell your executor that the cloud storage holding the recordings must not be cancelled until everything has been downloaded.
6. **Give a copy now.** The simplest protection of all: give your archive-keeper a drive with a copy of everything, today, and update it once a year. They cannot be locked out of something they already have.

### Sealed and timed messages

For messages meant to be opened later ("for Leo on his wedding day," "for Anna at thirty"):

- Put them in the `Sealed` folder, with the opening instruction in the file name and the index.
- Tell the archive-keeper, in writing, what the instructions are.
- If you use a service that promises to release messages on a future date, keep a copy elsewhere as well. Companies close, change their terms, and are bought. A twenty-year promise from a five-year-old company deserves a backup.
- Consider that the circumstances may change. A wedding message assumes a wedding. Add a line to the index: "If Leo does not marry, give him this on his thirtieth birthday instead," or simply, "Use your judgment."

### Transcripts

A transcript makes recordings searchable and gives deaf or hard-of-hearing relatives full access. Many phones and computers now offer automatic transcription. Check it for errors (it will mishear names) and save it as a PDF and plain text next to the recording, with the same file name.

## Chapter 31. Why a Video Is Not a Will

This chapter exists because people keep trying. They record a clear, sincere video saying who should get the house, and they believe they have made a will. In California and in Germany, they have not.

### California

California law requires a will to be in writing (Probate Code § 6110). A formal will must be signed and witnessed by at least two people present at the same time. A holographic will needs no witnesses, but its signature and material provisions must be in the testator's handwriting (§ 6111). A video is not writing and not handwriting. It is not a valid will in California, no matter how clearly you speak or how many people are in the room.

The statute's narrow rescue for defective witnessing, the "clear and convincing evidence" rule in § 6110(c)(2), applies to a will that was *in writing and signed* but not properly witnessed. It does not turn a recording into a will.

### Germany

German law allows a will in ordinary form only before a notary or entirely handwritten and signed by the testator (BGB §§ 2231, 2232, 2247). The emergency forms for people in danger of death require a mayor and two witnesses or three witnesses, and even then a written record must be made (§§ 2249, 2250). A video is none of these. It is not a valid will in Germany.

### What happens to the video, then

If you leave only a video, your estate passes as if you had no will, or under your last valid written will, whatever the video says (Case 23). Your family may agree to honour the video voluntarily, if every heir is an adult and willing. Often they will not, or not all of them will, and the ones who benefit from the statute have no legal obligation to give anything up.

### How video helps a valid will

Used alongside a proper will, video is valuable.

- **It explains.** A will says what; a video can say why. A calm, kind explanation of an unequal gift, recorded by you, can prevent a misunderstanding from becoming a feud. Keep the explanation consistent with the will.
- **It shows your state of mind.** Some lawyers record, or recommend recording, a short conversation at the signing of a will in which the testator explains the plan in their own words, as evidence of capacity and freedom from pressure. Whether that is wise depends on your health and your family; a hesitant recording can also be used against a will. Ask your lawyer before you do it, and let them decide how.
- **It comforts.** The will is read by a lawyer. The video is watched by the family. They need both.

### The rule

> **Record your face for the people you love. Put your wishes on paper for the court.**

If a recording says anything about property, add a sentence at the start: "This recording is not my will. My will is dated [date] and is held by [person]. If anything I say here differs from my will, follow the will." That one sentence turns a potential family argument into a family heirloom.

## Chapter 32. Consent and Privacy

Recording yourself is simple. Recording other people, and deciding who may see what after you are gone, needs more care.

### Recording other people

If you film conversations with family members (an interview with your father, a dinner, a grandchild's questions), ask first. It is courteous, and it is often the law.

- **California** makes it an offence to record a *confidential* communication without the consent of all parties (Penal Code § 632). A conversation at home with family can easily be confidential. Ask everyone, and record the "yes" at the start of the clip.
- **Germany** makes it an offence to record another person's privately spoken words without authorisation (StGB § 201). Again: ask, and record the consent.

Publishing images is a separate question. In Germany, pictures of a person may generally be published only with their consent, and for ten years after the person's death the consent of their close relatives is needed (Art Copyright Act, *KunstUrhG* § 22), subject to exceptions. If your family archive includes other people, assume that sharing it beyond the family needs their agreement, or their relatives'.

For children, ask the parents, and think about the child as a future adult who may not want a clip of themselves at nine shared with anyone.

### Who may see your recordings

Decide, and write it down. Appendix H is a permission sheet. The main choices:

- **Family only.** Shared among named people; never posted publicly.
- **Specific people only.** "The message for Anna is for Anna."
- **Wider sharing allowed.** May be shown at a memorial, posted on a family website, or used in a family history.
- **Destroy.** Some clips you may want deleted after your death, or after a certain time. Say which.

Without instructions, your archive-keeper will have to guess, and the safest guess is to share nothing, which is probably not what you want.

### Privacy law and family archives

The European General Data Protection Regulation does not apply to processing of personal data by a person in the course of a purely personal or household activity (Article 2(2)(c)). Keeping family videos privately is generally in that category. Publishing them on the open internet is generally not. If you plan to make recordings public, or if your archive-keeper does, think about the other people who appear in them.

### Privacy of the dead

In Germany, the Federal Court of Justice held in 2018 that a social network account passes to the heirs like any other contract, and that the heirs can access its contents, including private messages (BGH, judgment of 12 July 2018, III ZR 183/17). In California, the digital-assets law requires a provider to disclose the content of a deceased user's electronic communications to the personal representative if the user consented or a court directs it (Probate Code § 876); otherwise the representative may get only a catalogue of messages without their content (§ 877). Either way, assume that whatever is in your accounts may be read by your heirs. If there is something you do not want read, delete it yourself, or give clear written instructions.

### Protect the people who cannot answer

A recording outlives the argument it was made in. Before you record something harsh about a living person, ask who will watch it, when, and what it will do to them. Anger recorded on a bad day can wound for decades. If something has to be said, write it, put it away for a week, and then decide.

### Security

Recordings that include health information, family conflicts, or financial details should be stored securely: encrypted drives, accounts with strong passwords and two-factor authentication, and copies in the hands of trusted people only.

### Deepfakes and impersonation

Hours of clear recordings of your face and voice could, in principle, be used to imitate you. This is not a reason not to record; it is a reason to keep the archive private unless you choose otherwise, and to tell your family that any "new" message from you after your death is not from you. A family code word, known to a few people, is an old-fashioned and effective defence against impersonation of the living, too.
