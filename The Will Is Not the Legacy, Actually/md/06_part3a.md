# Part Three: The Will, Drafted in Public

*Educational guide, not legal advice. The sample clauses below are illustrations written to be read, not forms to be signed. A will must fit your family, your assets, and the law where you live.*

## Chapter 8. What a Will Is For

A will is a signed set of instructions, valid only if it meets the formal rules of the place whose law applies, about who receives what you own at death, who carries out those instructions, and sometimes who cares for your children. Until your death it does nothing at all. You can rewrite it as often as you like, provided you still have the capacity to make a will. In California that means being at least eighteen and of sound mind; in Germany a person can make a will from the age of sixteen (BGB § 2229), although a minor cannot use the handwritten form (§ 2247) and must go to a notary.

Marriage, divorce, a birth, a move across a border, and large gifts can change what a will means even if you never touch the paper. That is the first thing most people do not know about wills: they are not frozen. They are read on the day you die, against the facts and the law of that day.

### What a will is good for

- Naming an executor and a backup.
- Saying who receives property that does not already pass by beneficiary form, title, contract, or trust.
- Naming guardians for minor children, where the law lets a will do that. In California, a will can nominate a guardian; the court makes the appointment and generally follows the parent's nomination unless it is not in the child's interest. In Germany, parents can name a guardian (*Vormund*) for their children in a will.
- Revoking older wills.
- Leaving specific gifts: a watch, a manuscript, a sum of money.
- Creating a simple trust inside the will (a testamentary trust) for a child or anyone who should not receive a lump sum. This needs careful drafting.
- In Germany, choosing the law of your nationality to govern your estate, if you are a national of a country other than the one you live in (EU Succession Regulation, Article 22).

### What a will does not do

- It does not control life insurance with a named beneficiary, most retirement accounts, or payable-on-death accounts. The form on file wins.
- It does not control property in joint tenancy with right of survivorship. The survivor takes it.
- It does not let anyone act for you while you are alive.
- It does not avoid probate. In California, a will is the document probate carries out. In Germany, heirs usually need a certificate of inheritance or a notarial will plus the court's opening record to prove they are heirs.
- It does not explain you. Explanations belong in a letter beside it.
- It cannot always disinherit people. In California, a spouse has rights in community property that a will cannot override. In Germany, children, the spouse, and in some cases the parents have a compulsory share (*Pflichtteil*) of half their statutory share if they are cut out (BGB § 2303).

### Probate in one paragraph each

**California.** Probate is the court-supervised process of proving a will, appointing the executor (or an administrator if there is no will), collecting assets, paying debts and taxes, and distributing the rest. It is public, it takes many months at best, and it costs money. The statutory attorney fee for ordinary services is a percentage of the estate: 4 percent of the first 100,000 dollars, 3 percent of the next 100,000, 2 percent of the next 800,000, 1 percent of the next nine million, and so on (Probate Code § 10810), and the executor is entitled to a fee on the same scale. On a 900,000-dollar estate that is 21,000 dollars for the lawyer and the same again for the executor, if the executor takes it, before any extraordinary work. Much of a modern estate escapes probate by passing through beneficiary forms, joint title, trusts, and the simplified small-estate procedures described in Chapter 17.

**Germany.** There is no American-style probate. The estate passes as a whole to the heirs at the moment of death (BGB § 1922). The heirs are liable for the estate's debts (§ 1967), which is why German law gives an heir six weeks to disclaim an inheritance after learning of it (§ 1944), or six months if the deceased's last residence was only abroad or the heir is abroad when the period starts. The probate court (*Nachlassgericht*) opens wills, issues certificates of inheritance (*Erbschein*), and deals with disputes, but it does not run the estate. The heirs, or an executor if the will appoints one, do that.

## Chapter 9. A Sample Will, Annotated

What follows is a fictional will for a fictional Californian, Susan Example, a widow with two adult children and one grandchild. It is written in plain English to show what each clause does. Real wills drafted by lawyers are usually longer, because they handle more contingencies. The annotations explain why each clause is there and what can go wrong. For a German will, the same ideas apply, but the form is different: see Chapter 11.

### Article 1: Declaration and revocation

> I, Susan Example, of Sacramento County, California, declare this to be my will. I revoke all earlier wills and codicils.

**Why it is there.** The first sentence identifies the testator and states testamentary intent: this document is meant to be a will. The second cancels every earlier will. Without it, an older will might be read together with the new one, and the courts would have to work out which parts survive.

**What goes wrong.** If you have a will in another country covering property there, a blanket revocation may cancel it by accident. Tell your lawyer about every will you have ever signed, anywhere. If you want to keep a foreign will, the new will should say so: "I revoke all earlier wills except my German will dated 1 March 2024, which deals only with my property in Germany."

### Article 2: Family

> I am not married. My husband, Michael Example, died in 2019. I have two children, Laura Example and David Example. My son Peter Example died in 2010 and is survived by his daughter, Emma Example. All references to "my children" are to Laura and David.

**Why it is there.** It lets the court see who the natural heirs are and confirms you have not forgotten anyone. Naming a deceased child and his descendant shows you considered them.

**What goes wrong.** Forgetting a child, or failing to mention a child born after the will was signed. California has rules for children born or adopted after a will is made and not provided for (Probate Code § 21620), and for a spouse married after the documents were signed (§ 21610). Updating the will after every birth, adoption, and marriage avoids leaving these questions to the court.

### Article 3: Executor

> I nominate my daughter, Laura Example, as executor. If she is unable or unwilling to serve, I nominate my son, David Example. No bond shall be required of any executor named in this will. My executor may administer my estate under the Independent Administration of Estates Act.

**Why it is there.** The executor gathers assets, pays debts and taxes, and distributes the rest. A backup protects against the first choice dying, moving, or refusing. Waiving the bond saves the cost of an insurance-like guarantee the court would otherwise require. Authorising independent administration allows the executor to take many actions (selling property, for example) with less court supervision.

**What goes wrong.** Naming someone who lives far away, does not get on with the siblings, or was never asked. A surprised executor is an executor who declines. Ask first.

### Article 4: Specific gifts

> I give my wedding ring to my granddaughter, Emma Example, if she survives me. I give my piano to my son, David Example, if he survives me. If either gift fails, the item becomes part of my residuary estate.

**Why it is there.** Specific gifts settle the objects people care about most, which are often worth the least.

**What goes wrong.** If you no longer own the item when you die, the gift fails. Lawyers call this ademption. Families argue about whether a new piano was meant to replace the old one. The clause also says what happens if the gift fails, which avoids that argument.

**A California shortcut.** Probate Code § 6132 lets a will refer to a separate signed and dated writing that disposes of tangible personal property, such as jewellery and furniture, which you can update without re-signing the will. The writing can be handwritten or signed by you. The total value of property passing under it cannot exceed 25,000 dollars, and any single item worth more than 5,000 dollars is excluded and passes under the will instead. It is a useful way to handle the cutlery without calling the lawyer every time a grandchild expresses an interest in a teapot.

### Article 5: Cash gifts

> I give 10,000 dollars to the Sacramento Public Library Foundation. I give 5,000 dollars to my neighbour, Jane Neighbour, if she survives me. If my estate is not large enough to pay all cash gifts in full, they shall be reduced in proportion.

**Why it is there.** Cash gifts are simple to understand and easy to carry out.

**What goes wrong.** Cash gifts are paid before the residue. If the estate shrinks (because you spent the money on care, which is what money is for), the cash gifts can swallow the residue, leaving your main heirs with less than the neighbour. The final sentence says what happens if there is not enough. An alternative is to express gifts as percentages.

### Article 6: Residuary estate

> I give the rest of my estate, including all property not effectively disposed of above, in equal shares to my children who survive me, and one equal share to the descendants of any child who does not survive me, by right of representation. I give an equal share to my granddaughter, Emma Example, in place of her deceased father.

**Why it is there.** The residue is everything left after debts, expenses, and specific and cash gifts. In most estates the residue is the main event. Most will disputes are really disputes about this clause and about the forms the will never sees.

**What goes wrong.** Forgetting to say what happens if a beneficiary dies first. "To my children equally" needs a sentence about a child who dies before you: does that share go to the child's children, or back into the pot for the others? The clause above sends it to the child's descendants.

### Article 7: Survival requirement

> A beneficiary who does not survive me by 30 days shall be treated as having died before me.

**Why it is there.** If a beneficiary dies shortly after you (for example, in the same accident), the property would otherwise go through their estate too, to their heirs, after two sets of administration. A survival period sends it straight to your alternate beneficiary.

### Article 8: Gifts to young people

> If any property passes under this will to a person under 25, my executor may hold it in a separate trust for that person, with my daughter Laura as trustee, and may use income and principal for the person's health, education, and support. The trustee shall distribute the remaining property to the person at age 25.

**Why it is there.** Emma is fourteen. Without this clause, a court might need to appoint someone to manage her inheritance, and she would receive it outright at eighteen.

**What goes wrong.** Choosing an age without thinking about the beneficiary. Twenty-five is common; thirty is not unusual; some trusts distribute in thirds at different ages. And a child with a disability who receives means-tested public benefits may lose them if they inherit outright; that needs a special-needs trust, not this clause (Chapter 19).

### Article 9: Taxes and debts

> My executor shall pay my debts, funeral expenses, and administration costs from my residuary estate. Any estate taxes shall be paid from my residuary estate without apportionment.

**Why it is there.** Without it, there can be arguments about which gifts bear which costs. In practice, few estates owe federal estate tax: for deaths in 2026, the basic exclusion amount is 15 million dollars per person, according to the IRS. California has no separate estate tax of its own. But the clause matters if you leave large gifts outside the will, such as life insurance, and want to say who bears any tax.

### Article 10: Executor's powers

> My executor shall have all powers granted by law, including the power to sell, lease, or mortgage any property, to deal with my digital assets and electronic communications, and to continue any business I own.

**Why it is there.** The more powers the will grants, the fewer times the executor must ask the court for permission. Expressly granting authority over digital assets helps under California's digital-assets law (Probate Code §§ 870 and following), which lets a will authorise disclosure of electronic communications content to the executor, unless the user directed otherwise in the provider's own online tool.

### Article 11: No-contest clause (optional)

> If any beneficiary directly contests this will without probable cause, that beneficiary's gift shall be revoked.

**Why it is there, and when to leave it out.** A no-contest clause discourages challenges. In California it is enforced only against a direct contest brought without probable cause, and in a few other narrow situations if the clause expressly covers them (Probate Code § 21311). It works best when the person who might contest receives enough under the will to make the risk unattractive. If you leave someone nothing, the clause has nothing to threaten. And it can feel like an accusation when read aloud to a grieving family. Leave it out if the family is harmonious and the gifts are equal. Put it in, with care, if you foresee a challenge and are leaving the likely challenger a meaningful gift.

### Article 12: Signature and witnesses

> I sign this will on 10 October 2026 at Sacramento, California.
>
> [Signature of Susan Example]
>
> On the date written above, Susan Example declared to us that this document is her will and asked us to act as witnesses. She signed it in our presence, all of us being present at the same time. We now sign as witnesses in her presence and in the presence of each other. Each of us is over 18 and is not a beneficiary under this will. We declare under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California that the foregoing is true and correct.
>
> [Signatures, names, and addresses of two witnesses]

**Why it is there.** California requires at least two witnesses, present at the same time, who understand that the document is the testator's will (Probate Code § 6110). The declaration under penalty of perjury lets the will be proved later without the witnesses appearing in court in many cases.

**What goes wrong.** Witnesses who arrive one at a time. Witnesses who are beneficiaries (Case 13). Signing pages out of order, stapling and unstapling, or adding handwritten notes after signing. Keep the signing ceremonial and boring.

## Chapter 10. One Estate, Three Plans

Here is a fictional estate, planned three ways. The point is not that one plan is right. It is that the same family can be well served by quite different tools, and that each tool has a different failure mode.

### The estate

Robert and Helen Sample, married, live in San Diego. They have two adult children, Chris and Jordan. Jordan has a teenage son, Sam. Their assets:

- A house worth 1.2 million dollars, community property, no mortgage.
- Two 401(k)/IRA accounts, together about 900,000 dollars.
- A joint brokerage account, 250,000 dollars.
- Checking and savings, 60,000 dollars.
- Life insurance on Robert, 500,000 dollars.
- Two cars.
- A small rental condominium in Arizona, worth 350,000 dollars.

Their goal: everything to the survivor; after both deaths, equal shares to Chris and Jordan, with Jordan's share going to Sam if Jordan dies first, held in trust until Sam is twenty-five.

### Plan one: simple wills plus correct forms

Each spouse signs a will leaving everything to the other, then to the children equally, with a trust for any grandchild under twenty-five. Each updates the 401(k)/IRA beneficiary forms: primary beneficiary, the spouse; contingent, the two children equally. Robert's life insurance: primary Helen; contingent, the children.

**How it works.** At the first death, the retirement accounts and life insurance pass to the survivor by form. The house, as community property, can pass to the surviving spouse through a simplified spousal property petition rather than full probate, as the California courts' self-help pages describe. At the second death, the forms send the retirement money to the children.

**Where it fails.** The second death. The house, the brokerage account, and anything else without a beneficiary must go through full California probate, with statutory fees based on the gross value. And the Arizona condominium requires a separate probate proceeding in Arizona (often called ancillary probate), because real property is generally administered where it is located. Two courts, two sets of fees, two sets of delays.

**When it is good enough.** A smaller estate with no out-of-state property, or a family that does not mind probate.

### Plan two: wills plus beneficiary designations on everything possible

The same wills, but with every asset that can carry a beneficiary given one: payable-on-death designations on the bank accounts; transfer-on-death registration on the brokerage account; a California revocable transfer-on-death deed on the house, effective after the second death; an Arizona beneficiary deed on the condominium (Arizona has its own statutory version; check its requirements); retirement accounts and insurance with primary and contingent beneficiaries as in plan one.

**How it works.** Almost everything passes outside probate at the second death. The will catches only the cars and stray items, which may fit under California's small-estate procedures.

**Where it fails.** Coordination. Each designation is a separate document with separate rules, and each must name contingent beneficiaries correctly. None of them can hold Sam's share in trust if Jordan dies first; a TOD designation to a minor creates exactly the problem in Case 5. Some designations may not allow per-stirpes ("by family line") options. And the California TOD deed statute is set to expire on 1 January 2032 unless extended, although deeds signed before then stay valid. Every new account must be set up correctly. It is a plan that works perfectly on the day it is finished and then decays.

**When it is good.** Moderate estates, simple families, and people who are organised enough to update forms.

### Plan three: a funded revocable trust with pour-over wills

Robert and Helen create a joint revocable living trust, naming themselves as trustees and Chris as successor trustee. They retitle the house, the brokerage account, the bank accounts, and the Arizona condominium into the trust. The trust says: while both are alive, they control everything; after the first death, the survivor controls everything; after the second death, divide equally between Chris and Jordan, with Jordan's share going into a continuing trust for Sam if Jordan has died. The retirement accounts name the spouse as primary beneficiary and, after careful advice about tax, either the children or the trust as contingent. Each spouse signs a short "pour-over" will that leaves anything outside the trust to the trust.

**How it works.** At the second death, Chris, as successor trustee, administers everything in the trust privately, without court supervision, in both California and Arizona. Sam's share is held in trust automatically.

**Where it fails.** Funding. A trust controls only what it owns. If Robert buys a new brokerage account in his own name and forgets to title it to the trust, that account goes through probate under the pour-over will before it reaches the trust. Plan three also costs more to set up, and someone has to keep it funded.

**When it is good.** Larger estates, real estate in more than one state, minor or vulnerable beneficiaries, families who value privacy, and anyone who expects incapacity (a successor trustee can step in without a court if the trust provides for it).

### The lesson of three plans

| | Plan one | Plan two | Plan three |
|---|---|---|---|
| Set-up cost | Low | Low | Higher |
| Probate at second death | Yes, plus Arizona | Mostly avoided | Avoided for funded assets |
| Holds a minor's share in trust | Yes, via will | No | Yes |
| Main failure mode | Probate cost and delay | Forms drift out of date | Assets left outside the trust |
| Privacy | Low | Medium | High |

The best plan is the one you will keep up to date.
