# Part Nine: The Receiver's Half

*Educational guide, not legal advice. If you have just lost someone, you do not need to read all of this today. Read the first section, do what it says, and come back later. Appendix B is the same material as a printable checklist.*

## Chapter 37. The First Ninety Days

This chapter is written for the person who was named: the executor, the heir, the archive-keeper, or simply the family member who ended up holding the folder. Give it to them in advance, if you are the planner. If you are the receiver, start here.

### The first days

**Do not rush.** Very little has to be done in the first forty-eight hours, and almost nothing has to be decided. The exceptions are below.

1. **The death certificate.** In California, a doctor or coroner certifies the death and the funeral home usually files the death certificate and can order certified copies for you. In Germany, the death must be registered with the local registry office (*Standesamt*), usually by the funeral home, which can obtain copies of the death certificate (*Sterbeurkunde*). Order several certified copies. Banks, insurers, pension offices, and courts each want their own, and some keep them.
2. **Wishes about the body.** Check the Family List and any directive for wishes about organ donation, burial or cremation, and religious rites. Organ donation decisions are time-critical.
3. **Secure the home, the car, and pets.** Lock up. Collect valuables in plain view. Feed the animals, the same day. Arrange for mail to be collected or forwarded.
4. **Tell the people who need to know.** Close family, the employer if relevant, and the people named on the first page of the Family List.
5. **Find the documents.** Will, trust, powers of attorney (which end at death), the Family List, insurance policies. Do not write on the original will. Do not throw anything away.

### The first two weeks

6. **The will goes to the court.** In California, whoever has the original will must deliver it to the clerk of the superior court in the county where the estate will be administered within thirty days of learning of the death (Probate Code § 8200), unless a probate petition has already been filed. In Germany, anyone holding a will not already in official custody must deliver it to the probate court without delay (BGB § 2259); wills in official custody are found through the Central Register of Wills, which is notified of deaths. The German court opens the will and notifies the people concerned.
7. **The funeral.** Pay attention to cost; in many families, the funeral is the first large expense and is paid in a rush. The estate can usually reimburse reasonable funeral costs later, so keep the receipts.
8. **The lawyer or notary.** If there is a lawyer named on the Family List, call them. Ask whether probate (California) or a certificate of inheritance (Germany) will be needed, and what it will cost.
9. **Benefits and pensions.** Notify Social Security (United States) or the statutory pension insurer (Germany), and any employer or private pension. Payments made after the death may have to be returned; survivors may be entitled to new payments that must be applied for.

### In Germany: the six-week clock

German heirs inherit debts as well as assets (BGB § 1967). An heir who does not want to inherit, for example because the estate is overindebted, must disclaim the inheritance within six weeks (§ 1944). The period starts when the heir learns of the inheritance and the reason for it; for heirs named in a will, not before the court has announced the will. It is six months if the deceased's last residence was only abroad or the heir is abroad when the period begins. The disclaimer is made to the probate court in the prescribed form. If in doubt, take advice in the first weeks. Doing nothing means accepting.

A disclaimer passes the inheritance to whoever would be next in line, often the heir's own children, who may then need to disclaim too. Talk to the probate court or a lawyer before you act.

### In California: probate or not

10. **Work out what passes outside probate.** Beneficiary designations, joint tenancy, POD and TOD assets, and trust assets usually pass directly. The California courts' self-help pages explain the simpler procedures: for deaths on or after 1 April 2025, personal property up to 208,850 dollars can usually be collected by affidavit after forty days, and a primary residence up to 750,000 dollars can pass by a simpler court petition (Chapter 17).
11. **If probate is needed,** the executor files a petition, and the court issues "letters" that authorise the executor to act. Creditors then have, generally, until the later of four months after letters are first issued or sixty days after notice is mailed to them to file claims (Probate Code § 9100).
12. **If there is a trust,** the successor trustee takes over trust assets without court supervision, and must notify beneficiaries and heirs as the law requires. Ask a lawyer what notices are due and when.

### The first ninety days

13. **Make an inventory.** Use the Family List as your starting point. List every asset and debt, with values at the date of death. Keep statements.
14. **Open an estate account** once you have authority (letters in California, or the certificate of inheritance or equivalent proof in Germany), and run every estate payment through it. Do not mix estate money with your own.
15. **Notify institutions.** Banks, insurers, brokers, the tax authorities, credit card companies, utilities. Ask each what they need. Many have a bereavement team; ask for it by name.
16. **Do not distribute too early.** Debts, taxes, and expenses come first. An executor who pays out the inheritance and then finds a tax bill may be personally liable for it.
17. **Taxes.** The deceased's final income tax return is still due. Estates may have their own income tax filings. Few estates owe United States estate tax (the basic exclusion amount is 15 million dollars per person for 2026), but the executor of a married person's estate may want to file a federal estate tax return anyway, to preserve the unused exclusion for the surviving spouse; ask the tax adviser. In Germany, each heir may owe inheritance tax above their personal allowance (Chapter 15). An acquisition subject to inheritance tax must generally be reported in writing to the tax office within three months of learning of it (ErbStG § 30), subject to exceptions, for example where the court that opened the will has already informed the tax office. Take advice.
18. **Cancel what should be cancelled,** carefully. Not the cloud storage holding the photos and recordings until everything has been downloaded (step 21).

### Family pressure

Someone will want the watch now. Someone will ask when the money is coming. Someone will say, "Mum always said I could have the piano." Three rules help:

- **Nothing leaves the house until the will has been read and the inventory done**, except what the will specifically gives and the executor agrees to release.
- **Write things down.** Requests, promises, decisions.
- **When in doubt, wait.** Very few things get worse with a few weeks' delay. Relationships get worse with a hasty decision.

### When to hire the lawyer

Hire one, or see a notary in Germany, if any of these apply: there is real estate; there is a business; there are assets in more than one country or state; someone is challenging the will; the estate may be insolvent; there is a minor or disabled beneficiary; you are not sure whether you are allowed to do something. The cost of early advice is almost always smaller than the cost of a mistake.

### The meaning archive

19. **Open the recordings and letters only as the instructions say.** If a letter and the will disagree about property, the will and the titles win (Chapter 31). Keep the letter anyway; it may explain.
20. **Respect the permissions.** Share within what the permission sheet (Appendix H) allows. When in doubt, ask the other people named, or wait.
21. **Back up before you reorganise.** Copy every recording, photo, and document to two places before you rename, sort, or delete anything. Grief and accidental deletion go together.

## Chapter 38. Digital Access From the Other Side of the Lock

### What you can do with the right preparation

If the person who died followed Part Six:

- Use the password manager's emergency access to open the vault.
- Use the device passcodes to open the phone and the computer.
- Request data through any legacy-contact setting they turned on.
- Download the photos, recordings, and documents.
- Close, memorialise, or keep accounts as their instructions say.

### What you can do without it

If there was no preparation:

- **Ask each provider.** Most major services have a process for deceased users. They typically ask for a death certificate and proof of your authority (letters of administration in California, a certificate of inheritance in Germany). Some will close the account; some will release data; some will do neither without a court order.
- **California.** The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (Probate Code §§ 870 and following) gives a personal representative a framework for requesting access. Content of communications must be disclosed if the deceased consented or a court directs it (§ 876); otherwise, the provider may only have to provide a catalogue (§ 877). A direction the user gave through the provider's own online tool takes priority over the will (§ 873).
- **Germany.** The Federal Court of Justice held that a social-network account passes to the heirs, and that they are entitled to access it and its contents (BGH, 12 July 2018, III ZR 183/17). Heirs can rely on that when dealing with providers, although providers' processes vary.
- **Devices.** No law can decrypt a locked phone. If the passcode is lost, the data on the device may be lost too, although copies may exist in the cloud.

### What to export

Before closing anything, export:

- Photos and videos, at full resolution.
- Recordings and the index (Part Seven).
- Email folders that matter, especially anything with financial or legal correspondence.
- Contacts (you will need them for notifications).
- Documents and notes.
- Calendar entries (they reveal subscriptions, appointments, and obligations).

### What not to do

- Do not post on the deceased's social media as if you were them.
- Do not log into accounts in ways the provider's terms forbid, if you can avoid it; use the official deceased-user process. Logging in with a found password can be against the terms of service and, in some situations, the law.
- Do not delete anything until it has been exported and backed up.
- Do not share private messages beyond what the deceased permitted.

### Cryptocurrency and other bearer assets

If the deceased held cryptocurrency in self-custody, the will names who receives it, but access depends on the keys. Look for sealed instructions (Case 24). Be very careful: anyone who offers to "recover" crypto for an upfront fee is far more likely to be a fraudster than a rescuer.
