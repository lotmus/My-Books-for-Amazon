# Part Eight: Leaving Thoughts, at the Length of a Craft

*Educational guide, not legal advice. Nothing in this part transfers property. That is the point of it.*

## Chapter 33. The Ethical Will

An ethical will, sometimes called a legacy letter, is a statement of values, stories, and hopes. The practice is old in several religious traditions and has become common in secular life. It is not a legal document. Courts do not enforce it. That limitation is its freedom: you can be personal, contradictory, funny, and sorry without accidentally rewriting your estate.

### What it is for

- To tell the people you love what you believe and why.
- To pass on stories that explain the family.
- To say what you are grateful for and what you regret.
- To give permission: to grieve, to move on, to sell the house, to stop going to church, to start.
- To explain, gently, decisions that the legal documents make without explaining.

### What it is not for

- Giving away property. Put that in the will.
- Orders. "I expect you to become a doctor" ages badly. "I hope you find work you care about as much as I cared about bridges" does not.
- Settling scores.

### A workable shape

1. **Who you are writing to**, and a sentence saying this letter is not your will.
2. **A few scenes**, not an autobiography. Two or three moments that explain who you are.
3. **What you believe**, with the story of how you came to believe it.
4. **What you hope they understand** about a hard choice.
5. **Apologies that name the event.** Vague regret reads as performance.
6. **Gratitude**, specific.
7. **What you are not asking them to repeat.**
8. **Permission**: what they may share, publish, or destroy (Appendix H).

Two pages that are true will be reread. Twenty pages of advice will not.

### The same prompt, answered weakly and strongly

**Prompt: What do you want your children to know about how you lived?**

> **Weak:** "I always tried to do my best and be a good person. I hope you will do the same. Honesty and hard work are the most important things in life."

> **Strong:** "I was not always honest. In 1979 I let a colleague take the blame for a mistake that was mine, and he lost a promotion because of it. I told him eleven years later, at a retirement party, and he laughed and said he'd known all along. I have tried ever since to say 'that was me' quickly. It has never once been as bad as I feared. That is the only rule I am confident enough to pass on."

**Prompt: What are you grateful for?**

> **Weak:** "I am grateful for my wonderful family and all the blessings in my life."

> **Strong:** "I am grateful that your mother laughed at my jokes for forty-one years, including the bad ones, which was most of them. I am grateful to Mr Huber, who taught physics at my school and let me stay after class to break things. I am grateful for the winter of 1996, when we had no money and went sledding every evening because it was free, and you two still talk about it as the best winter."

**Prompt: What do you hope for them?**

> **Weak:** "I hope you will be happy and successful."

> **Strong:** "I hope you have at least one friend you can call at three in the morning, and that you are that friend for someone. I hope you are less worried about money than I was. I hope you go back to the lake."

The strong answers have names, dates, places, and the occasional admission. They sound like a person.

### A full sample ethical will

The following is fictional and offered as a model of tone and length.

> *To Sarah, Michael, and whoever comes after,*
>
> *This letter is not my will. My will is dated 4 March 2026 and is with our lawyer, whose details are on the family list. This letter is just me.*
>
> *I grew up above a bakery in a small town, and I still think the smell of bread at four in the morning is the smell of safety. My father worked from three until noon every day of my childhood and was asleep by seven. I thought he was a stranger until I was twelve, when I got up at three to help him, and found out he sang while he worked. Badly. He made me promise not to tell my mother. I am telling you now because it is the most important thing I know about him: he had a whole life I never saw, because I never got up early enough.*
>
> *So get up early, occasionally, for the people you love.*
>
> *The hardest choice I made was to move us to California in 2004. Michael, you were eleven and you hated it, and you told me so every day for a year. I want you to know I heard you. I did it because the job meant we could pay for both of you to go to university without debt, and because I was afraid of being my father, asleep by seven. I don't know if it was right. I know you made good friends there and that you still call the Pacific "my ocean."*
>
> *I am sorry, Sarah, for missing your recital in 2007. I said it was work. It was not work. I was afraid of hearing you play the piece your grandmother used to play, three months after she died. I should have told you that then. I'm telling you now.*
>
> *I am grateful for your mother, for thirty-eight years of her patience; for the dog, Bruno, who was a terrible dog and a wonderful friend; and for both of you, every day, including the days you did not speak to me.*
>
> *I don't want either of you to feel you have to keep the house. Sell it if it's a burden. Keep the bread knife, though. It was my father's.*
>
> *You may share this letter with your children when they are old enough. You may read it at my funeral if you want to, but you don't have to. The recordings in the "Dad" folder are for the family. Please don't put them on the internet. Delete the ones where I talk about the neighbours.*
>
> *I love you. Get up early, sometimes.*
>
> *Dad*

Notice what it does: it states that it is not a will and where the will is; tells a scene; explains a decision; makes a specific apology; expresses specific gratitude; releases them from an obligation; and gives clear permissions. It is under six hundred words.

## Chapter 34. Letters Beside the Will

### Explaining unequal gifts

You are usually free to treat your children differently in California. (Germany's compulsory shares limit how far you can go, Chapter 8.) If you do, explain it, but not in the will itself, where an explanation can be twisted into evidence in a challenge. Use a separate letter, factual and kind.

> **Usable:** "I am leaving the house to Sam because Sam has lived in it and maintained it since 2019, and because I helped Alex with tuition and with the deposit on the flat in 2021, which together came to about the value of Sam's extra share. I love you both equally. The money is not a measure of that."

> **Fuel:** "Alex never appreciated anything I did, so Sam gets the house."

Show the letter to your lawyer, so it does not contradict the will or create an argument that you were confused or pressured. If you also record a video (Chapter 31), keep the two consistent.

### Letters for an estranged person

Silence is not a plan. In California, a child born or adopted after you made your will who is not mentioned may be entitled to a share (Probate Code § 21620), and a lawyer will usually make any omission explicit. In Germany, a child cut out of the estate still has a compulsory share, which can be withdrawn only on narrow, serious grounds set out in the Civil Code (BGB § 2333).

Whatever the will does, a letter can do something the will cannot: speak to the person. It need not reconcile. It can simply say what happened from your side, without asking for a reply. Some people find, writing it, that they want to send it while they are alive. That is also allowed.

### Timed messages

Letters, like videos, can be labelled for a future moment: "for Jonah, high-school graduation," "for the business, if it is sold," "for whoever finds the box in the attic." Label by recipient and trigger, not by your mood. Tell your archive-keeper in writing where the letters are and when to deliver them. Allow for change: "If this moment never comes, give it to him at thirty, or use your judgment."

### Letters to people outside the family

Former colleagues, students, the friend from university, the neighbour who shovelled your path: these letters are rarely written and, when received, are often kept for life. Write a few.

## Chapter 35. From Voice Memos to a Short Memoir

Many people record hours of stories and then stall, because a pile of recordings is not a book. Here is a method that turns one into the other without sanding off the voice.

### Step 1: Record freely

Use the prompts in Chapter 28. One story per file. Do not worry about order or repetition.

### Step 2: Transcribe

Use automatic transcription, then correct names, places, and dates. Keep each transcript in a file with the same name as the recording.

### Step 3: Choose the stories

Read the transcripts and mark the ones that matter. A good short memoir has twelve to thirty stories. Leave out the ones that are only interesting to you.

### Step 4: Arrange

Chronological order is the simplest. Thematic order (work, love, places, mistakes) can be more interesting. Either works.

### Step 5: Edit lightly

This is where most memoirs lose their author. A professional editor, or a well-meaning grandchild, will want to make you sound like a book. Resist.

- Remove repetitions, false starts, and filler ("um," "you know").
- Keep your phrases, even ungrammatical ones. If you say "we was poor but we was clean," that stays.
- Keep the jokes, even the bad ones.
- Add a sentence of context where a listener needed to see your face to understand.
- Do not add things you did not say.

Here is a passage before and after.

> **Raw transcript:** "So, um, the house, the house on Kirchstraße, it had this, you know, this stove, a big tiled stove, Kachelofen, and in winter we, we all slept in the one room with the stove, all five of us, because the rest of the house was, well it was freezing basically, and my father, he would get up at, I don't know, five, and put the wood in, and you'd hear it, the door of the stove, clank, and that's how I knew it was morning. Yeah."

> **Lightly edited:** "The house on Kirchstraße had a big tiled stove, a Kachelofen. In winter all five of us slept in the one room with the stove, because the rest of the house was freezing. My father got up at five to put the wood in, and you'd hear the door of the stove go clank. That's how I knew it was morning."

Same voice. Fewer ums. Nothing invented.

### Step 6: Add photographs and captions

A photograph with a caption beside each story transforms the memoir. Caption with names, places, and dates; unlabeled photographs become a puzzle within a generation.

### Step 7: Produce it

A PDF that everyone in the family can keep. A printed copy, perhaps through a print-on-demand service, for the people who prefer paper. Link each story to its recording in the archive, so readers can hear the voice behind the words.

## Chapter 36. Unfinished Work

Code, a business, a manuscript, research, a garden, a restoration project, an archive of family history. Unfinished work dies quietly with its owner unless someone knows what it is, where it is, and what you want done with it.

### For any project, leave a README

A one-page note in the project's folder (physical or digital):

- **What it is.** One paragraph.
- **Status.** What is done, what is half-done, what is not started.
- **Where everything is.** Files, notebooks, accounts, materials.
- **Dependencies.** Software, subscriptions, collaborators, suppliers.
- **What is licensed from someone else**, and what is yours.
- **Who should have it.** A named person, an institution, or "anyone who wants to finish it."
- **What you want done.** Finish it; publish as is; give it away; destroy it.
- **What a successor should ignore.** The tangents, the dead ends, the folder called "ideas 2019."

### Intellectual property

Copyrights in your writing, music, photographs, code, and designs are property. They pass under your will or by statute like other property, and both in the United States (for works created since 1978, 17 U.S.C. § 302) and in Germany (UrhG § 64) they generally last for seventy years after the author's death. Name who receives them and who may decide on publication. If your work earns royalties, put the publisher or platform, account, and contact on the Family List. Domain names and websites need someone who can renew them; a lapsed domain can be bought by a stranger within days.

### Businesses

A business with other owners is governed first by its partnership, shareholder, or operating agreement (Chapter 18). A sole business needs an emergency plan: who can access the bank account, who can tell the customers, who can pay the staff, who can close it properly or sell it. Write that plan. It is a gift to your family and to the people who worked for you.

### Research and collections

Universities, archives, local history societies, and museums sometimes accept collections and research notes. Ask while you are alive; an institution is far more likely to accept a well-organised collection from a living donor than a basement full of boxes from an executor.

### Permission to stop

Not everything has to be finished. Say so. "If nobody wants to finish the boat, sell it for parts and don't feel bad" may be the kindest sentence in your papers.
