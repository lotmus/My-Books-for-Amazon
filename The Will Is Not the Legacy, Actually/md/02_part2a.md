# Part Two: Twenty-Five Cases

*Educational guide, not legal advice. Every family here is invented. The rules they run into are real, and where a case names California or Germany, the rule was checked against the statute or an official court source.*

The short version of this book says what a will does not control. This part shows what happens next, twenty-five times.

The cases are not all told the same way, because families do not discover their mistakes the same way. Some are laid out like a file: the situation, what the family assumed, what actually happened, what would have prevented it. Some are told as a story, with the lesson saved for a box at the end. Some are a conversation at a kitchen table, the week after a funeral, where the problem surfaces one sentence at a time. Some show the same family twice, once as it went and once as it could have gone. Every case ends with a short list of things to do, and every list is specific to that case.

For the full picture, read the cases in order; each chapter adds another way a plan can fail. If one problem is urgent, use the case titles to find it and come back to the rest when you are ready.

Where a case turns on a statute, I name it once, so you or your lawyer can look it up, and then explain in plain words what it does. You do not need to remember section numbers. You need to remember the shape of the trap. If one case makes you put the book down and go and find a folder, it has done its job.

## Chapter 3. Forms That Beat the Will

### Case 1: The retirement account that remembered the first marriage

**The situation.** The husband was a project manager in San Diego, the sort of man who kept his receipts in labeled envelopes and his car tires at the correct pressure. At twenty-six, in his first week at his first proper job, a cheerful person from human resources handed him a stack of forms. One was the enrollment for the company's 401(k) retirement plan. It asked for a beneficiary. He wrote his wife's name, because who else would he write, and went back to learning where the coffee machine was.

The marriage ended when he was thirty-four. It was not a dramatic divorce. They divided the furniture, sold the apartment, and agreed that each would keep their own retirement savings. He changed jobs twice. The old 401(k) stayed with the first employer's plan, because moving it seemed like paperwork, and its statements went to an email address he checked twice a year.

At thirty-nine he married again. They bought a house, had a daughter, and at fifty-five did what responsible people do: they went to a lawyer and signed new wills, each leaving everything to the other and then to their daughter. The lawyer asked for a list of assets. The husband listed the old 401(k) by its approximate value. Nobody asked who was named on it.

He died of a heart attack at fifty-eight.

**What the family assumed.** His second wife assumed the will covered everything, because that is what wills are for. The husband, when he had thought about it at all, had assumed the divorce took care of the old form. He had a vague memory of reading that California cancels gifts to an ex-spouse after a divorce. That memory was correct, which made it more dangerous, not less.

**What actually happened, and why.** California does have such a rule. When a marriage is dissolved, the Probate Code revokes gifts to the former spouse in a will (§ 6122), and a separate section makes many transfers outside the will, such as payable-on-death accounts, fail if they name a former spouse (§ 5040). Had the old account been an ordinary bank account with a payable-on-death designation, the ex-wife would probably have been treated as if she had died first.

But a 401(k) is an employer retirement plan, governed by a federal law, the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA), and federal law outranks state law where they conflict. In a 2001 decision (532 U.S. 141), the U.S. Supreme Court looked at a state statute very like California's, one that automatically canceled an ex-spouse's designation after divorce, and held that ERISA overrode it for ERISA plans. Plan administrators must be able to pay according to the plan documents without studying the family law of fifty states. So the administrator pays the person on the form.

The form named the first wife. The plan paid her.

His widow's lawyer explored whether she had a claim against the ex-wife after the payment, based on the divorce agreement in which each spouse kept their own retirement savings. That question turns on the exact wording of the divorce papers, and courts disagree about it. What was not in doubt was the cost: two years, a substantial legal bill, and a widow negotiating with her late husband's first wife about money he had meant for his second family. The two women had met once before, at a funeral. This was not an improvement.

**What would have prevented it.** One page: a new beneficiary designation filed with the plan, naming his second wife as primary and their daughter, or a trust for her, as contingent. The ideal moment was the week the divorce became final. The second-best was the remarriage, which matters for another reason too: many employer plans give a current spouse rights to the account that the employee cannot waive alone, so a marriage should prompt a review of every retirement form. The third chance was the lawyer's office at fifty-five. A lawyer who asks for printouts of the forms, rather than just a list of values, can spot the problem quickly.

**On the other side of the Atlantic.** German law also cancels gifts to a spouse in a will once the marriage has ended, and even when a divorce was pending and its conditions were met (BGB § 2077). But a life insurance or private pension contract that names a beneficiary is a contract for the benefit of a third party, and the named person acquires the right to the money at the death (§ 331). Whether a divorce changes that depends on the contract and on what the parties agreed, which is exactly the question you do not want your widow to be asking.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 1).

**What to do.**

- Ask the administrator of every employer plan you have ever belonged to for a printout of the beneficiary on file. Appendix D has a beneficiary audit sheet.
- The week a divorce is final, file new designations with each plan. For employer plans, the state's automatic revocation may not protect you.
- If you remarry, ask each plan whether your new spouse now has rights that need their written consent to change.
- Bring the printouts, not your memory, to the lawyer who drafts your will.

### Case 2: The life insurance that paid "my heirs"

The wife and the husband were both teachers in a small town in Bavaria, and when their first child was born the husband did the sensible thing. He took out a term life policy large enough to pay off the mortgage on their terraced house. The form asked who should receive the money, and he hesitated over it longer than he had over the amount. His wife, obviously. But what if they both died? What if they divorced? In the end he wrote *meine Erben*, my heirs, which felt flexible and fair and meant he did not have to think about it anymore.

They had a second child. They never made a will. They were in their thirties, and wills were for people with gray hair and holiday homes.

He died at forty-one, in a cycling accident on a Sunday morning, on a road he had ridden a thousand times.

His wife assumed the insurance money was hers, to clear the house, as they had planned. A friend told her, kindly and wrongly, that in Germany the spouse gets everything anyway. The insurer's letter, when it came, asked for a certificate of inheritance. That was the first sign that "my heirs" meant something more precise than the husband had intended.

Without a will, the Civil Code decides who the heirs are. A surviving spouse inherits one quarter alongside children (BGB § 1931). Most German couples live in the default property regime, the community of accrued gains (*Zugewinngemeinschaft*), and in that regime the spouse's share rises by another quarter on death (§ 1371). So the wife inherited one half, and the two children, aged eleven and eight, the other half between them (§ 1924). The insurance law has a rule for exactly the husband's wording: if a policy names "the heirs," then, unless the contract says otherwise, the people who are heirs at the death receive the money in proportion to their shares (VVG § 160). Half to the wife; a quarter to each child.

It was the children's half that changed her year. Their mother had to manage their money for them, not as her own, and the family court had to approve some significant transactions involving a child's assets (BGB § 1643). Using the children's money to pay off a mortgage on a house that now belonged partly to the estate and partly to her was not simply her decision. Were the children getting fair value? How would their interest in the house be recorded? Did the court need to sign off? Without a will, the family had also become a community of heirs (*Erbengemeinschaft*), co-owners of everything the husband left, in which nobody owns a particular thing and important decisions need agreement. When some of the co-owners are children, the parent acts for them only within limits the court supervises.

Nobody did anything wrong. The court was protecting the children, which is its job. The wife estimated later that the correspondence, the advice, and the delay cost her about three months of evenings and a modest fee. The real cost was that, every time she wanted to decide something about the house in that first year, she first had to ask whether it was hers to decide.

Two other rules in the insurance law are worth knowing, because they cut the other way. A beneficiary named in the ordinary, revocable way acquires the right to the money only at the insured person's death (VVG § 159), which is why the husband could have changed the form at any time with a letter to the insurer. And an heir who disclaims the inheritance, perhaps because the estate is in debt, keeps the insurance money anyway, because "the heirs" on a policy is only a way of naming people (§ 160). A form that names people, rather than a category, does all of this better.

The husband had wanted to protect his wife. Her name on the form would have done it: the money would have gone to her directly, as a contract in favor of a third party (§ 331). A will, such as the joint *Berliner Testament* many German couples use (Chapter 11), would have made her sole heir of everything else too. The children would still have had a compulsory share they could claim, but children rarely claim against a surviving parent, and a well-drafted will can discourage it.

!> **The takeaway.** "My heirs" is not a name. On a German policy it splits the money by inheritance shares, and if your children are minors, part of it goes under the family court's eye. Write the person's name, add a named contingent, and make a will.

**What to do.**

- Replace "my heirs" or "my estate" on every insurance and pension form with named people, unless an adviser has given you a reason to keep it.
- In Germany, ask the insurer in writing whether your nomination is revocable and whom it would pay tomorrow, on what documents.
- If your children are minors, decide who should manage any money they could receive, and say so in a will.
- If a policy already paid "the heirs" and children are among them, keep their share in a separate account and ask the family court or a lawyer which uses need approval before you spend any of it.

### Case 3: The account with the daughter's name on it

*A conversation, the Sunday after the funeral, at the mother's kitchen table in Sacramento. The will lies in the middle of the table. The daughter, who lives ten minutes away, has brought the bank statements. One brother has flown in from Oregon, the other from Texas.*

**The son from Texas:** The will's simple. Everything in thirds. Mom signed it fifteen years ago and never changed it.

**The daughter:** The bank says the accounts are mine.

**The son from Oregon:** What do you mean, yours?

**The daughter:** When her eyes got bad, we went to the bank together so I could pay her bills. The man at the desk had a form. She signed where he pointed. He said I'd be added to the checking and the savings. I didn't read it. She didn't either; she couldn't see it.

**The son from Texas:** So you've got signing authority. Fine. It's still her money. It goes through the will.

**The daughter:** That's what I thought. The bank's letter says they're joint accounts with right of survivorship. It says the balance "passes to the surviving owner." That's me.

**The son from Oregon:** How much is in them?

**The daughter:** About a hundred and ninety thousand.

*Silence. The rest of the estate is a car, some furniture, and a little jewelry: about twelve thousand dollars.*

**The son from Texas:** So the will divides twelve thousand dollars, and you keep a hundred and ninety. That can't be what she wanted.

**The daughter:** I know it isn't. I didn't ask for this.

**The son from Oregon:** Then give it back to the estate.

**The daughter:** I want to talk to my husband first. He's the one who drove me across town every Tuesday and Friday for three years. He has views.

It was the last easy conversation the three of them had.

What the bank's letter said was correct. A joint account with right of survivorship belongs to the surviving owner when one owner dies. It does not pass under the will or go through probate. California courts' self-help pages explain that jointly owned accounts are excluded when survivors calculate whether an estate qualifies for simplified procedures. They pass directly to the surviving owner.

California's rules for multiple-party accounts say two things worth knowing. While everyone is alive, a joint account belongs to the owners in proportion to what each actually put in (Probate Code § 5301), so the daughter never owned half of her mother's money; she only had access to it. At death, however, the balance belongs to the surviving owner unless there is *clear and convincing evidence* of a different intent (§ 5302). That is a high standard, higher than the ordinary "more likely than not." Strong evidence that the mother added the daughter only for convenience might persuade a court. But that means a lawsuit among siblings, with the daughter as defendant, about their mother's intentions, with the evidence being a bank form the mother could not read and the memories of people who disagree.

**What would have prevented it.** The goal was help with bills, and the right tool for that is a power of attorney (Chapter 20), which lets an agent manage accounts without owning them. California's Uniform Statutory Form Power of Attorney (§ 4401) is designed for exactly this, and banks are used to it. Some banks also offer a signer-only arrangement that gives signing authority without ownership; whether a given bank does, and what its form says, is a question to ask in writing. If the mother had also wanted the accounts to skip probate, a payable-on-death designation naming all three children equally would have matched her will.

The deeper failure was silence. The mother never told her sons what she had done, because she did not think she had done anything. A ten-minute phone call, "I've given her access so she can help; the will still divides everything three ways," would have exposed the problem while the mother could still fix it.

**In Germany.** The usual joint account is the *Oder-Konto*, on which each holder can act alone. It has no automatic survivorship in the American sense: the dead holder's heirs step into that holder's position, and between the holders the balance is presumed to belong to them in equal shares (BGB § 430) unless a different arrangement can be shown. The daughter could probably still withdraw as far as the bank was concerned, but would owe the estate an account of the mother's half. Any amount that counts as a gift to her may also be added back when a disinherited sibling calculates a compulsory share (§ 2325). The tool for help with bills is the same: a power of attorney (*Vollmacht*).

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 3).

**What to do.**

- Before you add anyone to an account, ask the bank in writing: will this person become an owner, and will they take the balance when I die?
- For help with bills, use a power of attorney or a signer-only arrangement, not joint ownership.
- If a child is already a joint owner for convenience, write a dated note saying so and that the money is to be shared under your will, and ask a lawyer whether the account itself should change.
- Tell all your children what you have arranged, on the same day.

### Case 4: The forgotten IRA and the beneficiary who died first

**The situation.** The man in this case was an engineer from Lower Bavaria who spent twenty years working in Texas and California before retiring to a house on the edge of Passau, with a view of the Danube and a neighbor who disapproved of his lawn. Over those years, he accumulated accounts the way other people collect fridge magnets. One was a small Individual Retirement Account (IRA), opened in 1995 with a US brokerage. He named his younger brother as beneficiary, because he was single and his brother was the person he trusted most. The form had a box for a contingent beneficiary. He left it empty; his brother was younger and healthy, and that is the sort of thing you do not write on forms.

Years later he met a retired pharmacist, and they lived together in the Passau house for fourteen years without marrying. "We're too old to need a piece of paper," she liked to say. He knew the inheritance did need one, and wrote a German will by hand, dated and signed, naming her as his sole heir.

His brother died in 2019. The engineer went to the funeral and never thought about the IRA, whose statements went to an email address he no longer used. He died in 2026, at sixty-nine, before his required beginning date for IRA distributions.

**What the family assumed.** His partner assumed the will made her heir to everything, which, as far as German law was concerned, it did. His brother's sons did not know the IRA existed.

**What actually happened, and why.** An IRA passes according to its beneficiary designation, and the designation named only a dead man. When the only named beneficiary has died and there is no contingent, the custodian's account agreement decides. Many default to the owner's estate; some to a spouse, then the estate. The engineer had no spouse. The IRA was payable to his estate.

That sounds harmless, since the estate went to his partner. In practice an American institution now had to be satisfied about who could act for the estate of a man who died in Germany. American custodians know letters from a probate court, small-estate affidavits, and trust certificates. His partner had a German certificate of inheritance (*Erbschein*) under BGB § 2353: an official document, in German, from a court the custodian's compliance department had never heard of. What followed was certified translations, an apostille to authenticate the court's document internationally, forms designed for American estates, and a great deal of waiting on hold to a call center nine hours behind.

There was tax, too. Since 2020, most individuals who inherit an IRA as named beneficiaries must empty it by the end of the tenth year after the death, which lets them spread the income tax. If the beneficiary is the estate and the owner died before withdrawals had to begin, the account generally must be emptied within five years instead (IRS Publication 590-B). That can mean more tax sooner. The payment to a German estate from an American account also raised questions in both countries. It took most of a year. His partner estimated the fees took about a fifth of the money.

**What would have prevented it.** A review of the form after his brother's death, naming his partner as primary beneficiary: the custodian would have paid her directly, on a death certificate and a claim form, with the treatment a named individual beneficiary can get. A contingent on the original form would at least have kept the account out of the estate. And a line on the Family List would have told her the account existed; the engineer, writing that line, would probably have noticed the problem himself. Writing a Family List is, among other things, an audit.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 4).

**What to do.**

- After a beneficiary dies, update every form that names them within the month. Put a reminder in the Family List under that person's name.
- For accounts abroad, record the institution, account type, last four digits, and an international phone number on the Family List.
- Ask each foreign institution what papers it would want from heirs living in another country, and file the answer with the account details.
- Change the email and postal address on every old account when you move. An unread statement is a warning that never arrives.

### Case 5: The twelve-year-old beneficiary

*The diagnosis and its eight months stay the same. A year earlier, the mother has time to name who will care for her son, who will manage his money, and what his aunt should know about his life.*

**As it happened.** The mother was a single parent in Fresno, a dental hygienist with a good sense of humor and a twelve-year-old son who was the center of her world. His father had not been involved for years. When she started a new job with good benefits, she enrolled in the employer's group life insurance and bought a term policy of her own. On both forms she wrote her son's name and date of birth, and felt a quiet satisfaction. Whatever happened, he would be looked after.

Her sister lived nearby, and the two had a firm understanding: if anything happened, the boy would live with his aunt. They had talked about it many times. They had never written it down.

The mother died at thirty-eight, of an aggressive cancer, eight months after the diagnosis. She was too ill in those months to think about paperwork, and nobody wanted to bring it up.

Two problems arrived at once. The first was custody. With no parent able to care for him, the boy needed a legal guardian. California lets a parent nominate a guardian in a will or another signed writing (Probate Code § 1500), and courts give that nomination great weight, but there was none. The aunt had to petition for guardianship of the boy's person. There was an investigation and notice to relatives, including the boy's father, who had not seen his son in years but now had views. The court appointed the aunt, because it was plainly in the boy's interest. It took months, and for those months nobody knew whether he would stay where he was.

The second problem was the money. Insurers do not hand large sums to twelve-year-olds, and a minor cannot legally manage significant property in his own name. That generally means a guardianship of the estate, a separate court role with its own requirements: a bond or a restricted account, inventories, periodic accountings, and court permission for significant spending. The aunt, named by nobody for anything, needed a judge's authority to spend money meant for the boy's own care. And when a minor's estate is managed under court supervision, the protection generally ends at eighteen. At eighteen, the court's protection would end on schedule, whether or not he felt ready to manage what remained.

**As it could have gone.** Same mother, same diagnosis, same eight months. But a year earlier, on a quiet evening, she had signed three things.

The first was a one-page nomination of her sister as guardian of the boy's person, with a cousin as backup, both asked first. When she died, her sister still had to go to court, but she arrived with the mother's signed wish, and the father's sudden interest met a document instead of a vacuum.

The second was a simple trust created in her will, with her sister as trustee and both insurance forms naming the trust as beneficiary. The insurers paid the trustee, who used the money for the boy's health, school, and support without asking a court. The trust gave him what remained in thirds at ages twenty-five, thirty, and thirty-five. A simpler alternative is a custodian under California's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act. That also avoids court, but ends at an age set by law that many parents find too young. Ask a lawyer whether it fits.

The third was a guardian brief (Appendix E): his doctor and allergies, his best friend's parents' phone number, the fact that he still slept with the light on and would deny it, and what she hoped for him. Her sister read it the night after the funeral and said it was like being handed the instructions to a life.

**The difference, line by line.**

- **Who raises him:** in both versions, the aunt. In the first, after months of doubt; in the second, with the mother's signed nomination before the judge.
- **Who controls the money:** a court-supervised guardianship of the estate, or a trustee the mother chose, with rules she wrote.
- **When he gets it:** eighteen, outright; or in stages, from twenty-five.
- **What it cost:** court costs, a bond, and accountings; or one evening and a lawyer's fee.

One thing does not change between the versions. The boy's father was alive, and in both California and Germany a surviving parent generally has strong rights to custody, even after years of absence, unless a court finds that custody with that parent would harm the child. A nomination does not cut those rights off; it tells the court what the parent who died wanted. If the other parent is absent or unsuitable, say so, calmly and factually, in a letter kept with the nomination, and ask a family lawyer what else you can do. The aim is not to win a fight from beyond the grave. It is to give the judge the information you would give if you could still be in the room.

Naming the intended guardian directly as beneficiary, trusting them to use the money for the child, is the shortcut some parents take. It avoids court, but gives the money to that adult outright, exposed to their creditors, their divorce, and their honesty, and it fails if they die first.

**If it has already happened.** See the first steps in [Appendix L](#If it has already happened: Case 5).

**What to do.**

- Nominate a guardian for your children in writing, and a backup, after asking both.
- Name a trust or a custodian, not the child, as beneficiary of insurance and retirement accounts, and choose the age at which the child gets control.
- Write a guardian brief (Appendix E) and give it to the person you name.
- If the other parent is absent or unsuitable, put the facts in a calm letter kept with the nomination.
