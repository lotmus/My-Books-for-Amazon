## Chapter 5. Wills That Went Wrong

### Case 11: The German will that was typed

**The facts.** Uwe, a retired engineer in Munich, was proud of his laser printer. He typed a clear, well-organised will on his computer, printed it, dated it, and signed it in blue ink at the bottom. He left his flat to his partner, Birgit, to whom he was not married, and the rest to his niece. He put it in the desk drawer with the warranty for the printer.

**The move.** A typed will is easier to read than handwriting. What could be more sensible?

**What governed.** Under BGB § 2231, a German will in ordinary form is either made before a notary or made by a declaration under § 2247. Section 2247 requires the declaration to be *eigenhändig geschrieben und unterschrieben*: written by hand and signed by the testator. A typed text with a handwritten signature is neither. Uwe's will was void. Because he had no valid will, the statutory rules applied. Birgit, as an unmarried partner, had no statutory right to inherit at all. Uwe's estate went to his relatives under the statutory order, and Birgit had to move out of the flat she had shared with him for eighteen years.

**What should have been signed.** The same text, copied out by hand from start to finish, with the date and place (which § 2247 says the testator *should* include), and signed with first and last name. Or a notarial will, where the notary records the testator's declaration; under § 2232 the testator may even hand the notary a document, including a typed one, declaring that it contains their last will. For an unmarried partner, a notarial will has a further advantage: it is registered in the Central Register of Wills (*Zentrales Testamentsregister*) kept by the German Federal Chamber of Notaries, so it will be found.

**The lesson.** In Germany, the hand matters. Either everything in your own handwriting, or a notary. The printer stays out of it.

### Case 12: The California form will, half filled in

**The facts.** Linda, in Oakland, bought a printed fill-in-the-blanks will at a stationery shop. She wrote her children's names in the blanks in her own handwriting and signed it. She did not have it witnessed, because the shop had closed for the day and, well, it was a printed form; surely that counted for something.

**The move.** Use a printed form as if it were a handwritten will.

**What governed.** California recognises two main kinds of individual will. A formal will must be in writing, signed, and witnessed by at least two people present at the same time who understand it is the testator's will (Probate Code § 6110). A holographic will needs no witnesses, but the signature and the *material provisions* must be in the testator's handwriting (§ 6111). The statute lets the statement of intent ("this is my will") appear on a commercially printed form, but the gifts themselves must be handwritten. Whether Linda's handwritten blanks were enough to form the material provisions, or whether the printed words about who receives what were doing the real work, became a dispute. A will in this grey zone may survive or fail depending on how a court reads it, and the family pays for the reading.

California also offers its own official statutory will form (Probate Code § 6240), available free on the California courts' website. It requires two adult witnesses. Its own instructions say, in capitals, that notarisation alone is not sufficient.

**What should have been signed.** Either the California statutory will, signed in front of two adult witnesses who also signed, or a will written entirely by hand. Or, for anything beyond the very simplest estate, a lawyer-drafted will executed with two witnesses.

**The lesson.** A printed form is not a holographic will just because you wrote on it. Find two witnesses. They are easier to come by than a lawsuit.

### Case 13: The witness who was also the heir

**The facts.** Frank, in Long Beach, signed a typed will at his kitchen table. His two witnesses were his neighbour and his son-in-law, Kevin. The will left a third of the estate to Kevin's wife, Frank's daughter, and a specific gift of Frank's restored 1967 car to Kevin himself, "because he helped me rebuild it."

**The move.** Use whoever is in the house as witnesses.

**What governed.** California does not void a will because a witness benefits from it. Probate Code § 6112 says a will is not invalid because it was signed by an interested witness. But unless there are at least two *other* witnesses who are disinterested, a gift to a witness creates a presumption that the witness obtained it by duress, menace, fraud, or undue influence. That presumption shifts the burden of proof: Kevin had to prove he had not pressured Frank. Frank's other daughter, who had wanted the car since she was nine, challenged the gift. Kevin kept the car in the end, but the legal costs would have bought a respectable second one.

**What should have been signed.** The same will, witnessed by two people who received nothing under it. A third disinterested witness would also have removed the presumption. Many lawyers have their own staff witness wills for exactly this reason.

**The lesson.** Your witnesses should be people who get nothing. Make it boring. Boring wills are the ones that survive.

### Case 14: The original that nobody could find

**The facts.** Elisabeth, a widow near Passau, wrote a valid handwritten will in 2015 leaving her house to her granddaughter, Lea, who had lived with her for years, and her savings to her two sons equally. She told Lea "it's somewhere safe." She died in 2026. Nobody could find it. One son remembered seeing it in a folder in the cellar. The cellar had flooded in 2024.

**The move.** Keep the will at home.

**What governed.** Without the original, the family had to prove that a valid will had existed and what it said, which is hard in any legal system. Without proof, the statutory rules applied: the two sons inherited equally as Elisabeth's children (BGB § 1924), and Lea, a grandchild whose parent was alive, received nothing. Lea moved out of the house.

German law requires anyone who holds a will that is not in official custody to hand it to the probate court as soon as they learn of the death (§ 2259). That duty only helps if the will survives and the holder is honest.

**What should have been signed.** The same handwritten will, deposited in special official custody (*besondere amtliche Verwahrung*) at the local court. Section 2248 says a handwritten will must be accepted into official custody if the testator requests it. A will in official custody is registered in the Central Register of Wills, and when the registry office reports the death, the register notifies the court holding the will. The registration fee listed by the Federal Chamber of Notaries is 12.50 or 15.50 euros, depending on how it is billed. A notarial will is registered in the same way.

In California, the equivalent advice is to keep the signed original where the executor can find it and the lawyer knows about it. Whoever has custody of a will must deliver it to the clerk of the superior court within thirty days of learning of the death (Probate Code § 8200).

**And on the Family List.** One line saying where the original is, and the custody receipt or registration confirmation in the binder.

**The lesson.** A will nobody can find is a will nobody can use. Put it where the system will find it for you.

### Case 15: The no-contest clause that scared nobody

**The facts.** Victor, a businessman in Irvine, left most of his estate to his second wife and their son, and a small gift to his daughter from his first marriage, with whom he had a difficult relationship. His lawyer added a no-contest clause: anyone who challenged the will would lose their gift. Victor was told this would "stop any fight."

**The move.** Rely on the no-contest clause as armour.

**What governed.** California enforces no-contest clauses only in limited situations. Under Probate Code § 21311, a no-contest clause applies to a direct contest (a claim that the will is invalid, for example for forgery, lack of capacity, or undue influence) only if the contest is brought without probable cause. If the daughter had reasonable grounds to think she could win, she could contest without losing her gift. She did have grounds: Victor had signed the will in hospital, two weeks after a stroke, with his second wife arranging the appointment and sitting in the room. The clause did not stop the challenge. The facts around the signing invited it.

**What should have been signed.** The same will, signed at a time when Victor's capacity was clear, with the lawyer meeting Victor alone, possibly with a contemporaneous note from his doctor if the lawyer thought it wise. And a gift to the daughter large enough that she would hesitate to risk it. A letter beside the will, explaining Victor's reasons in a calm voice, might also have helped her understand, even if not agree (Chapter 34).

**The lesson.** A no-contest clause is not a fence. It is a sign. Good process at signing is the fence.
