## Chapter 5. Wills That Went Wrong

### Case 11: The German will that was typed

**The situation.** Uwe was a retired engineer in Munich, seventy-one, precise, a little vain about his handwriting, which had been ruined by forty years of technical drawings and was now legible mainly to himself. He had lived for eighteen years with Birgit, a former bookseller, in a flat in Schwabing that Uwe had bought before they met. They had never married. Uwe's first marriage had ended badly, and he said, often, that he did not need the state's permission to love someone.

He did, however, want Birgit to have the flat. He also wanted his niece Katrin, the daughter of his late brother, to have his savings and his collection of antique slide rules, which she had admired as a child and had no idea what to do with as an adult.

Uwe was proud of his laser printer. One rainy Sunday he typed a clear, well-organised will on his computer, with numbered paragraphs and a heading in bold. He printed it, dated it, and signed it in blue ink at the bottom. He put it in his desk drawer, with the warranty for the printer, and told Birgit where it was.

He died of a stroke three years later.

**What the family assumed.** Birgit assumed the flat was hers; Uwe had told her so, and she had seen the will. Katrin assumed she would receive the savings and the slide rules. Both of them, if asked, would have said the document looked far more official than most handwritten wills.

**What actually happened, and why.** German law offers two ordinary forms of will (BGB § 2231). One is the public will, made before a notary. The other is the private will under § 2247, which must be written *eigenhändig*: in the testator's own hand, from the first word to the last, and signed by hand. The point of the rule is that handwriting is hard to forge across a whole page, and that a person who writes out their wishes in full has had to think about them. A typed text with a handwritten signature is neither form. It is not a will.

The probate court, when Birgit handed in the document, had no choice. The will was void.

Without a valid will, the statutory order of inheritance applied. Uwe had no wife and no children. His parents were dead. So the second order of heirs inherited: the descendants of his parents, which meant his surviving sister, Monika, and, in place of his late brother, his brother's children, Katrin and her brother (BGB § 1925). Birgit, as an unmarried partner, had no statutory right to inherit at all. German intestacy law knows spouses and registered partners. It does not know eighteen years of shared breakfasts.

Monika and Katrin's brother were not wicked people, but they were not rich either, and the flat in Schwabing was worth a great deal. They gave Birgit a year to find somewhere else. Katrin, who felt terrible, gave Birgit the slide rules.

Even if the will had been valid, Birgit would have faced a second problem: German inheritance tax. An unmarried partner falls into the least favourable tax class, class III, with a personal allowance of only 20,000 euros (ErbStG § 16), and tax on the rest starting at 30 per cent. A spouse would have had an allowance of 500,000. For a flat in Munich, the difference can be the price of the flat itself. Uwe's principles about the state's permission had a price tag, and Birgit would have paid it.

**What would have prevented it.** On the form, two easy options:

- **The same text, written out by hand.** Every word, in Uwe's own handwriting, with the date and place (which § 2247 says the testator should include) and his first and last name as signature. Legibility matters; a will nobody can read creates its own disputes, so take your time and write clearly.
- **A notarial will.** Uwe could have taken his typed document to a notary and handed it over, declaring that it contained his last will (§ 2232). The notary would have checked his capacity, recorded the declaration, deposited the will in official custody, and registered it in the Central Register of Wills. For an unmarried couple, whose arrangements the law does not assume, a notary's advice is especially valuable.

On the tax, the honest answer is that some couples marry or register partly for this reason, late in life, after decades of saying they never would. Others use lifetime gifts spread over ten-year periods, life insurance structured with care, or a right of residence (*Wohnrecht*) instead of ownership, which can reduce the taxable value. All of these need advice. None of them can be arranged after the funeral.

**Writing a German handwritten will that works.** If you choose the handwritten route, a few habits make the will stronger without making it longer.

- Use a pen, not a pencil, and plain paper. Write slowly enough that a stranger can read every word.
- Begin with a heading such as *Mein Testament* or "My Will," so nobody can mistake it for a draft or a letter.
- Name people fully, with dates of birth if you can, and describe property clearly enough to identify it: "my flat at [address]" rather than "the flat."
- Say who should inherit (the *Erbe*), not only who gets particular things. German law draws a sharp line between appointing an heir, who steps into your whole legal position, and leaving a particular item to someone (a *Vermächtnis*), who only has a claim against the heirs. Muddling the two is one of the commonest causes of disputes about German home-made wills.
- Write the place and date and sign with your first and last name at the end, below the last line of text. Anything added below the signature may not count.
- If you change your mind, write a new will rather than crossing things out.

None of this replaces advice. It does make the difference between a document a court can read and one it has to interpret.

**The California version.** In California a typed will can be perfectly valid, but only if it is witnessed by two people (Probate Code § 6110). Uwe's document, typed and signed but unwitnessed, would have failed in California too, unless the family could show by clear and convincing evidence that he intended it as his will, which California allows courts to accept in some cases. California also gives no intestate rights to an unmarried partner who is not a registered domestic partner. Both countries agree: if you are not married, you must write it down, properly.

**If it has already happened.** If you are the unmarried partner of someone who has died with an invalid will, or none, get advice immediately about your own position: whether you have any rights in the home under a tenancy or a contract, whether any accounts were joint, whether life insurance names you. Ask the heirs, early and in writing, for time. Many heirs will agree to a period of months if asked respectfully; few agree when they first hear from a lawyer.

**What to do.**

- In Germany: handwrite every word, or see a notary. Never type and sign.
- In California: a typed will needs two witnesses, or use the statutory form with two witnesses.
- If you are not married or registered, assume your partner receives nothing without a valid will, and ask about inheritance tax before you rely on the will alone.
- Tell your partner where the original is, and consider official custody so it cannot be lost.

### Case 12: The California form will, half filled in

**The situation.** Linda was a retired school secretary in Oakland, sixty-six, with two adult children, Marcus and Joy, and a stepson, Derek, from her late husband's first marriage, whom she had raised from the age of six and loved as her own. She had always meant to make a will. One afternoon, in a stationery shop, she saw a printed fill-in-the-blanks will kit and bought it, pleased to have finally done something.

At home, she filled in the blanks in her own handwriting. The printed text said things like "I give my residuary estate to the following persons in equal shares:" and Linda wrote "Marcus, Joy, and Derek." She signed and dated it at the bottom. The form had lines for two witnesses. She did not have it witnessed, because it was evening and she lived alone, and because, she reasoned, it was a printed legal form; surely that counted for something.

She died eighteen months later.

**What the family assumed.** All three children assumed the will divided the estate three ways, which is what Linda wanted and what the form appeared to say.

**What actually happened, and why.** California recognises two main kinds of individual will. A formal will must be in writing, signed by the testator, and witnessed by at least two people who are present at the same time and understand that it is the testator's will (Probate Code § 6110). A holographic will needs no witnesses, but the signature and the *material provisions* (who gets what) must be in the testator's own handwriting (§ 6111). The statute expressly allows the statement of intent, "this is my will," to be part of a commercially printed form. It does not allow the gifts themselves to be printed.

Linda's will had no witnesses, so it could survive only as a holographic will. The question was whether her handwritten words, "Marcus, Joy, and Derek," together with her signature, made up the material provisions, or whether the printed words around them, "I give my residuary estate ... in equal shares," were doing the real work. Courts have looked at questions like this before, and the outcome depends on the particular form and the particular handwriting.

The question mattered enormously, because of Derek. If the will failed, Linda's estate would pass under the intestacy rules to her children. Marcus and Joy were her children. Derek was her stepchild, and California's intestacy rules treat a stepchild as a child for inheritance purposes only in narrow circumstances, generally where the relationship began during the child's minority, continued throughout both lives, and there is clear and convincing evidence that the stepparent would have adopted the child but for a legal barrier (§ 6454). Linda had never adopted Derek because his mother, while alive, would never have consented. Whether that met the test was another question for another court.

Marcus and Joy, to their great credit, said at once that they wanted Derek to share equally, whatever the court said. That was generous, and it worked, but it required Marcus and Joy to make a gift to Derek out of their own inheritance, with possible gift tax paperwork, and it depended entirely on the goodwill of two people who could have chosen otherwise.

**What would have prevented it.** California offers a free statutory will form (§ 6240), published by the California courts. It is simple, it is designed for people like Linda, and it requires two adult witnesses. Its own instructions say, in capitals, that notarisation alone is not enough. Linda could have signed it on a Saturday with two neighbours, or at her bank, or at her church, in about ten minutes.

Alternatively, she could have written the whole will by hand, from "This is my will" to her signature, which would have been a valid holographic will. Or she could have seen a lawyer, who would also have asked about Derek and might have suggested that, since adoption of an adult is possible in California, the family consider whether it was wanted.

**The plain-language version.** A will needs either witnesses or the testator's own handwriting doing the important work. A printed form with a few words filled in has one foot in each camp and may fall between them. Witnesses are the cheapest insurance in estate planning.

**Signing the statutory will in ten minutes.** The California statutory will is a fill-in form, and it is designed to be completed without a lawyer. The process is simple and worth describing because so many people never get round to it.

1. Download the current form from the California courts' website and read its instructions all the way through, once.
2. Fill in the choices in ink. The form offers set options; it does not allow you to write in your own complicated arrangements. If you need something it does not offer, that is a sign you need a lawyer.
3. Find two adults who receive nothing under the will. Ask them to sit with you at the same table at the same time.
4. Tell them, "This is my will." Sign it while they watch.
5. Each witness signs in your presence and in each other's presence.
6. Put the original somewhere safe, write its location on your Family List, and tell your executor.

That is all. Ten minutes, two neighbours, one pen. It is the most underused free legal document in the state.

**If it has already happened.** If you find a parent's will on a printed form, unwitnessed, do not throw it away and do not try to "fix" it by adding witnesses afterwards; that can make things worse. Take the original, untouched, to a probate lawyer. Whoever has it must deliver it to the court within thirty days of learning of the death in any case (§ 8200). The lawyer can tell you whether it is likely to stand as a holographic will, and whether the family members who would inherit without it are willing to honour it voluntarily. Families often are, when they understand what the parent wanted and what a court fight would cost.

**Why kits are tempting and what to look for.** Will kits sell because they promise a finished document in an evening. Some are perfectly good, if they are written for your state and you follow their signing instructions exactly. The danger lies in the gap between buying the kit and signing it properly. If you use one, read the signing page first, before you fill anything in, and book the witnesses before you start. A kit that tells you only to sign and date is either written for a different jurisdiction or relying on handwriting rules it does not explain.

**What to do.**

- Use the California statutory will form or a lawyer-drafted will, and sign it in front of two adult witnesses who are present together.
- Choose witnesses who receive nothing under the will (Case 13).
- If you want a stepchild, a partner's child, or a friend to inherit, name them explicitly in a valid will. Do not rely on the intestacy rules.
- If you have only a handwritten or form will, ask a lawyer to look at it. A short consultation now is far cheaper than a court's interpretation later.

### Case 13: The witness who was also the heir

**The situation.** Frank was a retired machinist in Long Beach, eighty, with two daughters, Diane and Maria, and a fully restored 1967 convertible that he had rebuilt over four years with Diane's husband, Kevin. The car was red. It had won two prizes at local shows. It was, in Frank's words, "the only thing I ever finished."

Frank asked a paralegal to type a will for him, following his instructions: one third of the estate to Diane, one third to Maria, one third to his church; and the car to Kevin, "because he helped me rebuild it." Frank signed it one evening at his kitchen table. His two witnesses were his next-door neighbour, Harold, and Kevin, who happened to be there for dinner.

Maria, who lived in Sacramento and had loved the car since she was nine, did not know about the gift until the will was read.

**What the family assumed.** Frank assumed the will was valid, because it was typed, signed, and witnessed by two people. Kevin assumed the car was his. Maria assumed someone had talked her father into it.

**What actually happened, and why.** The will was valid. California does not cancel a will because a witness benefits from it (Probate Code § 6112). But the same section adds a safeguard. Unless there are at least two other, disinterested witnesses, a gift to a witness raises a presumption that the witness procured it by duress, menace, fraud, or undue influence.

In plain words: the law suspects the witness. Kevin, because he witnessed a will that gave him a car, had to prove that he had not pressured Frank into giving it. Ordinarily it would have been Maria's job to prove pressure. The presumption flipped the burden.

Maria challenged the gift. Kevin's evidence was good: photos of the two of them in the garage over four years, the prize ribbons, Harold's testimony that Frank had talked about the gift for months, the paralegal's notes. The court found that Kevin had rebutted the presumption, and he kept the car. But the case took ten months, and the legal fees on both sides would have bought a respectable second convertible. Maria and Diane, who had shared a bedroom as girls, now communicate mainly through their children.

**What would have prevented it.** Two disinterested witnesses. Harold and anyone else who received nothing: another neighbour, the paralegal, a colleague. Or three witnesses, with two of them disinterested. That is all. Many law offices have their own staff witness wills for exactly this reason: staff receive nothing, have no family connection, and will remember, if asked, that the testator seemed clear and calm.

The more subtle prevention would have been a conversation. Frank could have told Maria, while he was alive, "The car goes to Kevin. He helped me build it. You'll get your third of everything else." It might not have pleased her. It would have removed the suspicion, which was the real engine of the lawsuit.

**Choosing witnesses well.** A good witness is an adult who receives nothing under the will, is not married or closely related to anyone who does, knows you slightly or not at all, and is likely to be findable for years. A colleague from another department, a neighbour two doors down, staff at a lawyer's office. Lawyers usually include an attestation clause: a short paragraph above the witnesses' signatures in which they confirm that the testator declared the document to be their will and signed it in their presence. It is useful evidence if questions arise later, and it can spare the witnesses from being tracked down years afterwards to remember an ordinary afternoon.

**The German version.** German private wills need no witnesses at all; the handwriting does that job. For notarial wills, the law excludes notaries and certain persons from taking part in recording a will that benefits them or their relatives. The underlying idea is the same as California's: the people who make the document official should not be the people who profit from it.

**What the paperwork should have looked like.** A will that leaves a specific item to someone who helped the testator is perfectly normal. What protects it is the evidence around it. Frank's lawyer, had he used one, would have met Frank alone, asked why he wanted Kevin to have the car, and written down the answer. The will itself might have said, "I give my 1967 convertible to my son-in-law Kevin, who spent four years helping me restore it; I make this gift with love for both my daughters, who will share the rest of my estate equally." The signing would have taken place at the lawyer's office, with two staff members as witnesses, and Kevin at home. None of that costs much. All of it makes a challenge look unpromising.

**If it has already happened.** If you are named in a will you witnessed, tell the executor and get your own advice. Gather the evidence that shows the testator's wish was genuinely theirs: messages, photographs, conversations other people heard, notes from anyone who drafted the will. If you are the family member who suspects pressure, ask yourself honestly what you would gain from a challenge, after costs, and whether the testator's reasons, once you hear them, might be ones you can live with.

**Questions to ask before anyone signs.**

- Does either witness, or the spouse of either witness, receive anything under this will?
- Will both witnesses be reachable in ten years?
- Is there anyone who might be surprised by a gift? Have I told them?

**Why it was the car, not the money.** Disputes over estates are rarely about the largest asset. They are about the object that carries a story: the car, the ring, the piano, the cabin. Maria did not need the car. She wanted the afternoons she remembered sitting in it with her father, and the will seemed to give those afternoons to her brother-in-law. A gift with a story needs the story told, in the will or beside it, and if possible to the person who will be disappointed, while the giver is alive to tell it kindly.

**What to do.**

- Choose witnesses who receive nothing under the will and are not married to anyone who does.
- If a gift might surprise someone, tell them while you are alive, and write a short letter explaining why (Chapter 34).
- For a gift to someone who helped you, describe the help in the will or the letter. "Because he helped me rebuild it" was a good line. A few more sentences would have been better.
- Keep the will boring. Boring wills are the ones that survive.

### Case 14: The original that nobody could find

**The situation.** Elisabeth was a widow in a village near Passau, eighty-four, with two sons who lived in Munich and Stuttgart and a granddaughter, Lea, who had moved in with her at twenty-two, during a hard time in Lea's own life, and stayed. For nine years Lea cooked, gardened, drove Elisabeth to church, and listened to the same stories about the war more times than either of them could count.

In 2015, Elisabeth wrote a valid will entirely by hand: the house to Lea, the savings to her two sons equally. She signed it, dated it, and told Lea, "It's somewhere safe." She told one of her sons, on the phone, that she had "taken care of Lea."

In 2024 the river flooded, as it does in Passau, and the cellar filled with water to the ceiling. The family spent a week throwing away ruined boxes. Elisabeth died in 2026.

**What the family assumed.** Lea assumed the will would turn up. Both sons assumed there was a will, because their mother had mentioned it.

**What actually happened, and why.** Nobody could find the will. The desk, the bedroom, the kitchen drawer, the bank's safe deposit box: nothing. One son remembered seeing a folder labelled *Testament* in the cellar, years before the flood.

In Germany, a will that has been lost is not automatically revoked. If the heirs can prove that a valid will existed, and what it said, a court can act on it. But proving the content of a handwritten document nobody has seen for years is very hard. Lea had no copy. The son's memory of a folder proved that a document existed, not what it said.

Without proof, the statutory order applied. Elisabeth's two sons inherited equally as her children (BGB § 1924). Lea, as a grandchild whose own father was alive, inherited nothing; in German intestacy, a living child shuts out that child's own children.

Both sons were decent men. Both had mortgages. One wanted to sell the house and split the proceeds; the other wanted to let Lea stay. Lea, not wanting to be the cause of a quarrel, moved out within the year.

**What would have prevented it.** The same handwritten will, deposited in official custody. German law requires the local court to accept a handwritten will into special official custody (*besondere amtliche Verwahrung*) if the testator asks (§ 2248). The will is then registered in the Central Register of Wills kept by the Federal Chamber of Notaries. When the death is registered, the register informs the court holding the will, which opens it and informs the people concerned. It cannot be lost in a flood, hidden by a disappointed relative, or thrown away with the old newspapers. The registration fee, according to the Chamber, is 12.50 or 15.50 euros depending on how it is billed; the court charges a fixed fee for the custody itself.

A notarial will would have been registered in the same way.

**The California version.** California has no state-run will register. The standard advice is to keep the signed original where the executor can find it, often with the lawyer who drafted it, and to tell the executor where it is. Whoever has the original must deliver it to the clerk of the superior court within thirty days of learning of the death (Probate Code § 8200). A lost will is worse in California than in Germany: if the will was last in the testator's possession, the testator remained competent, and the original cannot be found, the law presumes the testator destroyed it on purpose to revoke it (§ 6124). The presumption can be overcome with evidence, but the family starts uphill.

**How official custody works in practice.** In Germany, you take your handwritten will to the local court (*Amtsgericht*) of your choice, usually the one where you live. The court puts it in a sealed envelope, records it, registers it in the Central Register of Wills, and gives you a deposit receipt. You can take a handwritten will back out of custody at any time; for a handwritten will, taking it back does not revoke it, but for a notarial will, taking it back out of official custody counts as revoking it (BGB § 2256), which surprises people. If you want to change a will in custody, the simplest course is to write a new one and deposit that as well; a later will overrides an earlier one to the extent they conflict.

**On the Family List.** One line: "Original will: in official custody at the local court in Passau, deposit receipt in binder, section 1." Or, in California: "Original will: with our lawyer at [office], copy in binder."

**What it cost.** The cost in money was small; in Germany, an estate without a will is often cheaper to settle than one with a disputed will. The cost to Lea was everything Elisabeth had meant her to have, and the knowledge that her grandmother had tried. The sons, who would both have honoured the will if it had been found, were left to decide on their own how generous to be, which is a burden nobody should inherit. One of them still wonders whether the folder in the cellar was really the will at all.

**If it has already happened.** If you believe a will existed but cannot find it, search thoroughly and systematically: desks, safes, files, books, the deceased's car, safe deposit boxes, the lawyer or notary the person used, and the local probate court, which can check whether a will is held in official custody. In Germany, the court will also learn from the Central Register of Wills whether any registered will exists. Write down who saw what, when, and what they remember it saying, as soon as possible, while memories are fresh. Look for copies, drafts, photographs, or letters that mention it. Then ask a lawyer whether that evidence is enough. Sometimes it is.

**Questions to ask now.**

- Where exactly is the original of my will?
- Who knows where it is?
- Would it survive a flood, a fire, or a well-meaning relative clearing the house?

**What to do.**

- In Germany: deposit your handwritten will in official custody at the local court, or make a notarial will. Keep the receipt with your Family List.
- In California: keep the original somewhere safe that your executor knows about. Give your lawyer or executor a copy and tell them where the original is.
- Never keep the only original in a cellar, an attic, or anywhere that floods, burns, or gets cleared out.
- Tell the people who matter that a will exists and where it is. You do not have to tell them what it says.

### Case 15: The no-contest clause that scared nobody

**The situation.** Victor built a successful logistics company in Irvine, sold it at sixty, and spent his seventies on golf and grievances. He had a daughter, Natalie, from his first marriage, with whom he had a relationship that both of them described, on good days, as complicated. He had a second wife, Sharon, and a son with her, Jake.

At seventy-seven, Victor had a stroke. He recovered partly, but his speech was slower and his memory unreliable. Two weeks after he came home from hospital, Sharon arranged for a lawyer to visit him there. In a single meeting, with Sharon in the room, Victor signed a new will leaving almost everything to Sharon and Jake, and 50,000 dollars to Natalie. The lawyer added a no-contest clause: anyone who challenged the will would lose their gift. Sharon was told, and told Victor, that this would "stop any fight."

Victor died eight months later. His estate was worth about eleven million dollars.

**What the family assumed.** Sharon assumed the clause would keep Natalie quiet, because 50,000 dollars was better than nothing. Natalie, who had seen her father in hospital and heard him struggle to remember her children's names, assumed something had gone badly wrong.

**What actually happened, and why.** A no-contest clause says, in effect, "if you challenge this document, you lose what it gives you." It is a deterrent: it works only when the person has something to lose and expects to lose the challenge.

California limits these clauses. Under Probate Code § 21311, a no-contest clause can be enforced against a direct contest, such as a claim that the will is invalid because of forgery, lack of capacity, or undue influence, only if the contest was brought *without probable cause*. Probable cause, roughly, means that the facts known to the person would lead a reasonable person to believe there is a reasonable likelihood the challenge will succeed after further investigation.

Natalie had probable cause and then some. Her father signed a will that cut her share drastically, two weeks after a stroke, at home, in a single meeting arranged by the main beneficiary, who sat in the room. She could contest the will without risking her 50,000 dollars. And because the stakes were eleven million, the clause was not much of a deterrent anyway.

She contested. The case turned on the evidence of Victor's capacity and on whether Sharon had exercised undue influence. California law looks at factors such as the vulnerability of the person, the influencer's apparent authority, the actions taken, and whether the result was fair. The litigation lasted three years. It ended in a settlement that gave Natalie considerably more than 50,000 dollars and gave everyone a lasting dislike of the others. The lawyers did well.

**What would have prevented it.** The clause was not the problem. The circumstances of signing were.

- **Timing.** If Victor wanted to change his will, the safest time was before the stroke, or, failing that, after a clear recovery, with his doctor's opinion of his capacity on record.
- **Process.** The lawyer should have met Victor alone, without Sharon, more than once, asked open questions about his family and his reasons, and kept notes. Many lawyers in these situations ask a doctor to examine the client close to the signing. Some arrange for the signing to be video-recorded; that can help or hurt, depending on how the client presents, so it is a judgment for the lawyer.
- **A real gift.** A no-contest clause deters only if the gift at risk is large enough to matter. Fifty thousand dollars against a possible share of millions deters nobody.
- **A letter.** A calm letter from Victor, written in his own words before the stroke if possible, explaining why he was dividing the estate as he did, would not have bound anyone, but might have helped Natalie understand. People sue less over decisions they understand, even when they dislike them (Chapter 34).

**What "capacity" actually means.** People often imagine that capacity to make a will means being perfectly well. It does not. In California, a person has the capacity to make a will if, at the time, they understand the nature of making a will, can recall the nature and situation of their property, and remember and understand their relations to their family and to the people affected by the will (Probate Code § 6100.5). A person with early dementia, or recovering from a stroke, can still meet that test on a good day. The difficulty is proving it afterwards. Victor may well have had capacity. Nobody wrote down the evidence that would have shown it.

Undue influence is a different question. A person can have full capacity and still be pressured into a will that is not really theirs. California's courts look at the whole picture: how vulnerable the person was, how much authority and access the influencer had, what the influencer actually did, and whether the result was fair. Two weeks after a stroke, a new will drafted at home, with the main beneficiary arranging the meeting and sitting in on it, ticks too many of those boxes to ignore. A lawyer who sees those boxes ticked should slow down.

**The German version.** German law has no exact equivalent, but wills often contain a so-called *Pflichtteilsstrafklausel* in a couple's joint will: a child who claims their compulsory share after the first parent's death is limited to the compulsory share after the second death too. It deters for the same reason as the California clause, and fails for the same reason: if the child has more to gain from claiming than from waiting, the clause will not stop them.

**If it has already happened.** If you are the child left with a small gift and a no-contest clause, do not decide in anger. Get a lawyer's view on whether you have probable cause, and on what you would realistically recover after costs. If you are the main beneficiary of a will signed in doubtful circumstances, take a challenge seriously from the first letter, and consider early mediation. Will contests are often settled in the end; settling them early saves the most.

**What to do.**

- Do not rely on a no-contest clause to prevent a challenge. Rely on good process.
- If you change your will after an illness, ask your lawyer about documenting capacity, and meet the lawyer alone.
- If you leave someone much less than they expect, give them enough to make a challenge costly, and explain your reasons in writing.
- Make big changes while you are well. The best evidence of capacity is a will signed long before anyone could doubt it.
