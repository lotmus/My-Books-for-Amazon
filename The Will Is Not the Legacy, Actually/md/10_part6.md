# Part Six: The Family List of Everything

*Educational guide, not legal advice. The Family List is a map, not a legal document. It transfers nothing. It tells your people where to look.*

## Chapter 22. Why the List Matters More Than You Think

When someone dies or becomes incapacitated, the first problem is rarely legal. It is informational. Nobody knows what exists. The family knows there was "a pension from the old job" and "some savings at a bank, the one with the blue logo." They know there was life insurance, probably. They do not know the policy number, the insurer, or whether it lapsed in 2011. They find statements for accounts nobody recognizes and miss accounts that sent no statements at all.

A surprising amount of money goes unclaimed this way. In California, a bank account with no owner activity for more than three years is generally reported and handed to the State Controller, who holds it until the owner or the heirs claim it. In Germany, if no relative, spouse, or registered partner can be found at all, the state itself inherits (BGB § 1936). Neither outcome is a tragedy on the scale of a war, but both are avoidable, and the government is a strange heir to end up with when you meant your daughter.

The Family List solves the information problem. It is one place, physical or digital, kept up to date, that tells your people:

- what exists,
- where it is,
- how it is owned and who is named on it,
- whom to call,
- where the original documents are kept, and
- how to get into the devices and accounts, without writing your passwords in plain text.

If all the family knows is "the bank with the blue logo," write down the actual name. Blue is popular in banking.

It also helps you. Most people who make one discover something: an old life insurance policy with the wrong beneficiary, a credit card they forgot they had, a subscription that has been charging them for a magazine they have not read since the previous decade.

## Chapter 23. What Goes on the List

Appendix C is the full printable checklist, organized as an "In Case of Emergency" binder. This chapter explains each section and what to write.

### 1. The first page: who and where

The first page is for the person who opens the binder in a crisis. It should answer, in under a minute:

- Who you are: full legal name, date and place of birth, citizenships, tax identification numbers (or where they are filed; see the security note below).
- Names used on documents: current and former legal names, alternate spellings or scripts, and any other name under which an account or property may be held. Keep identity documents securely; the Family List needs the name and where proof is kept, not copies of sensitive documents unless you choose.
- Languages and formats: the language each key person prefers, any interpreter or translator contact, and where accessible or translated handoff copies are kept. Mark which documents are originals and whether a translation has been reviewed or certified where required.
- Who to call first: your executor or heirs, your agent under the power of attorney, your healthcare agent, your lawyer or notary, your tax adviser, your doctor.
- If you want a neighbor to help in an emergency, complete the optional neighbor card in Appendix C, Section 15 and give that person a copy. Do not give a neighbor the full Family List unless you want them to have it.
- Where the important originals are: will, trust, powers of attorney, healthcare directive, marriage certificate, birth certificates, property deeds, insurance policies.
- Where the rest of the list is, if it is split between paper and a password manager.

### 2. Bank accounts

For each account: the bank, the type of account, the last four digits of the account number, how it is titled (sole, joint, POD), who the payable-on-death beneficiary is, and whether there is online access. Include foreign accounts. Include accounts you think of as trivial; they are often the ones nobody finds.

### 3. Investments and brokerage

For each account: the institution, account type, last four digits, title or TOD registration, beneficiaries. Include employee share plans, stock options, and any shares held in paper certificate form (say where the certificates are).

### 4. Retirement and pensions

For each: the employer or provider, plan type, last four digits or member number, primary and contingent beneficiaries, and contact details. In the United States, include 401(k)s from old employers, IRAs, and pensions. In Germany, include the statutory pension insurance number, company pensions (*Betriebsrente*), and private pension contracts. Survivors may have claims to survivor pensions that someone must apply for.

### 5. Insurance

Life insurance, accidental death, mortgage protection, employer group life, long-term care, health, home, car, liability. For each: the insurer, policy number, beneficiaries, and agent or contact. Note any policy that pays on death; note any that must be canceled or transferred.

### 6. Property

Each house, flat, plot of land, or timeshare: the address, how it is owned, where the deed or land register extract is, the mortgage lender and account, property tax details, home insurance, and any tenants or leases. Include property abroad.

### 7. Vehicles and other valuables

Cars, boats, motorcycles: where the title documents are, any loans. Jewelry, art, collections, instruments: where they are, any appraisals, any wishes (and remember that wishes belong in the will or in a legally recognized memorandum, such as the California writing permitted by Probate Code § 6132, if they are to be binding).

### 8. Business interests

Companies, partnerships, sole proprietorships, rental businesses: your role, your share, the key agreements (shareholder, buy-sell, operating), the accountant, the bank accounts, who can keep things running for the first month.

### 9. Debts

List mortgages, car loans, personal loans, credit cards, lines of credit, loans you have made to family, and guarantees you have signed. For each, record the lender, account, and approximate balance. Your executor needs to know what must be paid; your heirs need to know what they may inherit. In Germany, heirs inherit debts as well as assets. They can limit liability to the estate only through a formal procedure, such as court-ordered estate administration or insolvency (BGB § 1975). This is one reason an heir generally has six weeks after learning of the inheritance to disclaim it (BGB § 1944). A debt list can help them decide whether to do so.

### 10. Income

Pensions, Social Security or statutory pension, rent, dividends, royalties, consulting. When someone dies, payments that arrive after death may have to be returned, and income that stops may need to be claimed by a survivor. Note what comes in, from whom, and to which account.

### 11. Regular payments and subscriptions

Utilities, phone, internet, streaming, software, memberships, newspapers, recurring charitable donations, cloud storage. This section is boring and important. Some subscriptions should be canceled. Some must not be, at least not immediately: the cloud storage that holds your photos and video diaries (Part Seven) needs to stay paid until someone has downloaded everything.

### 12. Digital accounts

Email (and which address is the "master" account where password resets arrive), phone and computer, photo libraries, cloud storage, social media, domain names, websites, online shops, cryptocurrency, loyalty programs. For each: the service, the username or email, whether a legacy-contact or inactive-account setting is turned on, and what you want done with it (keep, download, memorialize, delete).

Do not write the passwords here. Chapter 24 explains what to do instead.

### 13. Key contacts

Lawyer or notary, tax adviser or accountant, financial adviser, insurance agent, doctor, employer's HR department, landlord or tenants, close friends who know things the family does not, and the person who knows how the heating works.

### 14. Wishes

Funeral and burial or cremation wishes. Organ donation. Who should be told. Where the ethical will and letters are (Part Eight). Where the recordings are (Part Seven). Pets and who will take them.

### A security note on identity numbers

Social Security numbers, tax IDs, passport numbers, and full account numbers are valuable to identity thieves. If the binder is paper, keep it locked. If it is digital, encrypt it. Writing only the last four digits of an account number is usually enough for an executor to identify an account; the full number will be on the statement.

The optional neighbor card is a separate, limited handoff. Share only what you choose, with a neighbor who agrees to help, and update or take it back when plans change.

## Chapter 24. Passwords Without Writing Them Down

The wrong way to pass on your passwords is a notebook in the desk drawer with "PASSWORDS" written on the cover. Burglars can read, too. The second-wrong way is to put them in your will, which in California can become a public court record during probate (Case 25).

### The pattern that works

1. **Use a password manager.** Store every password in one encrypted vault, protected by one strong master password and, ideally, two-factor authentication.
2. **Turn on emergency access.** Many password managers offer an emergency-access or digital-legacy feature: you name a trusted person, and if they request access, you have a waiting period to refuse. If you do not respond (because you cannot), they get in. Check how your password manager's version works, set it up, and test it with the trusted person.
3. **Seal the master key for the vault separately.** If your manager does not offer emergency access, or as a backup, write the master password and any recovery code on a single sheet and seal it in an envelope. Keep the envelope in a secure place separate from your will; a will may become part of a public court record, and an executor may not be able to access a safe-deposit box promptly. On your Family List, record where the envelope is and who may open it. Mark it "to be opened by [name] only in case of my death or incapacity." Some people split this into two halves held by two people. Review the arrangement if the trusted person or location changes.
4. **Do the same for devices.** The phone passcode and the computer login are the keys to everything else, including the two-factor codes. Put them in the vault and in the sealed envelope.
5. **Handle two-factor carefully.** If every login code is sent to your phone, and nobody can open your phone, nobody can open anything. Store backup codes for your important accounts in the vault. Consider a second registered device or hardware key kept with the sealed envelope.
6. **Turn on platform legacy settings.** Several major phone, email, and social media services offer legacy-contact or inactive-account settings. Under California's digital-assets law, a direction given through a provider's own online tool can override a contrary instruction in your will (Probate Code § 873). Use the tools.

### What never goes in plain text

- Passwords, PINs, and passcodes.
- Cryptocurrency recovery phrases and private keys.
- Answers to security questions.

### What can go in plain text

- The name of your password manager and the email address you use with it.
- The fact that emergency access is set up, and for whom.
- Where the sealed envelope is.

## Chapter 25. Storing, Updating, and Telling

### Paper, digital, or both

**Paper** is easy for anyone to read, needs no device, and survives software changes. It can be lost, burned, or flooded (Case 15), and it goes out of date the day you write it.

**Digital** is easy to update and to copy. It depends on devices, passwords, and file formats, and on someone knowing it exists.

Most families do best with both: a paper binder with the first page, the contacts, and the list of what exists; and a digital version, encrypted, with the detail. The password manager holds the secrets.

### Where to keep the binder

- At home in a fire-resistant, lockable place, known to your executor and agent.
- A copy with your executor, your lawyer, or a trusted family member.
- Not only in a safe-deposit box, unless someone else is authorized to open it. A box that requires a court order to open is a poor place for the instructions on how to get a court order.

### Keeping it current

The list is useful only if it is roughly right. Three rules:

1. **Update on change.** New account, closed account, new insurance, new beneficiary, sold the car: update the list the same week.
2. **Review on a schedule.** Once a year, at a fixed time. Tax season is good because the statements are on the table anyway. Your birthday also works, if you need a reason to be sentimental.
3. **Date every page.** "Last checked: March 2026" tells the reader how much to trust it.

Appendix A builds this review into the yearly to-do list.

### Who should know

At least two people should know the list exists and where it is: your executor and your agent under the power of attorney. If they are the same person, tell a second person as a backup. Your healthcare agent should know where the healthcare directive is.

They do not all need to read every page. You can seal sections. The point is that the right person can find the right page on the worst day of their year.

### The handoff conversation

Appendix G has a script for this. In short: sit down with the person, show them where the binder is, show them how the password manager's emergency access works, tell them what is private, and tell them whom to call first. Use a language and format they understand; arrange an independent interpreter for legal decisions if needed. Ask them to explain the next steps in their own words, without turning the conversation into a test. If a step is unclear, improve the instructions together.
