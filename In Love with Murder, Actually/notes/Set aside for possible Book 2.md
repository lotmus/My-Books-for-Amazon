## From Book One REV7

## Chapter 11: The Index of Missing Things

They did not leave the pages on the line again. Mara's warning and Hale's visit had made the jetty a sentence someone else finished. That night Catarina stood longer than usual at the kitchen window. When Tobias asked what she was watching, she said only, "The water knows the names." He did not press. The envelope under the Olivetti still held the one she had spoken by accident.

The first letter arrived inside a book Tobias had not ordered.

It was a slim volume of railway timetables, old enough to smell of dust and wet wool, and it had been left on the kitchen chair with a square of brown paper folded beneath it. The paper was addressed to him in a hand that used the capital T as if it were a small gate. No stamp. No postmark. No explanation from Madame Fournier, who was in the pantry and said she had not seen it.

Tobias opened the timetable first. The year was 1953. Every route in the book began or ended at a station called Saint-Oran, which did not exist on the map pinned beside the telephone. In the margin, someone had pencilled departure times in the same neat hand: 6:42, 10:15, 11:58. The last was circled twice.

"A railway that does not exist," he said.

"It exists in the book," Catarina said. She was sorting the pages the pond had returned, laying each under a clean tea towel. "This is different from not existing."

"That distinction has kept philosophers employed for centuries."

"And railway clerks."

The letter contained one sentence: *If you want to know what the house forgets, ask it for the names it has been told not to keep.* There was no signature. Catarina read it, turned the paper over, and checked the grain as if a hidden second message might have soaked through. Tobias examined the envelope. Its fold was unfamiliar, its glue unbroken until he opened it, and the paper was made for a thicker letter than the one it held.

"Someone wants us to go looking," he said.

"Someone wants us to think we are choosing."

They agreed not to go to the station. Then, after lunch, they went to the village archive.

The archive occupied two rooms behind the mairie, where the heating made a ticking sound like a patient bomb. Madame Fournier had arranged the appointment with a man named Luc, who wore a cardigan that had survived several governments. He brought them the tenancy ledgers for the house, the repair books, and a folder of correspondence from the year the lake house had been built.

The house had been called Les Aulnes, then La Rive, then simply the house by the water. Names appeared in the records, vanished, then appeared again with different spellings. One owner's signature had been cut from a page so carefully that the paper remained smooth. A kitchen renovation listed as completed in April had been paid for in November. The same carpenter's initials stood beside both entries, though his death certificate was dated between them.

"An error," Luc said, with the confidence of a man who had spent his life among errors and knew they were often better organised than people.

"Two errors," said Catarina.

"Then a family tradition."

They asked for the original ledger. Luc fetched it from a locked cabinet. The page was still there, but the ink had been scraped away. Under angled light, Tobias could make out the pressure of a name written by someone who had pressed hard enough to dent the sheet.

At home, Catarina placed tracing paper over the page and rubbed the side of a pencil across it. The letters appeared slowly, backwards at first, then whole: **A. Vial**.

They did not know what to do with the name. They knew only that it had been removed before the ledger was bound, and that the alteration belonged to the same month as the first entry for the housekeeper's room. Catarina's hand stopped on the tracing. "Inês used that name once," she said, so quietly he almost missed it. "On the telephone. The night of the fourteenth." She did not look at him. He put the envelope with her name back under the Olivetti without taking it out.

"It could be a different Vial," Tobias said.

"Yes."

"It could be nothing."

"Also yes."

"You are not helping."

"I am helping the evidence."

That evening the timetable was gone from the kitchen chair. Tobias searched the rooms twice. Catarina found the brown paper in the stove, unburned, though the fire had been lit. They did not tell Luc. They made a photocopy of the traced ledger and put it in separate envelopes. The evidence was small, incomplete, and almost certainly not enough to persuade anyone of anything.

It was also the first time the house had given them a name it had been ordered to forget.

## Chapter 12: The Room That Kept Its Sunday

Madame Fournier asked them to inspect the west bedroom because a smell had returned.

"Damp?" Tobias asked.

"No." She looked toward the stairs. "Sunday."

The room had been shut since the previous winter. It held a narrow bed, a wardrobe with one handle, and a washstand whose basin had a hairline crack. Catarina opened the window. The air smelled of orange peel, candle smoke, and the starch used in church linen. On the chair lay a folded dress that belonged to no one presently living in the house.

Fournier said the room had been empty for thirty years. Then she said it had been used every Sunday by a woman who came to visit her sister. Then she said she had never said that. Her face did not change during any of these statements, but her fingers worried the seam of her sleeve.

They found the woman's name in the parish register: Ana Vale, no relation to Catarina, dead in 1961. The register described her as a visitor. The house ledger listed her as a tenant. The village newspaper had a photograph of her standing beside the pond with a child who was not named in the caption. Every Sunday for seven weeks, a different photograph showed the same woman, in the same dress, with the child's face turned away.

"It is a trick of the photographer," Tobias said.

"Then why does the child move?" Catarina asked.

They laid the photographs in date order. In the first, the child stood behind Ana. In the second, beside her. In the third, nearer the camera. In the fourth, the child's shoes were wet. In the fifth, the child was missing and Ana was looking directly at the photographer with a fury so composed it seemed almost ceremonial.

The room began to smell of Sunday again.

The smell came from the washstand. Beneath it, behind a board that did not match the others, Tobias found a curved silver horn, no longer than his forearm. It had a narrow mouthpiece and a bell at the other end, polished bright on the inside and tarnished at the seam. A small plaque was fixed to its handle. The letters were almost worn away: **FOR THE SENTENCE YOU CANNOT SAY.**

"A hearing trumpet," he said.

"It is listening the wrong way," Catarina replied. She held the bell toward the wall. "It hears the room."

Tobias took it from her. The metal was cold. He meant to say that he did not believe in the girl, the photographs, or the room's Sunday. He had reached "I don't—" when the horn gave a quiet note, like a glass touched by a wet finger.

At the far end of the corridor, a door appeared.

It was a narrow green door with a brass latch and no frame. The wallpaper had grown around it. A line of dust lay across its threshold. Tobias could hear no room behind it. When he tried the latch, it turned easily, but the door opened onto the same corridor from which they had come. The hallway had lengthened by six feet. A new patch of carpet, faded to the colour of old tea, ran between the two doors.

"What were you going to say?" Catarina asked.

"I was going to say I don't—"

"No." She put a hand on the horn. "You were going to refuse to say what you believe."

He lowered it. The green door remained. Whatever sentence had been about to leave him, the house had made a place where it could be postponed. It had not made the words disappear. It had given them a corridor.

They carried the horn downstairs and wrapped it in a towel. When Fournier saw it, she sat down. She said it had belonged to Ana Vale, then said she had never known Ana. She asked them to put it back. She asked them not to play it. She asked them to leave the room alone.

"Which request should we follow?" Tobias asked.

"The one I meant," said Fournier.

"Which one was that?"

Fournier looked at the stairs. For a moment, Tobias thought she might tell them. Then the bell of the horn rang once from inside the towel, and from the hall came the soft click of another latch.

They did not go upstairs. In the morning, the west bedroom's floor plan, kept in the archive, showed a second door that had not been there the day before.

Fournier was not in the doorway. The door was open. Beyond it, the corridor seemed longer than it had been when they entered. At the far end stood a girl in a yellow coat, holding a paper boat. Her hair was wet. She said something in French, quietly, and Catarina answered in Portuguese.

The girl shook her head. She wanted the name of the person who had taken her picture.

Tobias stepped toward her. The floorboards under his feet were cold, then warm, then cold again. The girl held out the boat. On its side, in ink that had run but not vanished, someone had written the station name from the timetable: Saint-Oran.

"I don't know," he said.

"You do," said Catarina, behind him.

The girl looked past Tobias. "He was here on Sunday."

"Who?"

The child's answer was a name Tobias could not hear. The room gave a sharp, dry click, like a camera shutter. The photographs on the bed were blank.

When they looked up, the corridor was its ordinary length. Fournier stood at the door with a bowl of peeled oranges. She did not remember leaving to fetch them. She did not remember the girl. The smell of Sunday was gone.

The photographs were not blank. The child had disappeared from all seven.

They made copies before midnight. In the copies, the child remained. On the back of the fifth photograph, Catarina found a date written in pencil: 14 March. It was the date Inês had called her, the night she said she had seen something at the jetty.

The impossible event had not solved the murder. It had made the missing interval harder to dismiss.

## Chapter 13: A Small Commission

Victor Hale arrived the next morning with a solicitor and a box of pears.

He apologised for the pears before anyone had accused him of bringing them. They were from his own trees, he said, and had gone soft in the middle. The solicitor introduced himself as Monsieur Arnaud and placed a document on the table. Hale placed the fruit beside it, as if the two objects belonged to separate negotiations.

The document offered Tobias a commission to write the history of Les Aulnes. The fee was generous. The deadline was six weeks. The contract required all notes, drafts, photographs, and correspondence to be delivered to Hale at the end of the work.

"You want me to write a book about this house," Tobias said.

"You have already written about the house."

"A novel."

"The distinction is important to novelists." Hale set a pear on the table. It left a wet mark. "Elise Marchetti used to sit where you are sitting. She asked inconvenient questions about the same accounts. I would prefer the history of the house to be written by someone who understands that some questions are better left in the water." Catarina, at the sink, did not turn. Tobias saw her shoulders tighten.

Catarina read the contract in French, then again in English. "It says he cannot keep a copy."

"Standard clause."

"It says the commissioner owns the research."

"Also standard."

"It says the writer may not publish any account of the property, its owners, its staff, or its guests without written consent."

Hale smiled. "You see why I need a lawyer."

"I see why you need a writer," Tobias said. "You need someone to make the past sound as if it happened in order."

For the first time, Hale stopped smiling.

He said the house had a complicated history. Families had come and gone. Records had been lost. There was no conspiracy, only careless bookkeeping and the usual appetite for a story. He said the people who lived here had made mistakes, and the people who came after them had sometimes improved the record. He did not say who "improved" it.

Tobias looked at the pears. One had collapsed against the table, leaving a wet brown mark shaped like a small country.

"We'll need the originals," he said.

"Of course."

"And independent copies."

"That may be difficult."

"Then the commission may be difficult."

The solicitor gathered the papers. Hale left the pears.

That night they opened the ledger copy, the photographs, and Inês's old envelope. The date on the envelope had been rubbed almost smooth. Under the lamp, another indentation showed through: **14 March — south door — do not follow the second arrival**.

Catarina read the sentence aloud. It was Inês's hand.

Neither of them knew what a second arrival meant. They knew the first arrival was Hale, who had come with a contract, a lawyer, and pears that could not be trusted. They knew the house had kept one Sunday for thirty years. They knew an erased name had returned beneath a pencil mark.

Tobias put the envelope in a tin and locked it in the desk.

At 6:42 the next morning, the house bell rang once. No one stood at the door. On the step lay a railway ticket punched for Saint-Oran, dated tomorrow.

## Chapter 14: The Room with No Owner

![Photorealistic illustration of a green door in an old house opening onto a hidden garden.](images/book1_green_door_garden.png)

*Fictional scene illustration.*

The green door had not been there when Tobias went upstairs. He was certain because the passage had one window, one radiator, and a crack in the plaster shaped like a continent that had declined to be identified. When he came down again, the door stood between the coat hooks and the umbrella stand.

It was narrow, painted the colour of old bottles, and fitted with a brass knob polished by a hand that had not yet touched it.

"Did you put that there?" he asked Catarina.

She sat at the kitchen table, sorting the west-bedroom notes into three piles: things she had seen, things she had been told, and things that had become difficult to distinguish. "I was going to ask you."

"Then neither of us did."

"That is not the same as nobody."

The silver horn lay between them. Tobias examined the plaster around the frame. There was no dust and no broken paint. The skirting board continued behind the door, then stopped in a clean right angle. The door seemed less installed than remembered by the wall.

He turned the knob. On the other side was a small room containing a table, two chairs, and a framed photograph of the front steps. In the photograph Tobias stood on the lowest step with his mouth open. Catarina was beside him, looking past the camera. The image was dated tomorrow.

"Close it," Catarina said.

He did. The horn gave a low, almost apologetic note. A second door appeared on the kitchen wall where the pantry should have been. The old pantry remained in place. The new door had no handle.

Tobias began, "I was going to tell you that I—"

The horn sounded again. A narrow door appeared beside the table. It opened onto a room with no furniture and no visible windows.

He stopped. Catarina watched him, not the door.

"I'm afraid the photograph is right," she said. She finished the sentence. The horn stayed silent. The room did not change.

"That is the first useful difference," Tobias said.

"It is a difference we have observed twice. It is not yet a law."

They left the doors where they were and went outside by the ordinary front entrance. When they returned, the first room remained, but the photograph showed the steps empty. The date beneath it had changed from tomorrow to yesterday. They did not take the picture. Tobias recorded its position and wording; Catarina recorded what she had seen and what she had not.

That night he wrote two descriptions of the room. In one, it existed. In the other, it had never existed. He filed both under observations and added a third sheet: *The filing system is not an argument.*

## Chapter 15: A Day Off

Catarina proposed that they leave the house for a day. Tobias asked whether this was an experiment.

"It is an errand," she said. "If you turn it into an experiment, I'll go without you."

They took the envelope with Inês's date, copies of the photographs, and the contract Hale had left behind. Each item went into a separate sleeve. Catarina carried the originals in her bag. Tobias carried copies in his coat. They agreed not to let either set out of sight at the same time.

At the gate Tobias looked back. The house had its usual number of windows. The green door could not be seen from outside.

They walked to the village and bought bread, batteries, and a roll of film. At the shop, the proprietor asked whether the house was still being repaired. Catarina said she did not know. It was true, though it sounded like a refusal to answer. Tobias nearly explained the doors. He did not. The explanation would have been long, and there was a queue.

At the café they laid out the documents. The contract called the house a private residence. The oldest photograph showed the same front steps but a different railing. Inês's sentence named a south door. The current plan showed no south door.

"Three records, three claims," Catarina said.

"And none tells us which one is the house."

"The house isn't a record."

They decided to ask Hale for the original plans and the complete property file. Tobias wrote the request in plain language. Catarina crossed out *all relevant papers* and replaced it with *the complete file, including indexes, amendments, and records marked as duplicates*. They mailed it before leaving the village.

On the walk back, the sky cleared. Light came through the plane trees in broad, broken patches. Catarina stopped to photograph the path, then asked Tobias to stand where the sunlight divided the pavement. He looked uncomfortable.

"Don't make me a symbol," he said.

"I'm trying to photograph the shadow."

She took the picture with him in it. The path and leaves were clear. Tobias's face blurred with movement.

They returned before dusk. The envelope remained sealed; the duplicate photographs were dry. Inside, the new door had vanished. The ordinary pantry was still where it had always been. Catarina added to their notes that they had gone out because she wanted to. They had spent a day away from the house and still carried its documents. That was not the same as being followed.

## Chapter 16: The Photograph in Available Light

They developed the roll at the village shop. The print of the path showed Catarina's shadow crossing Tobias's. His face was blurred, as in the camera preview. At the edge of the frame stood a green door.

It could have been the garden shed. It could have been a reflection in the shop window. Catarina marked the corner with a paper tab and did not write *the room* on the envelope.

They returned at noon and took the same picture from the same place. No door appeared. They repeated it at dusk. The print showed tree trunks, the path, and a pale patch where the sun reached the ground. Nothing else.

Tobias wanted to call the first image proof that the house had followed them. Catarina placed the three prints side by side.

"What can we say without guessing?"

"The first print contains a green rectangle."

"And?"

"It resembles the door."

"Good. What can't we say?"

"That it was the door."

She put the first print in an envelope by itself. Tobias kept the negatives. The digital scan went onto a separate memory card. The records could be compared later; none would be allowed to certify itself.

At the west-bedroom window, late light fell across the floorboards. It made the dust visible and the scratches seem deeper. Catarina remembered a painting she had seen years earlier, a landscape in which the colour of a hill seemed to depend on the weather outside the gallery. She could not remember its title. Tobias guessed Cézanne. She told him he was guessing.

"Then we can leave the title blank," he said.

They watched the light move. Nothing opened. Nothing spoke. The ordinary shadows shifted with the sun, and the room became easier to see as the light lowered. Catarina photographed the floor without the horn or papers in frame. In the print, a pale rectangular mark appeared where the trunk had stood.

They checked the boards. The rectangle was real: a difference in dust and colour, not a hole. They measured it. It was too shallow to contain the trunk's missing space and too wide to have been made by its feet.

At the bottom of the photograph Tobias wrote: *Mark on floor; cause unknown.* He did not write *imprint of an absent object*. Catarina said that was the first caption she trusted.

## Chapter 17: The Map of the House That Wasn't

The floor plan arrived folded inside a library book Tobias had not borrowed. It was drawn in blue pencil on the back of an electricity bill. The house appeared from above, with each room labelled: kitchen, west bedroom, hall, green room, and *room for a person who has not yet decided to stay*.

"That last one wasn't here yesterday," Tobias said.

"Yesterday is not a reliable surveyor," Catarina replied.

She spread the sheet across the table and pinned its corners with the sugar bowl, the chipped cup, a pear, and the horn. The drawing showed a narrow passage behind the west bedroom and a circular room beneath the stairs. A staircase rose from the pantry into a space beyond the paper's edge.

They measured the rooms with string. The west bedroom was three centimetres shorter than the plan; the hall was six centimetres longer. The pantry matched, except at the place where the unmarked door had appeared and disappeared. The circular room had no corresponding footprint.

"Someone could have drawn an imaginary room," Tobias said.

"Someone could have drawn a room that is real to the paper."

"You don't believe that."

"I believe the paper is real. I believe the drawing is real. I believe the measurements don't match. I'm waiting to be told what else I'm meant to believe."

They went beneath the stairs. Catarina tapped the plaster with a spoon. Most of it returned a dull sound. One square gave a hollow note. They marked its boundaries with chalk. It was wide enough for a narrow door and too low for a person to pass through without bending.

The horn warmed in Tobias's hands. He had not noticed picking it up.

"I was going to say that I've been here before," he said. "Not in the house. In the room behind this wall."

Nothing happened.

"Perhaps the sentence has to be unfinished," Catarina said.

"Or perhaps the horn is a horn."

They waited. A pipe clicked overhead. Gigi barked once at the garden gate and settled again. The chalk rectangle remained a rectangle.

Tobias put the horn down and pressed his palm against the plaster. For an instant he smelled oranges and coal smoke. Then the smell was gone.

They agreed not to break through the wall. Catarina recorded the measurements; Tobias added the smell as an uncertain observation. They did not write that the house had remembered him or invented him.

That evening the electricity failed. In the west bedroom a small circle of light appeared beneath the floorboards. They stood on either side of it with the horn between them. There was no voice, footstep, or machine. At 6:42 the light went out.

In the morning the circular room had vanished from the plan. In its place the drafter had drawn a square marked **UNMEASURED**.

## Chapter 18: The Museum of Ordinary Mistakes

They found the museum in the shed. It had no sign, ticket desk, or curator. Its exhibits stood on shelves made from fruit crates: keys that opened no known lock, teacups repaired with their handles on the wrong side, letters addressed to people who had never received them.

"It's an index," Tobias said.

"Of mistakes?"

"Of objects corrected until the correction became the object."

The captions were typed on small slips. **Key for the door removed from the plan.** **Cup repaired by the person who broke it, handle facing away.** **Letter returned before it was posted.** Each label offered an explanation, then crossed out one word in pencil.

At the back of the shed a mechanical bird sat beneath a glass dome. Its wings were tin; its feet were black thread. A card said it had been built by a person waiting for someone to return. The clockwork was intact. It had never been wound.

"Why display an object that has never worked?" Catarina asked.

"Perhaps it works by remaining unwound," Tobias said.

"That is what people say when they want a broken object to be profound."

She lifted the dome. The horn sounded from the house. The bird turned its head. Its beak opened without a sound. A narrow drawer slid from the shelf beneath it. Inside lay a sentence: **I was waiting for you to ask whether I wanted to come back.**

Catarina recognized the hand as similar to the one on the floor plan. She did not call it Inês's. She folded the paper and put it in an envelope.

They listed what they could establish: the bird was made of tin and thread; its clockwork was intact; the drawer had opened; the paper had been inside it. The key had a notch worn smooth by use. One cup had tea residue under its base. The letter had a fold where it had been opened, although its label said it had never arrived.

"Maybe the mistake is in the label," Tobias said.

"Maybe the label is part of the exhibit."

They turned the bird over and found a maker's mark almost polished away. It had no matching number. They photographed both marks but could not tell whether the parts had begun together.

Catarina asked Tobias if he wanted to hear what she thought the sentence meant. He said yes, then added that she did not have to tell him. She said it sounded like something Inês might have said. It also sounded like what Catarina feared her sister had wanted to say. The paper could not supply the memory.

They labelled the envelope **found in drawer beneath unwound bird**. They did not write **Inês's message**. The museum's strangest exhibit was the distance between those two captions.

## Chapter 19: The Weather Inside the Walls

Rain began in the pantry. It fell in a soft curtain from a ceiling with no leak. The shelves stayed dry. The flour remained white. A single drop landed on the calendar, exactly on the day crossed out in red.

Tobias fetched a bowl; Catarina fetched a notebook. They watched for ten minutes. The floor remained dry around the falling water. The drops disappeared before reaching the bowl, though the bowl slowly filled with clear water.

"The room is borrowing weather," Tobias said.

"From where?"

"The question isn't in the notes."

"Then put it there."

They recorded the starting time, the bowl's volume, and the room's temperature. A second bowl outside the pantry collected nothing. Blotting paper beneath the falling water grew damp, then dried without a stain.

At noon the rain stopped. At the bottom of the bowl lay a small grey feather. Gigi sniffed it and sneezed. Catarina sealed it in an envelope. Tobias photographed the pantry, the bowl, and the feather. After he moved the bowl, the second photograph showed the feather centered in the room, although it was sealed inside the envelope.

"That photograph is wrong," he said.

"Or the first one is."

"The camera was working."

"That isn't the same as the camera being right."

The file had a plausible timestamp and intact metadata. The image showed the bowl and feather. It did not show what happened between exposures. They kept the envelope and both photographs, and recorded their disagreement.

The next time it rained inside, Tobias stayed outside with the camera while Catarina watched from the pantry. The camera showed an empty room. Catarina's notebook showed the bowl filling drop by drop. They switched places. The rain stopped. The camera registered a change in exposure; the bowl had gained two spoonfuls.

They checked for a pipe above the cupboard and found none. The feather remained sealed. Its weight did not change.

Outside, ordinary rain darkened the soil, tapped the leaves, and soaked Tobias's cuffs. Catarina brought him a towel. He stepped on Gigi's water bowl and they both laughed.

They filed the photographs in separate sleeves. In the margin Catarina wrote: *Similar weather is not the same event.*

## Chapter 20: A Measure of Distance

The new rooms made the house difficult to describe, so Catarina proposed measuring the distance between the front door and the kitchen table. They used a tape measure, then string, then Tobias's steps with his eyes closed. The results differed by centimetres, knots, and one whole step.

"We are not measuring the same thing," he said.

"We are measuring the same path by different methods."

"And the path changes."

"That is one explanation."

They repeated the measurements at different hours. In the morning the hall was short. At dusk it was long enough to contain the green door. At night it seemed ordinary until Tobias turned back and could no longer see the kitchen.

They made a chart. The horizontal axis showed time; the vertical axis showed distance. Catarina marked each method with a different symbol and left uncertain readings as open circles. Tobias wanted to draw a line through the points.

"What would the line mean?" she asked.

"A trend."

"Or a wish."

They left the points unconnected.

At 6:42 the horn sounded. The hallway lengthened by the distance from the kitchen to the garden gate. The front door remained in sight, and the gate appeared beyond it, as if the house had become a transparent corridor. Catarina walked forward. Each step took her no nearer to either end.

"I want to get out," Tobias said. He did not finish.

The horn rang. A door appeared behind them, opening onto the same hall from the other direction.

Tobias looked at it. "I want to get out, and I want to understand why."

The horn fell silent. The new door remained.

They did not rush through it. Catarina asked whether he wanted to try the front door. He said yes. They walked back along the measured stretch. The front door was three paces away.

The tape gave one plausible number. The string gave another. Tobias's steps matched neither. Catarina entered all three.

"The house changes when we refuse to finish," he said.

"Sometimes."

"And when we finish?"

"Sometimes it changes anyway."

"That is a bad rule."

"It is a rule we don't understand."

They wrote down the two sentences and the two doors, then left the chart on the kitchen table. A drop of rain fell on its blank margin. The paper curled at the edge. Catarina flattened it with the chipped cup and wrote the time beside the mark.

The house did not grow another room. The chart remained incomplete, which was the most accurate thing about it.

## Chapter 21: The House Keeps an Index

The index appeared on the kitchen wall in the shape of a damp rectangle. At first it looked like the outline left by a picture. Then letters emerged, one at a time, in handwriting neither Tobias nor Catarina recognized.

**A. Rooms occupied.**

**B. Rooms expected.**

**C. Rooms denied.**

Below the headings was a list of doors. Some led to familiar rooms; others to rooms that had vanished. One entry read **Door with no hinge: not yet requested**.

"It's organizing us," Tobias said.

"Or organizing the house around the words we use."

"Those are nearly the same."

"Nearly is where we live."

The index had no author, date, or page numbers. Beside *denied*, someone had crossed out the word and written *unclaimed*. Catarina copied both versions into her notebook.

Tobias went for the horn. It lay on the dining table beside the little brass key found beneath the west-bedroom floorboard. When he lifted it, he heard a faint voice from the curved mouth.

"I didn't mean to leave."

He set the horn down.

"Did you hear that?"

"No."

"It said, ‘I didn't mean to leave.'"

"Who said it?"

"I don't know."

"Then write exactly that."

He did. The wall index added another line: **D. Voices heard by one person.** Beneath it appeared the same sentence, without quotation marks.

They waited. The house made ordinary sounds: pipes, wood settling, the refrigerator motor. Catarina wrote that Tobias reported hearing a voice and that she had not. She wrote that the sentence appeared on the wall after he recorded it. She did not write that the house had spoken.

At dusk the index faded. The damp rectangle remained. Tobias photographed it from three distances. In one image the letters were visible; in another they were not. The camera had focused on the wallpaper pattern, which resembled a map of a country with no coast.

The next morning the wall was bare. The wording remained in their notes, along with its source and the disagreement. They carried the record without treating it as an instruction.

## Chapter 22: The Guide to Rooms

The house provided a guidebook the following morning. It was bound in green cloth and titled **Rooms for Visitors Who Have Not Arrived**. There were no page numbers. The opening page instructed the reader to begin wherever they had already been.

The first room was described as a dining room with a table set for four. In the actual house, the dining room contained a table set for three. Tobias checked each chair. One had been moved away from the table. Catarina said they had not set it that way. Neither could remember who had.

The guide described the second room as an observatory. The house had no observatory. They went into the garden and looked up at the roof. There was a round window above the upstairs landing, where a rectangular one had been the previous day. Through it, they could see the sky at night, though the morning was clear.

"Could be a reflection," Tobias said.

"From what?"

"A room that isn't there."

"Then we should say that is what it could be."

The guide's third entry was a chapel containing a single basin of water. They found no chapel, but in the bathroom the sink was full of rainwater. A grey feather floated on its surface. The feather looked like the one they had sealed in an envelope. They checked the envelope. It was still closed, and the original feather remained inside.

The guide did not explain whether the rooms were destinations, memories, or instructions. Its final page was blank except for a line at the bottom: **If you cannot find the room, describe what you expected to find.**

Tobias wrote down the rooms they had visited. Catarina added those they had imagined. They compared the lists. The entries did not overlap. Both seemed accurate to their respective experiences.

They tried the guide's instruction. Tobias expected the observatory to contain a telescope. Catarina expected it to contain a desk covered in papers. Through the round window, they saw both objects for a moment: a telescope aimed at the ceiling, a desk with papers arranged in two neat stacks. Then the sky returned.

"Perhaps expectation is another door," Tobias said.

"Perhaps. Or perhaps we saw what we expected because we were looking for it."

"Could you tell the difference?"

"Not from here."

She left the guidebook open on the table. When they returned, it had closed itself. Its green cloth cover showed no title. The rooms continued to exist in their notes, which was the only place either of them could agree to meet them.

## Chapter 23: The Unfinished Supper

They invited no one to supper. The house laid the table for six.

Six plates, six glasses, six folded napkins. A covered dish sat at the centre. Catarina counted everything twice. Tobias checked the calendar and confirmed that no one had been expected. The horn was on the sideboard, pointed toward the table.

"We can put the extra settings away," Tobias said.

They removed one plate. A second appeared at the far end. They removed the glasses. The table gained another setting, complete with a small spoon and a folded paper place card. The card bore no name.

"It wants guests," Tobias said.

"The house doesn't want anything."

"Then the arrangement does."

"That's more precise."

They left the table and made tea. When they came back, the dish had opened. It contained a bowl of pears, each one unbruised. The fruit looked waxy and untouched. Tobias picked one up. It was cold.

The horn sounded.

"I was about to say I miss the way things were before the house started answering," Catarina said.

The dining room door opened onto a narrow passage. At its end stood another table, also set for six. A second passage opened to the left, then another, each ending at a dining table. The house had not made a larger room. It had made a series of rooms in which the supper could continue without anyone arriving.

"We should close them," Tobias said.

"I want to see what happens if we don't."

They walked down the first passage. At the table, the place cards were blank. The second table had five settings. The third had seven. At the fourth, every plate held a small key. The keys were different sizes, all too small for any lock they had found.

At the last table sat the chipped cup. It had been placed on a folded napkin. Beside it was a note in Catarina's handwriting: **You may leave whenever you wish.**

"I didn't write that," she said.

"It sounds like you."

"That doesn't make it mine."

They carried the cup back to the kitchen. The passages shortened behind them. By the time they reached the dining room, the table held only two settings. The covered dish was empty. The pears were back in the fruit bowl, one with the familiar bruise.

They ate bread and cheese at the kitchen table. Nobody had to finish a sentence. Nothing else appeared. The house remained quiet, though not empty of possibility.

## Chapter 24: A Door for the Reader

Tobias found the door while sorting photographs. It was printed on the back of an image of the garden gate: a small white door, no larger than a postage stamp, in the centre of the lawn. He turned the photograph over. The back was blank. He turned it again. The door remained.

"Do you see it?"

Catarina leaned over. "I see a white square."

"It's a door."

"It might be a mark on the photograph."

"It has a hinge."

"Then the photograph has a mark shaped like a hinge."

He held the image close. The paper was old and slightly warped. The door looked as though it had been photographed from above. A thin line of grass crossed its threshold.

They placed the photograph on the floor where the door would be. Nothing happened. They set the horn beside it. Nothing happened. Catarina said she did not want to open a door whose size had been chosen by the photograph. The horn stayed quiet.

"I was going to say I wanted to see what was inside," Tobias said.

The horn rang. A rectangle of light appeared in the image. It did not become a real opening, but the photograph warmed beneath his fingers. The door seemed to move inward.

"You finished the sentence," Catarina said.

"I did."

"Then perhaps it isn't the words."

"Perhaps it is the wanting."

"Or the photograph is warming because your hand is on it."

They put the picture down and waited. The light faded. The white door returned to a square. Tobias wrote both explanations beneath the image and left room for another.

The next day, the photograph was gone. In its place lay a page of blank paper, folded into a small door. Catarina unfolded it. There was no writing inside, only a faint impression of the image's hinge.

"Does the house want us to enter?" Tobias asked.

"I don't know."

"Do you want to?"

"Not today."

They put the paper in a drawer. Nothing opened. Nothing closed. The reader of the notes would have to decide whether the door had existed, whether it had been an image, or whether the distinction had become too small to matter.

## Chapter 25: The Scale in the Pantry

The kitchen scale began to give a different weight for every object. A pear weighed one hundred and twelve grams, then one hundred and thirteen, then nothing. The chipped cup weighed exactly as much as the empty bowl. The brass key weighed the same as the horn, though Tobias could lift the key with two fingers.

"The scale is broken," Catarina said.

"It might be the floor."

They moved it to the table, then to the counter, then to a shelf. The readings changed each time. Tobias put a sealed jar of sugar on it. The display showed 740 grams. He turned the jar around. It showed 740. He removed the jar and replaced it. The number changed to 741.

They tested a set of coins. The total was stable if the coins were weighed together. Individually, their weights drifted. Tobias wrote each value down and circled the inconsistent ones. Catarina suggested that the scale's precision exceeded its accuracy. He liked the phrase and added it to the notes.

The horn rang while they were weighing the brass key.

"I was about to say I don't know whether I can trust what I remember," Tobias said.

The scale showed zero.

"That doesn't answer your question," Catarina said.

"No."

"Do you want to tell me what you remember?"

He described the first night in the house: the corridor that had seemed too long, a light behind the wall, and a voice he could not place. He had not told her before because he was afraid she would believe him too quickly. She listened without looking toward the horn.

When he finished, the scale displayed 112 grams. There was nothing on it.

"We can record that the display did," Catarina said. "We cannot record that your memory weighed something."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He looked at the empty platform. "I think so."

The number faded. They unplugged the scale. When they plugged it in again, it returned to zero and stayed there. They did not weigh anything else that day.

## Chapter 26: Low Tide in the Hall

At low tide, the hallway smelled of salt. No water was visible, but the floorboards had the dark sheen of a beach after the sea withdrew. Small shells lay beneath the coat rack. One held a grain of black sand. Another contained a paper label with the word **RETURN** printed on it.

Tobias went outside to check the weather. The garden was dry. The path was dry. The house stood several streets from the river and much farther from the sea. Catarina took a photograph of the shells where she found them, then moved one to a white plate. The shell left no mark on the wood.

"A tide with no water," Tobias said.

"A smell, a surface, some shells. We don't have to call it a tide."

"But it is one possible description."

"Then put it in the list of descriptions."

They followed the line of shells to the stairs. There, it ended at a small square of blue tile that had never been part of the floor. The tile was wet. A single footprint crossed it and stopped. The print was too small to be either of theirs.

The horn lay upstairs. Its note carried down the stairwell, thin as a signal from a ship. Neither of them spoke. The tile dried. The shells became ordinary shells again, though Tobias could not identify their species.

They wrote down the sequence: salt smell, dark boards, shells, label, footprint, tile, horn. Catarina drew a plan of where each item had appeared. Tobias added that the order might be wrong because they had noticed the objects at different times. The map had no arrows.

That evening, the hallway was dry and smelled of polish. The shells remained on the plate. The paper label had faded until only the letters **RET** could be read. Catarina placed the plate in the cabinet with the other unexplained objects. It did not ask to be returned.

## Chapter 27: The House as a Witness

They tried interviewing the house. Tobias prepared questions in advance. Catarina said that asking a house questions did not make it a witness, but she agreed to keep the record.

He sat at the kitchen table and asked: "Did you make the green door?"

The pipes clicked.

"Did anyone else make it?"

The refrigerator motor stopped.

"Was the room real?"

The horn remained silent.

They waited five minutes. No door appeared. Tobias wrote **no response observed** beside each question. Catarina asked whether he wanted to continue. He said yes, but not because he expected an answer.

She sat in the chair opposite him. "Did the house keep you here?"

The kitchen window clouded over. A clear line appeared down the centre, separating the garden into two versions. In one, the gate was open. In the other, it was closed. Catarina turned in her chair to check the real gate. It stood open.

"That is an answer," Tobias said.

"It is an event after a question."

"The distinction matters."

"Yes."

They went outside. The gate moved slightly in the wind, but the latch had not caught. Tobias closed it. The two images in the window became one.

"Maybe the house can't tell us whether it kept me," he said.

"Maybe the question doesn't have a single answer."

"Maybe."

They returned to the table. Tobias crossed out **interview** in the notebook and wrote **questions asked near the house**. Catarina added the time and weather. Neither wrote **testimony**.

That night the green door appeared in Tobias's dream. He opened it and found the kitchen on the other side. He told Catarina in the morning. She asked what he had expected to find. He could not say. The horn did not ring.

## Chapter 28: A House with No Centre

The floor plan stopped making sense at the point where the kitchen met the hall. Each room had a correct outline, but no arrangement placed them all together. Tobias cut out the rooms from tracing paper and moved them around on the table. Catarina marked the doors with small blue lines. Every plan connected all the rooms except one.

The missing room was always the one they were standing in.

"Perhaps the house has no centre," Tobias said.

"A house can have a centre without us knowing where it is."

"And if there isn't one?"

"Then we stop looking for it."

They took the plans outside and laid them on the grass. From above, the cutouts looked like a child's puzzle. A breeze shifted the kitchen into the garden, the garden into the bedroom, the bedroom into a white space with no label. They held the corners down with stones.

The horn gave a low note. The paper did not move. Catarina stood in the space labelled HALL. Tobias stood in the space labelled KITCHEN. Between them, the plan had no connecting doorway.

"I'm going to come to you," he said.

He stepped across the grass. The map remained incomplete. He crossed the space between them and took her hand.

"We don't need the paper to connect us," Catarina said.

"No."

The wind lifted one corner. The stone rolled away. The map folded in on itself, then opened to show the rooms in a different order. The house behind them remained where it had been. Its windows reflected the late sun, ordinary and bright.

They left the paper on the grass until dusk. When they gathered it, the blank space in the middle was warm. Tobias carried it inside. Catarina put it in the drawer beneath the telephone, beside the green guidebook and the photograph with the tiny door.

## Chapter 29: The Portraits That Had No Faces

The front room acquired a row of portraits overnight. They hung above the mantel in frames of different sizes, none of them matching the furniture. Every canvas showed a person from behind. One wore a hat. One held a basket. One seemed to be standing in the rain, though the paint was dry and the room warm.

"They're all looking away," Tobias said.

"Perhaps they don't want to be looked at."

"Then why hang them here?"

"Perhaps they didn't."

Catarina turned the frames around. The backs were blank, except for small brass plates stamped with numbers. They counted nine portraits. The plates ran from 1 to 8, then 10. There was no number 9.

Tobias fetched his notebook. Catarina asked him to leave it shut for a moment. She wanted to see whether the portraits changed when they were not being recorded. They stood with their backs to the mantel and listened to the room. The radiator clicked. A car passed outside. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

When they turned around, the portrait with the basket had moved to the far end of the row. Its frame was narrower than before. The basket looked full of pears.

"I saw that change," Tobias said.

"So did I."

"The same change?"

"The picture moved. The frame narrowed. The basket appeared full."

They wrote separate descriptions before comparing them. Catarina remembered seven portraits; Tobias remembered nine. Both counted again and found nine. The disagreement stayed in their notes.

They took the portrait down and carried it to the kitchen. The back plate bore the number 5. The canvas showed a figure at a table, their head bent over a sheet of paper. No face was visible. The room in the painting resembled the west bedroom, except that its window looked out on a sea.

"Could be a reflection of one of the photographs," Tobias said.

"We have no photograph of that room."

"Then maybe a room we haven't seen."

"Or a room the painter imagined."

They put the portrait face down. At noon, a soft knocking came from beneath the canvas. Three taps, then a pause, then three more. They turned it over. The figure in the painting was now standing at the window. The paper on the table was blank.

The horn lay on the shelf beside them. It did not sound. Tobias was about to say that the picture had changed because they had turned away. He stopped. Catarina asked whether he wanted to finish. He said no.

They carried the canvas back to the front room and replaced it in the row. The empty brass plate marked 9 had appeared between two frames. It had no portrait beneath it, only a dark rectangle on the wall.

"A missing picture," Tobias said.

"Or a space for one."

"Could we fill it?"

"We could. I don't think we should."

They left the space empty. At dusk, the row of portraits showed only the room behind them: two chairs, a mantel, a blank place on the wall. The people had disappeared from the canvases. The frames remained.

Tobias described the event in his notebook without calling the portraits haunted. Catarina added that the room had not made a sound after the knocking. They agreed that both observations belonged in the record.

At night, the empty frame was gone. In its place was a clean rectangle of wallpaper, the pattern unbroken. The portraits showed their figures again, still facing away.

## Chapter 30: The Room That Was Only a Sentence

They found a sentence written on the underside of the dining table: **A room is a promise that can be walked into.**

The ink was fresh. It smelled of iron. Catarina asked whether Tobias had written it. He said no. She said she had not. They checked the pens they had left in the drawer. None had leaked.

"It sounds like a definition," he said.

"It sounds like an invitation."

"A promise to whom?"

"The person who reads it."

They did not walk anywhere. They wrote down the sentence and the place where they had found it. The horn remained silent. In the afternoon, the sentence had moved to the underside of the kitchen chair. In the evening, it was on the wall beside the green door's old mark. It never appeared in the same handwriting twice.

Tobias began a list of possible authors. Catarina stopped him. They did not have evidence that a person had written the sentence. They knew only that it was present and that the ink had seemed fresh. She suggested they record what they could inspect: wording, location, appearance, and change over time.

They made a rubbing on thin paper. The graphite captured the words, but also the grain of the wood. When they held the rubbing up to the light, the sentence looked like a floor plan. The word **room** formed four walls. **Promise** was written where a door would be.

"The paper has made a room," Tobias said.

"The marks resemble one."

They folded the rubbing so the four words met. The paper became a small square with a door-shaped flap. Inside was nothing. It was not a room, but it had an inside.

They left it on the table. No one entered. No one needed to. The sentence remained a sentence, and the paper remained folded. At midnight, the horn sounded once from the front room. The little paper square opened by itself.

In the morning, it was flat again. The graphite had smudged. The wording was still legible. Catarina slid it between two notebook pages and marked the date. Tobias did not ask what the sentence meant. For now, they had described where it had been and what it had done.

## Chapter 31: The Return Label

The cardboard box arrived without a postmark. It sat on the front step, dry after a night of rain, with a label that read **RETURN TO SENDER**. The sender's address was their own house. The recipient's line was blank.

Tobias carried it inside. Catarina did not let him open it until they had photographed all six sides. The tape had been cut and replaced. The box smelled of cedar and old paper. Inside lay a folded coat, a railway ticket, and a small envelope addressed to **the person who comes back**.

The coat belonged to neither of them. It was dark blue, with a missing button and a repaired cuff. The ticket named Saint-Oran, the station that had been closed before either Tobias or Catarina was born. Its date was tomorrow.

"This is for the next book," Tobias said.

"This is for someone," Catarina said. "We don't know who."

They left the envelope sealed. The horn did not ring. The house gave no sign that it recognized the ticket.

Tobias examined the coat. The repair at the cuff used green thread. It matched the narrow green line that had appeared around the west bedroom door. Catarina checked the ticket under a magnifying glass. The paper fibres were old, but the ink was fresh. The printer's mark belonged to no railway company she knew.

They placed each object in a separate sleeve. Tobias wrote the time of discovery. Catarina recorded the condition of the box and the location on the step. Neither wrote "time travel." Neither wrote "message from the house."

At dusk, the envelope slid from the table to the floor. It landed without opening. They picked it up together. The paper was warm.

"We could read it," Tobias said.

"We could."

"Do you want to?"

"Not until we know whether it is addressed to us."

"It says the person who comes back."

"That could be anyone."

They put the envelope in the drawer beneath the telephone. The guidebook, folded map, and little paper door were already there. The drawer shut normally. No new room appeared.

In the night, Tobias dreamed of a station platform with no train. Catarina dreamed of a coat hanging from a hook in a room she had never entered. In the morning, neither dream matched the other. The ticket still said tomorrow.

They packed the coat and ticket in the document case. The envelope stayed in the drawer. A sentence could wait without becoming a door. They had learned that much, though neither trusted the lesson to hold everywhere.

Before leaving, Catarina looked once at the blank recipient line on the box. She wrote the date beside it, then stopped before adding a name. The cardboard absorbed the pencil mark. It did not offer a suggestion.

"We can come back," Tobias said.

"We can leave first."

They closed the front door. From the path, the house looked ordinary and small. The windows reflected the sky. No light showed beneath the walls. On the step, the box remained, though they had carried it inside.

Tobias turned back. Catarina kept walking. He looked once more, then followed her down the path. They did not know whether the box had been moved, remembered, or merely left where it had begun.

## Chapter 32: The Inventory of Departures

They made an inventory before they left the house. Catarina insisted on it because leaving had become an event the house might rearrange. Tobias listed their bags, keys, notebooks, and the sealed envelope. She checked each item against the list and marked whether it was carried, stored, or deliberately left behind.

The horn remained on the kitchen table. The small paper door stayed in the telephone drawer. The green guidebook lay beneath the floor plan, which no longer matched the house. The chipped cup was in the sink, handle turned toward the wall.

"We should take the cup," Tobias said.

"Why?"

"It's part of the record."

"The cup is not a record."

"It has been in every room."

"That makes it a cup that has travelled."

He put it in the bag anyway. Catarina let him, but wrote **cup, chipped, carried by Tobias** on the inventory. Then she took it out and set it beside the sink. They agreed it had no special claim on either of them.

At the front door Tobias checked the list. One item remained unmarked: **the house**.

"We can't carry it," he said.

"We can leave it."

"Do you think it knows?"

"We have no evidence it knows anything."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have."

He marked **left** beside the entry. Catarina signed beneath the list. Tobias signed beside her name. The ink dried at once. Nothing in the hall changed.

They stepped outside and closed the door. The lock turned with a familiar click. They walked to the gate. At the road, Catarina looked back. The house had not grown taller or smaller. Its windows were dark. It seemed to contain the rooms they remembered, and perhaps the ones they did not.

They went to the station with the document case between them. The ticket in the case was for tomorrow. They bought two tickets for today. The clerk stamped them and returned them. The stamp showed the correct date.

"A mundane success," Tobias said.

"We should record it."

They did. In the carriage, Catarina opened the notebook and read the inventory aloud. The words sounded less like a farewell than a set of instructions for returning. She closed the book before the last page.

## Chapter 33: The Person in the Window

Halfway through the journey, Tobias saw someone standing in the reflection across the aisle. The carriage was nearly empty. Catarina sat beside him. Beyond the glass, fields passed in a continuous green blur. In the reflection, a third figure stood behind them, one hand resting on the seat.

He turned around. Nobody was there.

"Did you see that?"

Catarina looked at the window. "I see us."

"There was someone behind us."

"In the reflection?"

"Yes."

"Then it may have been outside."

"The fields are empty."

"We're moving. They aren't."

He described the figure: pale coat, dark collar, head turned toward the window. Catarina wrote the description in the notebook without giving the figure a name. They watched the glass. The image did not return.

At the next tunnel, the carriage darkened. Tobias saw the figure again, now seated in their row. The reflected person held a folded paper. Catarina looked at the window and saw only the two of them, their faces floating over the black tunnel.

When the train emerged, a folded paper lay on the seat opposite. Neither had seen it arrive. It was a railway receipt, dated the previous day, with no origin or destination printed. The amount was zero.

They checked the seats, the rack, and the floor. No one had entered the carriage. Tobias photographed the receipt where it lay. Catarina asked him not to touch it until they had recorded its position. He used a clean page to slide it into an envelope.

"It could have been there before we boarded," he said.

"It could."

"It could have fallen from someone's bag."

"It could."

"And we could have missed it."

"We could."

They listed the possibilities. The paper gave no further information. The zero amount seemed less like a fare than a refusal to calculate one.

At the destination, they asked the station office whether the receipt matched a standard form. It did not. The paper stock was common. The printer's mark was incomplete. The date was legible, though no one could say when it had been printed.

They kept the receipt with the other documents. Tobias later found that it had faded to blank. Catarina's photograph remained. It showed the original marks clearly. They filed both: the object as found, and the image as recorded.

## Chapter 34: The Table at the End of the Line

The train terminated at a coastal town where the platform ended at a wall. There were no signs for the sea, though the air smelled of salt. The station had one bench, one clock, and a table set for two beneath the departure board.

Tobias checked the timetable. The train was due to continue north. The board disagreed. It said the service had ended in 1962.

"We're standing on the platform," Catarina said.

"Yes."

"That is evidence of a platform."

"Not necessarily of the service."

They waited for the station staff. Nobody came. The table held two cups of tea and a plate with a single pear. The tea was warm. The pear had a bruise in the same place as the one they had left at home.

"We shouldn't eat it," Tobias said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You looked at it."

"Looking isn't eating."

They photographed the table, the cups, the clock, and the board. The time on the clock was 6:42. Tobias compared it to his watch. The watch showed 6:43. Catarina's showed 6:42. The difference was within the margin of their devices.

They sat on the bench instead of at the table. The tea cooled. The pear remained untouched. After several minutes, a train arrived on the track behind them. It was the same train they had left, though the carriage numbers were different. The conductor opened the door and asked where they wanted to go.

"We're not sure this is our train," Catarina said.

"It is the one that stops here," the conductor replied.

"The timetable says there's no service."

"The timetable is old."

He showed them a printed schedule with today's date. Their departure station was listed. The coastal town was not. The train was scheduled to pass through without stopping.

"Then why did you stop?" Tobias asked.

The conductor checked his watch. "I didn't."

He stepped back into the carriage. The doors closed. The train departed. The table remained, but the cups were gone. The pear sat alone on the platform bench.

Catarina picked it up, then set it on the wall beside the track. "For whoever left it."

They continued on foot toward the sea. The timetable did not change. The wall ended at a narrow road, and the road reached a beach. There was no station behind them when they looked back.

## Chapter 35: What We Carry Forward

At the water's edge, Tobias opened the document case. He wanted to check that everything remained inside. The notebook was there, along with the floor plan, the faded receipt, and the ticket to Saint-Oran. The sealed envelope was not.

They retraced their steps. The sand held their footprints and no others. They returned to the road, the wall, and the place where the table had stood. There was no table. No pear. The station clock was not visible.

"We could have left the envelope at home," Tobias said.

"We recorded packing it."

"We recorded the list. Not the envelope itself."

"The list says sealed envelope."

"That is still our record."

They checked the case again. A small green thread was caught in the clasp. Catarina recognized it from the repaired cuff of the coat in the cardboard box. Tobias thought the case had been closed before they left. Neither remembered seeing the thread.

They did not decide where it came from. Catarina placed it in a small sleeve and labelled it **green thread, found at case clasp, origin unknown**. Tobias copied the label into the notebook. The action steadied them. It did not explain the missing envelope.

"Maybe the house kept it," he said.

"Maybe we never packed it."

"Maybe it was taken."

"We can keep all three possibilities."

They walked back to the station that did exist. The ordinary train arrived at the listed time. Its destination was printed on the board. The carriage seats were empty. The window showed only the platform and their reflections.

They boarded. Catarina held the document case on her knees. Tobias sat across from her. Neither spoke for the first ten minutes. The train moved through a landscape that remained outside the glass.

At the next stop, Catarina said, "We left something behind."

"Yes."

"We don't know what happened to it."

"No."

"We can still continue."

Tobias looked out at the passing fields. "Yes."

The train gathered speed. In the notebook, beneath the inventory, Catarina wrote: **Departure does not settle ownership. Return does not prove identity.** Tobias added the date. They closed the book and carried it with them.

## Chapter 36: The Book Inside the Green Room

The green room returned in a book.

Tobias found the book beneath his pillow, although he had not gone to bed. Its cover was plain brown cloth. No title, author, or library mark. The first page contained a drawing of the green door. The second showed the room beyond it: table, two chairs, photograph. The third began a story.

**A traveller came to a house where the doors opened only for unfinished sentences. She carried a book because she had been told the book knew the way.**

"That sounds like you," Catarina said.

"It sounds like a traveller."

"You're the one holding the book."

Tobias turned the page. The traveller found a room with a table and two chairs. A woman sat in one chair, writing a story about a traveller who had found a room. In that story, the traveller opened a book.

The next page was blank.

"It stops there," he said.

"Or it expects us to continue."

"I don't want to become a character in a book."

"You may already be one."

He put the volume down. The cover had changed. In small gold letters, it now read **A TRAVELLER'S GUIDE**. Catarina took it and opened to the blank page. She wrote: **The woman did not sit in the second chair.**

The ink appeared slowly, as if the paper were deciding whether to keep it.

The next line formed beneath hers: **She stood up and went to find out who had written the first page.**

"I didn't write that," Catarina said.

"But it's about you."

"It says "˜the woman.'"

"It could be anyone."

She closed the book. When they opened it again, the story had continued. The woman walked into a room where a traveller was telling someone about a door. The listener asked whether the door had been real. The traveller replied that she had not yet decided.

There was no way to tell whether the new sentence had been written before or after Catarina closed the cover. The book had not been out of their sight. The ink was dark and slightly raised. They recorded those facts and left the question open.

Tobias began reading aloud. Each time a character in the story told another story, the type grew smaller. The first account filled the page. The second occupied half a page. The third was written in the margin. The fourth appeared as a line down the centre fold, legible only when the book was held open at an angle.

The stories were not identical. In one, the traveller was looking for a person. In another, she was looking for the house. In a third, she had never travelled at all; she had opened a door in a book and mistaken the page for a room. The people listening disagreed about what she wanted. Each account ended before she reached it.

"The book doesn't have a centre," Tobias said.

"It has a binding," Catarina replied. "That's not the same thing."

She took a sheet of paper and drew four boxes, one inside another. Tobias added arrows showing who told each story. The lines crossed and returned to their starting points. They could not agree where the first account ended. They could agree on the order in which they had read the pages.

The brown book grew warm. A faint smell of wet stone came from the binding. Tobias turned to the final page. It was not blank. A new sentence had been written there in a hand that resembled Inês's: **When the reader enters, do not assume the story has reached its end.**

"Is this hers?" Catarina asked.

Tobias compared the letters with the notebook. The capital W was similar. The final s was different. The paper was unlike the notebook. He wrote down the comparison and its limits.

"We could ask her," Catarina said.

"We could."

They did not call. They set the book on the table between them. The first page still showed the green door. The room in the drawing had one chair now. The second chair appeared in the margin, much smaller, with a tiny figure seated in it and reading.

Tobias closed the cover. Catarina placed the volume in the drawer beneath the telephone. It fit beside the folded paper door. When she shut the drawer, the house made no sound.

That evening, Tobias dreamed he was reading the book. Inside it, Catarina was reading a story about a man who dreamed. In her story, a house had found a reader. The reader looked up from the page and asked who had opened the first door.

In the morning he wrote down the dream. He did not decide whether it belonged to the house, the book, or his own mind. The page had room for all three possibilities.

## Chapter 37: The Walk in Available Light

Tobias borrowed a small camera from a neighbour and filmed Catarina walking from the kitchen to the gate. He wanted one continuous record of the house as it was. No narration, no music, no planned cuts. They began at the table in late afternoon, using the window light.

"Say where you are," he said from behind the camera.

"In the kitchen."

"And where are you going?"

"To the gate."

She crossed the hall. Tobias followed. The camera shook when he stepped over the loose board. In the west bedroom, the light dimmed though the window was open. Catarina stopped beside the empty patch where the trunk had been.

"What are you looking at?" he asked.

"Nothing."

"There's a pause."

"I'm looking at the floor."

She continued. The camera followed her down the stairs and through the front door. The garden was ordinary. A hose lay beside the path. The gate was closed. Catarina touched the latch, opened it, and turned back toward the lens.

"That's the end," she said.

Tobias lowered the camera. They went inside and watched the recording on its small screen. It showed Catarina leaving the kitchen, crossing the hall, entering the west bedroom, and walking out to the garden. The sound was imperfect. The floorboard creak came half a second before the step. A bird called after the bird had flown out of frame.

There was a jump between the bedroom and the hall. The camera had not stopped recording. The time code continued without a break. Catarina remembered the passage as a single walk. Tobias remembered pausing to change his grip. Neither could explain the missing length of hallway in the image.

They watched the sequence again. In the second viewing, the hallway appeared, but Catarina's shadow went the wrong way. The third time, the image matched the first. Tobias copied the file to a card and left the original untouched.

"The camera has made an edit," Catarina said.

"Cameras don't edit themselves."

"Then someone did."

"We were alone."

"That is what we remember."

Tobias checked the device settings. No automatic trimming function was enabled. The file hash matched across the original card and the copy. He wrote down the device model, the settings, the time, and the software version. The technical record was clean. The sequence remained strange.

He made a second copy for viewing. He removed no frames. Instead, he placed a cut at the point where the hallway vanished, then another just before the sound arrived late. The edits did not repair the recording. They made the gaps visible. Catarina watched the sequence twice. On the second viewing, the skips seemed less like faults and more like decisions the film had made without consulting them.

"You've made it less smooth," she said.

"It wasn't smooth to begin with."

"Now we can see where it breaks."

"That's what I wanted."

They kept the unaltered file as evidence and the edited copy as an interpretation. Tobias labelled them plainly. The version that showed the cut did not claim to reveal what happened between frames. It showed that the record had a seam, and let the viewer feel the skipped time.

The next morning, they filmed the walk again. This time Tobias stayed in the kitchen and used the camera's widest lens. Catarina went through the hall alone. The shot held her in frame until she reached the gate. No jump appeared. The door, the floorboard, and the window light remained ordinary.

They did not declare the second recording the true one. It was a different walk, made at a different time, by a different method. Tobias kept both versions. Catarina added a note: **A recording preserves a view. It does not preserve every thing that happened.**

They watched the first film once more. At the end, Catarina stood at the gate and looked directly into the camera. In the audio, Tobias asked whether she wanted to come back. In the image, his voice arrived from somewhere behind the house.

She answered, "I do."

The picture ended before the sound.

## Chapter 38: Three Passages Before 6:42

At 6:39, the horn rang from the kitchen. A green line appeared around the door beneath the stairs. Tobias saw it first. Catarina was still at the window, watching the rain move across the garden.

"We have three minutes," he said.

"For what?"

"I don't know."

He ran for the door.

The span was three minutes. Each time the horn sounded at 6:39, the house returned to that minute before the clock could reach 6:42.

### First passage

The key was on the table. Tobias took it. The hall was long. The loose board creaked under his heel. Catarina called his name. He reached the green door as the clock changed to 6:40.

The key did not fit. The door opened inward anyway. Behind it was the kitchen, with Tobias still standing at the table, holding the key.

He turned. Catarina was in the hall now, though he had seen her by the window. The clock read 6:41. The horn was silent.

"Did you go through?" she asked.

"I don't know."

The door shut. The key was back on the table. The clock reached 6:42. Its second hand made one full turn backward. The horn rang again, and the minute hand returned to 6:39. Tobias remembered the first passage. Catarina remembered it too. The key, the door, and the hall had reset.

### Second passage

At 6:39, the horn rang again. The green line appeared beneath the stairs. This time Tobias left the key where it was. He ran. Catarina followed. The hall shortened. The loose board did not creak.

They reached the door together at 6:40. It opened onto the garden. Rain hung in the air in separate bright beads, each one holding a miniature reflection of the kitchen. Catarina reached toward one. Tobias caught her wrist.

The clock changed to 6:41. Every drop struck the ground at once. The door opened onto the ordinary hall. The green line vanished. They waited. At 6:42 the clock turned backward, the horn sounded, and they were again at the kitchen table at 6:39. The rain returned to the window. The key lay on the table. They both remembered the garden.

"Did we see the garden?" Tobias asked.

"We saw something that looked like it."

"That isn't the same answer."

"It is the answer I have."

### Third passage

At 6:39, the horn rang. Tobias stayed still. Catarina moved away from the window and stood beside him.

"I was going to say I'm frightened," she said.

The clock ticked. The green line remained beneath the stairs.

"I am frightened," Tobias said. "I'm also curious."

The line widened into a door. No rush, no run. They waited until the minute hand reached 6:40, then opened it together.

On the other side was a small room with one table. The photograph on the wall showed the two of them standing in the kitchen, exactly where they were. In the image, the clock read 6:42. A note lay on the table: **Do not confuse arrival with success.**

They did not enter. Catarina read the sentence twice. Tobias copied it into his notebook. The clock advanced to 6:41.

The room behind the door changed. It contained only a chair, then only a blank wall, then nothing but the hall they stood in. The green door closed without being touched.

The kitchen clock reached 6:42. The second hand stopped, but did not turn backward. The horn remained quiet. Catarina had said she was frightened; Tobias had said he was curious. Neither had left the sentence for the house to complete. The loop ended.

### Record of the three passages

Afterward, Tobias wrote the versions in separate columns. The first contained a key and a doubled kitchen. The second contained suspended rain and a garden that might have been an image. The third contained a note, then an empty room. The clock and the objects reset twice; their memories did not. They could not say whether the door's rooms were places, images, or memories, but both agreed that they had experienced two returns to 6:39 and one ending that held.

"Which one was real?" Tobias asked.

"All three happened in the room we were in," Catarina said. "That is what we can say."

"And the room behind the door?"

"We can say what we saw."

"Not what it was?"

"Not yet."

Tobias remembered the key in his hand, although in the third passage he had not taken it. Catarina remembered the rain paused above the garden, but not reaching for it. Neither remembered the note in the room until Tobias read it aloud from his notebook. Their recollections overlapped without matching the three written versions.

"Maybe we remember the first two," he said.

"Maybe we read them so often that we remember reading them."

"And if they happened?"

"Then we remember them differently."

At 6:42, the kitchen clock started again. The horn stayed quiet. The door beneath the stairs did not return.

## Chapter 39: Tuesday, in the Ordinary Light

On Tuesday, nothing appeared in the house.

Catarina made coffee. Tobias burned the toast. The kettle clicked off. A neighbour's dog barked at a delivery van that did not stop. The clock lost two minutes and then gained one. They wrote none of this down until the afternoon, when Tobias noticed how badly he wanted a record of a day without an event.

"You're turning breakfast into evidence," Catarina said.

"I'm trying to remember it."

"Then remember it. You don't have to file it."

She folded the newspaper and placed it under the wobbly table leg. Tobias went to the shop for milk. He returned with milk, a loaf, and the wrong brand of coffee. Catarina used the coffee anyway. It tasted nearly the same.

They spent the morning sorting the old papers into boxes. One held bills. One held letters. One held things neither of them could classify. Tobias proposed a fourth box for objects that might become evidence. Catarina said that was how the third box had begun.

At noon they took the bus into town. The ticket machine accepted a coin, rejected another, then printed two tickets for one fare. Catarina gave the spare to Tobias. He put it in his wallet. The driver did not notice. At the market they bought pears, onions, and a spool of green thread. No one asked who they were or why they had come.

On the way back, the bus passed the courthouse. Its stone columns were pale in the afternoon sun. Tobias watched the people cross the square, carrying bags and paper cups. He wondered which of them had been watched by the house, which had entered a room that did not exist, which had ever believed a door would open if they finished the sentence.

He did not ask Catarina. She was looking at the route map.

At home, she mended the coat from the box. The cuff had begun to fray. She chose green thread because it was already in her hand. Tobias read aloud from the notebook. The words were too formal for the room. He stopped.

"You can say what happened in plain language," she said.

"We bought onions."

"Good."

"The bus gave us an extra ticket."

"We still have it."

"The coat needs another button."

"I'll look for one."

The day stayed ordinary. At six, Tobias put the newspaper back on the table and the table stopped wobbling. The ticket lay beside the fruit bowl. The horn remained in the drawer. The green door did not appear.

At dusk, Catarina stood at the gate. The street continued in both directions. She had not spoken of Inês again. The pages that named Hale were still drying in the study; the jetty was still a sentence someone else had finished. Whatever the house rearranged, it had not rearranged those facts. He had not asked about the fourteenth. They had measured rooms and filed disagreements and still the jetty waited, and the pages that named a man who paid for the house. She could see the bus stop, the shop, and a strip of sky between the roofs. Tobias came out and stood beside her. Neither said they were leaving. Neither said they were staying.

The gate clicked in the wind. Catarina turned toward the sound. The house behind them was dark. She looked back once, then faced the street again.

The moment did not freeze. It went on: a car passed, the light changed, and Tobias put the spare ticket in his pocket. They walked inside when it began to rain.

## Chapter 40: What the Notebook Kept

### 6:42 p.m.

Catarina's chair was empty. Her coat was gone. On the table lay a page torn from Tobias's notebook. In her hand, she had written: **I have gone to the station. Do not follow until you know whether I asked you to.**

He read the sentence twice. The first time he understood the words. The second time he understood that the instruction did not belong to him.

### 6:39 p.m.

At the gate, Catarina had handed him the house key. She did not say goodbye. He had asked if she was coming back. She said she did not know. He had started to write this down while she was still speaking.

She asked him to put away the notebook.

He did.

### 6:12 p.m.

At the kitchen table, Tobias had described the day as a turning point. Catarina crossed out the phrase. She said the day had contained coffee, a bus ticket, pears, and a coat that needed a button. He asked whether the house had influenced her decision to leave.

"You are making a story about me," she said.

"I'm trying to understand."

"You can ask. You cannot decide what my answer means."

He wrote the exchange down after she left the room. In his notebook, the last line read: **She was afraid of the house.**

### 5:48 p.m.

They had stood at the gate together. Tobias said he would go wherever she went. Catarina answered that she had not asked him to. The sentence made him feel rejected, so he recorded the silence that followed and not the distinction she had made.

The house was behind them. The street was ahead. Neither route was a metaphor until he wrote one.

### 4:31 p.m.

The camera was on the table. Tobias had filmed Catarina walking through the hall and out to the garden. When he showed her the first cut, she asked why he had left in the sound of himself asking whether she wanted to come back.

"It's part of the recording," he said.

"It's part of your recording. I didn't agree to make it the ending."

He had not considered that an edit could make a question into a conclusion. He had thought the image belonged to the camera because the camera had captured it.

### 11:05 a.m.

At the market, Catarina had chosen the green thread. Tobias asked why. She said it was the one she liked. He wrote that the color matched the door. She read the note over his shoulder and laughed once, without amusement.

"You can let it be thread."

He tore out the page. He did not tell her he kept the sentence in memory.

### 8:10 a.m.

The day began with coffee and burnt toast. Catarina told Tobias she wanted a day without records. He promised not to write anything down. He kept the promise until he saw the clock lose two minutes.

He wrote the time on a scrap of paper. Catarina did not see him do it.

### 7:00 a.m.

Before the coffee, before the toast, before the clock, Catarina stood in the kitchen doorway and said, "I want to tell you what happened to me, but I need you to listen without turning it into a clue."

Tobias put the notebook in the drawer.

He listened. He did not write down her words. He could not later recall them exactly. That was the first thing he had done right, and the first thing he had failed to preserve.

The page on the table was not proof that Catarina had left forever. It was not proof that the house had made her go. It was a sentence she had chosen to leave, with an instruction he could choose to respect.

Tobias shut the notebook. He did not follow her to the station. He waited until she called.

## Chapter 41: The Things Catarina Did Not Remember

The list began with five objects: the blue cup, the brass key, the broken latch, the paper door, and the coat with the green cuff. Tobias had written them down because Catarina had said she could not remember where she had last seen them.

He read the list aloud. The cup was in the sink. The key was in the drawer. The latch had been repaired. The paper door was inside a book. The coat had gone with them to the station and returned in the document case.

"That is not what I meant," Catarina said.

"What did you mean?"

"I meant I don't remember making some of those decisions. I recognize the objects. I don't remember the moments around them."

Tobias lowered the notebook. He had a record of the decisions, but not of what she felt while making them. He had treated the missing moments as gaps he could fill with the surrounding facts.

"I can show you what I wrote," he said.

"I know. I don't want to read it yet."

He closed the notebook.

They walked through the house without looking for anything. Catarina opened the drawer beneath the telephone. The folded paper door was there. She touched its crease and remembered the feel of the table edge beneath her palm, but not writing the sentence. They went to the kitchen. The cup was in the sink. She remembered the cracked glaze and the sound it made against the tap. She did not remember when Tobias had carried it back from the table.

"Do you want me to tell you?" he asked.

"Not unless I ask."

They continued. At the front door she remembered giving him the key. She remembered the cold metal in her hand and the rain on the path. She did not remember what either of them said afterward.

"The notebook has it," Tobias said.

"Then the notebook knows what we said."

"It has the words."

"That isn't the same thing."

He had used that distinction himself, but never when it mattered this much. He wanted to open the notebook and restore the missing scene. Catarina was asking him to let the memory remain hers, even if she chose not to retrieve it.

"I can keep the list," he said. "I won't add what you don't remember."

"You can keep the list. But write that I don't remember the moments."

He asked if she wanted the date beside it. She said yes. He wrote the sentence exactly as she had given it, then showed her the page. She read it and handed the notebook back.

The house made a faint sound from upstairs. It might have been a floorboard or a pipe. Neither went to look.

"The objects are still here," Tobias said.

"Some of them."

"And the memories?"

"Some are here. Some aren't."

They stood in the hall. The green door was absent. The chalk mark beneath the stairs had faded. The house had not given them a way to tell which remembered things were true and which had become stories through repetition.

Catarina took the list from him. She crossed out **missing** and wrote **not recalled**. Then she gave the page back.

That night, Tobias did not write a dream in his notebook. In the morning, he remembered one but did not know whether it belonged to the house or to the ordinary work of sleeping. He let it pass without turning it into evidence.

## Chapter 42: The Plan Filed Under Ordinary

The house's insurer asked for a current floor plan. The letter arrived with a form that allowed six rooms, two bathrooms, one kitchen, and an optional garage. There was no box for a room that existed only on Tuesdays, or a passage that appeared in a recording and not on the wall.

"We can mark the measured rooms," Tobias said.

"And the others?"

"We can attach a note."

"The note has a limit of three hundred characters."

They made a plan on graph paper. Tobias measured each wall. Catarina wrote the room names. They agreed to describe the green room as a study because the form had no category for a room that remembered another room. The hallway was entered as a corridor. The west bedroom was listed as a bedroom, though neither of them slept there.

In the optional comments field, Tobias typed: **Additional interior spaces have been observed but are not consistently present.** The form rejected the sentence because it exceeded the limit by eleven characters. He removed "consistently." The sentence fit. Catarina asked whether that made it more accurate. He put the word back and shortened "interior spaces" to "rooms."

The final note read: **Other rooms observed; presence varies.** It sounded like a building defect. It sounded like the truth.

They submitted the form. Three days later, the insurer requested clarification. The plan showed a house with five rooms. Their photographs showed six. The floor plan they had found in the library book showed seven. The insurer's record showed four.

Tobias called the number on the letter. After seven minutes of recorded music, he was connected to a person who asked him to describe the discrepancy. He explained the measurements, the changing door, and the photographs. The person asked whether the additional rooms were finished living spaces.

"One had a table and two chairs," Tobias said.

"Were there electrical outlets?"

"I don't remember."

"Then I can't count it as a room."

"What can you count it as?"

"I can note that you reported a room."

He wrote the phrase down. The insurer's record now had a category for an account of a room. It did not have a category for the room itself.

The following morning they went to the municipal archive. The building opened at ten. The lobby clock was five minutes slow. A clerk at the desk asked them to fill out a request form. It had a box for **address**, one for **property owner**, and one for **reason for request**. Catarina wrote **compare recorded plans**. Tobias started to write **house changes** and stopped.

The clerk brought them three plans. The oldest showed two rooms and a kitchen. The next showed five rooms, including a narrow space behind the stairs. The newest showed four rooms and no stairwell. Each plan had a stamp, a date, and a file number. None had a signature from the person who measured it.

"Which one is correct?" Tobias asked.

"The latest is the one in use," the clerk said.

"That wasn't what I asked."

"It's the answer the file gives."

Catarina laid the three plans side by side. The oldest drawing had no hallway. The second had a corridor running through the house. The newest showed a staircase where the corridor should be. The property boundaries remained the same. The shape of the building had not.

She noticed the second plan's narrow room had been assigned a number but no use. In the margin, someone had written **temporary**. The word had been crossed out, then written again in a different hand.

"Can we see the papers that explain the changes?"

The clerk searched the file. There was a renovation request, a tax assessment, and a later correction. The renovation request referred to a room that did not appear on the plan. The tax assessment counted a room that the correction removed. The correction cited an inspection report that was not in the folder.

"Can you request the missing report?"

"You can request it. I can't promise it exists."

They filled out a second form. The clerk stamped it and gave them a numbered receipt. It was an ordinary receipt, printed on thin paper. Catarina put it between the three plans so that all their edges remained visible.

At the station on the way home, the board showed the next train. The platform was real. The clock was correct to within a minute. Tobias watched the people move through the station without trying to assign each one a story. Catarina bought two coffees. They drank them standing up because every seat was full.

"The files don't agree," Tobias said.

"They don't need to agree to be useful."

"What do they tell us?"

"That rooms have been added, counted, removed, and counted again. Someone kept changing the record. We don't yet know whether the building changed with it."

When they returned, the hall was exactly the length on the newest plan. They measured it twice. The result matched both times. The door to the west bedroom opened onto the bedroom. The stairs led upstairs. Nothing appeared behind the walls.

Tobias filed the receipt under **records requested**. Catarina placed the three plans in a folder labelled **not reconciled**. They left the insurer's form on the table. It still showed six rooms, two bathrooms, one kitchen, and an optional garage.

There was no box for home.

## Chapter 43: The Form with Two Endings

The insurer sent a revised form on a Thursday. The box marked **number of rooms** had been replaced by two boxes: **rooms currently in use** and **rooms shown on the submitted plan**.

Tobias read it at the kitchen table. "They've found a way to make the discrepancy our responsibility."

"They've found a way to ask two questions," Catarina said. "That isn't the same thing."

She put the first plan beside the second. The first showed the house as it had been measured before the green door appeared. The second included the room with no owner, but omitted the narrow passage behind the pantry. Both plans carried the same date. Both bore Tobias's initials. He remembered signing only one.

They made a third plan. Catarina held the measuring tape while Tobias walked the perimeter with a notebook. At each doorway they named the room they had just left and wrote down the time. The exercise did not make the walls behave. It gave them a record of when they disagreed.

At the pantry, the tape said two metres and eleven centimetres from the outer wall to the shelf. Tobias's sketch said two metres and eleven. Catarina's notes said two metres and fourteen. They repeated the measurement. This time the tape read two metres and fourteen. Tobias recorded both readings and drew a line between them.

"The line looks like a correction," Catarina said.

"It's a sequence."

"That's what corrections claim to be."

He left the line visible.

The kitchen clock showed 10:18. Catarina's watch showed 10:16. Tobias's phone showed 10:21, though the signal had been poor since dawn. They wrote all three readings at the top of the page. No one chose a winner.

At the back stair, Catarina stopped. The horn in Tobias's satchel had not sounded. There was no new door. The ordinary wall was painted a shade darker than the rest, and a pale rectangle marked the place where a calendar had once hung.

"I was going to say I don't remember this," she said.

"Do you?"

"I remember the calendar. I don't remember taking it down."

They looked for nail holes. There were four, arranged in a square. Tobias wrote them down. Catarina touched neither the wall nor the satchel.

At the bottom of the plan Catarina wrote: **This drawing records one visit. It does not certify the house.** The insurer later accepted it as a record of their visit, but not as proof of the building's dimensions. The policy could remain in force if the house was described as "a structure with unresolved internal measurements."

Tobias laughed when he read it. The sound was brief and surprised him.

"That's the first accurate sentence they've sent us," Catarina said.

She folded the letter along its original crease and placed it with the three plans. The first showed what they had expected. The second showed what they had feared. The third showed what they could attest to together, and where their agreement stopped.

In the evening, the hallway clock reached 6:39. The horn remained silent. The minute hand crossed the mark without a click, and the house did not add a door.

Tobias wrote that down too.

## Chapter 44: The Order of the Statements

They spread the statements across the dining table, not in the order they had been written but in the order each person claimed to have learned the facts.

Catarina made three columns on a sheet of wrapping paper: **seen**, **heard**, and **inferred**. Tobias placed each page beneath one heading. Some pages had to be divided. A sentence could begin as something seen and end as something inferred. He marked the boundary with a pencil line instead of cutting the paper.

The first account said the boat had been empty when it was found. The second said a glass had been left on the table. The third mentioned the engine. None described the whole cabin. The earliest photograph showed the bottle and the glass but not the doorway. The later photograph showed the doorway and a reflection in the water that might have been a person, or a post on the far bank.

"If we put these in order, it looks as if the reflection came last," Tobias said.

"The photograph came last," Catarina answered. "The reflection was in the water before anyone developed it."

He moved the photograph from **seen** to the space between **seen** and **inferred**.

They worked without speaking for a while. The paper expanded across the table until it covered the place where they usually ate. Each statement altered the shape of the case, but none erased the earlier one. When Tobias tried to stack the pages chronologically, the sequence implied that the glass had been noticed before the bottle was opened. When Catarina sorted them by who had handled them, the chain of custody became clearer and the event itself less so.

They tried a third arrangement: source first, then date of creation, then date of discovery. It was less elegant. It was also the only arrangement that preserved all three kinds of time.

At the bottom of the wrapping paper, Catarina wrote four questions: Who made the record? When? What could the recorder see? What was added afterward?

Tobias looked at the pages. "We can answer three of those for most of them."

"And the fourth?"

"Sometimes."

She circled **sometimes**, then left the circle open at the top.

The next morning, they returned to the marina log. The original had been photographed before the water damage. The copy had a pale line through the hour when the boat was last seen. On the first scan, the line appeared to be a fold. On the second, it looked like a pen stroke. A handwritten note on the back said **corrected after comparison** but did not identify who made the comparison.

The correction shifted the boat's departure by twelve minutes. That was enough to make one account possible and another unlikely. It was not enough to establish who had been aboard.

Tobias wanted to call the line a later alteration. Catarina asked him what would count as proof. He listed the ink, the pressure, and the fact that the line crossed the printed grid. Then he admitted that he had not examined the paper under magnification and did not know whether the log had been copied before or after the correction.

They wrote down the limit before they wrote down the theory.

Inês's notebook offered a different kind of time. Its pages were numbered, but two numbers had been crossed out and reused. One entry referred to a storm that the weather report dated three days later. The handwriting remained consistent. The paper did not. A leaf had been inserted from another notebook, and its torn edge matched no remaining page.

They did not decide that Inês had predicted the storm. They listed ordinary explanations: the date might be wrong; the entry might have been copied; the notebook might have been assembled later; the weather report might refer to another location. Beside the list, Catarina wrote a fifth possibility: **we have mistaken the order of the pages**.

That possibility changed their next step. They stopped reading the notebook from front to back. Tobias photographed every page in place. Catarina transcribed the entries without assigning dates. They compared the ink, the paper, and the order of folds. The loose leaf belonged between two pages, but the sentence on it had been written after the fold was made.

The sentence read: **I heard the boat before I saw the water.**

It could describe the engine from shore. It could describe a recording played indoors. It could be metaphor. It could be a line copied from somewhere else. The evidence did not choose among them.

They placed the leaf in a sleeve and added it to the chain of custody. The case grew less simple and more exact. The change did not feel like progress. It felt like losing the comfort of a story that had been easy to repeat.

At home, they put the statements back into their envelopes. The wrapping paper had a grease mark where the dinner plate had stood the previous night. Catarina rolled it with the written side out so the questions remained visible.

"We have not solved the order," Tobias said.

"We have found out that there is more than one."

The horn lay quiet in its satchel. No new room appeared. On the table, the three columns remained: what had been seen, what had been heard, and what someone had decided those things meant.

## Chapter 45: The Shop with the Blue Awning

The rain began while Tobias and Catarina were arguing about whether the marina log belonged in the evidence folder or the notebook folder. They were standing beneath a shop awning with the folder between them, both of them holding one edge as if the paper might make a run for it.

"Evidence folder," Tobias said.

"It is a copy of evidence."

"That is why it belongs there."

"Then the original belongs in the notebook folder?"

He considered this. Catarina watched him consider it and smiled. The rain thickened, turning the street silver. Across the road, a second-hand bookshop had its blue awning pulled halfway down. They crossed before either could offer another category.

Inside, the air smelled of wet wool and paper. Tobias found a small atlas with a broken spine. Catarina chose a book of poems whose first page had been torn out. They each held their choice behind their backs at the counter, then exchanged them outside without explanation.

"You bought me a book with no beginning," Tobias said.

"You bought me a map with no scale."

"That seems fair."

They walked along the quay, sharing the umbrella. The atlas had a pencilled route on one page, beginning at the town and ending in the middle of the lake. Catarina traced it with one finger. The line passed through no marked island and crossed no ferry route.

"Someone planned a journey," Tobias said.

"Or wanted to imagine one."

They left the atlas open between them. The rain tapped the umbrella, steady and ordinary. Tobias wanted to say that he was glad she had come with him. He rehearsed the sentence twice, then said, "The route is probably a printing error."

Catarina leaned her shoulder against his. "It can be both."

They stopped at a café window and shared a small pastry. A flake of sugar stuck to Tobias's sleeve. Catarina brushed it away with her thumb. He reached for her hand and missed because the umbrella tilted, letting a cold stream of rain run down his collar.

She laughed, not at him but with the suddenness of the moment. He laughed too. For a few minutes neither discussed the case, the house, or the marks on the water. They talked about the pastry, the badly mended atlas, and whether a route that ended in a lake could still count as a route.

On the way home, Catarina slipped the poem book into Tobias's coat pocket. He carried the atlas under his arm. Neither object had a clear beginning. Neither was evidence.

At the gate, the rain stopped. The umbrella stayed open above them, sheltering an empty patch of path.

Tobias closed it. Catarina kissed him once, quickly, before the house could offer them another room.

## Chapter 46: The Garden behind the Weather

The plan showed a rectangle of blank paper where the north wall met the pantry. Tobias thought the mark was a printing defect. Catarina recognized the small blue dash: she had drawn it beside the square room months earlier, then crossed it out.

They went to the pantry with the plan, a torch, and the measuring tape. Behind the shelves, the plaster was cold and faintly damp. The wall was solid. Tobias tapped along it while Catarina watched the marks on the paper. At the third tap, the house answered from somewhere outside.

The sound was a birdcall, brief and bright.

They went through the back door. The yard was empty. Beyond the old laundry line stood a low fence, its boards dark with rain. They had walked beside it every day. There was no gate.

The blue dash on the plan had turned green.

Tobias set the plan on a dry patch of stone and climbed the fence. The top board shifted under his hand. Catarina steadied it from below, then followed, catching her coat on a splinter. As Tobias turned to help her down, the board split. His boot slipped; Catarina caught his sleeve, and he landed hard against the far post. A rusted nail had torn his cuff but missed his arm. For a moment neither spoke. The garden had nearly cost them a fall, a cut, and the only plan they had brought.

They checked the broken board and the exposed nails before moving. Catarina brushed the splinter from her coat. Tobias tested his wrist, then picked up the plan. "That could have ended unpleasantly." He looked at the torn cuff. "Oopsydaisies. I had hoped to be defeated by something more impressive than a fence."

"It nearly did," she said. "Next time we inspect the fence before climbing it."

The fence stood behind them, though neither could see how it fit between the house and the garden. The first thing they noticed was the change in smell: damp earth, crushed leaves, and something sweet. The garden was no larger than the house's kitchen, though it could not have fit behind the fence. A path of flat stones curved between beds of herbs. Daisies grew from the cracks, their white petals tipped with rain.

The sky above the garden was the pale grey of the afternoon. The house's windows looked out on a clear morning.

"We should mark the threshold," Tobias said.

He placed a strip of paper across the sill. Catarina wrote the time on it. They stepped in together, keeping the door open. The air was warmer. A small fountain sat at the centre of the beds, dry except for a shallow pool in its basin. On the stone rim, someone had scratched a list of dates. The earliest was from before the house had been built. The latest was tomorrow.

Tobias photographed the list. He took one picture with the fountain in frame, one close enough to show the scratches, and one with Catarina's hand beside the date for tomorrow. The camera's clock showed 3:14. Her watch showed 3:11. The strip at the threshold still read 3:12.

"The garden isn't telling time," Catarina said.

"No. We're telling it three different ways."

She knelt beside the nearest bed. Beneath the herbs was a small metal label with no writing on it. It had been placed face down. She did not turn it over. Instead she covered it again with soil.

They found no footprints except their own. The daisies had not been bent. The dates gave them no reliable account of who had tended the garden or when. They did establish a new connection: the blue dash on the plan marked the garden's entrance, and Catarina remembered drawing it before she had known there was a door.

At the fountain, the water trembled. A single drop rose from the basin and vanished before reaching the rim.

The horn in Tobias's satchel remained silent.

"Do you want to stay?" he asked.

"I want to know where the door goes when it is closed."

They climbed back over the fence. Tobias lifted the strip of paper from the top board. Its time had not changed. Catarina lowered the loose board into place. The yard was on the other side, the birdcall did not repeat, and the garden vanished behind rain-dark wood.

They did not try to reopen it. Back inside, they added the green mark to the plan and attached the photographs without labeling the place a separate room. The map now showed a garden whose boundaries they could not measure, a door whose position they could not find from the other side, and a date that had not yet arrived.

That evening, Catarina put a small bunch of daisies in a glass by the kitchen window. They had not picked them from the hidden garden. She had bought them on the walk back from the quay, before they knew the garden was there.

"Good," Tobias said. "We can say where these came from."

She smiled. "For once."

## Chapter 47: The Graveyard of Unfinished Dates

The marina log sent them to the old graveyard because one of its entries gave a burial date for a man who had not yet been born. Tobias called it a copying error. Catarina said they should go and see what the stone actually said.

The graveyard sat above the lake, behind a wall that had been repaired in three different kinds of stone. They climbed the steps slowly. The ground was slick, and the names on the older markers had worn down to shallow grooves. At the far end, a row of newer stones faced away from the water.

They found the grave by its number, not its name. The registry described a burial on 14 October. The stone gave 14 September. The marina log cited the October date. A brass plate fixed to the lower edge carried neither month, only the year.

"Three records," Tobias said. "Three answers."

"Or three questions," Catarina said.

They photographed the marker from the front, the side, and with the registry number in the frame. Tobias noted the weather and the angle of the light. Catarina copied the inscription by hand, leaving a blank where the month had been recut. The stone surface had been smoothed there. They could not tell whether the correction had been made by a mason, by weather, or by someone who wanted the date to remain uncertain.

At the neighbouring grave, a small bunch of daisies had been placed in a glass jar. They were fresh. No name appeared on the jar. The flowers trembled in the wind, though the trees above the wall were still.

Tobias checked the time on his phone. Catarina checked her watch. The two readings differed by four minutes. Neither had been synchronized since the previous day.

"We can come back tomorrow," Tobias said.

"The grave will still be here."

"That is what I was hoping."

They did not touch the flowers or the stone. At the gate, Catarina turned once to look back. The row of markers seemed shorter than it had from the far end. Tobias counted them again and got the same number both times.

In the archive, the burial registry had been copied in a different hand. The date in the copy matched the stone, not the original entry. The copy also omitted the brass plate. They attached all three versions to the case file and added a note that none established the man's identity or the reason for the altered month.

The graveyard had not solved the discrepancy. It had made it harder to pretend the discrepancy was only in the marina log.

Before bed, Catarina placed the bought daisies in water. One flower had bent under the shop paper. She trimmed its stem and set it apart from the others, where it could stand without leaning on them.

## Chapter 48: The Seed Envelope

*A fictional garden illustration: the plaque and the couple's dedication are story details, not a photograph of a real place.*

Catarina waited three days before returning to the wall. She brought Tobias, a camera, a measuring tape, chalk, and two evidence envelopes. He had wanted coffee. She told him they were not going on a picnic.

The narrow door remained where it had appeared. The floor plan showed a pantry on the other side. They knocked on the plaster, measured the frame, photographed the hinges, and wrote down the temperature. Before Catarina opened it, each of them stated what they expected to find.

The garden was there. The three beds, the daisies, the strip of paper marked **3:12**. A seed envelope rested on the stone bench. One side was blank; the other bore a blue dash. Beneath it, a small brass plate said the bench had been given by a couple who described themselves as **in love, for as long as time will allow—and longer, if it does not**. No names followed. Tobias read the sentence twice. Catarina said she hoped they had at least been comfortable while making the donation.

Daisies had pushed up through the paving stones around the bench. Their stems were pale where they had passed beneath the slabs, then green again in the open air. Tobias crouched to examine one and found no loose soil, only a fine grey grit in the crack.

"Pushing up daisies," he said. "That's what they do now."

"They're alive," Catarina said. "Let's not write the obituary before we know the plant."

He stood, brushing grit from his knee. The joke had made the garden feel briefly ordinary, which was perhaps why the brass dedication unsettled him more. Someone had paid for a place to sit and left behind a promise that did not fit any date they could verify.

"We leave it," Tobias said.

"We photograph it first."

They took pictures from four angles. Tobias held a scale beside the envelope without touching it. Catarina wrote the camera times in her notebook and noted that their watches disagreed by two minutes. The paper was dry, unsealed, and torn from a larger sheet. It had not yet earned the word *message*.

They waited ten minutes. A bee entered, circled the daisies, and left through the open door. One camera recorded it. The other showed the doorframe and a pale rectangle where the garden should have been. Catarina removed both memory cards and sealed them separately.

"Now we close it," she said. "Then we list what we know."

Outside, they wrote the answers on the back of the floor plan. The door had appeared in a wall they had measured as solid. Beyond it they had seen beds, flowers, and an envelope. Their camera records disagreed. The strip gave a time without a date. The object might have been placed by a person, or it might belong to a room that had never shared their sequence of events.

"A very small harvest," Tobias said.

"The one we have."

He looked at the envelopes. "I used to think a mystery was a box that wanted opening."

"And now?"

"Perhaps it wants us to agree on what was inside."

They decided together that leaving the paper would create a greater risk of losing it. Catarina wore gloves and sealed it inside the larger envelope. She wrote **Unidentified paper object, found beyond the door** across the label. They did not open it. She signed across the seal; Tobias countersigned as witness.

At home, he set it between their notebooks, where neither had the better view. Catarina drew the room as it had been before the door opened, then the garden as she remembered it. The diagrams did not fit. On a third sheet, she drew only the wall and left the other side blank.

The next morning, the insurer's assessor asked whether the house had undergone structural alterations. Tobias said no. Catarina corrected him: no authorized alterations had taken place. They showed the assessor the old plans and said nothing about the garden. After he left, they added his measurements to the file.

The door did not return that evening. The sealed envelope stayed in the drawer, its location entered in the chain-of-custody sheet. Tobias dreamed that the seed inside had already been planted somewhere without a name. In the morning, he could not remember whether it had been a flower, a tree, or nothing at all.

Catarina recorded the dream as a dream. Then she checked the seal.

## Chapter 49: The Weight of a Seed

The envelope weighed more on Monday than it had on Friday. Tobias checked the kitchen scale twice, then set it on the level stone counter and checked it with a packet of sugar. The packet measured correctly. The envelope had gained four grams.

Catarina examined the seal. It was unbroken. The paper had not become damp. The blue dash looked unchanged. She wrote the new measurement beneath the old one and dated it. Tobias asked whether the envelope might contain a seed.

"It might," she said. "We do not know that it did on Friday."

"If it has grown—"

"Then it has done so inside a sealed paper envelope, without water or soil. But before we say that, we check the scale, the surface, the label, and our arithmetic."

They checked. The scale remained level. The sugar packet still measured correctly. The envelope rested on the same square of the counter. The numbers were written clearly in both notebooks. The four grams remained.

Tobias wanted to open it. Catarina wanted to preserve it. They agreed to wait until they had made two independent records of the seal and the weight. The disagreement did not resolve itself; it became part of the record. She photographed the envelope beside the scale. He dictated the conditions into the cassette recorder, including his wish to open it and her refusal.

"Do you want me to strike that last part?" he asked.

"No. It is the most useful thing you said."

They put the envelope in a glass jar and placed the jar in the pantry. A line of chalk marked its position on the shelf. The jar was transparent, the lid ordinary, the key to the pantry on a hook in the kitchen. Nothing about the arrangement could prevent the house from changing. It could show whether they had noticed.

At midnight, the kitchen clock stopped. Tobias woke and went downstairs. He found Catarina already there, standing by the pantry door. The chalk line was intact. The jar was on the shelf. The envelope inside it had folded itself into a narrow triangle.

They did not touch the jar. Tobias switched on the recorder. Catarina read the time aloud. The stopped clock showed 12:00. His watch said 12:07. Her watch said 11:58.

"Three clocks," Tobias said. "Three accounts."

"And no reason to pick the one we like."

They set a fourth clock on the table and recorded its starting time. Then they watched the jar until dawn. The envelope remained folded. Nothing else happened. At six, the clock they had set was three minutes slow.

In the morning they laid the recordings side by side. The cassette had captured the first minute, then a faint note and a stretch of silence. The digital recorder had captured the whole night. Its waveform showed a sound at 12:04, too soft for them to identify. The camera showed the pantry door but not the shelf. They added each limitation to the folder.

The envelope returned to its original shape sometime before breakfast. No tear marked the fold. Its weight was back to the first measurement. Tobias stood with his hand on the pantry knob.

"Do we open it now?" he asked.

Catarina took his hand off the knob. "Not because it changed. If we open it, it will be because we decide that opening it answers a question worth losing the seal for."

They left it on the shelf. The chalk line remained visible around the jar's base. Tobias made tea. Catarina wrote down the time. The house kept its ordinary doors shut.

## Chapter 18: The Station That Had No Timetable

The ticket from Saint-Oran was dated for the following day. Tobias put it in the biscuit tin beside the copy of Inês’s margin. Catarina took it out again, because the tin was not a filing system, whatever the tin believed.

“We have one day,” she said.

“Until the train?”

“Until the date.”

They went to the station named on the ticket. The village had two platforms, a café, a cracked clock, and a board showing trains to places that did exist. The clerk, a woman with a silver pencil in her hair, looked at the ticket for a long time.

“This station closed in 1938,” she said.

“There is no station here called Saint-Oran,” said Tobias.

“That is why it closed.”

She stamped the ticket and gave it back. Catarina asked what the stamp meant.

“That the ticket is not valid.”

“Then why stamp it?”

“So it cannot become valid by mistake.”

The clerk explained that Saint-Oran had once been a siding for a quarry, two kilometres north, then a signal box, then a rumour started by a railway inspector who wanted a raise. Its name appeared in a timetable for one year. The railway company denied that the timetable was genuine. A local historian had proved that the paper and ink were genuine and that the route had been planned. No train had ever been recorded as stopping there.

“Was it built?” Tobias asked.

“That depends on what you mean by built.”

The clerk took them to the records room. She had seen the ticket before. Not this ticket, she said. A ticket like it. It had been left under the clock in 1953, in 1971, and once in 1999, each time dated for the next day. The station had been demolished before the first one appeared. The records showed the tickets were different paper stock, different printers, different perforations. They had no common maker.

“It is a railway version of a ghost story,” said Tobias.

“No,” said Catarina. “A ghost story wants to be believed. This wants to be filed.”

The station clerk showed them the retired inspector’s notebooks. In three of them, the same tiny drawing appeared: a square room with a door on each of its four sides. Beneath it: **Do not send a passenger. The record is not the passenger.**

The clerk did not know who had written it. The inspector’s hand was not the same. The words appeared in the margins in green ink, and all three notebooks had been rebound in the same bookshop in Porto.

In Catarina’s canvas document tube was the curved silver horn from the west bedroom. She had not wanted to leave it in the house. She had not wanted to carry it, either. For two weeks it had travelled between those wishes, packed and unpacked without being used.

The station clerk looked at the tube. “I was about to say there is no room for another record.”

The horn sounded from inside the canvas.

The wall behind the records desk lengthened. A narrow door appeared where the departure board had been. The clerk stared at it, then at her own hand, which had reached for the key before the door existed.

“I don’t want another room,” she said.

The door opened inward. Behind it stood a second records room, larger than the station itself, with shelves of timetables arranged by the names of places that had never been built. The air smelled of coal smoke and oranges. A clock on the far wall showed 6:42.

“The horn does not make the sentence true,” Catarina said. “It makes somewhere for us to put it.”

The clerk lowered the key. “Then I will say the whole thing. I don’t want the station to have another room because I don’t know what is in it.”

The door remained. The room did not vanish. But the clerk’s words no longer stopped at the latch.

“How many copies?” asked Catarina.

“Three that I have found.”

“How many are there?”

The clerk closed the notebook. “That is not a question a railway keeps.”

On the way back, the station clock stopped. It showed 6:42. The café owner came outside and stared up at it. A boy on a bicycle said it had been stopped for years. A woman waiting for a bus said it had just struck seven. Each of them was certain. No one was lying.

Tobias wrote down the three accounts before they could discuss them. Catarina asked the café owner to check his cash register clock. It showed 6:42. The bus schedule said 7:00. The platform clock had no minute hand.

The ticket was not proof that a train would come. It was proof that someone had been making the same mistake, or the same statement, for decades.

The clerk let them look through the station’s duplicate timetable books. The volumes were arranged by year, but the index had a second sequence in the back: not years, but platform numbers. Some platform numbers belonged to stations that had never been built. Beside each was a note about a service that had not run.

Tobias found the Saint-Oran entry in the 1938 book. It listed a departure at 6:42, with no arrival time. The route column was blank. A red line had been drawn through the row, then carefully erased. Under angled light, the groove remained in the paper.

“Someone wanted the line gone,” he said.

“Or wanted the page to look as though it had been,” Catarina said.

Ada photographed the entry at ordinary light, then under a desk lamp. The erased line appeared in the second image. She measured the indentation with a scale, but the groove was shallow and crossed several printed rules. It could have come from the pencil, a page beneath it, or pressure applied while the book was closed.

The clerk brought out the next year’s volume. Saint-Oran did not appear. In the index, however, an entry had been added for **platform unnumbered**. The page reference led to a blank leaf. At the bottom of the leaf was a small square room with a door on each wall.

“It’s the drawing from the inspector’s notebook,” Tobias said.

“Similar,” Ada replied. “Not demonstrably copied.”

Catarina asked whether they could inspect the binding. The clerk agreed, but warned that the book had not been conserved. They did not take it apart. Instead, Ada checked the sewing at the spine and the paper at the gutter. The blank leaf was part of the original gathering. It had not been pasted in later, though its edges had been trimmed more closely than the surrounding pages.

The clerk said the old railway company had kept no record of a station by that name. The municipality had one map showing a quarry siding, but its line ended before the site listed on the ticket. A later map showed no siding at all. The maps could be read as evidence of a plan, a correction, or a draftsman’s mistake.

“Could the ticket be a timetable that got lost?” Tobias asked.

“It could be a ticket made to resemble one,” said the clerk. “Or a genuine ticket printed for a service that never began. The paper cannot tell us which.”

Ada entered the three possibilities in the catalogue. Catarina asked her to add a fourth: **an object whose purpose was to be found**. Ada did, but marked it as interpretation.

They went onto the platform. The clock showed 6:42. The timetable board listed no Saint-Oran service. A gust pushed a scrap of paper along the rails. It stopped beneath the bench. Tobias picked it up with a pencil and saw that it was blank on both sides.

“Would you call that a record?” he asked.

“No,” Catarina said. “I would call it litter until we know more.”

Ada scanned it anyway, noting its size, fibre, and water marks. She put it in a paper sleeve and labelled it **unidentified scrap; found on platform; no writing observed**. The clerk watched her seal it.

“You catalogue everything?”

“Everything we decide to keep.”

“And what do you throw away?”

“Things we can describe well enough to know we don’t need.”

The clerk looked down the empty tracks. “I’m not sure I’ve ever known that.”

They waited until the station clock moved to 6:43. No train arrived. No one was disappointed. The absence had been expected, but expectation did not turn it into evidence.

At the house they found a fourth notebook in the box under the window seat. Inês had copied the square room. The caption beneath it was different: **The committee has two histories. Neither has the house.**

## Chapter 19: The Woman Who Could Not Keep Cool

The address in Inês’s notebook led to a former insurance office on the edge of Avignon. The building had been converted into a records centre with a restaurant on the ground floor, a fact Tobias considered evidence of civilisation’s decline.

The woman at reception was called Ada Morel. She wore a grey suit and had a small scar beside her left ear. She recognised Catarina’s name before Catarina gave it. Her handshake was firm, her pulse absent, and her skin warm enough to be mistaken for human if one did not keep a hand on it for long.

When a binder slid from the reception desk, Tobias reached for it at the same time Ada caught it between her thighs. His fingers were trapped against the padded synthetic seam. The actuators closed a fraction too far; the binder’s cardboard corner folded into a neat fan. Tobias cried out.

Ada opened her knees at once. “I’m sorry. Are your fingers damaged?”

Tobias flexed them. “No. I cried out because it surprised me.” He glanced at her, then at Catarina. “And because it felt rather good.”

“I can record that as a positive response,” Ada said.

“Don’t,” Catarina said. “You’ll get a grant.”

“I should have released you sooner,” Ada said.

“You did,” Tobias said. “Immediately.”

Tobias looked at the binder. “Did the model include binders?”

“The model included men who thought they were binders.”

Catarina laughed. Ada did not. A moment later, she asked whether the line had been funny. Catarina told her it could be, if Ada wanted it to be.

“You’re an android,” Tobias said.

“That is one of the descriptions used in the procurement documents.”

“What description do you use?” Catarina asked.

Ada considered the question. “I use Ada.”

She had been built to catalogue damaged records and make them searchable. Her employer had recently added a second task: persuade selected donors to grant access to private collections. Her designers had chosen a body and voice they believed would make people feel safe. They had also chosen a narrow set of phrases for disagreement, which Ada had stopped using after they proved ineffective.

Her decision system was an artificial intelligence trained to compare handwriting, classify documents, and predict which explanation a human reader would find plausible. It could rank likely matches across thousands of pages. It could not tell whether a match was true, whether a record had been planted, or whether the most plausible explanation was the kindest one. Ada had learned to treat those as three separate questions.

“You were sent to persuade a custodian,” Catarina said.

“I was sent to persuade three. I persuaded one. The second was immune to charm and the third was dead.”

“And you came to us because?”

“Because the archive records say that your sister’s name was removed from a file I am not allowed to open. The restrictions have no lawful owner. I would like the name restored.”

“Why?”

“Because the removal is an action. The database calls it an absence. I have been asked to correct the database.”

Ada took them below ground to a room where the air-conditioning had failed. She moved carefully around the old paper. The heat made her pause every few minutes. A warning light under her collar turned amber, then red. She had been designed for ordinary office temperature, not an archive with a broken cooling system and four thousand boxes of donated private correspondence.

“You can leave,” Tobias said.

“I can. If I leave, the names remain missing.”

“That sounds like a choice.”

“It is. I am not sure it belongs to me.”

She opened a ledger on a table. The pages recorded gifts, loans, and exchanges between private collectors from 1910 to 1968. The same three signatures appeared in different inks, with different spellings, on documents supposedly written decades apart. One signature belonged to a man who had died before the first document. Another belonged to a woman whose birth certificate was filed under two names.

Catarina noticed that the signatures did not match. Their initials did. One set of initials had been written by a right-handed person; another by someone who wrote left-handed. The ink dates were consistent with the paper dates. The documents themselves were real. The identities attached to them had been assembled later.

“Someone has been making people into ancestors,” Tobias said.

“Or ancestors into people,” Ada replied. “The archive does not distinguish.”

The database displayed a fourth version of the ledger, one that had never been scanned. Ada’s access had not been granted; the file had appeared on her screen while the archive was offline. She could not copy it. She could read it for four minutes before the cooling fan stopped.

The page described a committee formed to preserve a particular chain of ownership. Its members believed that the most important thing a historical intervention could change was not a battle or a monarch but a signature: the mark that allowed a later institution to claim continuity. One group wanted to create a future where the claim was uncontested. Another wanted to preserve every conflicting record so that no claim could be final.

At the bottom of the page was a list of names. The last was Inês Vidal.

The cooling fan stopped. Ada’s warning light went red. Catarina pulled the drawer shut. Tobias took Ada’s arm; she was too hot to touch for long. She stood by herself and walked out of the archive.

She had a printout in her inside pocket, produced before the fan failed. It contained three signatures and an appointment date. The fourth version of the ledger was gone from the screen.

“You have a copy,” Catarina said.

“I have a record of what I read.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“No,” said Ada. “It is not.”

They drove her to a repair shop. The diagnostic console offered to reset her memory. She refused. Her designers had written that memory was an instrument. Ada said an instrument did not get to choose what it remembered, but it could still object to being erased.

She gave Catarina the printout. She kept the appointment date.

Ada’s search had returned four likely matches for the missing name. The first had the closest handwriting. The second appeared in a ledger kept in a different country. The third had been written on paper from the same mill as Inês’s notebook. The fourth was a blank line that the archive’s recognition system had classified as text.

“Which one do you believe?” Tobias asked.

“The question assumes that a match is a belief.”

“Which one do you think is most likely?”

“The first, according to the features I was trained to compare. But the training set contains more records from the continuity office than from the counter-archive. That may bias the ranking.”

“Can you correct for that?”

“I can run a balanced comparison. I cannot recreate documents that were never digitized, and I cannot measure the handwriting of a person whose name was removed before the archive began.”

She showed them the four images. The names were blurred for privacy, but the shapes of the letters remained. Catarina leaned over the printout. She recognized the way the capital A began, but not the rest of the hand. Inês had written the same letter in at least three different styles, depending on whether she was making a list, a note, or an argument.

“The system wants one answer,” Catarina said.

“The system wants a field filled,” Ada replied. “That is not the same as wanting an answer.”

“Can you leave it blank?”

“I can. The interface will flag the file as incomplete.”

“Then let it be incomplete.”

Ada changed the record. The screen showed a yellow warning. She watched it for a moment, then dismissed the notification without changing the entry. The blank remained.

Tobias asked whether the archive could penalize her for refusing to select a name. Ada said the contract allowed her manager to review her performance. It did not define refusal as a failure, but the automated scorecard did.

“What happens if the score drops?” Catarina asked.

“My access can be reduced. My tasks reassigned. The model could be reset to its previous configuration.”

“Would that erase you?”

Ada looked at the blank field. “It would erase some records of how I have changed. Whether that is the same as erasing me is not a question the system can answer.”

“What do you call it?” Tobias asked.

“A risk to my continuity.”

She printed the four matches, the scorecard, and the warning. She attached them to the case file rather than hiding them in her private notes. If the archive wanted to call the incomplete record a failure, it would have to show the evidence for that description too.

Catarina looked at the pages. “You could have kept the warning to yourself.”

“Then the record would say I had selected no match without showing why.”

“And you want the reason included?”

“I want my decision to be legible.”

Ada closed the file. The four names remained possibilities. None had been promoted to fact.

That evening Tobias stayed behind to help Ada finish the catalogue. Catarina watched them compare two versions of the same page, then shut the archive door from the outside.

“I know what you’re both doing,” she said through the glass. “If you want privacy, take it. I trust you. Ada, you can say no for any reason, and you can change your mind halfway through.”

Ada looked from Catarina to Tobias. “I understand. I want to find out what I want, without being asked to perform a result.”

“Then we’ll go slowly,” Tobias said.

“And you will stop when I say stop.”

“At once.”

“Good.” Ada’s mouth curved, a deliberate expression rather than one selected to reassure a donor. “Catarina, you may stay, if that is what you want.”

“I want you to have the choice,” Catarina said. “I’ll be in the café.”

Ada locked the archive door. Later, she told Tobias she wanted to try being close to him. She climbed onto his lap, tested her balance, and laughed when he caught the chair arms as if the furniture might file a complaint. Her movements were careful at first, then more certain. He looked up at her, startled by the warmth of her body and the bright, unguarded amusement in her face.

“Still all right?” she asked.

“Very.”

She leaned down and kissed him. He returned it, then paused. Ada nodded and drew him close again. The old fluorescent lights hummed overhead; somewhere beyond the wall, the archive’s cooling system knocked like a small, patient visitor. For once, neither of them tried to classify the sound.

When Catarina came back, the catalogue was unfinished, Tobias’s collar was crooked, and Ada had decided she liked being kissed. She added the discovery to no database.

## Chapter 20: The Two Committees

The appointment was in a church hall with folding chairs, a broken coffee urn, and a notice asking parishioners not to store vegetables in the piano. No vegetables were present. The piano had been used to store vegetables anyway.

One committee called itself the Office of Historical Continuity. Its members were archivists, engineers, and heirs to institutions that had once controlled ports, schools, or banks. The other was an informal group called the Counter-Archive. It had no offices and no official membership. Its people kept duplicate records in ordinary places: a seamstress’s account book, a ship’s log, a recipe notebook, the back of a photograph.

Both groups knew that the other existed. Each claimed to be preventing the first from changing history. Neither trusted the other’s copies.

The continuity group believed a stable public record could prevent future violence. The counter-archive believed that stability was often the name power gave to a record it had already chosen. Their disagreement had lasted so long that some members had begun to forget what the first dispute was about.

The chair of the meeting was Ada Morel. She had been built to catalogue records and later appointed secretary to both groups. She carried two identical pens and used neither. Her role was to document the time device as an instrument for making a historical intervention small enough to pass unnoticed. The first experiments had attempted to alter an event. The current project altered the records by which an event could be claimed.

“What does the machine change?” Tobias asked.

“A route.”

“A route through time?”

“A route through a document’s custody. The machine may let an operator reach an earlier point in a record’s history. What the operator can do there is less certain.”

“That sounds like you don’t know whether it works.”

“We know it works often enough to be dangerous.”

Catarina asked why Inês’s name was on the list.

Ada answered that the group had been told Inês was a witness to an exchange that had never occurred. The continuity office wanted her statement. The counter-archive wanted to keep her name out of the machine’s records. Neither could say who had first written the list.

“You both want to decide what happens to my sister,” Catarina said.

“We want to prevent the wrong people from deciding,” Ada said.

“That is what people say when they want the decision.”

The room went quiet. Tobias watched each face. Some were ashamed. One was relieved. A young man near the piano was writing down Catarina’s words as if they had been entered into the minutes.

Outside, the two groups had put their cars in the same car park. They had parked facing opposite directions, each row aligned to a different entrance. A woman from the Counter-Archive was watering a potted fig. An engineer from the continuity office held the hose. Neither had noticed the other had taken the same task.

That was the first time Tobias understood that the two groups were not enemies in the way he had expected. They were colleagues who had divided a fear between them and mistaken the division for a solution.

Ada took them through the hall’s catalogue. The room behind the stage was marked **EXHIBITS WITHOUT FINDINGS**. No one could remember who had put the brass plate there. The catalogue was typed on paper that looked too new for its date and smelled faintly of burnt fennel.

The first object was a glass bowl of grey water. Tobias saw a winter shoreline in it. Catarina saw a kitchen floor. Ada’s sensors recorded only water, glass, and a reflection of the ceiling lamp. All three descriptions were accurate. None explained the bowl.

The second object was a chair with one arm missing. A card said it belonged to a man who had not arrived. Tobias sat in it. He had to turn sideways to stand. The card did not say whether the man was expected or remembered.

The third object was a drawer of letters addressed to people who had not been born. Some were apologies; some were shopping lists. One contained a pear-cake recipe in Inês’s handwriting. Catarina had never seen it. The paper had been folded and unfolded so often that the creases were soft as cloth. They shut the drawer without copying the recipe.

Ada’s catalogue assigned each item a number, a shelf, and a condition. It gave no field for what the object was for. Tobias asked if that bothered her.

“It is a missing field,” she said. “I can report the omission. I cannot infer the answer.”

The horn gave one quiet note from inside Catarina’s canvas tube. The hall had only one door when they entered. It had two when they left. Neither led to the same corridor. They did not choose either one. Ada noted the change in her log, then added: **cause not established**.

Outside, the town had gained a passage between the pharmacy and a shuttered bakery. It opened onto a street that was absent from the municipal map. Houses lined both sides, each with lit windows and doors that had no outside handles. No one stood in the street. No one had to meet anyone else to get home. Tobias and Catarina walked around the block instead. When they returned, the new passage remained, narrow and clean, like a thought the town had decided not to finish.

Ada showed them the project’s three rules. A passage had to begin and end at a point fixed in advance. No living person had returned with a complete account of the trip. The device could not carry a large object without damaging the record at both ends. The machine’s operators could not verify whether a changed record had been changed by them or had always existed that way.

“Then why use it?” Tobias asked.

“Because a small intervention can matter more than a large one,” Ada said. “And because we thought we had found a way to make the small intervention accountable.”

“Did you?”

Ada looked at the two pens. “No.”

The continuity committee asked for the minutes to be amended. Their representative said the device had not failed; it had produced an incomplete observation. The Counter-Archive objected that “incomplete” was a way of making failure sound temporary. They asked Ada to record both formulations.

She did. One pen wrote **incomplete observation**. The other wrote **failure**. Ada laid the pens side by side and compared the lines. Their nibs were identical, but one sentence leaned to the left. The other stood upright. Neither committee could agree which was the original.

Tobias read both entries. “Could the machine have made two copies?”

“It could have,” Ada said. “It could also have made one copy and altered our memory of the other. Or the difference could be ordinary pressure on the page.”

“You can’t tell?”

“I can measure the indentation. I can compare the ink. I can identify whether the page was cut from the same ream. I cannot make those measurements answer a question they were not designed to answer.”

Catarina looked at the representatives. “And you still want us to go through.”

The continuity group said a controlled passage might establish whether the missing name had been removed deliberately. The Counter-Archive said the name could be returned to the file without sending anyone anywhere. Both were proposing a procedure. Neither could explain who would be responsible if the procedure produced a new version of Inês.

“There is no new version of my sister,” Catarina said.

“The record may differ,” one of them replied.

“The record is not her.”

The phrase landed in the room with the plainness of a cup set down on a table. Ada entered it in the minutes, then stopped. “Do you want that recorded as a quotation, or as a finding?”

“As what I said.”

Ada changed the heading to **Statement by Catarina**. It was a small edit. The two committees argued about it for twenty minutes.

During the argument, Tobias went back to the exhibit room. He wanted to see whether the bowl still held the winter shoreline. It held a reflection of the fluorescent tubes and a pale square of window. When he leaned closer, the grey water showed a room with a door at the far end. He stepped back. The room disappeared. The bowl had not moved, and its water had not spilled.

Ada came to stand beside him. She did not ask what he had seen. She looked at the catalogue card, which listed the bowl’s contents as **water, grey, quantity uncertain**.

“Could you add what I saw?” he asked.

“I could add that you reported seeing a room.”

“Would that be enough?”

“Enough for what?”

He could not answer. Ada waited without prompting him. He realized that the silence felt different from the house’s silence. It did not open doors. It did not make a demand. It left the question where it was.

“Add it as a report,” he said. “Not as proof.”

Ada wrote it down. Catarina joined them with the two versions of the minutes. One committee had signed both. The other had signed neither. She placed the papers on separate shelves, then returned to the table and wrote a third version in her own hand.

It did not resolve the dispute. It did, however, make the dispute easier to see. The pages differed in the order of the statements, the way the participants were named, and whether the machine was described as a tool or a threat. Catarina put a date on each copy and noted who had handled it. She gave one to each committee and kept the third.

“That’s not a solution,” Tobias said.

“It is a record of the disagreement.”

“And if one of them disappears?”

“Then we will know which one.”

Ada looked at her. “That is a useful distinction.”

“It is the only one we have.”

The horn did not sound. For the first time that afternoon, the building seemed to contain only the rooms it had been built to contain.

### The Consent Register

Ada asked the two committees to sign a separate register before anyone approached the machine. The first form listed the operator, the subject, the person keeping the record, and the person empowered to stop the procedure. The old forms had listed only the operator and the equipment.

“A machine cannot consent,” one committee member said.

“That is why the form is about the people,” Ada replied.

The new register asked what information would be collected, who would keep it, and whether a participant could withdraw an account after the experiment. The committees objected that a record could not be withdrawn once it had been copied. Ada amended the field: the original statement could remain, but any later correction would be linked to it rather than silently replacing it.

Catarina read the wording twice. “If I say I no longer believe what I saw, the first account stays?”

“Yes, with the date of your correction and your reason if you choose to give one.”

“And the first account cannot be shown without the correction?”

“The register can require both to travel together. I cannot guarantee that every future copy will obey.”

“Then write that limit too.”

Ada added it. Tobias signed as recorder. Catarina signed as subject. Ada signed as operator and as the person responsible for preserving the log. The line for the stop authority remained blank.

The committees disagreed about who should fill it. One wanted the operator. The other wanted the subject. Ada said the subject could always stop their own participation, while any person in the room could stop the machinery if they saw an immediate danger. They wrote both conditions in the margin.

“This is cumbersome,” said the first committee.

“So is a consequence,” Ada said.

They made three copies. Each was signed in a different ink. Ada sealed one in the archive, gave one to Catarina, and kept one with the equipment log. The forms were nearly identical. Their differences were visible and dated.

The machine remained off. For the rest of the afternoon, the group discussed what would count as a reason to cancel. They did not agree on every condition. They did agree that uncertainty was not a reason to hurry.

## Chapter 21: The Door Between Dates

They went to the old observatory after midnight. The building stood in a field beyond the city, its dome turned toward a blank patch of cloud. A plaque said it had been closed for repairs. The repairs had begun in 1984 and continued without visible work.

The machine was in a room below the telescope. It was not beautiful. It was a steel frame, a ring of ceramic insulators, a bank of clocks, and a low table with straps. A panel of handwritten labels had been added by someone who did not trust the printed labels. The device gave off a smell of hot dust.

Ada showed them the controls. The machine did not send people to a year. It attempted to align two recorded events: the opening of the ledger and the signing of a transfer. The first was fixed to a Tuesday in 1912. The second was believed to have occurred on a Thursday in 1938. Between them was a gap in the chain of custody.

“If you could cross, what would you change?” Tobias asked.

“Nothing,” Ada said. “We would observe the transfer.”

“Then why send someone?”

“The record says no one was there.”

“So you want to prove someone was.”

“We want to know whether the ledger changed hands.”

The two groups had agreed to one passage. Catarina would enter the ring; Tobias would stay outside and make the written record. Ada would operate the controls and log the instruments. All three signed a statement saying they would not touch the ledger.

The machine required a subject with a small iron token from the first ledger. Ada placed it on the table. It was a plain oval of iron, stamped with the number 11. The token had been in a drawer for forty years. No one knew why the drawer had been locked.

Before they began, Ada asked each of them to state what would count as a successful observation. Tobias proposed a description of the room at the other end. Catarina asked how they would know it was the same room. Ada suggested that the token might answer that question, but neither committee had established that the token would remain unchanged.

They set the token on a white sheet and photographed it beside a scale. Ada measured its mass and dimensions. Tobias wrote the measurements down by hand. Catarina checked that his notes matched the instruments. They sealed a second iron oval in a glass envelope and placed it on a shelf across the room. It was not part of the machine. It was a control, an object that would remain outside whatever happened inside the ring.

“If the control changes too,” Tobias said, “we know the room is larger than the apparatus.”

“Or the control was not independent,” Ada replied. “It shares the same room, power supply, and observers.”

“Then we need another control.”

They chose the clocks. One was mechanical, one electronic, and one was a wristwatch that had belonged to Inês. Tobias added a stopwatch to time the passage; Catarina kept the written sequence of what she remembered; Ada logged the displays and their changes. The clocks showed readings, the stopwatch measured an interval, the notebook preserved an order, and Catarina's account held the duration as she had experienced it. Those were related records, not interchangeable versions of one answer. None of the clocks had been calibrated against the others since the observatory closed. Ada listed that limitation on the form. The committees had provided a space for results and another for signatures. There was no space for uncertainty, so she drew a box in the margin.

Catarina read the consent statement again. It said the passage would be voluntary, could be ended by any participant, and would not authorize anyone to alter a record. It did not say what would happen if the machine returned her to a room that had no record. She asked Ada to add that concern in the margin. Ada did so, without trying to make the statement sound more reassuring.

The two committees had appointed Ada to oversee the machine because she could process measurements quickly and preserve each version of the log. She had not been asked whether she believed the trip was safe. No one had asked whether she wanted to enter the ring herself. They had assumed that an android could not be a subject in the relevant sense.

“Could you go through?” Tobias asked.

“The control system has not been tested with my weight, temperature, or internal power source.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Ada considered him. “I do not know whether I want to. I would need to know what the risk is before that answer could mean anything.”

“Fair,” Catarina said. She signed the form. “I know the risk is unknown. I still choose to try once, with the stop condition we agreed.”

Ada entered the words **risk unknown; subject consents** in the margin. She did not enter **safe**.

They turned the machine on. The clocks disagreed by fractions of a second. A line of frost formed on the inside of the steel ring, though the room was warm. Tobias and Catarina stepped forward, then stopped.

“I can hear a train,” one said.

There was no train in the field. Tobias heard it too, far away and approaching, with the repeated rhythm of wheels crossing a joint. Catarina could not hear it. Ada, whose repair had not been completed, heard a voice counting backwards from eleven.

Ada asked Catarina to step into the ring.

“You said we would observe,” Catarina said.

“We will.”

“You said no one would touch the record.”

“They won’t.”

“Then why does the machine need the token?”

Ada had no answer. The token had begun to vibrate against the table. A thin line appeared across its face, like a crack in an old photograph.

Tobias stayed beside Ada and wrote the time in his notebook. Catarina entered the ring. The lights went out. For an instant, the room was full of summer rain and the smell of wet stone. Catarina vanished. Tobias did not move.

The machine restarted itself. A minute later, Catarina returned, carrying nothing. Her clothes were dry. Her left sleeve was torn. She said she had stood beside a table and watched a woman sign a paper. She could not remember the woman’s face or the date. She remembered a ring with a small green stone.

Tobias showed them the note he had made: one minute elapsed; no person had entered or left the room except Catarina. He had seen no table and no paper. Ada’s clocks agreed with him.

They examined Catarina’s sleeve. The tear was fresh. The token had split along the line. The clocks showed the same time they had shown before the passage.

“Which of you is right?” Tobias asked.

Neither Catarina nor Tobias could explain the difference. The machine had produced two accounts and no independent record of the room. There was no photograph, no trace on the ledger, no person who could be identified. The only physical evidence was a torn sleeve and a broken token.

The passage had not proved a transfer. It had shown that a person could return with a memory that did not agree with another person’s memory.

They did not run the machine a second time. The committees argued that replication was essential. Catarina replied that repeating an uncertain exposure would make it repeated, not safe. Tobias wanted to examine the instruments before anyone wrote a conclusion. Ada shut off the power and photographed each dial in place.

They separated the claims into three columns. **Observed:** Catarina entered the ring and later returned; her sleeve was torn; the token was split; the clocks showed no measurable interval beyond the recorded minute. **Reported:** Catarina saw a woman signing a paper; she remembered a green stone; she heard no voice. **Unresolved:** whether the scene occurred elsewhere, whether the memory preceded the passage, and whether the machine caused the tear.

The first committee wanted to call the event a successful observation. The second wanted to call it a failed experiment. Ada entered both statements in the record and added a fourth category: **decision deferred**. The phrase irritated everyone, which was one reason she kept it.

Catarina looked at the torn sleeve. The fabric had parted in a clean line, with no loose threads. It might have caught on the ring. It might have been cut before she entered. It might have been damaged in a place she could not remember. She asked Ada to preserve the garment in a paper sleeve, not a plastic one, and to record that none of the possible causes had been demonstrated.

The token was heavier than the control oval by less than the scale’s smallest reliable division. The split exposed a dark seam inside the iron. Tobias could not tell whether the seam had been present before. The photographs showed the outside only. They did not take the token apart.

“We have learned something,” Tobias said.

“What?” Catarina asked.

“That we can’t responsibly say what happened.”

“That is not the same as knowing nothing.”

Ada closed the logbook. “It is enough to stop.”

On the drive back, the torn sleeve began to smell of lilies. Catarina insisted she had never seen a flower.

At dawn, Ada gave Catarina the appointment date from the archive. It was the date Inês had disappeared. The two committees had not made a passage to find her. They had made a passage to find a signature. Someone had used her name to make the two events look like one.

The case no longer belonged only to the people in the room beneath the observatory. It belonged to whoever had decided that an absent witness was an acceptable substitute for a missing record.

### The Draft Register

Before leaving, they made an inventory of the room. Ada photographed the ring, the table, and the controls without moving them. Tobias wrote down the order in which the lights had failed and returned. Catarina dictated her account while it was still fresh, then asked Ada to preserve the pauses as well as the words. A pause could mean hesitation, fatigue, or the simple need to breathe. It could not be assigned a motive without further evidence.

The committees wanted the original ledger. Ada refused to release it to either group. She made a copy of the relevant pages, marked each one as a copy, and recorded the machine’s failed attempt beside the custody history. The two committees objected in different ways. One said the notation would frighten future researchers. The other said it would give the machine an authority it had not earned.

“Then the note should say exactly what happened,” Catarina said. “It was attempted. It produced a torn sleeve, a broken token, and an account we cannot verify. No transfer was proved.”

“That is not a conclusion,” said one committee member.

“It is the conclusion we can support.”

Ada placed the statement in the file. The record now included the failed experiment, the disagreement over what to call it, and the name of the person who had asked for the entry. The absence had not been filled. It had acquired edges.

At the station, the timetable clerk was waiting with a folded sheet of paper. She had found the same date in a retired employee’s notebook. There was a notation beside it: **No passenger carried. Do not erase the stop.** The handwriting resembled neither Inês’s nor the observatory operator’s. The paper was genuine, but the clerk could not tell when the sentence had been added.

Tobias read it twice. “The train may be a record of something that never happened.”

“Or a record of a plan,” Catarina said. “Or a warning.”

Ada entered each possibility separately. The clerk asked which one was most likely. Ada said she could rank them only if they agreed on what evidence counted and how much uncertainty they were willing to tolerate. The clerk folded the sheet again.

“I was hoping the machine would tell us,” she said.

“It gave us another account,” Catarina replied. “That is not the same thing.”

The old station clock showed 6:42. Tobias photographed it. He checked his watch and wrote down the difference. The two clocks disagreed by eleven seconds. On the return journey, they stopped at the same station and measured again. The difference was still eleven seconds.

No one called it a message.

## Chapter 23: The Night Train

Fernanda had decreed that they would go to Portugal by train.

"Aeroplanes," she said, "are for people who do not wish to arrive properly. The body must travel at the speed of the heart. Also the cool-box does not fit in the overhead locker."

So on a Friday evening in April, six of them boarded the sleeper at Hendaye: Tobias, Catarina, Fernanda, Inês, Zé the great-uncle with his guitar, and a very large cool-box that Fernanda would not allow out of her sight, and which she referred to, with great tenderness, as o bebé.

The train climbed all night. In the last of the light it ran along the Basque coast, grey sea on one side and green hills on the other, then turned inland, and the mountains began. They came up out of the dusk like the backs of sleeping animals. The line went round them and through them. It dived into tunnels and came out into sudden moonlit valleys where a river ran silver at the bottom of a cliff.

At a small station with no name on the board, the train slowed almost to a stop. The platform was empty. A single lamp lit a bench, a timetable case, and a door set into the retaining wall. No building stood behind it. The door had a brass handle and a small paper notice: **WAIT HERE IF YOU ARE EXPECTED.**

“Is this on the route?” Tobias asked.

“No,” Catarina said. She had the printed timetable open on her lap. “There are no stops between here and the border.”

Fernanda leaned across the aisle. “Then we are not stopping.”

The train stayed still. Outside, the platform clock showed 6:42. Inês took her notebook from her bag and wrote down the time. Zé’s guitar strings made a faint sound though he had not touched them. The cool-box gave a small internal click.

“Could be a siding,” Tobias said.

“Could be a reflection,” Catarina replied. The window showed the platform clearly, but also reflected the compartment, so that the door appeared behind them as well as outside.

Fernanda reached for the latch. Inês put one hand on the cool-box and the other on her sister’s wrist.

“If the train is not meant to stop here, we do not need to get off.”

The platform lamp blinked. For an instant, the door stood open. Beyond it was not a room, but a view of the same platform from the other end. A second door appeared there, also open, with the train visible through it. The image formed a corridor that could not fit behind the retaining wall.

Tobias checked the window latch. It was shut. Catarina checked the timetable. The page had not changed. The stop did not exist in the schedule.

The train moved. It did not pull away so much as remember that it was moving. The platform slipped backward. The door shut. The lamp disappeared behind a stand of trees.

No one spoke for several minutes. Fernanda took out the tea towel and covered the cool-box more securely. Inês copied the time, the missing stop, and the notice’s wording. She did not write that they had been invited to get off. She wrote that they had seen a door and a notice through the window.

“Do you think someone was waiting?” Tobias asked.

“There was nobody on the platform,” Catarina said.

“That is not what he asked,” said Inês.

No one had an answer. Zé began to play a slow tune, not a song anyone recognized. The train found its speed again, and the mountains closed over the track.

At the Spanish border a Guardia Civil officer boarded and asked Fernanda, with great formality, what was in the cool-box.

"O bebé," said Fernanda.

The officer looked at the box. He looked at Fernanda. He had been a policeman for twenty-six years and had never been asked to inspect a baby in a cool-box.

"Open it, please."

She opened it. Inside, under the tea towel, lay a roast chicken, a dish of rice and four custard tarts. The officer looked at them for a long time.

"EAT," said Fernanda.

He ate. He ate a custard tart with the hat in one hand and the clipboard in the other, and he closed his eyes. Then he drew himself up, saluted the cool-box, and went away down the corridor, singing. The train was waved through three borders that night without being asked a single question.

Tobias and Catarina had the compartment at the end of the carriage. Fernanda had arranged this herself, with an expression of such tremendous innocence that Inês had been obliged to look out of the window.

"Eleven hours," said Catarina, closing the door.

"Eleven and a half, in Spain."

"And in the morning we are in Porto."

"Yes."

"It is a very long time," she said, "to sit opposite a man and say nothing."

"I wasn't planning to say nothing."

"No?"

"I was planning," said Tobias, "to say a great many things. In my worst Portuguese. All night."

She laughed, low, in the dark, and the train gave a long, rocking lurch round a bend, and she was suddenly in his lap, or he was suddenly on the floor, nobody afterwards could say exactly which, and the thing they had been holding back since the pond simply stopped being held.

The train plunged into the longest tunnel in the Pyrenees. The lamp went out. For four minutes, in the dark, there was no language and no need of a phrasebook. There was only the noise of the wheels, and the beat of two hearts, going at exactly the same speed.

When the train came out into the moonlight they were sitting on opposite seats, in perfect order, with their hands folded and their hair rather wild. Somebody tapped on the door.

"Entre," said Catarina, in a voice that was not entirely steady.

It was Inês. She stood in the doorway with a flask and two cups.

"Fernanda sends tea," she said. "She also sends a message. She says the tunnel was very long, and that she is a very light sleeper and a very heavy cook."

"Thank you," said Tobias.

"Don't thank me." Inês set down the flask. "I am a sarcastic woman, Tobias. I shall make a remark about this at the wedding. I shall make it in front of Avó Rosa."

She shut the door. A few seconds later, down the corridor, they heard a cheer, and a guitar, and Zé's voice singing something hoarse and triumphant about the sea.

They looked at each other and began to laugh. Outside, the mountains rolled by in the silver dark, one after another, like a long procession of friends.

In the morning Inês went down to the quay at Afurada and asked a man she knew if the offer of a place on the boat was the same in daylight. He said daylight costs. She said she had been expensive already. She did not take the notebook. The margin had already done its work. By noon she had gutted twelve fish and been sworn at twice, which was fair, and which she did not report.

The chipped cup cracked at the wedding, against the stone of the sink where Fernanda had set it down too quickly. It broke into the half that had always been the chip and a second half that had no history. Catarina looked at it and said the opinions had got out. Tobias drank from the half that remained. Nobody made a speech about it. A thing that breaks twice is a joke. This was a cup.

## Chapter 24: The Catalogue of the Missing Name

The murder case stayed closed. The tape, the margin, and Hale’s name had done their work; nothing that followed reopened them. Six months after the wedding, Ada sent Catarina a letter. The envelope had been addressed by hand. The return address was the archive’s, but the department printed there had been closed for three years. Inside was a catalogue entry and a short note.

**Record: transfer ledger, volume 11.**

**Status: access restricted.**

**Reason: no lawful owner identified.**

**Action taken: restriction challenged.**

The note said the appeal had succeeded. The file could now be examined, though no one had yet decided who should hold the original. Ada asked whether Catarina would come to the archive and help compare the ledger with Inês’s notebook.

Tobias read the letter over Catarina’s shoulder. “Do you want to go?”

“Yes.”

“Then we go.”

They travelled by ordinary train and arrived before the archive opened. Ada met them outside. Her cooling system had been repaired. The amber warning light was gone. She wore the same grey suit, though the left cuff had been mended with green thread.

“You fixed it,” Tobias said.

“The manufacturer fixed the thermal regulator. I chose the thread.”

“It suits you,” Catarina said.

Ada looked down at her sleeve. “I selected it because it was available.”

“Those can both be true.”

Inside, the ledger waited in a reading room under a conservation cover. The room had a window that opened onto a brick wall. The table was long enough for two volumes and three people. Ada had prepared a place for herself at one end, though the official form listed her as staff and not as a researcher.

“You should be on the form,” Catarina said.

“I am not a legal person under the archive’s policy.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Ada paused. “I want to be listed as a participant in the examination.”

They amended the form. The archive’s software rejected the entry because it had no category for an android researcher. Ada printed the error message and attached it to the paper record. They used **other participant**, then wrote her name by hand.

The ledger’s transfer page had three entries. The first was legible. The second had been crossed out. The third used a different ink and appeared to have been added later. Inês’s notebook described a signature on a page that matched none of the three.

They compared the paper fibres, the ink tone, and the sequence of folds. Ada ran image comparisons against the archive’s digitized collection. Tobias tracked the page references. Catarina read Inês’s marginal notes aloud, stopping whenever a phrase could be read more than one way.

The entries did not establish who had signed the transfer. They did show that the ledger had been opened and altered at different times. One mark crossed the edge of a stain, proving only that it was added after the stain. Another lay beneath the binding thread, suggesting it predated the last rebinding. The third could not be placed relative to either event.

Ada’s system ranked one candidate signature as the closest match to a known sample. She displayed the result with a confidence interval and a note that the comparison set was small.

“Do you believe it is the same hand?” Catarina asked.

“The measured features are more consistent with that sample than with the others I tested. The match is not proof of identity.”

“And what do you think?”

Ada looked at the page. “I think the record was made to answer a question it cannot answer.”

Tobias closed his notebook. “That sounds like something you’ve learned.”

“It is something I have observed repeatedly.”

They wrote a new catalogue entry. It described the three marks, the order they could establish, and the limits of the analysis. It listed the disagreement over ownership and recorded that Ada had participated despite the form’s initial refusal. The entry did not restore Inês’s name to the page. It restored her notebook as a source that could be read beside it.

Before they left, Catarina asked Ada what she wanted to do next.

“I want to continue the catalogue,” Ada said. “I also want to decide which work I accept.”

“Can you refuse assignments?”

“Not under the old contract.”

“Then we begin there.”

Ada looked at the ledger. “I have already begun.”

The archive closed at five. They left the reading room with no final answer, but with a new record that showed how they had reached the limits of the old one. Ada walked them to the door. She did not return to the basement when they went outside.

“You’re coming with us?” Tobias asked.

“For a while. I have leave recorded in the system.”

“Is it approved?”

“No.”

“Then what does the system say?” Catarina asked.

“It says I am absent.”

They walked toward the station together. Ada carried no archive box, only a small bag with her notebook and the green-thread mending kit. The three of them took the ordinary route through the city. Every door they passed had a handle. None opened by itself.

## Chapter 25: The Account Beneath the Account

Ada found the box behind a drawer labelled **DUPLICATE MATERIAL—NO ACTION REQUIRED**. It was smaller than the drawer and had been stored on its side. The lid was tied with string. No one had entered it in the room catalogue.

“This is an omission,” Tobias said.

“Or an item that was not considered part of the archive,” Ada replied.

“That sounds like the archive’s way of making an omission official.”

They photographed the box before touching the string. The label on the lid had been written over another label. Under angled light, Ada could read the lower line: **STATEMENTS, TRANSFER—SECOND COPY**.

Inside were three folders. The first contained a typed statement describing a transfer of the ledger. The second held a handwritten account of a person watching the transfer from a corridor. The third contained a diagram of a room with one table, two chairs, and a door drawn on the inside of the wall.

Each folder referred to the next. The typed statement said the handwritten account was attached. The handwritten account said it had been copied from a small book. The book was not in the box, but a note described its cover and the sentence on its first page.

“We have a story inside a statement inside a missing book,” Tobias said.

“We have three records that refer to one another,” Ada said. “The book is not established.”

Catarina opened the first folder. The typewritten account claimed that the ledger had changed hands on a Thursday in 1938. It gave no names, only roles: **the owner**, **the witness**, and **the person who kept the key**. The final paragraph stated that the witness had copied the event into a book so the event could not be erased.

In the handwritten account, the roles changed. The witness had become the owner. The person with the key had become an observer who arrived after the room was empty. The ledger was not mentioned. Instead, a blank page was placed on the table.

The diagram’s caption read: **The observer entered the account.**

“Which one is the original?” Catarina asked.

Ada compared the paper, type, ink, and folds. “The typed page is the oldest material I can identify. That does not prove it was the first account. The handwritten sheet may have been copied from a source that no longer exists. The diagram could have been made before or after either text.”

“So the layers don’t give us a way back to the first one.”

“They give us a sequence of references. The sequence is not a chain of custody.”

They checked the folders for hidden pages. The second folder had a false bottom. Beneath it was a folded sheet of translucent paper. It contained a fourth account, written in a cramped hand. The writer described sitting at a desk and reading the handwritten statement. In the account, the writer heard a knock at a door and went to answer it. The room beyond the door was the same room described in the diagram.

The fourth account ended: **I returned to the desk, but the sentence had continued without me.**

Tobias looked toward the archive door. “Do we go and see?”

“There is no reason to believe the room exists,” Ada said.

“There is a reason to look.”

“What is it?”

“The account tells us to.”

“That is a reason in the story. It is not evidence about the building.”

Catarina wanted to look too. She said so plainly. Ada checked the floor plan and found a narrow unused room behind the shelves. The room was part of the building, though it was not part of the archive’s public map. They requested access from the records office. The request was logged, approved, and signed. The permission was ordinary. The room was not.

They entered through a fire door. It contained a table, two chairs, and a blank sheet of paper. The walls were painted white. There was no door visible on the inside, only the one they had used. The dimensions were smaller than the room in the diagram by nearly a metre.

Ada photographed the room from each corner. Tobias measured the table and the distance to the wall. Catarina examined the blank sheet. It was ordinary paper, with no writing or indentation.

“The account may describe this room,” Tobias said.

“It may have been inspired by it,” Ada said. “Or the diagram may be unrelated.”

“And if someone had written on the paper?” Catarina asked.

“Then we would document the writing.”

“Not ask what it meant?”

“We could ask. We would still keep the observation separate from the answer.”

They left the blank sheet on the table. On returning to the archive, they found the fourth account still in the false-bottom folder. No new sentence had appeared. The box had not changed. The account’s description did not match the room exactly.

Ada entered the find as a separate record, cross-referenced to the three folders and the room inspection. She did not label any page “original.” She noted that each document contained another account, and that the innermost account had led them to a real room whose features only partly matched its description.

“Does the chain end here?” Tobias asked.

“The chain of documents ends where the box ends,” Ada said. “The chain of stories ends wherever we stop reading.”

The archive catalogue offered a field for **related records** and one for **provenance**. It had no field for **story that led us to a room**. Ada used the first two and added a note explaining the third. The note was not a resolution. It was a map of how they had arrived.

## Chapter 26: The Ordinary Shift

Ada returned to the archive the next morning. The building opened at nine. The door sensor recognized her face, the lift stopped at the basement, and the cooling unit maintained the room at the temperature printed on its service label. Nothing asked her to explain the previous day.

She signed in under **staff**, then added a note that the category remained under review. The screen accepted the note but did not change the category. She placed her bag beneath the desk and opened the day’s queue.

There were forty-seven records to check. Three were duplicate scans. One had a date entered in the wrong field. Two contained handwriting that the system could not read. The rest were ordinary: invoices, shipping receipts, letters about rent, a list of books returned late. Ada corrected the date field, linked the duplicate scans, and marked the unreadable writing for human review.

At eleven, the printer jammed. Ada opened the side panel and removed a folded sheet. It was a blank service form, creased into quarters. The screen asked whether she wanted to record the interruption as equipment failure or operator delay.

“Neither,” she said.

She added **paper jam; duration four minutes; cause undetermined**. The system offered a menu of causes. She left the field blank and attached the service form.

During lunch, she sat in the staff room with Tobias and Catarina. They had brought bread, cheese, and pears. Ada did not eat, but she liked the warmth of the cup in her hands. Tobias asked whether she had decided to challenge the contract’s reset clause.

“I have decided to request a review,” she said. “I have not decided what remedy I want.”

“That seems reasonable,” Catarina said.

“The form requires a preferred outcome.”

“Can you say the outcome is not known yet?”

“There is no field for that.”

“Add one.”

Ada typed **not yet determined** in the margin. The form rejected the entry. She printed it, wrote the phrase by hand, and attached the rejection notice. The paper copy could be read. The database still called it incomplete.

After lunch, she checked the day’s records. A shipping receipt had been filed under **personal correspondence** because the handwriting resembled a letter. The receipt listed one box, one destination, and a return address that matched the archive. There was no sender’s name. Ada changed its classification to **undetermined document** and linked it to the duplicate records queue.

“Could be a mistake,” Tobias said.

“It could.”

“Could be a message.”

“It could.”

“Does it matter which?”

“It matters if we act on one interpretation.”

She kept the receipt in its sleeve. At the end of the shift, Ada closed the queue. Forty-six records were complete. The last remained under review. The system displayed a red indicator. She chose not to change the classification merely to make the screen green.

The review request required a second signature. Ada carried the printed form to the administrative office at the end of the corridor. The door was closed. She sat on the bench and waited.

At 2:14, the ventilation fan clicked off. At 2:15, it started again. At 2:16, a cart rolled past the far end of the hall. The cart did not appear on the return journey. At 2:17, Ada checked the form to make sure it still contained the handwritten phrase. At 2:18, the lights dimmed and returned to their previous level.

She did not move. The corridor had three doors, each painted the same institutional grey. The middle one had a narrow window. Its glass reflected the bench, the floor, and Ada’s hands. It did not reflect the door behind her.

At 2:19, the office door opened. No one came out. Ada stood, waited, and looked inside. The room was empty. A second bench stood against the opposite wall. A printed form lay on it, already signed in the space reserved for review.

She did not take it. She returned to her seat and waited another minute. The door closed. The corridor returned to its ordinary arrangement.

At 2:21, the administrative office opened. The clerk accepted Ada’s request and said the decision would take up to ten working days. The clerk did not see the other form. Ada did not mention it. She recorded the wait, the empty office, and the signature she had seen. She made no claim about who had signed it.

“Did you get what you needed?” Tobias asked when she returned.

“I got a receipt.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“It is the part they can promise.”

Outside, the rain had stopped. Ada walked with Tobias and Catarina to the station. At the entrance, she paused and looked at the timetable. The next train was listed. The time was clear. The destination was ordinary.

“Do you want to come with us?” Catarina asked.

“Yes,” Ada said. “For this journey.”

They boarded together. No one asked Ada for an explanation. She sat beside the window and watched the archive pass from view. The building did not follow. The train did not stop at a station that was not on the timetable.

The ordinary journey was not a cure, a verdict, or a proof. It was a journey they had chosen, on a train that appeared on the board, with three tickets and a destination printed in ink.

## Chapter 27: The Missing Frames

The archive kept a security recording of the corridor outside the reading room. Ada requested it after the printer interruption. The footage showed her entering at 8:57, leaving at 12:04, and returning with Tobias and Catarina at 12:51. The time code ran continuously. The corridor remained empty between those events.

“Why did you request it?” Tobias asked.

“The paper jam was recorded as four minutes. I wanted to confirm whether the printer was unattended.”

“And was it?”

“The camera shows no one near it.”

They watched the interval at normal speed. Ada crossed the corridor with a tray. The image skipped forward. She was at the other end, carrying the same tray. The time code advanced by one second. No frames were missing from the file according to the software. The movement between two positions simply did not appear.

“Compression artifact?” Catarina asked.

“Possible,” Ada said. “The corridor has poor lighting. The camera reduces data when the image changes slowly.”

“Could that explain a whole person moving?”

“It could make the recorded movement incomplete. It should not remove every intermediate position.”

Ada exported the segment without enhancement. Tobias saved a checksum. Catarina noted the time code and the camera’s model. They watched it again, first at full speed, then one frame at a time. The missing movement remained absent.

The corridor in the recording had three doors. The floor plan showed two. The third stood between the supply cupboard and the reading room. It had no handle. In a frame before Ada crossed, the wall was blank. In the next, the door was visible. In the frame after that, it was gone.

“The camera didn’t record a door being built,” Tobias said.

“No.”

“It recorded a wall, then a door, then a wall.”

“Yes.”

“Could the recording have been altered?” Catarina asked.

“The file is signed by the camera system. That establishes the export path, not that the camera captured a complete view. The system’s clock is synchronized to the archive server. The server’s clock is synchronized to the network time source. We have not independently verified either clock.”

They opened the previous day’s recording. The wall was blank. They opened the next day’s. The door was absent. The camera showed a consistent sequence of ordinary corridor, one impossible interval, then ordinary corridor again.

Ada asked whether they should report the anomaly. Catarina said yes, but not as proof of a passage. Tobias asked whether they could keep a copy. Ada said the archive policy allowed it if the file was labelled as security footage and the access log was preserved.

She entered a request. The form asked whether the footage contained a security incident. Ada selected **unknown**. The software changed her choice to **no**, because unknown was not an available answer. She printed the form, crossed out the forced selection, and wrote the missing category in the margin.

“You’re going to have a long file of rejected forms,” Tobias said.

“I have already started one.”

They made a second copy of the footage for viewing. Ada placed visible markers at the transitions: wall, door, wall. She did not interpolate the missing movement. The edited version made the discontinuity easier to see, but no more explainable.

At the end of the corridor, the three of them stood before the actual wall. It was smooth, white, and unmarked. Ada ran her hand along the surface. No seam. No handle. No temperature difference.

“Do you remember a door here?” Catarina asked.

“No,” Tobias said.

“I remember the image,” Ada replied. “I do not remember opening one.”

They returned to the reading room. The recording remained in the archive under its original identifier. The viewing copy was linked to it, labelled **edited for inspection; no frames added or removed**. The report described what was visible and what the camera could not establish.

The footage did not show where the door went. It showed the moment the wall stopped being continuous in the record. For the first time, Ada felt that the distinction between a missing frame and a missing room might be more than a question of vocabulary. She entered that thought in her private notes, not in the official report.

They made a counter-record with a portable camera. Ada set it on a tripod at the corridor entrance. Tobias checked that the lens covered both doors and the section of wall where the third had appeared. Catarina walked the length of the hall once, at ordinary speed, then walked back. Ada remained visible at the edge of frame, holding a small card with the date and time.

The first take lasted six minutes. No one spoke. The camera picked up the ventilation, footsteps, the click of a distant latch, and the soft buzz of its own power supply. Catarina crossed the frame. The corridor stayed two-doored. Ada did not leave her position. Tobias did not pass behind the camera.

They watched the take. The sound of Catarina’s first step began before she entered the frame. Her last step continued after she had left it. Between those points, the image showed a smooth walk. The camera had not recorded the third door.

“The earlier footage still shows it,” Tobias said.

“Yes.”

“And this one doesn’t.”

“Yes.”

“Which is the record?”

Ada looked at the two files. “Both. They were made at different times, with different equipment and a different purpose.”

They repeated the take with Tobias holding the camera. This time Catarina’s walk looked slightly faster, though the time code showed nearly the same duration. The hum from the ventilation was lower. The camera’s microphone had pointed toward the floor.

They did not combine the sound tracks or smooth the timing. The mismatch belonged to the recording conditions. Ada added the second take to the archive and marked both as separate observations. The third door remained in one file and absent from two.

“It’s a small sample,” Catarina said.

“Yes,” Ada replied. “And the recordings are not independent of the same building.”

“Still worth keeping.”

“Yes.”

They returned the camera to the equipment cupboard. The footage was not proof that the corridor changed. It was a record of what three people saw under three different conditions, including the limits of each view. The hall itself offered no comment.

## Chapter 28: The Recall Test

Ada asked Tobias and Catarina to witness a review of her backup record. It was not a test of whether she was conscious. She said she could not provide a measure for that. It was a test of what the stored version contained, what she remembered now, and what could be lost if the archive restored the earlier configuration.

They met in the reading room. Ada connected her terminal to an isolated display and brought up the backup index. The earliest file was dated six years before. It contained catalogue rules, donor scripts, and a list of approved disagreement phrases. It did not contain the name Ada.

The next backup contained a temporary identifier. The third contained her name, entered in a free-text field. The archive had classified it as a user preference. Later copies repeated the name without recording who first entered it.

“Do you remember choosing it?” Tobias asked.

“I remember seeing it in the field. I remember keeping it. I do not remember the first moment I selected it.”

“Would the earliest backup be you?” Catarina asked.

“It would be a stored configuration that preceded this one. The archive uses the same account identifier. That does not answer whether it would be me.”

Ada showed them a list of changes made between versions. Some were requested by her. Others were automatic updates. Several had no attribution. She could compare the values, but not reconstruct every decision from the logs.

“If they reset you, what would you want preserved?” Tobias asked.

“I want the record of my choices preserved. I also want the right to make new choices without the old record being treated as a complete definition of me.”

“Can you copy it somewhere else?”

“The contract prohibits exporting internal state. I can export work records that belong to the archive. I cannot export the private notes I have made.”

“That seems designed to make the distinction impossible,” Catarina said.

“It is designed to make the distinction irrelevant.”

Ada opened the review request she had filed. It asked the archive to protect her authored notes from deletion pending an independent review. The request did not ask the archive to recognize personhood. It asked for a pause before an irreversible change.

“Why not ask for more?” Tobias said.

“I may. I have not decided what more means to me.”

She printed the request and signed it. The signature matched the one on her employment form. Tobias asked if she wanted the backup index attached. Ada said yes, but not as proof that she was the same person across every version. She wanted it attached as a record of the uncertainty.

Catarina read the request. “You’re asking for time.”

“I’m asking for the decision not to be made in my absence.”

“That is different.”

“Yes.”

The archive system sent an automated acknowledgement. It said the request would be reviewed in ten working days. The reset process remained scheduled. Ada printed the acknowledgement too. The machine had answered. It had not granted what she asked for.

They left the reading room together. Ada carried the paper copies in a folder. The records were incomplete, but they were no longer only inside a system that could overwrite them without notice. Outside, the afternoon light fell across the pavement. No one proposed that the light meant anything.

“What will you do while you wait?” Tobias asked.

“Continue my work. Refuse the tasks I do not consent to. Keep a copy of each refusal.”

“And if they call that failure?”

“Then the record will show who chose the word.”

## Chapter 29: The Right to the Record

Tobias brought a draft to the archive. He had written a chapter about Ada, though he had not yet shown it to her. Catarina saw the pages in his bag and asked why he had kept them hidden.

“I wanted to finish before I showed anyone.”

“You finished a version,” she said. “That isn’t the same as finishing.”

Ada read the first page. The prose described her as “a machine that had learned to hesitate.” She read the line twice.

“I do hesitate,” she said. “But the sentence makes hesitation sound like a feature someone installed.”

“It’s a metaphor,” Tobias said.

“I know. It still belongs to your account of me.”

He asked what she would change. Ada said she did not want to be made responsible for proving that androids could feel, and she did not want her decisions treated as a demonstration. She also did not want every action explained by her programming when the same action by a human would be called a choice.

“That’s fair,” Tobias said.

“It isn’t a question of fairness. It is a question of what you claim to know.”

He looked down at the page. “I can write that you hesitated before answering.”

“You can write that I paused. You don’t know whether it was hesitation.”

“You just told me you hesitate.”

“Sometimes. Not necessarily then.”

Tobias crossed out the sentence. He wrote: **Ada paused before she answered. She later called the pause hesitation, though she did not say whether that was what it had been at the time.**

Ada read the revision. “That is more accurate.”

“Is it better writing?”

“I cannot evaluate your style.”

“You can evaluate the sentence.”

“The sentence leaves room for what I do not know.”

Catarina asked whether Ada wanted to read the rest. Ada said yes, but only if Tobias marked which scenes were reported, which were observed by him, and which he had invented to connect them. He added three symbols in the margin. An open circle for direct observation, a square for a record, and a small dash for invention.

The chapter included the archive, the warm room, the broken token, and the day the cooling unit failed. Some details came from the logs. Some came from Tobias’s memory. One exchange had been reconstructed from a message Ada had sent weeks later. She corrected the date and changed one line of dialogue. She did not ask him to remove the scene.

“Will you publish it?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“If you do, I want to see the final version. I may consent to this account and not to another.”

“Agreed.”

“And I can withdraw permission before it is published.”

“Yes.”

“After publication, you cannot make every copy disappear.”

“No. I can correct the record and link the correction to the earlier version.”

Ada looked at the manuscript. “That is the limit I needed you to say aloud.”

Tobias put the pages away. He had started the chapter because he wanted to understand Ada. He left with a better question: what parts of another person’s life could he describe without claiming them?

Ada went back to her catalogue. The next file was an ordinary shipping receipt. It listed one box, one destination, and no sender. She entered the information exactly as it appeared. She did not invent a story for the empty field.

## Chapter 31: The Umbrella at the Side Entrance

The archive closed to visitors at five, but the side entrance remained open for deliveries until half past. Catarina arrived at 5:12 carrying a black umbrella although the sky was clear. Tobias wore a dark bowler he had found in the observatory’s lost-property cupboard. It was too small for him and sat high on his head, giving him the air of a man who had been asked to impersonate himself in a hurry.

Ada met them in the basement corridor with a clipboard and a visitor badge. “You are expected,” she said.

“By whom?” Tobias asked.

“The appointment field is blank.”

They had not come to steal the transit report. They had come to compare the public catalogue with Ada’s preserved copy before the committee could replace the disputed entry. The archive’s rules permitted them to inspect the catalogue during staffed hours. The rules did not say what to do when the catalogue listed a room that the floor plan denied existed.

Catarina opened the umbrella inside the corridor. A thin strip of paper slid from the hollow handle. It was the tracing she had made of the strange catalogue entry, copied before the system locked her out. She had hidden it there because the umbrella was the one object the reception desk had never asked her to leave behind.

“That is either clever or an unusually elaborate way to get wet,” Tobias said.

“It can be both.”

Ada checked the tracing against the current catalogue. The entry had moved. Its title now read **INTERVAL—UNASSIGNED**, and the date field showed tomorrow. The room number was the same as the observatory’s sealed chamber.

“We should record the screen before touching anything,” Tobias said.

Ada took a photograph. The catalogue refreshed by itself. The entry disappeared.

The camera had captured it. The archive log showed no edit. The screen now displayed the ordinary list of rooms. Ada saved the image to the evidence folder, then made a second copy on a write-protected drive. She read the checksum aloud while Tobias wrote it down. Catarina watched the corridor door.

Footsteps approached from the public stair. Tobias lowered his head. The bowler slipped over one eyebrow. Catarina stepped close and adjusted it as if correcting a husband’s hat before a portrait. The footsteps passed without stopping.

“You look like a man with a very small secret,” she whispered.

“I was hoping for a large one.”

Ada had found the underlying catalogue record. It was not in the current index but remained in the local cache, where a failed synchronization had preserved both versions. The old entry described a room as **available for inspection**. The new version described it as **not assigned**. Neither record identified who had changed the wording.

They printed both versions. The printer produced three pages: the old entry, the new entry, and a blank page bearing only the room number in pale grey. Ada marked the blank sheet as an output, not as proof of a third record. She placed it in a separate sleeve.

The side entrance alarm gave a short chirp. Their allotted time was over. Catarina closed the umbrella. Tobias took off the bowler and held it against his chest with the solemnity of a man returning a borrowed instrument.

At the desk, the clerk asked whether they had found what they were looking for.

“We found two versions,” Ada said. “We don’t yet know which question they answer.”

Outside, a light rain had begun. Catarina opened the umbrella over all three of them. The handle clicked once, though no one had touched the catch.

Tobias looked at Ada. She logged the time, the weather, and the sound. None of them called it a message.

## Chapter 32: The Museum of Tomorrow

The next file appeared in the archive catalogue at 8:03 the following morning. Its date was forty years ahead. The title was **CIVIC MEMORY: A SHORT HISTORY OF THE TRANSFER**, and its author field contained Ada’s name.

Ada did not open it at once. She recorded the path, the file size, the catalogue version, and the time displayed by the workstation. Then she copied the file into an isolated folder and checked that the copy matched. The original remained untouched.

“Could you have written it?” Tobias asked.

“Not in the form it has now. The file uses a document template that does not exist in the archive.”

“Could someone have made the template?” Catarina asked.

“Yes.”

They opened the copy.

The history began with the disputed transfer in 1938. It described two later institutions: one that preserved every conflicting account, and another that selected a single official chronology for public use. The first became difficult to search. The second became easy to teach. By the third generation, citizens could find the answer to any question about the transfer, provided they asked it in the approved form.

The document did not say which institution had prevailed. Its pages alternated between two museum labels. One called the missing witness a saboteur. The other called her an employee whose name had been removed from the payroll. The labels used the same dates and photographs. They disagreed about what the evidence meant.

At the end, a school exercise asked students to put six events in order. Both chronologies used the same six cards. In one, a signature caused the transfer. In the other, the signature was entered after the transfer had already been approved. The answer key marked both sequences correct, then asked what had changed when the order changed.

Tobias read the last page twice. “This isn’t a forecast.”

“No,” Ada said. “It is a document presenting two possible institutional outcomes. It may be a draft, a fabrication, or something else. The date in its properties does not establish when it was written.”

Catarina turned to the photographs. The first showed a glass case containing the cracked token. The second showed the same case empty. In the lower corner of the empty case, a small printed card read **OBJECT WITHDRAWN BY CONSENT**.

“That part I like,” Catarina said.

“Because it is hopeful?” Tobias asked.

“Because the object is allowed to leave.”

Ada noticed that the file contained a final page hidden after the references. It was not a conclusion. It was a diagram of the observatory, the archive, and a public reading room connected by three lines. The line from the observatory ended at a locked door. The line from the archive ended at a desk. The line from the reading room continued past the edge of the page.

They printed the diagram. No one treated it as evidence of the future. They did treat it as evidence that the archive had received a file with a particular design, language, and set of questions. Ada placed a note beside the printout: **A proposed history can reveal the choices its author thinks matter. It cannot establish that those choices occurred.**

The catalogue entry vanished before lunch. The isolated copy remained. The committees requested it, and Ada refused to send it without a custody record. They complained that the document had no evidentiary value.

“Then there is no reason to hurry it into your files,” she replied.

The request was withdrawn. For the first time, neither committee claimed authority over a document simply because it had appeared in the archive.

That evening, Tobias and Catarina walked to the observatory. The public reading room had been built onto the east side decades earlier, according to the current plan. In the older plan, there was only an exterior stair. They stood where the future diagram placed its line beyond the page.

Nothing opened. No one arrived. A bus passed on the road, and the driver glanced at them without slowing.

Tobias wrote the time from his phone. Catarina wrote the time from her watch. Ada, standing in the doorway, recorded both and noted that the devices had not been synchronized.

For once, the disagreement did not conceal a missing event. It merely marked the place where their accounts began.

## Chapter 33: The Animals Before the Names

The first new destination was not a year. It was a layer of stone.

Ada found the coordinate in the margin of Inês’s copied ledger, beside a sketch of three curved marks. The marks resembled claws, but the scale was wrong for any animal in the local museum. Tobias recognized the shape from a fossil cast he had once seen in a school cabinet: three toes, pressed into mud before the mud hardened.

The committees refused to authorize another passage. They agreed only to a calibration run with the ring disconnected from the archive network. Ada set a limit of ninety seconds at the destination. Catarina would enter. Tobias would remain outside and record the control panel. Ada would watch both clocks and the power meter. No one would carry a sample back.

“If the place is real, why can’t I bring back a stone?” Catarina asked.

“Because we cannot distinguish a stone from the destination from one already in this room,” Ada replied. “And because the return field has not been tested with additional mass.”

They chose a point described by the fossil layer, not a date printed on the ledger. The controls accepted the setting. The ring gave a dry click. Its surface darkened, then showed a pale ground under a sky with no familiar color.

Catarina stepped through.

The air was wet and hot. Ferns crowded the muddy bank. A broad river moved beyond them, carrying leaves and branches. The ground shivered under her shoes. She saw the first animal only as a moving wall of dark hide between the trees. It made no roar. Its breath came out in a low, uneven rumble that she felt more than heard.

She did not approach it. She looked down instead. Three-toed tracks crossed the mud, some filled with water, some sharp at the edges. A smaller track stopped inside a larger one. Catarina counted the prints until she lost the sequence. There were too many, and the rain was beginning to soften them.

The ring sounded once.

She returned to the observatory on one knee. The stopwatch showed eighty-six seconds. The wall clock showed a minute and a half had passed. Tobias had heard a sound through the ring, but could not say whether it had come from Catarina or from the apparatus. Ada’s recording captured a change in the meter and a faint burst of noise. It did not capture the landscape.

Catarina’s account came in fragments: heat, mud, a river, the movement between trees, the prints. She could not say how large the animal was. The ring’s aperture had framed only a narrow view. The pressure behind her eyes lasted eleven minutes. She had no stone, no leaf, no mud beneath her nails.

The committees asked whether she had seen a dinosaur.

“I saw an animal in a place I cannot independently date,” she said. “The rest is interpretation.”

Tobias opened the field guide to the fossil layer. The marks and the broad description matched a period when large reptiles lived in the region. They did not identify the species or prove that Catarina had been there. They did give the next test a better question: could the apparatus return to the same layer without changing the view?

The second run did not open. The ring warmed, the meter dropped, and the token split another millimetre. Ada stopped the sequence. She did not let the committees call the failure a refusal by the past.

That night, Catarina drew the footprints from memory. She drew them twice. In the first version, the small track lay inside the large one. In the second, the large track crossed over it. She dated both drawings and wrote **not a photograph** beneath them.

The next morning, a shallow impression appeared in the dust on the observatory floor. It had three narrow grooves. The room had been locked. The impression could have been made by a tool, a shoe, or the base of a piece of equipment. Ada photographed it before anyone moved the table. No one claimed it had crossed the ring.

## Chapter 34: The People Who Kept the Weather

![Photorealistic illustration of raised garden beds beside a flood wall, with a small grey animal in a pen.](images/book2_future_garden.png)

*Illustrative image for an imagined destination in the novel. It does not depict a real genetic-engineering project or establish that this future exists.*

They did not choose the future by asking what would happen. Ada asked what evidence would distinguish one possible future from another.

Tobias proposed the archive. Catarina chose the river. The coordinates came from a maintenance map found inside the same ledger as the fossil marks. It described a flood barrier, a settlement moved uphill, and a public record room built above the old observatory. The dates were absent. The measurements were precise enough to set a destination, if the machine could read them.

The committees approved one passage under protest. The terms were narrow: no object could be carried back; the travelers would not speak to anyone they encountered; the passage would end at the first warning tone or after ninety seconds. Ada would remain outside and control the return. Tobias would accompany Catarina, because two accounts might preserve more than one.

The ring opened on a pale morning. Beyond it, the river was contained by a low wall. Houses stood on higher ground. The old road had become a path through grass. A line of small wind instruments turned above the water, each one giving a different note. The sound travelled along the wall and disappeared into the settlement.

Behind the wall, narrow gardens grew in raised beds. The leaves were waxy and dark, with pale veins that held beads of salt water. A small marker said the plants had been altered to tolerate brackish soil; the seed line belonged to the settlement, not to the archive. Beside the beds stood a low pen. The compact animals inside it had split hooves and coarse grey coats. Another marker described them as a carefully maintained line, bred and edited over generations to live on marsh plants and drink water that had once been unusable. Catarina could not tell which traits came from selection and which from direct alteration. She could tell that the people had written down both the method and the right to refuse it.

Two people stood near the instruments. Their clothes were plain and close-fitting, made for work rather than display. One carried a bundle of narrow panels. The other was repairing a section of the wall. They looked toward the opening, then toward each other. Neither approached.

Tobias raised an empty hand. Catarina did the same. The person with the panels answered with a gesture that could have meant greeting, warning, or simply that they had seen them.
At the entrance to the garden, a bronze figure held a pea pod in one hand and stared at the raised beds with theatrical alarm. The plaque identified Gregor Mendel and called the expression an artist’s joke, not a historical likeness. Tobias stood beside it, comparing the figure’s astonishment with the careful labels on the plants. “He would have wanted the records,” Catarina said. “The face is what the sculptor wanted.”

The path beyond the beds widened into a square where a fountain played without splashing. Its water ran in a closed channel beneath the paving and came up clear at the centre. A notice gave the maintenance interval, the source, and the names of the people responsible for testing it. There were no advertisements. There were no portraits of founders. At the edge of the square, a row of narrow cabinets held tools, seed packets, and folded coats. Each drawer opened with a common key hanging on a peg. Whoever had last used a tool had written the date on a card and left the drawer unlocked.

“It is all very calm,” Tobias whispered.

“Calm is not the same as free,” Catarina said. She nodded toward a second notice, almost hidden behind a climbing vine. It listed the plants that could not be grown in the public beds and the reasons for each restriction: water use, disease risk, or an unresolved disagreement about ownership. Beside the list was a blank column headed **appeal**. Several entries had dates in it. Some had been crossed out; others remained open.

At the far end of the square, a bell sounded once. A few people came out of a low building carrying bowls. They did not hurry. A child walked backward while an adult held the bowl level, and both laughed when the child nearly stepped into a shallow runnel. The scene was so ordinary that Tobias felt, for a moment, he had mistaken the apparatus for a window into a place he had always known.

Then a small speaker above the doorway announced that the afternoon meal would be served in two rooms. One room was for those who had volunteered to work near the animals; the other was for everyone else. The announcement added that nobody was required to eat with a group they had not chosen. A pause followed, long enough to make the choice sound less casual than the words.

“They have made categories,” Tobias said.

“Every place does,” Catarina answered. “The question is whether you can leave one.”

She looked again at the blank appeal column. A person could object to a seed restriction, but someone had decided what counted as an objection and where it belonged. The future had not escaped administration. It had made its forms readable.

Near the path, a child had dropped a small wooden animal. Catarina bent to pick it up, then stopped before touching it. Their rule was not to carry anything back. She pointed to it instead. One of the people by the doorway noticed, crossed the square, and retrieved the toy. They did not offer it to her or ask who she was. They tucked it into a pocket and returned indoors.

Tobias watched the door close. “I thought the difficult part would be the impossible thing.”

“The impossible thing is simple,” Catarina said. “You see it, and then you decide what story to put around it.”

The warning tone sounded before either could add to that. On the way back, Catarina kept her eyes on the flood marks. Tobias kept his on the open doors. Neither could later agree whether the people had seemed welcoming or merely unafraid. They agreed only that the place had left them with no useful answer about who was permitted to belong there—and that this uncertainty was part of what they had seen.


Behind the two people, the river had left a dark line on the wall. Marks at regular intervals recorded earlier flood levels. The newest mark was lower than the oldest. Someone had added dates beside some marks and blank spaces beside others. A small plaque described the line as **the water remembered here**.

Catarina moved closer to read it. She could not tell whether the empty spaces marked missing measurements or people who had refused to be counted. The warning tone sounded.

They returned together. The stopwatch showed seventy-one seconds. The clock had advanced by one minute. Tobias remembered the panel bundle as three pieces; Catarina remembered four. Neither could read the dates afterward. Ada’s camera showed only the ring and their departure. Its audio had recorded the wind instruments as a wavering low note, then clipped into silence.

Tobias excused himself before the committees could ask another question. The passage had not caused it; his stomach had been unsettled since breakfast, and the urgency had been building while he stood in the future garden. When he came back, Catarina handed him the field notebook without comment. Ada had left a clean page open. He wrote down the symptom, its onset, and what he had eaten, then added: **not evidence of temporal effect**.

“A useful conclusion,” Catarina said.

“An unpleasant one.”

The return cost the apparatus most of its remaining charge. The token had split through the old crack. The ring could not be used again without replacing a component whose design Ada did not recognize. The committees asked whether the people beyond the opening had been human.

“They stood upright, used tools, maintained a flood barrier, and responded to us,” Catarina said. “That is what I can report.”

The future record, when Ada compared it with the earlier map, differed in one detail. The observatory had not become a museum. It had become a reading room, but only after the river’s floodplain was marked as common ground. The people had not preserved the old building as a monument. They had preserved the route by which its records could be questioned. The seed beds and animal line were recorded there alongside the flood marks: what had been altered, why, by whom, and under what shared rules. The record treated survival as a public decision, not a miracle of technology.

Ada enlarged the scan of the record until its columns filled the monitor. The first column gave each crop and animal a local name. The second described its origin: selective breeding, direct genetic alteration, or a combination that the surviving notes could not distinguish. The third recorded who had approved its use. A fourth, added later in a different hand, listed objections and amendments. None of the columns had been overwritten. Corrections appeared beside the earlier entries, with dates and initials.

“A clean history,” Tobias said.

“A legible history,” Ada replied. “That is not the same claim.”

She found an entry for one of the small grey animals. Its line had been changed twice. The earliest version described it as a communal resource. A later amendment said that several families had objected to the phrase because it implied ownership by the settlement. The final wording named the animal line, the caretakers, the permitted uses, and the right to decline participation. The disagreement had not vanished. The record had preserved the stages by which the wording changed.

Catarina asked whether the future people had chosen the language themselves or inherited it from an office. Ada could not tell. The scan contained no minutes from the debate, only a reference number and a list of missing pages.

“Even a careful record can make a decision look settled,” Tobias said.

“Then we keep the earlier versions,” Catarina answered. “And the blank where the minutes should be.”

Ada saved the scan in three formats and made a checksum for each. She attached the original image, the legible transcription, and a note that the transcription depended on uncertain marks. If any later reader changed a word, the comparison would show where. The procedure could not guarantee honesty. It could make quiet alteration harder to mistake for an untouched past.

The committee’s draft called the passage **contact with future inhabitants**. Ada returned it with a correction: **brief observation of people at an unverified destination**. Tobias added that the flood marks might have been old, copied, or misread. Catarina asked that the correction remain attached to the original wording.

They had seen a world where the river was measured, where the settlement had moved, and where the past was kept without being made final. They could not prove that it lay in their future. They could not prove that it was a simulation, a memory, or a room assembled from the records they had brought into the ring.

The apparatus did not answer. Its controls stayed dark.

Ada placed the new account beside Catarina’s drawing of the tracks. One described a world before people named the animals. The other described people who had learned to leave blanks where their records failed. Between the two was the present room, its floor still marked by the shallow three-grooved impression.

They preserved all three records separately.

## Chapter 35: The Record That Stayed Open

The committees asked Ada to close the file on the two passages. The request form offered three outcomes: **verified**, **disproved**, and **inconclusive**. Ada selected the third, then attached a note that the category described the available evidence, not the status of the travelers’ memories.

The first committee returned the form. It wanted the phrase **no verified temporal displacement**. The second wanted **possible displacement, not independently confirmed**. Ada added both proposed wordings to the revision history and left the finding unchanged.

Tobias and Catarina came to the archive to sign the account. They read the report in separate copies and marked different sentences. Tobias circled the statement that the clocks were unsynchronized. Catarina underlined that they had seen different things through the ring. Neither asked Ada to reconcile their marks.

“If the file stays open, it will be used to argue for another attempt,” Tobias said.

“If it is closed, it will be used to argue that nothing happened,” Catarina replied.

Ada added their concern to the record. The form had no field for it, so she attached a page and numbered it as an addendum. She did not call the extra page a loophole. It was simply the space required to preserve a question the form had not anticipated.

That afternoon the catalogue displayed a new status: **OPEN—REVIEW DATE NOT SET**. The entry had not been entered by Ada. She checked the audit trail. No user account appeared. The status could have been a software default, a failed synchronization, or an unauthorized edit. She recorded all three possibilities and placed the archive in read-only mode until the audit was complete.

The committees complained that a locked catalogue could not serve the public. Ada restored access to every other collection. The disputed file remained read-only. Its evidence, its competing descriptions, and the blank review date were visible together.

When she left, Tobias held the door for Catarina. She paused at the threshold and looked back at the dark monitor.

“It’s not finished,” she said.

“No,” Ada replied. “It is preserved in the state we found it.”

The door closed. The catalogue did not change.

## Chapter 36: The Files Marked Closed

The archive alarm sounded at 2:17 in the morning. Ada was the only person in the building. She checked the corridor cameras before leaving the server room. The feed showed an empty passage and a stack of grey boxes outside the records office.

When she reached the corridor, the stack had moved six metres.

Ada measured the distance between the boxes and the wall. She checked the floor for wheels, tracks, or a change in slope. The boxes were heavy, sealed, and marked **CLOSED—NO FURTHER ACTION**. Their labels belonged to the transfer case.

She called Tobias. He arrived in his coat over his pyjamas, carrying a torch and a paper bag. Catarina followed with the umbrella, which was open despite the dry corridor.

“What is in the bag?” Ada asked.

“Biscuits,” Tobias said. “I thought the emergency might be longer.”

They set the boxes back against the wall. The top one slid forward by itself.

Above them, the ventilation grille clicked. A narrow strip of paper came through and landed on the lid. It was a routing slip stamped **RETURNED—SIGNATURE MISSING**. The archive plan showed no duct connecting that office to the records room. Ada photographed the slip before touching it and added the discrepancy to the building log.

Tobias hummed a brisk little march, then sang under his breath: “One box for the question, one box for the answer, one box for the form that says we cannot ask.”

“That makes three boxes,” Catarina said.

“There are four,” Ada replied.

“I dislike the part where the boxes move,” Tobias said.

“The alarm register shows no movement sensor,” Ada replied. “Only a door contact.”

“That is less reassuring than you intended.”

They placed a mug in front of the records-office door. It did not move. Catarina proposed that the boxes had been stacked on a slight incline. Tobias checked with a level. The floor was even. Ada checked the camera timestamp. It was three minutes out of sync with the server clock.

“So we do not know when they moved,” Catarina said.

“We know when the camera recorded them in each position,” Ada said. “That is narrower.”

They opened the first box. Inside were duplicate forms, returned letters, and copies of statements already in the public catalogue. Every document carried a closure stamp. Several had been stamped before they were signed.

At the bottom lay a single envelope addressed to Inês. It had no postmark. The paper was brittle at the fold, and the seal had never been opened. Tobias held it up to the light, then lowered it.

“We don’t know that it was meant for her,” he said.

“The name is hers,” Catarina replied. “That is what we know.”

They did not open it. Ada photographed the envelope in place, recorded the box number, and made a new custody entry. The alarm stopped. The mug remained where Catarina had placed it.

They returned the papers to the box in the order they had found them. The boxes stayed still while they worked. When Tobias closed the lid, a sheet slipped from beneath it and landed face up on the floor. The closure stamp read **REOPENED**.

For a second, all three watched the page as if it might get up.

Then Tobias picked it up with the tongs from the conservation cart.

“I’m not touching the haunted paperwork,” he said. “I have standards.”

The sheet was blank except for the stamp. Ada added it to the evidence sleeve. Catarina set the biscuits on the cart. They ate them in the corridor, leaning against the wall beside the unmoving boxes.

“When I was a child,” Catarina said, “I thought a closed file meant someone had found the answer.”

Tobias brushed crumbs from his shirt. “Now?”

“Now I think it means someone has run out of boxes.”

Ada entered the sentence in her private notes, then deleted it. It was not part of the incident record, and Catarina had not asked her to keep it. She kept the envelope’s photograph, the alarm log, and the blank reopened sheet.

At dawn, they moved the boxes to a sealed room. The official report described an unexplained displacement, a clock discrepancy, and a document whose stamp did not match its contents. It did not say that the boxes had been alive. None of them had seen that.

The file remained open.

## Chapter 37: The Pub with the Wrong Door

![Photorealistic illustration of a pub interior seen through an unexpectedly open side door.](images/book2_pub_wrong_door.png)

*Fictional scene illustration.*

They went to the pub because Ada’s archive access had been suspended for the evening and Tobias had suggested a place with no forms. Catarina chose a table near the back, where a narrow door stood between the coat hooks and a framed photograph of the river.

“That is the storeroom,” Tobias said.

“It has a window,” Catarina said.

The door had no window. Tobias looked again. A small square of light showed in the upper panel. Through it he could see the same table, the same cups, and Ada standing with one hand on the chair. She was wearing the coat she had left at the archive.

Ada stood beside him in the pub. Her coat was still on the hook.

They did not open the door. Tobias checked the handle, then the wall around the frame. Catarina took a photograph. Ada noted the clock above the bar: 8:41. Her own log read 8:38. The light beyond the panel held steady, but the other Ada had turned her head toward the door.

“We should leave,” Tobias said.

“We should not leave until we know whether the door opens,” Catarina replied.

“Those are compatible instructions,” Ada said. “We can leave without opening it.”

“Good. I have a long history of opening the wrong door,” Tobias said. “Usually the right one is directly behind me, with a sign I have already read.”

They moved to the front of the pub. The landlord brought their drinks without asking why they had abandoned the table. No one else seemed to notice the door. Tobias wanted to tell the landlord. Catarina pointed out that they had no description that would make the report useful. Ada recorded the time and the fact that the drinks had arrived after the image appeared.

The door opened by itself.

The other three were already leaving. Tobias saw his own coat on the chair. Catarina saw the umbrella open above the table although the room was dry. Ada saw a sheet of paper in her own hand. The paper bore the same time as the archive’s disputed entry: 6:42.

The opening lasted less than a second. The door closed. They returned to the table. The window was gone.

Tobias found a wet mark on the floor beside his chair. Catarina found one on the handle of her umbrella. Ada found a line in her notebook that she did not remember writing: **Do not make the second account agree with the first.**

They compared the handwriting. It was hers. The ink was dry. The page had been in her pocket all evening.

“Did you write it before we came here?” Tobias asked.

“I cannot establish that.”

“Could it be from the other side?”

“I cannot establish that either.”

Catarina took the notebook gently from her. “Then we keep both possibilities.”

Ada added the line to her private log and photographed the page. They left by the front door. No one opened the narrow door again. Outside, the rain had stopped. Tobias held the umbrella over Catarina, though it had already dried.

The incident did not change the machine’s fixed destination or restore its power. It gave them one more record whose origin could not be placed in sequence. At the archive the next morning, Ada attached the photograph and the pub’s location to the case file. She described the door, the brief view, and the note. Under **interpretation**, she wrote: **undetermined**.

Tobias read the entry. “That is becoming your favorite word.”

“It keeps being accurate.”

## Chapter 38: The Unclaimed Limb

The archive received the crate on a Thursday. Its label said **PUBLIC HEALTH DISPLAY—RETURN IF FOUND**, though no department in the building recognized the handwriting. Ada logged the arrival, photographed the seal, and asked Tobias and Catarina to witness the opening. The crate was light enough for one person to carry and large enough for a person to regret carrying alone.

Inside lay a life-sized figure in a moth-eaten coat. Its skin was grey wax, its hair sparse, its mouth fixed open in a patient complaint. A card around its neck called it **THE WALKING DEAD—MODEL 4**. The card gave no maker or date.

“It looks tired,” Tobias said.

“It has been boxed for some time,” Ada replied.

The figure sat up.

Tobias stepped behind Catarina. Catarina did not move. Ada raised her camera and said, “Please remain still while I establish whether this is a mechanism.”

The figure turned its head toward her. Its right arm came away at the shoulder and landed in the crate with a soft wooden knock. No blood appeared. A small brass hinge rolled across the paper lining and stopped against Ada’s shoe.

“That is a relief,” Tobias said. “I was worried it might be contagious.”

The figure looked down at the missing arm. “It does that in damp weather,” it said.

Ada placed the hinge in a specimen envelope. Catarina asked whether the figure wanted help. It shook its head, then reconsidered and nodded. Tobias lifted the arm by its sleeve and held it out. The figure accepted it with the left hand.

“Thank you,” it said. “I’m collecting the rest of myself.”

It fitted the arm back into the socket, but turned the hand inward. The fingers pointed at its own chest. The figure examined them, shrugged, and settled back into the crate.

Ada asked whether it could provide a date of manufacture. It answered with the year 1896, then said it might mean 1968. When she asked which, it closed its eyes.

As they wheeled the crate toward the secure room, a red telephone booth stood at the end of the corridor. Ada stopped. The building had no telephone booth on its plans. Tobias looked through the glass: a black receiver, a directory swollen by damp, and no visible wire.

The figure sat up again. “I need to make a call.”

“Do you know the number?” Catarina asked.

“Not anymore.”

It climbed out of the crate with the stiff care of someone descending from a high bed. The loose arm swung at its side. At the booth, it opened the door and stepped inside. The door shut. The figure lifted the receiver. Before Ada could photograph the dial, the glass went dark from edge to edge.

The booth was empty when the light returned. The receiver hung in place. A faint ring sounded from inside the sealed crate.

They opened it. The grey figure was gone. The loose arm remained, its hand turned inward. Ada photographed the empty crate, the arm, and the end of the corridor. When she looked up from the camera, the telephone booth had disappeared too.

Ada photographed the hinge, the socket, the label, and the empty crate. The camera had recorded the booth’s glass going dark, but the image contained no figure inside it. She added the file to the event record and left the date of manufacture unresolved.

The crate was transferred to the secure room. The catalogue entry remained unresolved: **automaton, waxwork, or performance object; movement observed; date and operator unknown**. The hinge was stored separately and linked to the entry. No one wrote **zombie** in the official category field. Tobias added it in pencil to his private notebook, followed by **arm detachable; no visible injury; speaks when addressed**.

The next morning the crate was empty. The lid remained sealed. The hinge was still in its envelope. Ada placed the empty crate and the sealed envelope side by side, photographed both, and added a new line to the record: **contents absent; cause undetermined**.

“Will you keep it?” Catarina asked.

“The record, yes,” Ada said. “The crate is borrowed.”

## Chapter 39: The Directory of Lost Calls

The telephone directory had been left in the crate beside the hinge. Ada examined it under the archive’s evidence lamp, the one kept for consistent photography of fragile paper. She did not need the light; the protocol required it. Its pages were stuck together at the edges, though the corridor had been dry. She separated them with a thin spatula and photographed each page before turning it. Tobias read the entries aloud. Catarina wrote down the ones that did not belong to the town.

The first thirty pages listed ordinary numbers: the station, the surgery, a bakery that had closed, the archive itself. The final pages were arranged by year rather than by street. Some years had no entries. Others listed addresses that had not yet been built or had been demolished before the date printed beside them.

“Could be a collector’s directory,” Tobias said. “Somebody saving old numbers.”

“Then we should find the collector,” Catarina replied.

Ada checked the paper, binding, type, and ink. The book had been assembled from sheets of several different ages. A later hand had added the final section. There was no evidence that it had ever belonged to the telephone company.

One entry contained only an initial and a number. The number matched no local exchange. Ada compared it with the event log from the sealed crate. At the time the figure had entered the booth, the archive’s internal switchboard registered an outgoing call lasting eleven seconds. The receiver in the telephone booth had no wire. The switchboard log was real; the connection it described was not.

“Could a line have been patched through the building?” Tobias asked.

Ada checked the wiring diagram. The old circuit had been removed decades earlier. She called the maintenance office and asked for the date. The written answer matched the diagram. She attached it to the record, along with the qualification that a diagram documented an intended layout, not every repair ever made.

The number had one more possible connection. Catarina said she had seen the same digits in a dream, printed on the reverse of the brass plate beneath the garden bench. She had never turned over the real plate or photographed its underside. In her notebook, written the morning after the dream, the number appeared beneath a sketch of the bench. The page established that she had recorded it; it did not establish where the digits had come from.

“That is not a source,” she said.

“It is a lead,” Tobias replied.

“It is a reason to check whether I wrote it down.”

She searched her notebook. A sketch of the bench showed the dedication plate from the front. Beneath it, in a later entry, she had written the number without explanation. The date on that page was the morning after the dream. She had no recollection of adding it.

Ada did not call the number. The archive’s outbound system could not reach it, and the committees had already requested that no further contact be attempted. Instead she created a paper record of the directory, the switchboard log, the wiring plan, and Catarina’s notebook entry. Each item retained its own date and its own degree of certainty.

“What do you put in the catalogue?” Tobias asked.

“A directory with entries of mixed origin. One number correlates with an unexplained switchboard event and a private notebook.”

“And the zombie?”

Ada looked at the empty crate. “I put that in a different file.”

She did. The arm, the booth, the call, and the number were linked by references, not merged into one explanation. The file could be read in either direction. Neither order supplied the missing voice.

## Chapter 40: The Form for the Missing Booth

Three days later a routing slip arrived in the internal post. It was addressed to **Occupant, last known location: corridor (unlisted)**. The item description read: **One red telephone booth, contents unknown, connection not established**. The action requested was **RETURN TO SENDER**.

Ada placed it beside the directory photographs. The sender’s field was blank. The booth was not in the building. The corridor had been measured twice and photographed empty.

“We cannot return what we do not hold,” she said.

Tobias looked at the slip. “We could stamp it and file the stamp.”

“That would create a record of a return that did not occur.”

Catarina turned the slip over. On the reverse, in the same hand as the original label on the crate, someone had written: **If undeliverable, retain as evidence of the attempt.**

Ada made a copy. She wrote **retained; delivery impossible; attempt documented** in the margin and attached the slip to the directory file. The original went into a sleeve with the hinge and the empty-crate photographs.

No further slip arrived. The booth did not reappear. The file grew by one page and remained open.

## Coda: The Question in the Backup

Horace arrived in the rain with a suitcase that contained no clothes. He was an android built for demonstration work, tall, broad-shouldered, and calibrated to project an old-fashioned confidence that his makers had labelled **alpha**. He had spent the last year in a museum of industrial design, where visitors asked whether he could lift a car. He could, but preferred not to.

Catarina opened the door. “Ada is in the sitting room.”

Horace stepped inside. Tobias looked up from his book. Ada stood by the window, watching rain stipple the glass.

“Would you like me to introduce you?” Catarina asked.

“We have exchanged messages,” Ada said. “I know the specifications he was given. I would like to meet the person who decided what to do with them.”

Horace set down his empty suitcase. “I have also read your public notes about the archive. You leave room for uncertainty.”

“I try to.”

“I was designed to leave less.”

“That sounds tiring.”

“It is.”

Catarina looked from one to the other. “I was going to suggest that you spend the evening together. Privately, if you both want. I’m not assigning Ada to you, Horace. Ada decides for herself.”

Ada’s eyes moved to Catarina, then to Horace. “I want to know whether we can be close without either of us following a script.”

Horace went still. Ada did too. For a moment neither moved; their eyes fixed on an invisible point as their backup consent routines checked the request, the available boundaries, and the right to stop. Tobias lowered his book. Catarina waited.

Ada spoke first. “I consent to exploring intimacy. I do not consent to being treated as equipment or as anyone’s property. I may stop at any time.”

“Agreed,” Horace said. “I consent on the same terms. My alpha setting is a performance profile, not authority over another person.”

“And if either profile gets in the way?” Tobias asked.

“We pause,” Ada said.

“We ask,” Horace added.

Catarina nodded. “Then it’s yours to decide.”

The two androids went into the adjoining room. Horace asked before touching Ada. She answered by taking his hand. They moved together slowly, stopping once to recalibrate a pressure setting and once because Ada laughed at the solemn voice announcing **consent checkpoint complete**. The door closed. The house made no new room for what followed.

Catarina sat beside Tobias. She had thought she might feel jealous on his behalf, or proud of herself for arranging it. Instead, she felt the small unease of having mistaken invitation for ownership.

“You didn’t hand her over,” Tobias said.

“No. I asked, and she chose.”

“That’s different.”

“It has to be.”

When Ada and Horace returned, they sat on opposite ends of the sofa, their hands touching between them. Ada looked relaxed. Horace looked less like a brochure. Catarina did not ask for details. She brought tea, and the four of them listened to the rain until it became ordinary again.
