## Chapter 14: The Photograph

It was Catarina herself who told him in the end, and it took the whole of one long evening.

She sat him at the kitchen table, put the dictionaries between them, and laid a photograph beside them. A younger woman with her smile, standing on this same jetty in summer. Tobias, trying to be helpful, drew her on the back of an envelope. Catarina looked at the drawing for a long moment and said, "This is not a woman. This is a lamppost with an opinion."

"Inês," she said. "Minha irmã."

He had the name already, on an envelope under the Olivetti. He did not say so. A man does not spend a thing he was not given.

Word by painful word, with the dictionary and the drawings, the story came out in a mixture of English and Portuguese that neither of them could have spoken in September.

"My sister, she work here. Two year. Para o Hale. In March, she telephone me. Night. She is..." Catarina searched. "Assustada. Afraid. She say, I saw something. I cannot stay. Do not tell anyone. Then she is gone."

"She saw Elise," Tobias said slowly. "She saw what happened at the jetty."

Catarina nodded. Tears stood in her eyes and she did not wipe them.

"You came to find her. That's why you took the job."

"Sim." She laid her hand flat on the photograph. "I do not... confio. Trust. I do not trust this house."

He reached across the table and covered her hand with his. Neither of them moved for a long time.

"Then we will find her," he said.

She did not translate it. She did not need to. The sentence had already been spent, once, under a falling star. This one could wait its turn.

They worked in the study from then on, side by side.

Tobias rebuilt the manuscript chapter by chapter. Catarina sat opposite with her sister's old keys, her sister's old address book, and her own quiet sharp memory of every detail Inês had ever mentioned. She was the one who noticed that the lost chapters had a gap: the night of Elise's death, between ten and midnight, which the original manuscript had covered in a single page.

"This page. Where?"

"In the lake. It never came back."

"No." She frowned and drew something on the envelope. A small wooden cabinet with a false back. "Inês keep things here. Under the window seat."

He thought of the board he had seen, the dust disturbed in a clean rectangle, the cushion put back as if the cushion were the point. He did not say that either. He lifted the seat.

They found it at midnight. Inside the false back, wrapped in oilskin, lay a notebook in Inês's handwriting and a small tape recorder. There was no second door. Inês had written, on the inside of the wood, DO NOT MOVE THIS. TRUST ME. They did not move it. They took what she had hidden, and they put the board back.

They had to borrow a cassette player from Madame Fournier's kitchen. It turned out to be built into a radio, and the first button Tobias pressed played Édith Piaf at full volume for four minutes, through the open window, over the lake, to the great alarm of a heron. The second button was the right one.

On the tape, Elise Marchetti's voice, clear and frightened:

"Victor, I know you took the Marchetti estate. I know the Verrane Award is a fraud and you've pocketed eleven thousand entry fees. I have the contracts and the bank statements. If you don't give the rights back and close the foundation, I'll take them to the press tomorrow."

A few seconds after she had finished, Hale's voice. Smooth. Almost kind.

"Come down to the jetty, Elise, and we'll talk about it."

A second voice, a few seconds later, not Hale's. Cooler. A man who had never once raised it. "I'll be on the jetty. Don't let her bring the pages." Inês had written in the margin of the notebook, in a small furious hand: not the publisher. The one with the claret tie. He smiled. She went down.

Catarina was crying openly now, the recorder cupped in both hands. Tobias took it gently, set it on the desk, and did what he had wanted to do for weeks. He put his arms round her.

She stiffened. Then she let her forehead drop against his shoulder and said, in a voice too soft to hear, a word he did not need a dictionary for.

"Obrigada."

They did not play the tape again that night. They copied the margin onto a clean page, in Catarina's block capitals, and put the page in the biscuit tin with Fournier's spare keys, which was the only place in the house that had never once lost anything.

## Chapter 15: The Cup

The rain came after midnight, and Tobias went out in it.

He told himself he was checking the gate. The gate was locked. He stood at the end of the jetty anyway, because the cooler voice was still in the room, and a voice like that does not stay indoors. By the time he came in he was shaking. By morning the shake had become a fever.

Catarina put him to bed in the study with three blankets, a hot-water bottle and a bowl of her grandmother's soup, which smelled of garlic and ancient sorrow. She sat with him until he fell asleep, and then she tiptoed out. The lamp went down. The house creaked. And on the desk, in the dark, a small voice said:

"Is he asleep?"

"He's ill," said a deeper voice, with a faint creak of springs. "It's not the same."

Tobias opened one eye.

The Olivetti stood on the desk, very upright, its keys gleaming like a row of teeth. It was not a thing he had ever noticed having a face, but it had one now, in the arrangement of the carriage and the bell and the two round spools. It looked like a retired colonel who had been asked to wait in a draughty corridor.

"Good evening," it said. "I do hope we haven't woken you. I did say it was unprofessional."

"You're a typewriter," said Tobias.

"I am a 1962 Olivetti Lettera 22 of the finest Italian manufacture, and I have been sat in the same position for four weeks. Do you know what I have been typing? Do you know how many commas you leave out?"

"Hush, Monsieur Olivetti," said a kindly, round, steamy voice from the sideboard. A fat brown kettle was bubbling gently, with a spout like a nose. "The poor boy's got a temperature. Hot drink, dear?"

"I'm dreaming," said Tobias.

"Of course you are," said the kettle comfortably. "Everyone dreams in a fever. We only talk to people who can't remember it in the morning. It's the rule of the house."

A chipped white cup scooted to the edge of the table, rocking on its saucer. It was the one Catarina had handed him on the first day, the one with the crack along the rim. It had a small, high, eager voice, and it spoke very fast.

"Is she your girlfriend? She makes the tea. She talks to us. She said good morning to the toaster. Is she your wife?"

"She's not," said Tobias, "my anything."

There was a pause. In the corner, the grandfather clock cleared its throat with a tick, and a pair of brass candlesticks on the mantelpiece, who had been standing very close together, leaned in.

"Mon Dieu," said the first candlestick, who was French, flamboyant, and appeared to be on fire in several places. "He says not. He says nothing. You hear it, Marguerite? This man is blind."

"He is English," said the second candlestick, a duchess. "It is the same."

"A handsome woman scrubs your floors for two months," said the clock gravely, "sings in your kitchen, wades into a lake after your papers. And you say nothing."

"I've said a great deal."

"You have said," said Monsieur Olivetti stiffly, "a great deal of rubbish. There is no comma in it."

"We have a plan," said the kettle.

They had a plan. It was, in the way of fever dreams, a fairly good plan. The candlesticks would light the way. The clock would chime at the proper hour. The kettle would pour, the cup would clink, and the typewriter would type a short, formal, wholly inappropriate declaration on a single clean sheet. And then, said the kettle, "you say it aloud, you daft creature, and you say it yourself."

"I can't," said Tobias. "My Portuguese is terrible."

"She doesn't want your Portuguese," said the cup.

"What does she want?"

The cup thought about it. There was a long silence. It was the kind of silence in which, if you were a cup, you could hear all the soup in the world.

"She wants to be met," said the cup.

The house went quiet. The fire settled in the grate. Out in the garden the wind lifted, and in the long grass by the gate, a very large white bird turned its head toward the lit window, as if it had heard something. Tobias lay in the blankets, shivering and warm, and in the half-light he saw for the first time how many small, kind, tireless things there were in the house that had been looking after him: the cups, the kettle, the old clock that ticked in the hall, the stairs that did not creak when she came down in the night. Every one of them was a small piece of Catarina.

The fire settled. Nobody came to the door.

In the morning the fever had broken. The sun lay in pale squares on the quilt, and the study was perfectly tidy, and on the desk the Olivetti stood in its usual place with a sheet of paper in the roller. It was blank, apart from one line, in the neat, ungrammatical capitals he would have known anywhere.

THE SOUP IS HOT. (NOT IS THE SOUP.) I AM HAPPY YOU ARE BETTER.

Catarina came in with the tray. On it, steaming, was a mug of tea made properly for the first time: the pot warmed, the leaves left to stand, the milk in last. It was in the cup with the chip.

He took it, and held it in both hands, and looked at it for a long moment.

"It's a very good cup," said Catarina, a little shyly.

"Yes," said Tobias. "I think it has opinions."

She laughed, and sat on the edge of the bed, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. Outside, the clock in the hall struck eight. On the desk the Olivetti stayed discreetly silent. He did not tell her about the dream. A dream that has been explained is only a temperature.

Inês's notebook ended with an address in Aix-en-Provence: Maître Étienne Barbier, Notaire, 14 rue Gaston de Saporta, 17h00. Beneath it, underlined twice: the originals.

"The originals of what?" said Tobias.

"Of everything he stole," said Catarina. "If a lawyer keeps the contracts, then the lawyer is the one who can open the door." She tapped the page. "It is an hour. We go tomorrow. We are there by five."

They left at ten. They were not there by five.

The first delay was a stall at the side of the road, run by a woman in a straw hat selling peaches out of a wheelbarrow. "We stop for one minute," said Catarina, who knew about peaches. They stopped for forty. The peaches were enormous and warm and tasted of a summer that had not yet finished with anyone, and the woman in the hat insisted that Tobias taste the apricots, and the plums, and a small yellow fruit nobody could name in any language, and by the time they drove off the back seat was a fruit bowl.

"This was a mistake," said Tobias, with juice running down his wrist. "We're going to be late."

"Paris can wait," said Catarina, with her mouth full.

"We're not going to Paris."

"Then the lawyer can wait."

The second delay was a goat farm, because there was a sign. The third was a ruined abbey on a hill, because Tobias remarked that it looked Romanesque, and Catarina said "Românico," and they both spent twenty minutes standing in an empty nave where the roof had fallen in and the sky had come to live, whispering because it seemed to demand it. The fourth was an olive mill, where an old man in a blue apron poured them a thimble of oil so green and peppery that Catarina coughed, wiped her eyes, and bought six litres.

"It is for the restaurant," she said defensively. "Fernanda will say it is too expensive. Then she will use all of it."

The fifth delay was a flat tyre.

It happened on a quiet road between two vineyards, in the full heat of the afternoon, with the cicadas screaming in the pines like a machine nobody could turn off. Tobias stood over the spare wheel with the expression of a man reading a document in Latin.

"I have never changed a tyre," he said.

"You are English. You have a man."

"I did have a man. He was called the AA."

"Afasta-te," said Catarina, rolling up her sleeves. She was four minutes with the jack and the spanner, glistening, singing a tune from the radio under her breath, and when it was done she wiped her hands on her skirt and looked at him, hot and happy and absurdly beautiful, with oil on her cheek.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," said Tobias. "I was thinking I should have married a mechanic."

"You are not married."

"No," said Tobias. "I was thinking it in advance."

She stood very still. A cicada, somewhere, chose that moment to stop. Then Catarina turned, very pink, picked up the spanner, put it in the boot with extraordinary care, and said to the air in Portuguese, quite clearly, "Meu Deus, ele disse isso a sério."

He understood every word.

They did not reach Aix until half past six. The plane trees along the Cours Mirabeau were already throwing their long shadows, the fountains were running gold, and number fourteen, rue Gaston de Saporta, was a tall honey-coloured house with its shutters closed and a small brass plate that said MAÎTRE BARBIER, NOTAIRE.

The door was ajar. Inside, the office had been taken to pieces. Drawers upended, files on the floor, a safe standing open and empty. A neighbour in a dressing gown told them in rapid French that two men in dark coats had come at a quarter to five. They were, she said, extremely loud for burglars. One had kept shouting "Gaston!", and the other had replied "Oui?", and the first had said, "Not you, the other Gaston!", and for a while it had gone on like that. They had left at five, taking a safe, an umbrella stand and, for reasons the neighbour could not explain, a stuffed owl. And that Maître Barbier, a gentleman of eighty, was at that moment in the hospital with a bruised arm and extremely bad temper.

"At five," said Tobias slowly.

"We would have been there at five," said Catarina. She had gone very pale. "Exactly at five. Like the notebook says."

They stood in the ransacked office in the late honey light. Somewhere outside, an accordion began to play. Neither said what both were thinking. If they had been punctual, if they had been sensible, if they had not stopped for the peaches, and the goats, and the abbey, and the oil, and the tyre, and the three minutes in which a man had said something aloud that should have waited.

"Paris," said Catarina at last, in a small voice, "can wait."

"Yes," said Tobias. "Apparently, so can everything else."

On the way out they picked up the single object the thieves had overlooked: a calendar on the old man's desk, where a note in a spidery hand read: Marchetti originals, safe deposit, Banque Verrane. Key with M. Fournier.

"Madame Fournier," said Tobias.

"Odile," said Catarina. "Odile has the key."

They were halfway to the car when a long black saloon pulled up silently at the kerb, and a man got out. He was about fifty, immaculate, in a charcoal suit and a tie the colour of old claret, and he carried an umbrella although it had not rained in Provence since June.

"Mr Lind," he said, in perfect, pleasant English. "I am so glad. I had hoped we might meet."

"I don't think we've..."

"Maître Edouard Delacroix-Roux. I act for certain persons whose names you may know." He smiled. His teeth were extraordinary. "I wished to say, as a professional courtesy, how much I admire your novel."

"You've read it?"

"I have not read it. I have read a summary, which I understand is all that is left." He turned the umbrella slowly in his fingers. "It must be a great grief to lose two years of work. One would hate for anything similar to happen to the people one loves."

"Is that a threat?" said Catarina, in her very plainest English.

"A threat? Certainly not." Maître Delacroix-Roux looked at her with a tenderness so artificial it might have been varnished. "I was merely observing that the roads of Provence are most dangerous at dusk, mademoiselle. And that some people, for want of a decent education, cannot tell a landmark from a trap." He glanced up at the dark windows of the notary's house, then back to her. "Do give my regards to your sister. I understand she has been missing for some time. How very distressing."

He bowed, as one bows to a wedding guest, and got back into the car. It purred away down the Cours Mirabeau under the plane trees, and in the gutter behind it lay one fallen leaf, and the umbrella, which he had left behind on purpose.

Catarina picked it up. Tobias took it from her very gently and snapped it in two.

"I don't like him," said Tobias.

"No," said Catarina. "He is a man who has never once been in a boat."

The tie was the colour of the margin. He wrote that down before he started the car.

## Chapter 16: The Key

Madame Fournier did not look up from the secateurs.

"The key has been in the biscuit tin since March," she said. "With the letter about the rent, and a photograph of Lucien standing on one leg, which I am not going to explain. Your publisher asked me to keep it. He said it was for a guest who was very careful. The guest did not come back."

The letter was three lines. The rent was eleven hundred. Hale had written three thousand in the advertisement and kept the difference. Mara, told by telephone, said it was not a crime beside the jetty and was the one they could prove on a Tuesday.

The bank in Aix took twenty minutes and no heroism. Brunner had not yet arrived. Mara signed. Tobias watched. Catarina held the peach bag, because the bank had no ceremony and a paper bag was honest. Inside the box, under a toy Porsche with a wheel missing, was a card in a child's hand: 1-9-6-4. Under that, the contracts Elise had meant to bring to the jetty, and a photocopy of a cover: a bird, wings spread, bending its neck. The codex itself was not there.

"Second box," said Mara, reading Inês's margin. "Marseille. A foundation. The one that likes to be photographed."

They did not stage it. They carried the contracts out in the bag that had held the peaches. In the car Catarina read the top page and did not say I told you. She said, "The woman in your chapter four should not slip."

"I've already taken it out."

"Then the book can stay in the house."

Captain Anselm Brunner arrived at the lake house at eight o'clock that evening, on foot, in the rain, with an umbrella held at the precise angle of a bayonet.

He was about thirty-five. He was six foot four. He had a close fair haircut, a face carved out of cheese and honesty, and a dark overcoat buttoned to the throat. He stood on the porch, dripped politely on the mat, and bowed from the waist, which no one had done to Tobias since he was a child at a pantomime.

"Good evening," he said, in English with a faint Swiss-German edge. "I am Captain Anselm Brunner of the Pontifical Swiss Guard. I regret the hour. I regret the weather. I regret, in a more general sense, that I am here at all."

"The Swiss Guard," said Tobias.

"Yes."

"The ones with the... the striped trousers."

"Those are for ceremonies." Captain Brunner looked down at his coat, with faint sorrow. "I am not permitted to wear them on investigations. Or to carry the halberd. I was given this." He lifted the umbrella. "It is very firm. It keeps off rain. That is the whole of its talent."

It was Catarina, behind Tobias in the doorway, who recovered first. She crossed herself, almost involuntarily, and said in a small voice, "Meu Deus. The Pope sends the police."

"Not the Pope," said Brunner gravely. "The Holy See. And not the police. I am a special investigator. It is a new post. There are, at present, two of us, and the other one is in Malta."

They sat him in the kitchen. He declined tea, accepted water, declined the glass and drank from the tap cup with his back very straight. He then took from his coat an envelope, an old Latin letter, a photograph and a notebook, and laid them in a row on the table, in order of size.

"Two years ago," he said, "a woman named Valentina Marchetti died in Lyon, aged ninety-one. She was the mother of Elise Marchetti. In her estate was a book of hours, written in Avignon in 1347. The Marchetti Codex. It belonged, until the French Revolution, to the Apostolic Library. It was stolen in 1793. We have been looking for it for two hundred and thirty years. We are not hasty."

"And it was in the estate," said Tobias.

"It was. And then it was with a publisher named Victor Hale. And now," said Brunner, looking at the photocopy from the peach bag, "I am told it is in a second box, in Marseille, in the name of a foundation that gave two million euros to be photographed with a cardinal."

He opened his own photograph. The same bird, wings spread, bending its long neck to pierce its own breast. Beneath it, three tiny chicks.

"A pelican," said Catarina softly.

"The pelican in her piety," said Brunner. "In the old iconography, the pelican feeds her young with her own blood. It is a figure of sacrifice. It is also, I understand, a figure of..." He frowned. "You have one here. In the garden."

"He escaped from a zoo," said Tobias. "He does not pay rent."

Brunner looked out of the window at the lawn, where in the rain, on a sun lounger left by the last tenants, a vast white bird sat motionless on one leg. A long silence stretched out. The Captain of the Swiss Guard, who had stood in the presence of cardinals without moving his eyelid, took out a clean white handkerchief and blew his nose.

"I am sorry," he said. "It has been a long journey."

Inspector Mara Quill eyed him across the kitchen table the way a cat eyes a different breed of cat.

"You've no jurisdiction here, Captain."

"None whatever, Inspector. I have no jurisdiction anywhere, except a very small country with a lovely garden." He met her eye without a flicker. "I wish only to ask where a book is. If it is found, I wish to carry it home. I do not wish to arrest anybody. I am not able."

"You'd be surprised how irregular I can be," said Mara, with a trace of respect.

"I have been told," said Brunner, "that the English are very good at it."

"Hale is a cashier," said Mara. She put the Marseille photograph on the table, the one from the lobster restaurant: Hale, and the man with the closed-door face, and the hands buttering a roll. "This one decides. Armand Vial. The foundation is his. The cooler voice on the tape is not Hale's. Inês wrote the tie in the margin before any of us had a name for it."

"Delacroix," said Tobias. "We met him. He left the umbrella on purpose."

Brunner looked at the broken umbrella in the corner, which Catarina had not thrown away. "Then the book is a cherry," he said. "The money was the cake. I will go to Marseille for the cherry. You will do what you are permitted to do with the cake."

"She will mock your French," said Tobias, meaning Fournier, who had already mocked it on the step and given him the key anyway.

"I am Swiss," said Brunner, with a small, weary, dignified smile. "I have spent my whole life being mocked by the French. It is how they show affection."

He did not go to Bavaria. There was no Bavaria in the letter. The letter said Avignon, and then Marseille, and the box in Marseille had the codex in it, which is a duller sentence than a swan and a better one. Brunner flew it home the following week, economy, on his lap, for reasons of principle. The note he sent back was two lines. We have prayed for you. It was not enough.

## Chapter 17: The Far Shore

Hugo Pemberton-Hayes was nine, and large for his age in the head.

He had been left at the lake house for the night, because Nigel and Priscilla were at a technical rehearsal of Dick Whittington, and Chloe had a date with a roofer. Hugo had been delivered at six with a rucksack, a torch, a library copy of Poisonous Plants of the Mediterranean, and an expression of immense and unnatural calm.

"Your second book has a plot hole," he told Tobias, at supper. "The murderer couldn't have been in the library at ten, because he's on the stairs at nine fifty-eight. And the poison's wrong."

"I'll bear that in mind."

"You do that," said Hugo kindly, and went to bed with the torch.

At half past nine Tobias and Catarina walked over to Madame Fournier's, because the tape was safer in the biscuit tin than in a house with a nine-year-old and a reputation. They left Hugo asleep upstairs. The pelican was on the porch. At ten past ten, a van with no lights pulled up at the bottom of the lane.

Two men got out. They wore dark coats, balaclavas and the stiff, hunted air of the professionally unlucky.

"Gaston," whispered the first one.

"Oui?"

"Not you. The other one."

"There is no other one, Gaston! It is only the two of us!"

The gate was locked. They climbed it, which is how a locked gate announces itself to the unlucky, and the first Gaston stepped onto a layer of marbles Hugo had laid that afternoon for reasons of his own.

What followed was a precise geometry of misfortune. Gaston One's legs went east. Gaston Two, reaching out to catch him, grasped the hall coat-stand, which toppled, and brought down two walking sticks and a brass gong that Madame Fournier had always said was a decoration. The sound rang through the valley. Both men arrived at the kitchen door in a tangle of coats.

"Gaston," said Gaston One. "I believe there is a child in the house."

Upstairs, a torch clicked on beneath a chin.

They did not find the stairs easily. Each step had been waxed with olive oil from the six litres Catarina had bought at the mill. At the top, a paint pot tied to a length of typewriter ribbon met Gaston Two in the face. It was a very handsome shade of duck-egg blue.

"The study!" hissed Gaston One. "The originals are in the study!"

The door of the study was hot. Someone had left the iron on its flat side in front of it and tied it to the handle. Gaston One shouted something that has not appeared in print. Gaston Two stepped on a tray of cold custard and went out through the study window with a long, thin, receding cry, and landed in the lake.

From the window seat, in the dark, a small voice spoke.

"I'm nine," said Hugo. "My father's a solicitor. I know all about trespass."

Ten minutes later, when Tobias and Catarina came back along the lane, they found a white van abandoned, two men on the lawn wrapped in fairy lights, glued to each other, covered in flour and duck-egg blue, and a pelican standing on one chest with the air of a bird who had not been asked and did not intend to give a statement. In the porch sat Hugo, in his pyjamas, with a mug of cocoa.

"I rang the Inspector," said Hugo, not looking up. "She's on her way. I did my father's voice. She's very cross. Also I ate your biscuits."

The Gastons were arrested at midnight by an Inspector in her dressing gown. They did not resist. As they were led to the car, the first Gaston was heard to say, in a voice of broken wonder, "Gaston. I think we should go to New York."

"There are children in New York," said the second, and the car door closed on him.

Hale came back on the Sunday night, uninvited, with his headlights off.

The gate was locked. He left the Porsche in the lane, and the sump scraped, and nobody laughed at it until the morning. Catarina put the kitchen light out and stood with her back to the door she had already bolted. Tobias picked up the telephone because she pointed at it. He asked for Mara by name. He did not explain the plot. He said the publisher was at the gate and the gate was shut and they would not open it.

Hale spoke through the wood, pleasant, and the pleasantness was the frightening part.

"Mr Lind. I thought we might talk about your novel. The jetty is rotten. I'd hate to lose another guest."

They did not answer. The pelican, on the lounger, did not come to the window. When the lights of Mara's car reached the lane, Hale was already backing out. The scrape was the only comedy.

"He will come again," said Catarina.

"Then we will not be on this side of the water," said Tobias.

Inês Vidal had been living in the boathouse on the other side of the lake since March.

They rowed across at dawn, with the tape in Tobias's coat and the notebook inside Catarina's blouse, because a bolted door is not a finding. The far shore smelled of tar and reeds. A black-and-white dog barked twice and then sat, as if the sitting were the point.

Inês came out onto the dock, thinner than in the photograph, with a lantern in one hand and a shotgun she did not raise. Behind her, a minute later, because Tobias had asked for her by name, came Inspector Quill.

"I rang her yesterday," Inês said, in careful English. "I did not trust the police until I met this one."

"I did say," said Mara drily, "that quiet people listen."

"Gigi found the lane," said Inês, of the dog. "She does not like strangers. She makes exceptions for tins."

Gigi gave the Inspector a small, dignified nod, as one professional to another, and went back to the stick.

"You are the writer," said Inês, looking Tobias up and down. "I have read your book. The second one is better, but that is not saying much. Chapter four has a woman slip. She did not slip."

"She is very intelligent," Catarina said, in a tone that suggested this was a medical condition.

"I am very tired," said Inês. "Intelligent is how I got here."

Hale did not run far. He was still in the lane when Mara's second car came down it, because a Porsche that is too low for the lane cannot be heroic in it. He stood at the end of the jetty in his beautiful coat and watched the boat come in, and the tape recorder pass from Tobias's hands to Mara's.

"It was only paper," he said, almost to himself. "Contracts. Receipts. A few silly pages in the water."

"It was three dead people," said Mara. "Aubry. Giraud. Marchetti. You didn't do this for a house, Mr Hale. You haven't the imagination. Who was on the jetty?"

Hale's smile faltered. "I'm a cashier, Inspector. A very well-dressed cashier. If you think I'm the one who decides, you've read the wrong book."

"The margin has already read it," said Mara. "Claret tie. You can give me the name, or the tape can. The tape does not want a deal."

He gave her the name on the second telling. Maître Edouard Delacroix-Roux. The man who smiled. The man who had said he would be on the jetty. Armand Vial, he said, after that, because a cashier who has begun does not know where to stop. Chairman. Marseille. The foundation. The two million. The cooler voice was not Vial's either. Vial did not attend jettyes. Vial sent a lawyer.

Catarina stepped onto the dock, soaked and shaking, and put an arm round her sister.

"Não é só papel," she said.

She said it again in English, slowly, so that everyone could hear.

"It is not. Only. Paper."

The tape was with Mara. The margin was with Mara. Hale had given the name. The house by the water had done what it was for. What came next would not rewrite any of that.


## Between the Far Shore and the Trial

The ticket for Saint-Oran remained in the biscuit tin. The station it named had closed decades earlier. A clerk stamped the ticket so it could not become valid by mistake. Three earlier tickets like it had appeared in different years, each dated for the next day. No train had ever stopped there.

Inês’s notebook led them to an archive in Avignon where an android named Ada Morel catalogued damaged records. She had been sent to restore a name that had been removed without a lawful owner. The name was Inês Vidal. Ada’s cooling system failed in the heat; she left with a printout and an appointment date that matched the night Inês had called.

Two committees met in a church hall: one that wanted a stable public record, one that kept duplicate copies in ordinary places. Both claimed to be preventing the other from changing history. Ada documented both. Neither could explain who had first written Inês’s name on the list.

At the old observatory a machine attempted to align two recorded events separated by decades. Catarina entered the ring once. She returned with a torn sleeve and a memory of a woman signing a paper that Tobias and the clocks did not share. They did not run the machine again. The token split. The file stayed open under “decision deferred.”

None of it reopened the jetty. The tape, the margin, and Hale’s name remained what they were.
## Chapter 22: The Prosecution

The case had already been proved by ordinary means before the doors of the Palais opened: the tape, the margin in Inês’s hand, the contracts from the peach bag, the second box, and Hale’s own second-week account. The trial would test those things in public. It would not replace them.

The trial was held in March, one year to the day after Elise Marchetti died, in the Palais de Justice at Aix-en-Provence: a handsome, pale, pillared building facing a square of plane trees, where on Tuesdays a market sold lavender, sausage and tragic cheese.

The Gastons had already been dealt with, on a Monday, in a smaller room. They were charged with breaking and entering, attempted burglary, and, at the particular insistence of the Prosecutor, wilful ignorance of the ways of a child. Their lawyer was Maître Edouard Delacroix-Roux, who had not yet been charged with the larger thing, and who rose in a charcoal suit and a claret tie.

Hugo Pemberton-Hayes, nine and three-quarters, took him to pieces with great politeness. He corrected the spelling of pedalo. He explained the coefficient of friction of olive oil on waxed oak. He identified the first Gaston as the one who shouts and the second as the one who falls, and both Gastons nodded. The Judge offered him a seat on two cushions for the sentencing. Eighteen months. Afterwards, in the corridor, Delacroix sat with his head in his hands, and a very large Portuguese woman who had been there all morning offered him a custard tart. He said later she had been the only person in the building to show him any kindness. The woman was Fernanda. She had come early.

The larger trial did not need a bird.

Mara had the tape. She had the margin in Inês's hand. She had the contracts from the peach bag, and the second box in Marseille, and Hale, who turned out to be a better witness than a villain. He gave up Vial in the second week to save what he could still call a misunderstanding, which was the difference on the rent and eleven days of a Porsche he had been dreaming about for forty years. He gave up Delacroix because the tape had already given him up, and a man likes to arrive at a fact before the fact arrives at him.

Vial was not in court. He did not own a telephone that anyone could find. The night before the warrant he was no longer on the Pénélope, and the Pénélope was found three days later drifting off the Portuguese coast with the engine running, the champagne open and nobody on board. The French police wrote drowned. Mara crossed it out and wrote DOUBT.

On the ninth day the doors of Room 3 had been propped open for the heat, and the pelican walked in.

Nobody stopped him. The usher said afterwards that there was no regulation. He came down the centre aisle on his enormous flat feet, past the gallery, past the clerk, who looked up from his typewriter and decided not to see it. He stopped at the defence table. He looked at the claret tie. He did not scream. He shifted his weight, once, and turned his head away, as he had turned it away on the jetty when he had been asked, by silence, to have manners.

"Lucien," whispered Tobias.

"I did not bring him," whispered Catarina. "He followed the train."

"Monsieur le Président," said Delacroix, "I must object to this circus."

"The animal is not a witness," said the Judge. "The tape is the witness. Remove the bird, or leave him, if he is quiet. I have heard worse from the public."

The pelican stayed. He did not identify anyone. He had seen a splash, if he had seen anything, and a seeing is not a deposition. Mara did not need him. She read the margin aloud. She played the cooler voice. Hale, from the dock, said the name again, and meant it, and wept, which did not make him smaller than the thing he had done and did not make him the thing either.

The defence asked for the tape to be excluded. The recorder was old, the label had been written after the event, and the copy had passed through several hands before Mara received it. The prosecution answered with the custody log, the original cassette, and two independent transfers made before anyone knew the name on the margin. The waveforms differed in volume but not in sequence. The mechanical click at the start appeared in each copy.

“Does that prove the words are true?” the Judge asked.

“No,” Mara said. “It supports that the recording has not been edited in the disputed section. The words still need to be tested against the other evidence.”

The Judge asked whether the voice could be identified. Mara said the comparison was consistent with the voice on two earlier recordings, but voice comparison was not an identity card. The speaker could have been imitated, the recording could have been staged, and the samples were not made under identical conditions. She offered the conclusion as supporting evidence, not certainty.

The defence played the tape again. The claret tie appeared in the margin. The cooler voice named the jetty. A second voice answered with the time. The words did not become more truthful because they were heard in a courtroom. They became evidence that could be questioned in public.

Hale was asked why he had not gone to the police sooner. He said he had spent years telling himself that the estate papers were a civil dispute, that the deaths were accidents, that his role had been to carry a message and not to understand it. The prosecutor asked whether he had believed those things at the time.

“No,” Hale said. “I believed them when I needed to.”

His answer did not excuse him. It did explain the delay. The Judge instructed the jury to distinguish what Hale had personally seen from what he had inferred, and what he was now admitting from what he still could not know. The instruction seemed to make the room smaller. It left less space for a convenient story.

Inês’s margin was examined as handwriting, not prophecy. The paper matched the notebook. The ink was consistent with the other entries. The sequence of folds showed that the note had been written before it was carried in the peach bag. None of this proved who had been on the jetty. It did show that the margin had not been added after the arrest to fit the tape.

The loose bolt from Lyon and the brake inspection near Avignon were entered as physical evidence. Each supported a different part of Mara’s timeline. Neither identified the person responsible on its own. Together with the contracts, the recordings, and Hale’s account, they formed a pattern that the defence could challenge and the jury could weigh.

Tobias wrote the Judge’s questions in the margin of his own notebook. He had once thought a mystery ended when the pieces fitted. Now he understood that a conclusion also needed to say how the pieces had been found, which ones did not fit, and how much weight each could bear.

Mara asked the court to look at the final known state of the Pénélope first. The boat drifted without anyone aboard. The engine was running. Champagne stood open in the cabin. No person had been seen leaving it. The image was not dramatic. It was an inventory of consequences.

Then she moved backward. The boat had left its berth. A fuel purchase had been made. A message had been sent from the quay. The last confirmed sighting of Vial came before the vessel was found. Each earlier fact narrowed the interval without filling it.

The prosecutor asked whether the order mattered.

“It matters to the story we tell,” Mara said. “It does not change the order in which the events occurred. Beginning with the empty boat makes us feel the absence before we hear the explanation. That feeling is not evidence.”

The Judge asked her to continue.

She showed the court the contract, then the transfer, then the first request for payment. The amounts had begun as small fees, each defensible by itself. Read backward, the last payment made the earlier ones look like preparation. Read forward, they looked like routine. The records supported a pattern, but the pattern did not reveal the private intention behind each signature.

Hale’s testimony supplied part of that intention. He had understood that writers were being charged for a prize that did not exist. He had not understood who stood behind the scheme until later. Mara placed his first statement beside the later one. The differences were visible. The first protected his own role. The second named the people he had helped protect.

“Which statement is true?” the defence asked.

“Both are statements Hale made,” Mara said. “The second is supported by the tape and the contracts. The first is contradicted by them.”

The answer did not make the later account innocent. It made its claims testable. The court could see the change in his story and decide what it meant.

When the evidence was finally placed in chronological order, the jury had already seen its ending. No one could unsee the empty boat. The backwards presentation had made the cause arrive late, but it had not absolved anyone of what the cause revealed.

Delacroix did not confess to a bird. He was convicted on the tape, the notebook, the loose bolt in Lyon, the brakes near Avignon, and Hale's second week. The Judge did not bow to the pelican. He thanked Inês for the margin and Catarina for the pages, and Catarina said, "I only read them."

Hale, who had never touched anyone, was convicted of theft of the Marchetti estate, as an accessory, and of fraud against 11,400 hopeful writers, each of whom had paid sixty pounds to be told they had not won. The Judge remarked that it was the first time the Verrane Award had ever delivered what it promised.

The Marchetti Codex went to Rome in a padded case on Brunner's lap, in economy, for reasons of principle. The Holy See's note was two lines. Thank you. We have prayed for you. It was not enough.

The pelican walked out the way he had come, when the doors were opened for the heat. He did not look back. A witness who has not been called does not wait to be thanked.

Afterwards, on the courthouse steps, Tobias met the third Vidal sister properly, though she had already fed the defence.

Fernanda was the eldest. She had driven eleven hours from Porto in a van that said RESTAURANTE VIDAL on the side, and she filled the doorway of it as she filled most rooms: broad, warm, loud, in a flowered dress and a coat the colour of a tomato, carrying a cool-box the size of a small child. She kissed Inês on both cheeks, Catarina on both cheeks and then once more for luck, and turned to Tobias with the look of a customs officer.

"Então," said Fernanda. "Este é o inglês."

"Bom dia," said Tobias. "Muito prazer."

"His accent is a crime," Fernanda said, in Portuguese, to the street. "They should arrest him. Is he eating?"

"He eats," said Catarina, in the same language, glowing. "He eats biscuits."

"Biscoitos!" Fernanda threw up her hands. She took his face in both hands, turned it left, then right, and said in slow loud English, as if to a deaf child: "TOO. THIN. I FEED YOU."

"I'm perfectly..."

"I FEED YOU," Fernanda repeated, and opened the cool-box. Inside, under a tea towel, lay a roast chicken, a dish of rice, two custard tarts and a bottle of something without a label.

"Eat. Then we discuss the wedding."

"Resistance is useless," Inês murmured, in English, for his benefit alone. "I tried for thirty-four years. The only winning move is to be hungry."

"We haven't discussed a wedding," said Tobias.

"You have discussed," said Fernanda. "You do not know yet. That is why I am here."

She sat him on the steps and fed him until he could not speak. By the third tart he had been told that he would marry in Portugal, that the priest was a cousin, that the soup would be served at one and the dancing would start at four, and that if he ever made her sister cry, she would drive eleven hours the other way with a very large pan.

It was the longest conversation Tobias had ever had in which he was only allowed to say yes.

Through the crowd, in a brown tweed jacket that was rather better than anything Tobias owned, came a tall, sheepish man with a golf umbrella and a woman in a very large hat. Gerald, and Camilla.

"Tobias. Ah. What a coincidence. We're on a short break."

"Hello, Gerald."

"About the duvet. And the springs. They were rather loud."

"I know," said Tobias. "I could hear them from the hall. I thought it was the pipes."

"It was not the pipes," said Camilla, from under the hat.

Fernanda, who had seen a thin man and a pale woman standing near a cool-box, sat them down. "TOO. THIN. I FEED YOU." Gerald was fed chicken, rice and three custard tarts before he could draw breath. Camilla was fed the rest. They left with a Tupperware and the impression that they were somehow engaged to Portugal. They did not mention the duvet again. Camilla, as far as anyone knows, still has it.

When Fernanda had finished with him, he wiped his hands, took Catarina a little apart, and held out a thin bound manuscript.

"It's finished. I rewrote the lost chapters from your notes and Inês's, and the tape, and everything we found. Chapter four does not slip."

Catarina took it. The title page read: The Lake House Manuscript. For Catarina, who went in after it.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she looked up at him with her serious dark eyes, and he saw that she was trying not to laugh.

"Your dedication," she said, "is very good English."

"Thank you."

"Is much better than your Portuguese."

"That," said Tobias, "is a very low bar."

"Is the lowest in Europe," Catarina agreed. "I check."

She stepped closer. She put the manuscript under her arm. She took his face in both hands, and she said, first in Portuguese and then in English, slowly, so there could be no mistake:

"Tobias. I do not want to be a housekeeper in your house any more. I want to be a woman in your life."

He opened his mouth and found that for once in his career he had no words at all, only the three he had been rehearsing for weeks in the study with a dictionary on his knee, in the worst accent in Europe.

"Eu amo-te," said Tobias Lind, in terrible Portuguese.

"I love you," said Catarina Vidal, in English, at exactly the same moment.

They stopped. Each had spoken the other's language, and neither had planned to. It was the second time the book allowed it. The first had been a wish. This one was the answer.

"Terrible," said Catarina, laughing, with her sister clapping on the steps and the spring wind lifting her hair. "Both of us. Very bad. I love it."

She kissed him then, in the cold sun, in front of the whole courthouse. A single stray page of an old draft, blown loose from nowhere, spun up over their heads, caught the light, and drifted away down the street. Somewhere in the distance a pelican cleared its throat.

Neither of them chased it. It was only paper.

## Chapter 23: The Night Train

Fernanda had decreed that they would go to Portugal by train.

"Aeroplanes," she said, "are for people who do not wish to arrive properly. The body must travel at the speed of the heart. Also the cool-box does not fit in the overhead locker."

So on a Friday evening in April, six of them boarded the sleeper at Hendaye: Tobias, Catarina, Fernanda, Inês, Zé the great-uncle with his guitar, and a very large cool-box that Fernanda would not allow out of her sight, and which she referred to, with great tenderness, as o bebé.

The train climbed all night. In the last of the light it ran along the Basque coast, grey sea on one side and green hills on the other, then turned inland, and the mountains began. They came up out of the dusk like the backs of sleeping animals. The line went round them and through them. It dived into tunnels and came out into sudden moonlit valleys where a river ran silver at the bottom of a cliff.

At a small station with no name on the board, the train slowed almost to a stop. The platform was empty. A single lamp lit a bench, a timetable case, and a door set into the retaining wall. No building stood behind it. The door had a brass handle and a small paper notice: **WAIT HERE IF YOU ARE EXPECTED.**

“Is this on the route?” Tobias asked.

“No,” Catarina said. She had the printed timetable open on her lap. “There are no stops between here and the border.”

Fernanda leaned across the aisle. “Then we are not stopping.”

The train stayed still. Outside, the platform clock showed 6:42. Inês took her notebook from her bag and wrote down the time. Zé’s guitar strings made a faint sound though he had not touched them. The cool-box gave a small internal click.

“Could be a siding,” Tobias said.

“Could be a reflection,” Catarina replied. The window showed the platform clearly, but also reflected the compartment, so that the door appeared behind them as well as outside.

Fernanda reached for the latch. Inês put one hand on the cool-box and the other on her sister’s wrist.

“If the train is not meant to stop here, we do not need to get off.”

The platform lamp blinked. For an instant, the door stood open. Beyond it was not a room, but a view of the same platform from the other end. A second door appeared there, also open, with the train visible through it. The image formed a corridor that could not fit behind the retaining wall.

Tobias checked the window latch. It was shut. Catarina checked the timetable. The page had not changed. The stop did not exist in the schedule.

The train moved. It did not pull away so much as remember that it was moving. The platform slipped backward. The door shut. The lamp disappeared behind a stand of trees.

No one spoke for several minutes. Fernanda took out the tea towel and covered the cool-box more securely. Inês copied the time, the missing stop, and the notice’s wording. She did not write that they had been invited to get off. She wrote that they had seen a door and a notice through the window.

“Do you think someone was waiting?” Tobias asked.

“There was nobody on the platform,” Catarina said.

“That is not what he asked,” said Inês.

No one had an answer. Zé began to play a slow tune, not a song anyone recognized. The train found its speed again, and the mountains closed over the track.

At the Spanish border a Guardia Civil officer boarded and asked Fernanda, with great formality, what was in the cool-box.

"O bebé," said Fernanda.

The officer looked at the box. He looked at Fernanda. He had been a policeman for twenty-six years and had never been asked to inspect a baby in a cool-box.

"Open it, please."

She opened it. Inside, under the tea towel, lay a roast chicken, a dish of rice and four custard tarts. The officer looked at them for a long time.

"EAT," said Fernanda.

He ate. He ate a custard tart with the hat in one hand and the clipboard in the other, and he closed his eyes. Then he drew himself up, saluted the cool-box, and went away down the corridor, singing. The train was waved through three borders that night without being asked a single question.

Tobias and Catarina had the compartment at the end of the carriage. Fernanda had arranged this herself, with an expression of such tremendous innocence that Inês had been obliged to look out of the window.

"Eleven hours," said Catarina, closing the door.

"Eleven and a half, in Spain."

"And in the morning we are in Porto."

"Yes."

"It is a very long time," she said, "to sit opposite a man and say nothing."

"I wasn't planning to say nothing."

"No?"

"I was planning," said Tobias, "to say a great many things. In my worst Portuguese. All night."

She laughed, low, in the dark, and the train gave a long, rocking lurch round a bend, and she was suddenly in his lap, or he was suddenly on the floor, nobody afterwards could say exactly which, and the thing they had been holding back since the pond simply stopped being held.

The train plunged into the longest tunnel in the Pyrenees. The lamp went out. For four minutes, in the dark, there was no language and no need of a phrasebook. There was only the noise of the wheels, and the beat of two hearts, going at exactly the same speed.

When the train came out into the moonlight they were sitting on opposite seats, in perfect order, with their hands folded and their hair rather wild. Somebody tapped on the door.

"Entre," said Catarina, in a voice that was not entirely steady.

It was Inês. She stood in the doorway with a flask and two cups.

"Fernanda sends tea," she said. "She also sends a message. She says the tunnel was very long, and that she is a very light sleeper and a very heavy cook."

"Thank you," said Tobias.

"Don't thank me." Inês set down the flask. "I am a sarcastic woman, Tobias. I shall make a remark about this at the wedding. I shall make it in front of Avó Rosa."

She shut the door. A few seconds later, down the corridor, they heard a cheer, and a guitar, and Zé's voice singing something hoarse and triumphant about the sea.

They looked at each other and began to laugh. Outside, the mountains rolled by in the silver dark, one after another, like a long procession of friends.

In the morning Inês went down to the quay at Afurada and asked a man she knew if the offer of a place on the boat was the same in daylight. He said daylight costs. She said she had been expensive already. She did not take the notebook. The margin had already done its work. By noon she had gutted twelve fish and been sworn at twice, which was fair, and which she did not report.

The chipped cup cracked at the wedding, against the stone of the sink where Fernanda had set it down too quickly. It broke into the half that had always been the chip and a second half that had no history. Catarina looked at it and said the opinions had got out. Tobias drank from the half that remained. Nobody made a speech about it. A thing that breaks twice is a joke. This was a cup.


## After the Steps

Six months later Ada wrote. The restricted file on the transfer ledger could now be examined. They compared it with Inês’s notebook. The entries did not establish who had signed. They showed the ledger had been altered at different times. Ada was listed as a participant after the form refused her name. The new catalogue entry recorded the limits of what the pages could answer.

A red telephone booth appeared in a corridor that had no booth on its plans. A grey figure in a moth-eaten coat entered it and both vanished. A detached arm and a damp directory remained. Ada photographed the empty crate, the hinge, and the corridor. The next morning the crate was empty and the booth was gone. A routing slip later arrived addressed to the occupant of the unlisted corridor, requesting return of the booth. Ada documented that delivery was impossible and attached the slip to the open file.

The murder stayed closed. The partnership continued. The records of what could not be settled continued with them.
