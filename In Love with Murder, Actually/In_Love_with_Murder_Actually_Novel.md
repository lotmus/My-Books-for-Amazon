# In Love with Murder, Actually


# Part One: The Lake

## Chapter 1: Leeds

Tobias Lind came home early from a book fair in Hull, where he had sold four copies of his last novel, three of them to the same man, who had wanted to complain about the ending.

Leeds, that afternoon, was doing its best. The brick was the colour of a biscuit just out of the oven. A plane tree on the corner had decided to be yellow and was succeeding. The newsagent had put the daffodils out too soon, which was the local form of hope. A woman with a tartan trolley nodded at him because she always did, and he nodded back, and the nod was the whole of their friendship and had lasted nine years. He liked the street. He liked the particular dirty gold of the light on the wet setts, and the smell of the brewery when the wind was idle, and the fact that the city did not ask him to be interesting. He had been going to tell Camilla about the man who hated the ending. He had bought buns.

The hall light was off. A jacket hung on the banister, brown tweed, better than anything he owned. On the table by the door the anniversary wine stood open. He had been saving it since March. He stood with his keys in his hand and listened to the bed springs tell him what the jacket had already said.

He went up because it was his house. Camilla was there. Gerald, from the golf club, was there. The duvet was his. He asked if it was, because the other questions were too large for the landing. Nobody answered usefully. He went down. He put the wine back in the fridge, out of habit, took it out again, out of dignity, and drank it at the sink. He wrote a note. Gone to France. Keep the duvet. He considered adjectives, and refused them. A person cannot improve that sort of note. He put the Olivetti in its case, and the only copy of the new book in a satchel, and a postcard from a dead friend in his breast pocket, and left the lamps.

The house in France had been booked since June. He had taken it for the autumn, from a publisher who had been very kind about the price, because a friend of his had drowned in front of it in March and he did not believe she had slipped. A novel was the only instrument he had ever owned for finding things out. He had meant to go on the first of October, with a packed lunch and a plan. Camilla had simply moved the date.

He caught the last train to London and, in the morning, the first train that went anywhere near Avignon. Somewhere south of Lyon he realised that he had brought the buns. He ate one. It tasted of a street he no longer lived on.

## Chapter 2: The Pond

The wind off the pond took the first page at 6:42 on a Tuesday morning.

It was a pond. The advertisement had said lake, because a lake can be let by the week and a pond cannot. Madame Fournier called it the étang and did not discuss it. Tobias called it the lake for eleven days, because he was English and recently ruined and needed a body of water large enough to have taken a friend. It was not. You could see the far reeds from the jetty. You could wade it, if you were angry, or pushed, or simply unwilling to let paper go. A heron used it. A pelican would later occupy it, and look too big, which was the pond's opinion of grandeur.

He had arrived eleven days earlier, by train to Avignon and by taxi up the valley, and had been let in by a brisk young woman from a letting agency who gave him three keys, a Wi-Fi password that did not work, and the information that the old lady in the house next door was away at her sister's in Lyon and was, the young woman said, choosing her words, *formidable*. The house had a study, a kitchen with a stove like a locomotive, a jetty, and a housekeeper. Nobody had mentioned the housekeeper.

Tobias Lind had carried the manuscript out to the end of the jetty because the house was too quiet to read in. He wanted wind and cold and the sound of something bigger than his own failure. The pond did not provide the sound. It provided a surface. He sat down with the satchel on his knees, a Thermos of tea at his elbow, and a gust came across the water and opened the bag like a hand.

Four hundred pages went up, turned once in the grey light, and settled on the lake like a flock of gulls.

Yes, pages. Real ones. Tobias wrote on a 1962 Olivetti typewriter, bought from a man in Harrogate, on the principle that nothing worth saying should be easy to delete. He owned a laptop. It was in a drawer, in Leeds, being respected from a distance. When people asked whether he backed anything up, he said that he had a very good memory, which was true, and a very poor sense of danger, which was why he was now standing on a jetty.

He did not shout. He was English, and Leeds-born, and the family instinct in a disaster was to say nothing and put the kettle on. He stood very still and watched two years of his life drift away. It was, he reflected, a fairly standard Tuesday.

Then a woman ran past him along the planks and into the lake.

She went in fully dressed, in apron and wool cardigan, and gasped when the cold took her to the chest. She did not stop. She waded out and gathered the pages in both arms, one after another, and turned back toward the shore with her hair stuck to her face.

"Oh, for heaven's sake," Tobias called, helplessly. "Don't! It's only paper! You'll catch your death!"

He had been told once that the English only ever shout about the weather and other people's dogs. He was pleased to find the theory held.

She stopped, water streaming from her sleeves, and looked at him as though he had said something very stupid in a language she barely knew. Which he had.

"Não é só papel," she said.

It was the first thing Catarina Vidal ever said to him. He would not understand it for another three weeks.

## Chapter 3: The Housekeeper

She had come to the lake house in September, sent by an agency in the city. Tobias had rented the place for the autumn from a publisher named Victor Hale, who had been very generous about the price, and who had implied, in several warm emails, that the house was his. Too generous, in hindsight. The house came with a housekeeper, and the housekeeper was Portuguese, spoke perhaps thirty words of English, and had never looked at him for longer than politeness required.

That morning she spread the ruined pages across the kitchen table, on every towel the house possessed, and dried them one by one with the same grim patience she gave to scrubbing the stove.

"Catarina, you really don't have to."

She held up a sheet. The ink had run into grey rivers. "Escrito," she said, and pointed at him. Then at the page, then at the lake, then shrugged. Lost, then.

"Yes. Lost."

She shook her head firmly and went on drying.

He sat across from her for an hour and said nothing, because he had nothing in a language she could use. At the end of it she put a mug of tea in front of him, so weak that the tea bag had clearly been introduced to the water only briefly, in a cup with a chip in it, and walked out. He realised it was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in a year.

"Thank you," he called after her. "Obrigada," he added, proud of his one word. He had heard her say it to the baker's van that morning, and had been practising.

She paused in the doorway. She pointed at herself. "Obrigada," she said. Then she pointed at him, with a small, firm finger, as at a dog that has nearly understood. "Obrigado. O. You are a man. I think."

"Sorry?"

"A woman says obrigada. A man says obrigado." She considered him. "You say it like my aunt."

He understood, eventually, that a man who learns his first word of a language by eavesdropping on a woman will thank people as that woman for some time, and that the Portuguese are too kind to mention it, and that this one was not quite kind enough.

## Chapter 4: What the Water Left

By noon only a third of the pages could be read, and Tobias was struck by what he was reading.

It was a crime novel, the best he had written, and it was built on something true. In March a woman named Elise Marchetti had drowned in this lake. She was a literary editor, forty-one years old, and she had been Tobias's friend. The police had called it an accident. She had slipped from the jetty at night, the report said, after a few glasses of wine.

There was more. Elise's last emails had mentioned a literary prize, the Verrane Foundation Award, which charged hopeful writers a £60 entry fee and had never once, in nine years, awarded anything. She had also mentioned that her late mother's estate, including the rights to a bestselling backlist, had somehow ended up in the hands of her publisher. Tobias had read the emails four times. Murder, theft and a scam, he thought, and all of it with excellent letterhead.

That morning, with the pages drying, he rang the Foundation's hotline, to be sure. A man answered in the most aristocratic voice Tobias had ever heard, a voice like a cashmere blanket being lowered onto a duke, and said, "Welcome to the Verrane Award, where every writer is a winner." In the background, a hundred voices were murmuring the same sentence in the same velvet tone. Tobias asked, innocently, how many prizes had actually been awarded. There was a pause, and the voice slipped, just for one second, into the flat bark of a Marseille docker. "Putain," it said, "qui c'est, celui-là?" Then the velvet came back down, and the line went dead.

In July, with the help of a newspaper archive and a very long afternoon, he had found the other two. In April an auditor named Pascal Aubry had fallen from a hotel balcony in Lyon. In June a notary, Maître Giraud, had driven off a bridge near Avignon. Both deaths were ruled accidents. Both men, Tobias discovered, had spent their last weeks examining the accounts of the Verrane Foundation. Nobody had ever put the three names on one page. That, he realised, was what his manuscript was: the first page they were all on.

It was a terrible thing to have written, and a worse thing to have lost, and he was rather proud of it.

He had pinned the three names to the study wall with red string, as detectives do in films. It was a dramatic arrangement. He stepped back to admire it, and the string, the pins, the three photographs and, as it turned out, the left-hand curtain all came down on him at once, and he stood for some time in a shroud of toile de Jouy, wondering where it had all gone wrong.

Tobias had never believed it. Elise had once refused to cross a puddle on the grounds that it looked ambitious. That was why he had booked the lake house in June, long before Camilla and the jacket on the banister: to stand where she had stood and prove it. The novel was his method, because it was the only way he knew how to think. Every chapter reconstructed the night she died, and he had disguised the real names so thinly that anyone who knew the story would spot the villain on the first page.

Now the pages that named him were gone.

He went up to the study to see what the water had spared, and found he was not the first person there.

Catarina was on her knees by the window seat. The basket of linen was on the floor, untouched. One hand was under the cushion. The other was flat on the wood, as if she were listening to it. She had not heard him on the stair. When she did, she stood too quickly, and the cushion dropped, and for a moment she looked at the jetty through the glass as if the glass had accused her.

"Inês," she said.

The name came out on its own. She heard it. She put a hand to her mouth, not quite in time.

"No," she said. "Catarina. I am Catarina." She picked up the basket. "Sheet," she added, which was not what she had been doing, and went past him with her eyes on the banister, and down to the stove.

He stood in the doorway with a wet page in his hand. He had heard the name. He did not ask. There are questions that arrive before you have a language for them, and he had already used up his Portuguese on a thank-you.

Later she came back with the linen she had pretended to be fetching, and stood in the kitchen doorway while he read. For the first time she did not look away.

"Livro bom?" she asked quietly.

"Yes," he said. "I think it may have been a rather dangerous book."

She did not understand the words. She understood his face. She put down the basket, crossed the kitchen, and laid one wet hand briefly on his sleeve.

Then she went back to work, and he sat very still for a long time. The name stayed where she had left it. Inês. He wrote it on the back of an envelope, so that he would not lose it the way he lost everything else, and put the envelope under the Olivetti, and did not look at it again that day.

## Chapter 5: The Gatepost

Inspector Mara Quill came to the lake house that afternoon. She had been first officer at the scene in March, and she had never liked the report either. She was English by birth and French by her mother, the gendarmerie and twenty years of paperwork, and she spoke both languages in the same tone, which was the tone of a woman who has already read the end.

"A neighbour rang in a splash and a shout this morning, just before seven," she told him on the porch. "At the jetty. Would that be you?"

"The wind took my manuscript. Catarina went in after it."

"Your manuscript was on paper," said Mara.

"It was typed."

"On paper. In a satchel. On a jetty. In a wind." She wrote something in her notebook. "I'm sure there's a reason."

"It has no notifications."

"It also has no backup." She closed the notebook, not unkindly. "In my experience, Mr Lind, the people who distrust the cloud always end up in the lake."

"Ah." Mara looked past him through the open door, where Catarina was pegging pages to a line strung across the kitchen. "She knows the lake, then."

"I'm not sure she knows anything. She barely speaks English."

"Mr Lind." Mara gave him a long look. "In my experience, people who say very little are almost always listening. In my experience, people who say a great deal are almost always writing a book."

"I resent that."

"You're holding a pen."

She went down the steps, and then turned at the bottom, as people in her trade do, so that the last question arrives when the door is already closing.

"Officially," she said, "I'm here about a noise complaint. The file on Elise Marchetti is closed. My commandant closed it himself, in April, with a very good pen. If you happen to be writing a book about it, Mr Lind, I would very much like not to know."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially I would like a copy." She got into the car. "Typed. On paper. Kept somewhere dry."

Lake Verrane was in the south of France, which Tobias had known for several weeks and had somehow still failed to prepare for.

When she had gone, he walked to the garage in the village and hired a small car, because there was a bookshop in the next town and he needed, urgently, to be able to say more to his housekeeper than thank you in the voice of her aunt. The man at the garage had handed over the keys with the expression of a vet releasing a pet to an owner he did not trust. "À droite," the man had said, three times, pointing at the road. Tobias had nodded three times and driven out of the yard on the left.

The tractor was the first casualty of the afternoon, in the sense that it had to stop. The hen was the second, in the sense that it had to run. By the roundabout he had discovered that French drivers regard the indicator as a form of admission, and by the second roundabout, which he entered the wrong way round, he had discovered what the French regard as a suitable response to that. It involved a great deal of horn and one grandmother in a Citroën who leaned out of her window and said something that, even in a language he did not speak, lost nothing in translation.

He reached the gates of the lake house at four o'clock, with two dictionaries and a children's phrasebook on the passenger seat, parked, got out, and watched the car roll gently forward into the stone gatepost.

It was a small noise. A polite one. The kind of noise a car makes when it would like to apologise.

"Monsieur."

Behind him, on the gravel, stood a woman of about seventy-three, in a quilted waistcoat and gardening gloves, holding a pair of secateurs and a look of enormous patience. This, he understood at once, was Madame Odile Fournier, back that morning from a fortnight at her sister's in Lyon, who had heard about the splash from the baker before she had taken off her coat. She was the owner of the gatepost. She was also, as he would shortly learn, the owner of the house, the jetty, the lake and, in a legal sense, the tractor.

Tobias rehearsed the sentence he had learned from the phrasebook on the way over, and delivered it with his hand on his heart.

"Bonjour, Madame. Je suis très excité de vous rencontrer."

Madame Fournier looked at him for a long moment.

"Ah," she said. "Moi aussi, monsieur. Mais j'ai soixante-treize ans, alors, soyons raisonnables."

"I... sorry?"

"Excité," said Madame Fournier, in the voice of a woman explaining a fire extinguisher to a child. "It does not mean excited. It means excited." She nodded gravely at his trousers. "You understand."

Tobias went the colour of a boiled beetroot and changed the subject, which is to say he made it worse.

"Mon voiture," he said, gesturing at the gatepost, "est dans le... le... pilier."

"Ma voiture," said Madame Fournier. "Ma. It is feminine, like me. The pilier is masculine, like most things that stand in the way. You have now offended all three of us, and only one of us is a wall."

"Je suis désolé."

"That one is correct. Congratulations." She stepped over the gravel to inspect the stone, tapped it twice with the secateurs, and turned back to him. "Come. I make coffee. You look like a man who has eaten nothing since the war."

"Non, merci," said Tobias, with great dignity. "Je suis plein."

Madame Fournier stopped on the step.

"You are plein," she repeated slowly.

"Full. I am full."

"Plein. For a man, monsieur, it means drunk. Pleine, for a cow, it means she is expecting." She looked him up and down with enormous sympathy. "I do not know which you are claiming. For either one, the doctor is in the village on Tuesdays."

By now he had stopped answering. He followed her into the kitchen with the bearing of a man marching to a firing squad, and sat where she pointed, and drank the coffee she gave him, and was mocked quietly, steadily and with real skill, for the next forty minutes.

It was only when she was refilling his cup that she mentioned the rent.

"Victor Hale pays me eleven hundred euros a month for this house," she said. "He tells me he rents it to a very quiet English writer."

"I pay him three thousand."

Madame Fournier set down the coffee pot with a small, precise click.

"Ah," she said, in a very different voice. "Monsieur Lind. We should talk about this Monsieur Hale."

It was the first sentence she had said all afternoon that he understood completely, in any language.

## Chapter 6: The Machine

That evening Tobias carried the dictionaries and the phrasebook into the kitchen. He set them on the table between himself and Catarina like a peace offering.

She opened the first one. She read slowly, with a finger under each word, and then said, with the care of a woman laying stones across a river:

"Thank. You."

"You're welcome."

"Is... welcome." She repeated it twice, testing the shape of it. "Is a good word. Is like a house."

It was the best definition of welcome he had ever heard. He wrote it down.

On the third day Tobias announced that he would scan the pages.

"Scan," said Catarina, to the dictionary. "Digitalizar." She considered this. "You will put the paper in a machine and the machine will write it again."

"Precisely. It reads the words and turns them into text. Optical character recognition. Then I'll have a proper copy, saved, safe, backed up."

"Like a man with a cow who builds a fence after the cow is in the lake."

"Yes. Exactly like that."

Madame Fournier owned a printer-scanner, which she kept in the back kitchen under a lace cloth, like a relative she did not wish to discuss. Tobias fed it the first dried page. The machine groaned, thought about it, and displayed its verdict on a laptop that Madame Fournier's grandson had left behind at Easter, along with a charger and a strong smell of crisps.

He read it aloud. "'She was already dead when the clock struck, which is why nobody believed the clock.' The computer says: 'Shoe was alley dread when the cloak stuck, which is why no body believed the cluck.'"

"Cluck," said Catarina, delighted. "Like a hen."

"It has also changed 'murder' to 'mother' in six places."

"Perhaps it knows something."

He tried the next page. It produced an elegant paragraph about a man named Gerald (the machine had, for reasons known only to itself, mistaken a water stain for the word) and a long passage in a language that appeared to be Finnish. Page twelve came back entirely as a row of tiny cats.

"The ink ran," Tobias said, with the dignity of a man defending his work against an appliance. "It's the water damage. It can't cope."

Catarina took the printout, held it at arm's length, squinted at the damp original, and read the same line aloud, slowly, in her careful English, with every word right.

"She was already dead when the clock struck." She looked up. "The machine is stupid. I am better."

"You're reading my handwriting, which is a typewriter."

"Is also a kind of handwriting." She set down the page. "Every day I read the stove. The stove is more difficult than this."

He looked at the printout, then at her, then at the machine blinking its small green light under the lace cloth, and understood that the pages could not be saved by clever software. They would have to be saved the way she had saved them in the first place, one at a time, by someone who could read.

He gave the laptop back to Madame Fournier, who put it under the lace cloth with the scanner, so that they could keep each other company in disgrace. For the first time in his life, he typed the next chapter on the Olivetti on purpose.

They fell into a routine that neither would have called a routine.

Every morning Tobias wrote in the study, rebuilding the lost chapters from memory, and every morning he heard her in the house below: the kettle, a radio tuned to a Lisbon station, a low voice singing along, off-key and unselfconscious.

At noon she left a tray outside his door. She never knocked. Sometimes there was a note under the plate in careful block capitals, copied out of the dictionary.

EAT.

REST.

THE SOUP IS HOT. (NOT IS THE SOUP.)

He began leaving notes of his own. Thank you. Good morning. Later, haltingly, with the phrasebook open on his knee: Bom dia, Catarina. Como está?

The reply came under his door that night, in pencil, with a line of Portuguese crossed out and written again in English:

I AM VERY WELL. YOU ARE SPEAKING PORTUGUESE LIKE A COW.

He laughed until his tea went cold. He learned later that she had meant to write a cow on holiday, which in her mind was a kindness. In Portuguese it would have been a compliment. In English it was merely a cow.

He did not ask her about the name. Once, drying a page, he said it aloud to see what it would do. "Inês." She did not look up. The cloth in her hand stopped for the length of a breath, and then went on. He let it go. A man who has just learned thank you does not spend it on a locked door.

## Chapter 7: Birds

The lake lay at the bottom of a valley in the French countryside, and it did not appear to be aware that anyone was writing a murder mystery on its shore.

In the mornings, mist lay on the water like a held breath. Plane trees lined the lane, their bark peeling in grey-and-cream patches, as though the trees were quietly undressing. Beyond the garden wall the land rolled away in vineyards, then lavender gone to seed, then a field of sunflowers all facing the wrong way in autumn, heads bowed, looking like a congregation that had been told the sermon would be long.

And there were birds. Tobias had not expected so many birds.

Herons stood in the reeds on one leg, like retired bank managers considering a transaction. Coots paddled in the shallows with the fuss of men who have lost their keys. Swallows stitched the air above the jetty at dusk, and once, at dawn, a kingfisher went past the study window in a single blue streak, so brilliantly that Tobias dropped his pen.

"Guarda-rios," said Catarina, behind him, with a tea towel over her shoulder.

"Sorry?"

"Guarda-rios. River... watch-man." She mimed a small figure with a clipboard. "He guard the river."

"Kingfisher," said Tobias. "He is a kingfisher."

"King. Fisher." She gave it a moment's serious thought. "In Portuguese he is a policeman. In English he is a king. English people, they make everything a king."

"We have a lot of kings."

"You have one king," said Catarina, "and two hundred tea."

So it became their game. In the evenings they sat on the jetty with a flask and the binoculars from the hall cupboard and traded names. The heron was garça for her and heron for him. The swallow was andorinha, a lovely word that he practised for days and still got wrong. The hoopoe, which strutted across the lawn one morning in a crown and a barred coat and the confidence of a man who has never once been criticised, was poupa.

"You say it," she said. "Poupa."

"Poo... pa."

"Poupa."

"Poo-pah."

"Meu Deus." She closed her eyes, delighted and appalled. "The bird is better at saying it."

The hoopoe, on cue, said oop-oop-oop from the lawn, perfectly, and looked at him with something very like pity.

And there was the pelican.

It arrived on the fourth evening, a large white bird the size of a small sofa, which landed on the end of the jetty with the air of a man arriving late to his own surprise party. There are no wild pelicans in the south of France. Tobias said so, aloud, to the bird.

"Pelicano," said Catarina, unimpressed. "He is lost."

"He's a long way from home."

"Everyone here," said Catarina, "is a long way from home."

Madame Fournier, consulted over the garden wall, shrugged with the whole of her waistcoat. "He has been here since the summer before last. He escaped from a bird park near Montpellier, and the bird park has not asked for him back. He likes the jetty, and he does not pay rent." She considered the bird. "He is the only honest tenant I have."

The pelican stayed. It fished at dawn, stood about at noon looking gravely at nothing, and bobbed its enormous beak whenever Tobias went by, as if it knew exactly what he was writing and had notes.

He would think about those evenings later, when he was sitting in a courtroom in Aix-en-Provence being asked to describe the lake. He would say that it was very quiet. That it was very beautiful. That he had not, at first, noticed anything sinister about it at all.

He had been watching the birds. It had seemed more important at the time.

On the fifth day of October, at approximately eleven in the morning, Tobias saw a snake.

It was in the study, under the window seat. It was long, brown and entirely unhurried, and it looked at him with the polite expression of a lodger who has been there longer than the landlord. Then it slid behind the bookcase and was gone.

He ran downstairs at a speed he had not achieved since school.

"Cobra!" said Catarina, dropping a tea towel, when he had explained, in four languages, by miming. "Cobra, in the house?"

"Under the window seat. Behind the bookcase. About a metre."

"Is a metre?"

"Possibly more."

They returned to the study in a tight formation, Catarina with the broom, Tobias with the Olivetti's dust cover held out in front of him as a shield. They moved the bookcase. There was nothing behind it. They moved the window seat. Nothing. They shook the curtains, the cushions, the blanket box and the rug. They took the books off the shelves one by one and looked inside each, which Tobias felt was a little excessive.

The snake could not be found.

Madame Fournier was summoned. She came over the garden wall with her secateurs, examined the study with a face of bottomless calm, and announced: "It is a grass snake. It is harmless. It eats frogs and mice and the occasional mouse's idea of a nice evening. You are lucky. It is good luck."

"Where is it, then?"

"Who knows? It is a snake." She shrugged with the whole waistcoat. "It is not lost. It is only not where you are looking."

They did not find it. They did not find it for the rest of the week. The neighbour from the big house up the lane, a retired Englishman with two conservatories whom Tobias had not yet officially met, came to search with a butterfly net and a sense of mission. Mara Quill, called by the neighbour's wife, searched with a torch and visible disbelief. A postman looked under the mat, which was his contribution to natural history. The pelican, consulted, declined to assist.

But everyone, ever after, felt it. In the evenings, when the light went, Tobias would sit at the Olivetti with his feet drawn carefully up onto the chair. In the kitchen, Catarina would open a cupboard and wait a long moment before she looked in. Something was in the house with them: patient, brown, a metre or more, and perfectly content.

"It does not matter," said Catarina, one night, with great dignity, stepping over a loose slipper in the hall as if it were a bomb. "We are not afraid."

"No," said Tobias, lifting his feet.

"It is only a snake."

"Yes. It's only a snake."

The snake, as far as anybody ever learned, was never found.

What Tobias did find, when they lifted the window seat and found no snake, was that the board beneath it had already been lifted. The dust was disturbed in a clean rectangle, the size of a hand. Catarina saw him see it. She put the cushion back with great care, as if the cushion were the point, and said nothing. He said nothing. The house was getting very good at that.

## Chapter 8: The Leaflet

The leaflet arrived on a Wednesday, pushed under the door by a postman who would not meet Tobias's eye.

LA MAISON DES CŒURS, it said, in a cheerful font. A residence for the recently unattached. Reserve your stay now. Rules apply.

"It is for single people," said Madame Fournier, over the wall, in the voice of a woman describing a public health measure. "On the hill. A very big house. You have forty-five days to find a partner. If you do not, you are sent for the winter to count birds in the Camargue, for the Département, without pay, in a hat."

"I'm sorry?"

"It is in the contract." She shrugged her whole waistcoat. "Lucien went in 2011. He was a widower for six weeks. He went to count the birds, and he did not come back."

Tobias looked at the lawn. On a sun lounger left by the last tenants, the pelican regarded him with an expression of ancient and ruined patience.

"Is that why..."

"I do not say," said Madame Fournier. "I only say that he stands like Lucien."

Within the hour a second note came, on thick cream paper, informing him that his single status had been recorded by the mairie and that he had been admitted to the trial period. He had until the end of the month. He was to report to the front desk by four o'clock, where he would be assigned a partner or a hat.

"They cannot do this," said Catarina, who had come out of the kitchen with her fists on her hips. "This is not legal."

"They're French," said Tobias. "I think it might be."

He spent the afternoon with the phrasebook open, preparing his defence. He did not own a trait. He had a typewriter and a recently ruined marriage and a housekeeper who had said a name that was not hers. None of these, he felt, would satisfy a clipboard. At four o'clock he put on a clean shirt, because a man on his way to spend the winter counting flamingos in a hat ought at least to be pressed, and went to the wall to ask the way.

Madame Fournier did not look up from the secateurs.

"There is no such house," she said. "Lucien was a man, and then he was dead, and the bird is a bird. I was seeing whether you would believe a contract. You did. Writers do."

He stood in the clean shirt with the leaflet in his hand. The pelican shifted on the lounger and declined to be Lucien.

"You made it up."

"I had the paper printed in the village," she said. "It was not expensive. The postman was ashamed, which I did not pay extra for." She snipped a dead head off a rose. "If a man will believe a house on a hill can send him to count birds, he will believe a publisher who says a house costs three thousand. I wished to know which sort you were."

"And?"

"You are the sort who puts on a shirt." She glanced at him, not unkindly. "Go in. The stove has been on too long. I can smell it from here."

It was the burnt pan that did it.

Tobias was in the kitchen at one in the morning, hunting for a biscuit, when the smoke alarm went off. Catarina came down the stairs in her dressing gown at the same moment. They both stopped in the doorway. The pan was black. The alarm was opinionated. Neither of them had left the ring on, or both of them had, and there was no dictionary in reach that could settle it.

She took the pan off with a cloth. He opened the window. The alarm gave up. They stood in the smoke with their mouths half open, and then Catarina began to laugh, and Tobias, helplessly, did too.

"I was going to count flamingos," he said, when he could. "In a hat."

"No," said Catarina. She looked at the pan, not at him. "Nor I."

After that the house changed its mind about them, quietly, and without a contract.

He would reach for the dictionary just as she reached for the page. She would stop on the stairs to listen to the lake at night and find him already standing at the window, listening to the same thing. Once, in the garden, they both bent for the same fallen apple, and both straightened up with it, and neither of them made a sentence of it. The apple went into the bowl. The bowl did not require a translation.

## Chapter 9: The House Next Door

The neighbours who had telephoned the police were an English couple called Nigel and Priscilla Pemberton-Hayes. They had retired to France eleven years ago to "live more simply," and had since built a conservatory, a wine cellar and a second conservatory in which to store the first.

They invited Tobias to supper on Thursday. Priscilla called it "nothing fancy, just a few friends." There were four friends, none of them French, and one Frenchman, who had come, Priscilla said, for the cheese.

"We never really mix," Nigel confided, over the sherry. "The locals are charming, but one can't tell what they're thinking."

"They're thinking about the tractor you backed into," said Priscilla, from the kitchen.

"That was one time."

It was a Thursday of marvellous, suffocating politeness. Tobias sat between a retired orthodontist and a woman who wished to discuss the royal family, and ate a vol-au-vent the size and texture of an ear.

The Frenchman sat opposite. He was about fifty, immaculate, with silver at the temples and a tie the colour of old claret, and he had arrived with a furled black umbrella on a night when there had not been a cloud over the valley for a week. He was, Nigel said, "our notaire, well, our lawyer, Edouard, did the house for us, marvellous chap, terribly busy, sits on boards." Edouard smiled at this as a man smiles at a dog that has brought him the wrong stick.

"You are the writer at the lake," he said to Tobias, across the vol-au-vents, in perfect English. "I heard there was an accident with your pages. I am so sorry. One always wonders, with writers, whether there is a copy."

"Only in my head," said Tobias.

"Ah." Edouard considered the head with polite interest, as if it were a property he might one day be asked to value. "Then I hope it is well insured."

He did not eat the cheese. At nine o'clock he looked at a thin gold watch, kissed Priscilla's hand, which made her go pink, and left, with his umbrella, into the dry night. Tobias watched him go down the path and did not know why he was watching. The orthodontist leaned over.

"Lovely tie," said the orthodontist. "Always the same one. He says claret doesn't show the claret." At ten past nine, a few minutes after the Frenchman's car had gone, Priscilla rose, tapped her glass, and announced that she was going to pop to the village hall, for a committee thing about the Christmas pantomime, and would be back at eleven. Nigel would look after everyone. Chloe, the babysitter, was upstairs with Hugo, and would be no trouble.

At twenty to ten, with a rattle of keys, Priscilla came back.

She stood in the hall in her coat and silk scarf with a face like a barometer falling. "The committee was cancelled," she said. "Mavis had a cold. I thought I'd surprise..."

She stopped.

From the top of the stairs came a thump, a muffled yelp, and the sound of someone saying "Not there, not there, mind the lamp." A door opened. Down the stairs, in the following order, came: a nineteen-year-old babysitter named Chloe, flushed to the roots of her hair and holding a pillow in front of her, and Nigel, in his shirtsleeves, with his trousers on back to front and a tea towel tied round his head like a turban.

The room went very quiet. The orthodontist put down his sherry. Somewhere on the mantelpiece, a clock considered chiming and thought better of it.

"Priscilla," said Nigel.

"Nigel."

"It's not what it looks like."

"It looks like," said Priscilla, in a voice of marmalade and steel, "my husband and the babysitter coming downstairs in a state of disorder, with a pillow."

"There's a perfectly good explanation."

"I'm sure there is. I've known you thirty years, Nigel. I'm sure it's a very good one." She unbuttoned her glove, finger by finger. "You may begin."

Nigel opened his mouth. Chloe, behind him, whispered, "Tell her, Mr Pemberton-Hayes."

"We were rehearsing!" Nigel burst out.

"Rehearsing what?"

"The pantomime! Dick Whittington! You said Mavis couldn't do the Dame this year, because of her hip, and I thought, well, someone must, and I've always had a certain flair, and Chloe was good enough to run the lines, and I was trying on the costume, you see, and the skirt got stuck, and she was helping me out of the, ah, the corset..."

He held up the tea towel. It was, on closer inspection, a very large floral bonnet. Chloe, from behind the pillow, produced a script and a pair of enormous false eyelashes.

"Not there," said Priscilla slowly. "Not there. That was the line."

"It's a very difficult scene," said Chloe in a small voice. "He has to fall off the ladder."

There was a long, ringing pause. Then the orthodontist began to laugh. The woman who liked the royal family laughed. Tobias, to his shame, was laughing already, helplessly, into a vol-au-vent. And Priscilla, who had stood in her coat through the whole of it with the stillness of a Roman senator, drew a long breath and said, with great dignity:

"Nigel. You are a terrible Dame."

"Yes," said Nigel miserably.

"The skirt's on back to front. And the orthodontist can see your knees."

"Yes, dear."

"I will be Dame," said Priscilla, "and you will be the back half of the horse." She turned to Tobias, her face quite calm. "I do apologise. It's always like this at rehearsals."

On the walk home through the dark lane Tobias passed the garden wall of the lake house and thought about it all. Three people were dead and a murderer was still at large, he thought, and the village's great scandal of the week was a man in a bonnet. It struck him as the most English thing he had ever heard.

He told Catarina about it in the kitchen. She listened with her chin on her hand.

"He was in the dress?"

"In the dress."

"And the wife, she is not angry?"

"She was horribly angry," said Tobias. "And then she wasn't. She's going to be the Dame."

Catarina considered this for a long time. "The English," she said at last, "are the only people in Europe who can be caught with the babysitter and say, it was for the pantomime, and everybody believes."

"The remarkable thing," said Tobias, "is that it was true."

"That," said Catarina, "is the most English part."

## Chapter 10: Três Marias

Her English improved in jumps, and always by theft.

She learned lovely from the postman. She learned cheers from the plumber. She learned the most important word of all by accident one afternoon, when Tobias lost three hours of work to a power cut and shouted from the study:

"Oh, bloody hell!"

She appeared in the doorway holding a feather duster. "What is bloody?"

"It's, ah. A word you use when you're annoyed."

"Bloody." She tasted it. "Is a good word. Is red, like blood. I like."

"Please don't say it to the vicar."

"What is vicar?"

By the end of the week she was saying it to the boiler, the stairs, a sparrow in the garden, and a very surprised Inspector Quill, who was passing through to take a statement. "Your Portuguese friend," said Mara, with a perfectly straight face, "has just called my car a bloody ornament. I asked her where she'd learned it. She said, and I quote, 'from the lovely man upstairs.'"

"I'll have a word with him."

"I'd hope so. It's your face he's using."

She let that sit for exactly as long as was cruel, then added: "Your Portuguese friend has a vocabulary like a sailor."

"She learns fast."

"So do you, I hear." Mara glanced at his notebook, which lay open on the porch rail. In his unmistakable scrawl: Eu sou quente. "What does that say?"

"I'm warm. I was trying to say I was warm."

"Mr Lind," said Mara kindly, "I don't speak Portuguese, but I'm fairly certain that is not how a person says that."

He found out that evening. Catarina, stirring the pot, asked him in her shining new English how he was, and he answered, with great dignity, "Eu sou quente."

She dropped the spoon. She turned the colour of a ripe tomato and then began to laugh so hard that she had to hold onto the stove.

"No, no, no," she managed. "You say, tenho calor. I have heat. Sou quente is..." She waved her hands, searching for it. "Is saying you are... how you say... very hot. Like a film star."

"Oh God."

"Is okay," said Catarina, wiping her eyes. "I think. Is a little bit true."

They stood there, the Englishman and the Portuguese housekeeper, both scarlet to the ears, neither of them saying a word, and neither of them looking away.

The power cut came back that night, as power cuts do, and this time it took the whole valley with it.

Tobias was in the study with a candle and the Olivetti. He had typed six words and deleted five, which on a typewriter is a matter of correction fluid and character. Then he heard the kitchen door, and a light step on the gravel, and went to the window.

Catarina was walking down toward the jetty with a blanket under her arm.

He took his coat. He did not decide to. His coat simply happened to be on him.

She was lying on her back at the end of the planks, wrapped in the blanket, and she did not seem surprised when he arrived. She moved over, a small movement, enough for one more person if that person was careful, and he lay down beside her, with a polite foot of cold air between them, and looked up.

He had not seen a sky like it in his life. In Leeds the sky was a sort of lid. Here there was no light for twenty miles, and the stars were not points but a crowd, hundreds and hundreds, and then thousands, and then, as his eyes learned, a pale river of them poured from one horizon to the other.

"Via Láctea," said Catarina softly.

"The Milky Way."

"The milk road." She frowned at the translation. "In English is a chocolate bar."

"In English," said Tobias, "everything is a chocolate bar."

She laughed, a quiet one, that went out across the water and did not come back. Beside them, in the dark, the lake made small private sounds. Somewhere a heron shifted its weight.

She raised an arm and drew a line in the sky.

"As Três Marias," she said. "You see? Three stars, in a line. My grandmother says they are three women who wait for a ship."

"We call them Orion's Belt."

"Orion's... cinto. A belt. For what?"

"It's a hunter. He's supposed to be a very large man with a club."

"In Portugal," said Catarina, "we have three women waiting. In England, you have a man with a club." She nodded gravely at the sky. "I think this explains many things."

He turned his head and looked at her, her profile pale against the dark, the blanket pulled up to her chin, the faint pencil line of a smile. He thought: there is a great deal I could say. He thought of the dictionary, which was in the study. He thought of the phrasebook. He thought, with desperate clarity, that no phrasebook in the world had a chapter for this.

She turned her head at the same moment and looked at him.

Neither of them said anything. Neither of them needed to. A star fell, long and white, from one end of the sky to the other, and they both drew a breath, and both said, at the same instant, in their own languages, the same thing:

"Make a wish."

"Pede um desejo."

She laughed, softly, and held out her hand, palm up, on the blanket between them. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he put his own over it, and she closed her fingers, and they lay there on the cold planks under ten thousand stars, not speaking, holding hands like two people carrying something very fragile across a very long room.

"What did you wish?" he asked at last.

"Is not allowed to say. Is the rule."

"Then I won't tell you mine."

"Good." She squeezed his hand. "I think they are the same one."

She said a poem then, the one her grandmother said to the sea when the boats were late. She said it in Portuguese, and did not translate, and he did not ask. He heard the shape of it.

Três estrelas à espera, um barco que não vem, e eu, que fico na pedra, a fazer de ninguém.

Three stars waiting. A boat that does not come. And I, on the stone, practising being no one. He learned the meaning years later. By then he had already known it.

From the far end of the jetty came a long, offended clearing of the throat. The pelican, who had been standing there in the dark the whole time, shifted its feet, turned its enormous head away with great dignity, and gazed pointedly at the lake.

"He is shy," whispered Catarina.

"He's a pelican."

"Even so," she said. "Even a pelican has manners."

## Chapter 11: Flamingos

"I am not allowed a holiday," said Catarina.

"Everybody is allowed a holiday."

"Férias." She tried the word in both languages. "In my family, a holiday is when you stay at home and the stove is lit but nobody is angry. I had one in 2019. It was a Tuesday."

"Then you're overdue," said Tobias. "Tomorrow. I'll drive."

"No," said Catarina, with the swiftness of a woman who has read the file on the gatepost. "I will drive."

The Camargue lay an hour to the south, where the Rhône spread out into the sea and the land gave up all pretence of hills. It was an unseasonably warm day for October, twenty-seven degrees by the car's thermometer, which Catarina did not trust. The sky was a hard blue lid. White horses stood knee-deep in the marsh with an air of complete indifference, rice paddies glittered like sheets of green glass, and in the salt pans the water turned a faint, impossible pink.

"What is making the water pink?"

"Algae," said Tobias. "Tiny shrimps eat the algae, and the flamingos eat the shrimps and the algae, and that's what makes the flamingos pink."

"So the pink is..." She searched. "A rumour. Passed along."

"Yes."

"I like this country," said Catarina, with great conviction. "Everyone is pink because of what they eat."

And then they came round a bend in the track and the flamingos were there. Thousands of them. They stood in the shallows on one leg, tipped over, heads upside down in the water, a vast pink parliament in the sunshine, muttering. A few lifted into the air as the car stopped, long necks stretched, legs trailing, black-tipped wings opening like lit lampshades.

Catarina got out and stood with her hand over her mouth. She did not say anything for a long time.

"Flamingos," she said finally.

"The same word," said Tobias. "In both languages."

"Then we cannot get it wrong," said Catarina, and took his hand.

They ate lunch at an inn on the water's edge, under a vine. It was the best meal Tobias had had in years. It came in small, unhurried waves: salty little clams from the sea, a gardiane of bull stewed in red wine until it fell apart at a look, black rice with a streak of orange pepper, a goat's cheese so fresh it was almost a rumour itself, and a bottle of cold rosé that sweated gently in the heat. They ate all of it. Catarina closed her eyes at the stew and said something very quietly in Portuguese that sounded like a prayer.

It was a perfect lunch, and the English family at the next table did their best to spoil it.

They were the Hargreaveses: a father in a Panama hat, a mother in a very large pair of sunglasses, and two teenage children in a state of advanced, sullen boredom. They had been complaining for twenty minutes about the heat, the flies, the prices, the bread, and the French in general, whom they regarded as a minor but persistent illness.

"Is she with him?" said Mrs Hargreaves, loudly, in the tone of a woman describing a fox in her garden. She was looking at Catarina. "She's very dark for that sort of man. Is she the maid or something?"

"Probably the guide," said Mr Hargreaves. "They always have a girl. Does she speak English, do you think?"

Catarina set down her glass. She dabbed her mouth with her napkin, rose, and walked the four steps to their table with the elegant stillness of a heron about to take a fish.

"A little," she said, in her careful, beautiful, almost entirely correct English. "Enough to hear you."

There was a very long silence. The Panama hat sank slowly toward the table. One of the teenagers made a small noise, quite possibly of joy.

"I am Catarina Vidal," she continued, pleasantly. "I am not the guide. I am not the maid. I am the woman who is going to take the last of your bread, because you do not like it." She lifted the basket off their table. "Enjoy the flies."

She returned, sat down, and buttered a roll. Tobias, who had been holding his breath for most of it, let it out as a kind of wheeze.

"That," he said, "was magnificent."

"Is not magnificent. Is only a little rude." She bit the bread and chewed, considering. "It is good bread."

At that moment, fate intervened, as fate in the south of France often does, in the form of a bird. A flamingo that had been wading in the shallows beyond the terrace for some time with a look of mild interest decided that the Hargreaveses' table was an excellent place to be, strolled up the steps with the confidence of a bailiff, and lowered its enormous bent beak into Mr Hargreaves's soup.

The scream could be heard in Arles.

"Is only a flamingo," called Catarina over her shoulder, kindly. "He is not rude. He is just pink."

By the second bottle of rosé Tobias had become convinced that the flamingos were organised. He said so, loudly, to a waiter. He said they were in formation, that they had a leader, a sergeant-flamingo with a tragic face, and that one of them, the third from the left, had the very expression of his old accountant in Harrogate. "He knows what I did with the receipts," he whispered. Catarina led him gently to the car by the elbow, murmuring in Portuguese, and the rest of the lunch has never been spoken of.

Afterwards they drove home with the windows down, sunburnt, sticky, slightly drunk on rosé, and so happy that neither of them spoke for the entire hour. At a red light, Tobias looked across at her and found her looking at him, and for a moment neither of them could say what they were both thinking.

It was the first holiday she had taken since 2019. He did not mention that it was also his.

## Chapter 12: The Mountain

They went to Aix on a Sunday, with no purpose at all, which Catarina regarded as the most decadent thing she had ever done.

It was a town built for walking. The Cours Mirabeau ran down the middle of it like a green tunnel, two rows of enormous plane trees whose branches met overhead and let the sun fall in coins; there were cafés on one side, honey-coloured mansions on the other, and fountains at every turn, one of them so furred with green moss that it looked like an animal dozing in the heat. Students on bicycles flowed past in ones and twos. An old man sat on a bench reading a newspaper from the previous Thursday with the satisfaction of someone who has never needed to know what happened next.

They ate calissons from a paper bag: little pale almond sweets in the shape of boats, so sweet that Catarina went silent for ten seconds and then announced, "These are illegal," and bought a second bag.

In the afternoon they climbed the hill to Cézanne's studio, a plain room with a great north window, a hat on a peg, a coat that looked as if its owner had only stepped out, a few withered apples on a table, and the faint smell of a man who had been very difficult to live with. The paintings on the walls were reproductions. The guide said so in the voice of a woman who had said it ten thousand times: the originals were in Paris and London and Philadelphia and anywhere else with money. A small group of tourists stood in a respectful half-circle round the apples, which were at least original, in the sense that somebody replaced them. Tobias felt that something should be said.

"Maçã," he offered, pointing.

"Maçã," agreed Catarina, delighted. "Good. Very good. And this?"

"Mulher." It was a print of a woman. He had meant to say woman.

"Mulher," she agreed, her mouth twitching. "And this?"

It was a print of a mountain. Tobias thought. He had a feeling that the word for mountain began with an m, and that Portuguese had a strong view about its vowels. "Montanha," he said, with great confidence.

"Montanha," said Catarina gravely. "Correct. And this?"

It was a print of some bathers, three of them in the foreground. Tobias looked at them for a very long time, at their pale, strange, geometric limbs.

"Banho," he said.

"Yes."

"Três... pessoas... em... banho."

"Three people in a bath," said Catarina, translating for the room, because the tourists had begun to listen. "He is saying, there are three people in a bath. It is a very modern interpretation."

"I'm doing my best."

"I know." She slipped her arm through his. "It is why I do not correct you more."

In the late afternoon the light turned amber, as it does in Provence, and began to slide from the walls like honey off a spoon. They sat on the steps of the cathedral square with the last of the calissons, watching the pigeons argue and the shadows lengthen, and Catarina said, almost to herself:

"Cézanne painted the same mountain eighty times."

"More or less. In oil and in watercolour."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I think," said Tobias, "he kept wanting to get it right."

"And did he?"

"No. That was rather the point."

She nodded slowly, leaning against his shoulder, watching the light go. She did not say anything else. She did not need to. It was the kind of silence that two people only arrive at after months of trying to find words in two languages, and then finding that the silence was a language too.

They climbed Mont Sainte-Victoire because Catarina said a thing Cézanne had painted eighty times was worth seeing from above, and they came down again because the sun was a hammer and the rock was a rock.

"I would do it again," said Catarina, on the way down.

"The climb?"

"The day." She looked back at the ridge. "Not the sermon. There was no sermon. That was the good part."

## Chapter 13: The Visitor

The call came on the Monday, while the calissons were still on the kitchen table and the sunburn was still explaining itself.

Tobias was at the sink. The house phone, which he had not known was connected, rang with the indignation of an object that has been ignored. He picked it up. The voice was the one from the hotline: cashmere, lowered onto a duke.

"Mr Lind. We understand there was a mishap at the lake. Pages, in the water. How distressing."

He did not answer. On the other end, very faintly, the same sentence was being murmured by other voices, as if the office were full of men practising being kind.

"We would be grateful," the voice said, "if what came out of the water were not read. Damp paper is unreliable. It upsets people. It has already upset one or two of them, I believe, in March."

"Who is this?"

The cashmere slipped. For one second the docker was back. "You know who it is. Put the pages away." Then the velvet came down, and the line went dead.

Catarina was in the doorway with a tea towel. She had not understood the English. She had understood his face, which was becoming her other language.

"Who?" she said.

"The prize," he said. "They know the pages came out."

She looked past him, at the jetty, the way she had looked at it from the study. She folded the towel once, very neatly, and put it down.

"Then we do not leave them on the line," she said.

They took the pages in. They closed the shutters on the lane side. Madame Fournier, told over the wall, snipped a rose and said, "Lock the gate. The postman is a gossip, and gossip is how a house dies." Tobias locked it. It was the first useful thing he had done with a key since Leeds.

Mara came on the Thursday, not for the snake.

"Two men asked for you in the village," she said, on the porch. "English writer. Papers. They had a car too low for the lane, and they did not buy anything, which in that bakery is a kind of confession." She looked at the shutters. "You've heard from someone."

"The prize. They knew about the water."

"Then someone saw her go in," said Mara. "Or someone was already watching the jetty." She closed the notebook. "Do not be heroic, Mr Lind. Heroic is how people slip."

She had used Elise's verb without noticing. He noticed. He did not say so.

Victor Hale arrived on the Friday, in a silver Porsche that was far too low for the lane. It scraped on every pothole with a noise like a rich man clearing his throat. The gate was locked. He left the car in the lane and came up on foot, smiling, as if a locked gate were a form of shyness.

He was a handsome, silver-haired man with a warm voice and a very expensive coat, and he shook Tobias's hand for slightly too long. "How's the great novel? I hear there was a mishap."

"Pages in the lake. I'm reconstructing it."

"Tragic. Do be careful with that house. The jetty is rotten. I'd hate to lose another guest."

Tobias felt the sentence like a blade laid against his skin. It was the same sentence as the telephone, wearing a better coat. But Hale was already smiling, already turning, already looking past him at Catarina, who stood in the hall holding a tray of tea and watching the publisher with an expression Tobias could not read.

"And you must be the new housekeeper. Where did you say you were from?"

Catarina's face went blank and polite. "Não percebo," she said.

Hale kept smiling. "No matter." Tobias noticed that he smiled the way other men checked their watches. He also noticed that Hale had said new. The house had not had a housekeeper in the advertisement. Someone had told him there was one.

But later, when the car had gone, scraping its sump on the way out like a man who wished to be remembered, Tobias found her at the kitchen sink with her hands clenched white on the edge of the basin, and he understood that she had understood every word.

"You know him," he said.

She did not turn round. The water ran over her wrists.

"I know the house," she said. "He is only the man who pays for it."

It was not the whole answer. It was the first one she had trusted him with. He put the envelope with the name on it back under the Olivetti, where it had been since the afternoon of the curtain, and did not take it out. Not yet. The gate was locked. The pages were in. The jetty, which had been a place for birds, was now a sentence someone else had finished for him.

Inspector Mara Quill did not take holidays. She took leave, which is different. On the Saturday she drove to Marseille, to the old harbour, on the pretext of a dentist.

She was not at the dentist. She was at a table by the window of a restaurant called Le Homard Bleu, where the tablecloths were starched, the waiters moved like surgeons, and a lobster cost a hundred and forty euros, a price printed on the menu in smaller type than the rest, in the way a bank prints its fees.

At the corner table, under a brass lamp, sat Victor Hale.

He was eating lobster. He ate it with enormous attention, cracking each claw as if it owed him money, and across the table from him a second man, silver-haired, older, with a face like a closed door, was telling him something at length. Mara could not see the face clearly. She could see the hands. They were large, patient, tanned hands, and they were buttering a roll with the unhurried precision of a man who has never been in a hurry in his life, because other people have always been in it for him.

She photographed the pair of them with her phone, as one photographs a menu.

She could not hear the conversation. She did not need to. A waiter, bringing a second bottle of white, said it for her, because he was French and could not help himself.

"Et pour la Porsche, monsieur Hale?" he asked, pouring. "Elle est prête?"

"Not yet," said Hale, with the shy, burning joy of a man who has been dreaming about a thing since boyhood. "Soon. Two more months. One more good autumn."

"Ah," said the waiter kindly. "Il faut de la patience."

"I have waited forty years," said Hale.

Mara watched him lift a claw and smile at it, and thought of eleven thousand four hundred writers sitting at kitchen tables from Dundee to Durban, each of whom had put sixty pounds in an envelope along with a hopeful short story about a lighthouse. She thought of a man on a jetty with an Olivetti. She thought of Elise Marchetti, who had once, according to a letter in the file, refused to cross a puddle because it looked ambitious. She thought of the commandant who had closed the file in April with his very good pen, and who played golf, she happened to know, on Thursday mornings at a club outside Marseille where the membership list was not published.

Some of us, thought Mara, eat lobster.

She did not finish the thought. She had never been a woman for speeches. She paid for her coffee, left a generous tip for a waiter who would never know why, and went out into the grey Mediterranean light, and made a telephone call that would take eleven weeks to come to anything at all.

On the lake, that evening, the pelican stood at the end of the jetty and did not fish. Catarina stood at the kitchen window and did not sing. Tobias sat with the pages in a stack, drying at the edges, and did not type. The house had been a place to begin again. It had remembered, overnight, what it was for.

# Part Two: The Paper Trail

## Chapter 14: The Contract

The contract came by courier, which in that valley was an event.

The courier was a young man on a motorbike who had never been up the lane before and did not intend to come up it again. He stood at the locked gate with a padded envelope held out in front of him like a summons, and waited for Tobias to walk down the gravel, and said, "Lind?" in the tone of a man who has been told the name will be difficult. Tobias signed a screen with his finger. The signature came out as a small, despairing wave. The courier looked at it, looked at Tobias, decided that it was close enough for the south of France, and left.

The envelope was thick and cream and smelled faintly of a very good office. Inside was a letter, two copies of a contract bound with a black clip, and a fountain pen. The pen was the worst part. A contract tells you what a man wants. A fountain pen tells you he has already imagined you signing it.

*My dear Tobias,* the letter said. *I was so very sorry to hear about the lake. I have been thinking about little else. I know what that book means to you, and I would like it to mean something to the rest of the world. Hale Books would be honoured to publish it. I enclose our standard terms, which you will find more than standard. Do sign and return both copies, with the typescript, at your earliest convenience. A cheque for the first half of the advance will follow by return. Warmest regards, as ever, Victor.*

The advance was forty thousand pounds.

Tobias read the number four times, the way he had read Elise's emails, and then sat down on the bottom stair with the contract in his lap. Forty thousand pounds was more than his last three novels had earned together, including the four copies in Hull. It was a new boiler in Leeds. It was a solicitor who could say *duvet* to Camilla's solicitor in a voice that would carry. It was, if he was honest, and he was trying to be, the first time in eleven years that anyone in publishing had wanted anything he had written badly enough to put a figure on it.

He was still sitting there when Catarina came in from the garden with a basket of windfalls and stopped in the hall.

"Bad letter," she said. It was not a question. She had learned to read his face before she had learned the word for envelope.

"Good letter," said Tobias. "Very good letter. That is what is bad about it."

She put down the basket. She sat on the stair beside him, a polite foot of air between them, which had been getting smaller all week, and held out her hand for the pages. He gave them to her. She did not read English contracts. Nobody reads English contracts, not even the English. But she held the first page at arm's length and frowned at it with the patience she gave to the stove, and her finger stopped on the figure.

"Quarenta mil," she said softly. "Libras."

"Yes."

"For the book that is in the lake."

"For the book I'm rebuilding."

She turned the page. She turned another. Her finger moved down the clauses the way it moved down a column of the dictionary, one line at a time, refusing to skip. At clause fourteen it stopped.

"This word," she said. "*Materials.* What is materials?"

He looked. Clause fourteen said that the Author hereby assigned to the Publisher, absolutely and without reservation, all right, title and interest in the Work and in all Materials relating to the Work, including but not limited to typescripts, manuscripts, drafts, notes, research, correspondence, recordings and documents, in whatever form and whether or not in the Author's possession, and that the Author would deliver all such Materials to the Publisher on signature and retain no copies.

He read it twice. The second time, the hall became noticeably colder, though nobody had opened the door.

"It means everything," he said. "Not just the book. The pages. My notes. The photographs on the study wall. Anything I've written down about Elise. Anything I might find."

"Whether or not in the Author's possession," said Catarina, slowly, sounding out each word as if it were a stone she was testing before she put her weight on it. "What is this?"

"It means things I don't have yet."

She looked at him. She looked at the clause. She looked, with great deliberation, at the window seat in the study above them, which they could not see from the stair, and which they both saw.

"This," she said, "is not a contract for a book. This is a contract for a house."

---

Madame Fournier read it at her kitchen table, with her reading glasses on a chain and a pot of coffee at her elbow, and a pencil, which she licked.

Her husband Henri had been a notary's clerk in Apt for forty-one years. He had read contracts the way other men read the racing results, every morning, with a pencil and a sense of grievance, and he had taught his wife to read them in the same way, because, she said, it had been that or listen to him explain them. Henri had been dead for nine years. She still read every contract that came into the valley, including the ones that were none of her business, which in her opinion were the ones most worth reading.

"Fourteen," she said, after a while, tapping it. "You have seen fourteen."

"We've seen fourteen."

"Twenty-two." She turned the page. "The Author warrants that the Work is entirely a work of the imagination and contains no reference, direct or indirect, to any real person living or dead, and shall indemnify the Publisher against any claim arising." She took off her glasses. "Your book is about a real dead woman, monsieur. You have told me so at this table. If you sign this, you swear that she is not real. And if anybody says that she was, you pay."

"Yes," said Tobias. "I'd noticed that one."

"Thirty-one. You may not discuss the terms of this agreement, or the Work, or the Materials, with any person, for any reason, in perpetuity." She licked the pencil and drew a small, neat line under *in perpetuity*. "Henri used to say that a contract with *in perpetuity* in it was a contract with a cemetery in it."

"And thirty-eight?" said Catarina, who had followed every word on the faces at the table if not in the language.

Madame Fournier found thirty-eight. She read it, and for the first time something like pleasure crossed her face, the pleasure of a woman who has found the hair in the soup and been proved right about the chef.

"Thirty-eight," she said. "Notices. All notices under this agreement shall be sent to the Publisher's registered office." She turned the contract round so they could see the address. "Read it."

Tobias read it. It was an address in Marseille. A street off the Canebière, a number, a floor.

"I don't understand," he said. "Hale Books is in London. Bloomsbury. I've been there. There's a brass plate and a receptionist called Philippa who hates me."

"Perhaps the receptionist is in London," said Madame Fournier. "The company is in Marseille." She went to the dresser, opened a drawer that seemed to contain the entire administrative history of the valley, and came back with a leaflet. It was glossy, and blue, and had a gold seal on the front. *The Verrane Foundation Award for Unpublished Fiction. Entries now open.* On the back, in very small type, under the bank details for the sixty-pound fee, was the Foundation's address.

It was the same street. The same number. The same floor.

The three of them looked at it for some time. Out in the garden the pelican, who had come over the wall in the hope of bread, made a noise like a door that needs oil.

"Where did you get that?" said Tobias at last.

"It came in the post in July. It comes every July. It came to Henri for six years after he was dead, because one year he entered a story about a goat." Madame Fournier put her glasses back on. "It was a very bad story. It was highly commended."

---

He did not sign it.

He also did not tear it up, though he wanted to, and though Catarina, who had a practical view of documents that threatened people she was fond of, had already picked up the matches. He rang Mara instead, from Madame Fournier's telephone, because he had begun to suspect that the house phone was the kind of object that listened.

Mara listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of verdict.

"Don't sign it," she said, when he had finished.

"I wasn't going to."

"Don't refuse it either." There was a pause on the line, and a sound that might have been a pen being tapped against teeth. "A man who sends you a contract like that is telling you what he's afraid of. If you say no, he'll find another way to be afraid. If you say *maybe*, he'll wait. Waiting men make mistakes. They ring people. They get in their cars."

"You want me to string along a man who may have killed three people."

"I want you to write him a letter," said Mara. "You're a writer. Write him a long, grateful, slightly vain letter in which you say you are honoured and that you have one or two small queries about the royalty on audio, and that you'll need a few weeks to put the typescript in order, because the water damage was so very bad. Make it boring. Make it the most boring letter a publisher has ever received. Nothing makes a crook feel safer than an author asking about audio."

"And meanwhile?"

"Meanwhile, Mr Lind," said Mara, "I am not working on this case, because there isn't one. My commandant reminded me of that on Monday, in front of witnesses, in a voice I would describe as Burgundian. So I have nothing to say to you about Victor Hale, and I would be very grateful if you would keep sending me nothing in particular."

"Is that why you're whispering?"

"I'm in the ladies'," said Mara, and rang off.

---

He wrote the letter that evening, at the Olivetti, with Catarina sitting on the window seat behind him, reading the phrasebook and pretending not to read over his shoulder.

It was, in its way, the most difficult thing he had written since he came to France. He had to sound delighted. He had to sound greedy, a little, because a writer who is offered forty thousand pounds and is not greedy is a writer who is suspicious. He had to sound like a man who had forgotten Elise. He wrote *I can't tell you what this means* three times and crossed it out twice. He asked about the audio royalty. He asked, in a postscript, whether Hale Books would consider a different cover from the last one, which had made his detective look like a man selling double glazing. He thought this was a convincing touch. He had wanted to ask it for years.

"Read," said Catarina, when he pulled the sheet out of the roller.

He read it to her, slowly. She listened with her chin on her knees.

"It is very good," she said when he had finished. "It is a very good bad letter. He will believe it."

"You think so?"

"He believes everybody is like him." She held out her hand for the page, and he gave it to her, and she read it again on her own, moving her lips over the long words. At the end she frowned. "Why do you say *my dear Victor*? He is not your dear. He is not anybody's dear."

"It's what you say. It's a convention. It doesn't mean anything."

"In Portuguese, *querido* means something." She folded the letter along the lines he had made, very precisely. "This is the problem with English. You say *dear* to a man who kills your friend. Then when you mean it, to someone, what is left to say?"

He opened his mouth. He discovered there was nothing in it. Outside, the pond lay flat and dark under a sky that was making up its mind about rain.

"I suppose," he said carefully, "you would have to say it in another language."

Catarina looked at the letter in her hands for a long moment. Then she gave it back to him, and stood up, and went to the door, and stopped there with one hand on the frame.

"Then you will have to learn one," she said, without turning round, and went downstairs.

He sat with the letter for a while. Then he typed the envelope, and stamped it, and put it on the hall table for the morning. Under the Olivetti, the old envelope with the name on it had acquired a second line. He looked at it. What was there, in his own handwriting, in pencil, was a single word he had looked up that afternoon and not yet dared to say aloud.

*Querida.*

---

The second envelope came on the Saturday. It was not from Hale.

It was from a firm of solicitors in Leeds, in a long white envelope with a window, and it informed him, in language as smooth as cold butter, that Mrs Camilla Lind wished to proceed with the sale of the matrimonial home, that a valuation had been obtained, and that her client would be grateful for his written consent within twenty-eight days. A copy of the valuation was enclosed. It valued the house at slightly less than Tobias had paid for it, and the duvet, he noticed, was not mentioned at all.

He read it standing in the kitchen. Catarina was at the stove. She did not ask. She took one look at the window in the envelope, which was the same in every language, and moved the soup to the back of the stove where it could think about itself, and came and stood beside him.

"From England," she said.

"From my wife's solicitor. She wants to sell the house."

"Your house."

"Our house. It was. The one with the jacket on the banister." He put the letter down on the table, beside Hale's contract, which was still lying there because nobody had wanted to touch it. The two documents looked at each other across the oilcloth like two cats who have been introduced too quickly. "Forty thousand pounds," he said, to nobody. "It would buy her out. I could keep it. I could go home."

It was a stupid thing to have said aloud. He knew that before he had finished saying it. He heard it go into the kitchen and settle there, like smoke from a pan.

Catarina did not say anything. She stood beside him for another moment, very still. Then she went back to the stove, and moved the soup to the front again, and stirred it for a long time, much longer than it needed. When she spoke, it was to the pot.

"Is a nice house?"

"It's a perfectly ordinary house. It's brick. It's next to a man who mends lawnmowers in his front garden on Sundays."

"And you like it."

"I liked it." He looked at the contract. "I don't think I'd like it if I'd bought it with this."

She stopped stirring. She tapped the spoon twice on the rim of the pot, exactly as Madame Fournier had tapped the gatepost with her secateurs, and laid it across the top.

"Good," she said. "Then we will not buy it with this. We will put this," she picked up Hale's contract between finger and thumb, as if it were something the pelican had brought in, "in the biscuit tin, with Odile. And the other one, from your wife, you will answer later, when you are not hungry. A man should never sell a house when he is hungry. My grandmother says this. She sold a house once before lunch and it was a disaster."

"What happened?"

"She bought it back after lunch," said Catarina, "for more money." She put a bowl in front of him. "Eat."

He ate. The soup was green and tasted of something he could not name, and of garlic, and of the kind of patience that is not the same thing as waiting. After a while Catarina sat down opposite him with her own bowl, and they ate in a silence that was not uncomfortable, and when he looked up she was watching him, and did not look away, and neither did he.

On the hall table, the boring letter to Victor Hale waited for the post. In the biscuit tin next door, under a photograph of Lucien on one leg, a contract that wanted everything waited for nothing at all. And in the study the Olivetti, which had typed forty thousand pounds' worth of nothing that week, stood in the last of the light with a clean sheet in the roller, as if it had something to say and was too well brought up to start.

## Chapter 15: Highly Commended

"We will enter it," said Catarina.

It was the Monday, and the leaflet with the gold seal lay on the kitchen table between the bread and the dictionaries, where it had been since Madame Fournier had handed it over the wall with the words "for your research, and for Henri's goat."

"Enter what?"

"The prize." She tapped the seal. "Sixty pounds. We write a story. We send. We see what comes back. If the prize is real, we lose sixty pounds. If the prize is a lie, we have it in writing." She considered. "In my village, when a man says his cow gives twenty litres, you do not argue. You buy one litre."

Tobias looked at the leaflet. *The Verrane Foundation Award for Unpublished Fiction. First prize £10,000 and publication. All entries read by our distinguished panel.* The distinguished panel was not named. The photograph showed a fountain pen lying across a sheet of cream paper, in a shaft of light, with nothing written on the paper. He thought it was probably the most honest thing in the leaflet.

"It would have to be a story they'd reject," he said slowly. "If it's a real prize, a story that no real judge could possibly praise."

"A very bad story."

"A catastrophically bad story."

Catarina's face lit up with the particular joy of a woman who has spent two months watching a man fail to write one good sentence a day, and has just been offered the chance to help him write a bad one on purpose.

"I know about bad stories," she said. "My aunt writes poems for funerals. Nobody has ever died who did not deserve better."

---

They wrote it that evening, at the kitchen table, with a bottle of the Camargue rosé and the Olivetti brought down specially, and it was the happiest three hours either of them had spent since the flamingos.

It was called *The Lighthouse Keeper's Gull*. It concerned a lighthouse keeper named Ambrose, who lived alone on a rock off the coast of Cornwall, and who fell in love with a herring gull named Philippa. Tobias contributed Philippa. He did not explain why. Catarina contributed the weather, which was described in every single paragraph, usually twice, and always as "angry", "very angry" or "angry like a mother-in-law". Tobias contributed adverbs. He had spent twenty years removing them from his own work, and he poured them into Ambrose's life with the abandon of a man emptying a cellar. Ambrose gazed longingly, sighed wistfully, and paced restlessly. Philippa screeched meaningfully. The sea crashed thunderously, angrily, like a mother-in-law.

"We need a plot twist," said Catarina, who had read a great many detective novels in Portuguese translation and held strong views.

"Philippa is actually a seal."

"No. Better." She thought, with her pencil against her lip. "Ambrose is actually the gull."

"How can Ambrose be the gull? He's the lighthouse keeper."

"He is the gull," said Catarina firmly, "the whole time. And the lighthouse is his mother."

Tobias typed it. He typed it with tears running down his face. The Olivetti, which had been built to type Italian poetry and had been asked in its time to type a great many sentences it did not respect, typed *and the lighthouse was his mother all along* without a single jam, which he felt showed character.

It was at this moment that Hugo Pemberton-Hayes arrived, unannounced, through the kitchen door, holding a jar.

"I've brought you a scorpion," he said. "It's quite small. It was in our cellar. Mummy says I'm not allowed to keep it in the house, so I thought you could have it, because you've got a snake already." He put the jar on the table, between the rosé and the gull. The scorpion inside was pale yellow and the length of a paperclip and looked, Tobias thought, much less dangerous than the story. "What are you writing?"

"A very bad short story," said Tobias. "On purpose."

Hugo picked up the top sheet. He read it with the expression of a nine-year-old who has read *Poisonous Plants of the Mediterranean* twice for pleasure. He read the second sheet. He turned to the third, where Ambrose was revealed to be a gull, and his face went through several stages, like weather over a hill.

"This can't happen," he said at last.

"It's fiction," said Tobias.

"No, I mean on page two he's holding a cup of tea. Gulls haven't got hands." He put the pages down with great kindness. "And herring gulls don't live in lighthouses, they live on rubbish tips, mostly. And it says Cornwall, but on page one the sun sets over the sea on the east side, which is wrong unless the lighthouse is in Kent."

Catarina looked at Tobias. Tobias looked at Catarina.

"Leave it all in," they said, together.

Hugo considered them both with real pity. "I'll take the scorpion back," he said. "I don't think you're ready."

---

They sent it on Tuesday morning, in a brown envelope, from the post office in the village, where the postmistress weighed it as if it might contain explosives and then, when she saw the address on the envelope, gave Tobias a look of such profound disappointment that he nearly asked for it back. Tobias paid the sixty pounds by card, from his own account, with his own name, which was Mara's idea, by telephone, from the ladies', because, she said, if this was going to be evidence it ought to be clean evidence, and nothing is cleaner than a writer paying to be lied to.

Then they waited. It was the first time they had waited for anything together, and they did it badly.

Tobias waited by rewriting chapter seven three times. Catarina waited by cleaning the oven, which had not been cleaned since Henri Fournier's lifetime, and which turned out, at the back, to contain a small cast-iron saucepan that Madame Fournier said she had been accusing her sister of stealing since 1998. They walked to the village every morning to meet the post van. The postman came to expect them. On the fourth day he began, without being asked, to shake his head from the end of the lane, so that they would not have to walk all the way.

On the ninth day the envelope came.

It was cream, and thick, and had the gold seal embossed in the corner, and it had been posted in Marseille. Tobias opened it on the lane, with Catarina reading over his shoulder and the postman, who had stopped his van to watch, reading over hers.

*Dear Mr Lind,*

*It is with the greatest pleasure that we write to inform you that your story,* The Lighthouse Keeper's Gull, *has been selected by our distinguished panel as* Highly Commended *in this year's Verrane Foundation Award for Unpublished Fiction.*

*Your story was singled out for its emotional depth, its vivid evocation of place, and its daring structural invention. Our panel was particularly moved by its ending.*

*As a Highly Commended author, you are now eligible for inclusion in* The Verrane Anthology of New Voices, *a prestigious hardback edition distributed to leading publishers and literary agents throughout the English-speaking world. To secure your place, kindly remit the editorial and production contribution of £395 within fourteen days. Places are strictly limited.*

*Warmest congratulations on your achievement.*

The signature was illegible. It was printed, not signed. It had been printed, Tobias noticed, slightly crooked.

"Daring structural invention," said Catarina, in a voice of enormous satisfaction. "The lighthouse is his mother."

"Particularly moved by its ending," said Tobias. "They didn't read it. Nobody read it. Nobody has ever read anything." He found, to his surprise, that he was not laughing. "Elise read things. Elise read everything. She read the acknowledgements. She once rang me at midnight because I'd thanked my editor twice."

The postman, who had followed the letter with intense interest, if not the English, asked in French what it said. Catarina told him, in her careful French, that the monsieur had won a prize for a story about a seagull.

The postman's face fell. He put one hand on Tobias's shoulder. "Ah, non," he said, with real sympathy. "Pas eux. Ma belle-mère a gagné aussi. Trois fois."

Not them. My mother-in-law won too. Three times.

---

The postman's mother-in-law was not the only one.

That night, on Madame Fournier's grandson's laptop, which had come out from under the lace cloth for the purpose and still smelled of crisps, Tobias found the forum. It was a writers' forum, a quiet, earnest place full of people who signed their posts with the titles of their unpublished novels, and it had a thread that had been running for six years. The thread was called *Verrane — anyone else Highly Commended???*

Everyone was. A retired headmaster in Fife had been Highly Commended for a story about his late wife's garden. A nurse in Leicester had been Highly Commended twice. A woman in Cape Town had been Highly Commended, had paid the £395, and had received, eleven months later, a hardback anthology of four hundred pages in which her story appeared on page 311, in eight-point type, between a poem about a horse and a story that, as far as she could tell, had been printed upside down. She had sent it to six literary agents. None of them had heard of it. One had written back to say that he had received four copies that year from different hopeful authors and was using them to level a bookcase.

There were hundreds of posts. Some were angry. Most were not. Most were from people who had been told, for the first time in their lives, that something they had made at a kitchen table late at night was *vivid* and *daring* and *moving*, and who had wanted very badly for it to be true, and had paid, some of them twice, some of them every year, for the privilege of believing it for a few more weeks.

Catarina read over his shoulder, slowly, with her finger on the screen, which left marks he did not wipe off.

"Is it a crime?" she said at last. "To tell people they are good?"

"It's a crime to take their money for a prize that doesn't exist. It's fraud. Obtaining money by deception." He scrolled. "First prize ten thousand pounds. Nine years. Nobody on this forum has ever heard of anyone winning it."

"And eleven thousand people."

"Eleven thousand four hundred, according to Elise. At sixty pounds each." He did the sum in his head, and then, because it was too large to trust, on the back of the envelope. "Six hundred and eighty-four thousand pounds. And that's before the anthology."

Catarina was quiet for a while. Then she reached past him and scrolled back up to the woman in Cape Town, and the photograph she had posted of the anthology, open at page 311.

"She put a ribbon in it," Catarina said. "To mark the page. Look."

He looked. There was a ribbon, a thin red one, the kind that comes with a box of chocolates, laid carefully across her story, so that the book would fall open in the right place for anyone who picked it up. The picture had been taken on a kitchen table. There was a teapot at the edge of the frame.

"This," said Catarina, "is why your friend went to the jetty." She stood up and went to the window and stood with her back to him. "Not the money. Not the house. Not even her mother's books. The ribbon."

He did not answer. He did not have to. He printed the thread, all forty-one pages of it, on Madame Fournier's printer-scanner, which complained bitterly but did not turn anything into cats, and put it in a folder with the Highly Commended letter and the receipt for sixty pounds. On the front of the folder, in his best capitals, which were now very nearly as good as hers, he wrote: NOT ONLY PAPER.

---

There was one more thing to do, and it was Catarina who insisted on it.

"You ring them," she said, the next morning. "The telephone. The man with the voice like a blanket. You say you have won. You say you are very happy. You ask how to pay."

"Why?"

"Because a man who is happy talks too much." She put the house phone in front of him, then thought better of it, took it away, and brought Madame Fournier's cordless instead, carried over the wall wrapped in a tea towel as if it might catch cold. "And because I want to hear him."

He rang. The voice answered on the second ring, the way it always did, cashmere being lowered onto a duke.

"Welcome to the Verrane Award, where every writer is a winner."

Behind it, faint and regular as a sea, came the murmur of the other voices, the hundred velvet men saying the same thing.

"Hello, yes," said Tobias, in the voice of a man who has been Highly Commended and has told his mother. "My name is Lind. I've just heard. About my story. *The Lighthouse Keeper's Gull.* I'm so terribly thrilled. I wondered if I might pay the contribution by bank transfer, as I'm abroad at the moment, in France, and..."

"Of course, sir. Many congratulations. A remarkable piece, I understand. Our panel was quite overcome." The voice did not hesitate on the title. It did not hesitate on anything. "If you will hold for one moment, I shall find you the details."

There was a click, and music: a string quartet playing something that wanted to be Vivaldi. Catarina leaned in very close to the receiver, so close that her hair brushed his cheek, and he held very still, partly for the call and partly not.

The music stopped. Not cleanly. Somebody had put a hand over a receiver who did not know how to put a hand over a receiver, and on the other end of the line, in a room in Marseille, in the flat bark of the docks, a man said to somebody else:

"Fan de chichourle, c'est encore l'Anglais. Le rigolo. Celui du phare."

The hand came off. The cashmere came back.

"I do apologise for the delay, sir. Our sort code is..."

Tobias wrote it down. He thanked the voice warmly. He said he would transfer the money that afternoon. He put the phone down and looked at Catarina, who had straightened up and was looking at the window with the expression of a heron that has seen the fish.

"What did he say? In the middle. I got *the Englishman* and *lighthouse*."

"He said, it's the Englishman again. The funny one. The one with the lighthouse." She frowned. "And before. *Fan de chichourle.*"

"What does that mean?"

"Nothing. It means nothing. It is what old men say in Marseille when they are surprised. Like your *blimey*. My cousin's husband is from Marseille. He says it to the television." She turned round. "Nobody says it who is not from there. Nobody says it who is under fifty. And nobody," she added, with great satisfaction, "says it who is a duke."

"So the voice is one man."

"The voice is one man," said Catarina, "from Marseille, more than fifty years old, who says *encore*. Encore means he has talked about you before. To someone." She picked up the folder that said NOT ONLY PAPER and held it against her chest. "We go to Marseille."

"Mara said not to be heroic."

"We will not be heroic. We will take the train." She was already looking for her coat. "Heroic is a car."

## Chapter 16: The Voice

They took the slow train from Aix, because Catarina said that if they were going to arrive in Marseille they should arrive properly, with time to change their minds.

Neither of them changed their minds. They sat opposite each other in a carriage that smelled of warm plastic and oranges, with the folder on the table between them and a paper bag of calissons left over from the Sunday, and watched Provence go past the window in long dry strokes: pines, limestone, a vineyard, a village on a hill like a pile of bread, a man on a tractor who raised one hand to the train as if it were an old friend who never stopped.

"What do we say?" said Tobias, somewhere after Gardanne. "When we find him. If we find him."

"We say good morning."

"And then?"

"And then we see what kind of man he is." Catarina took a calisson and examined it as if it, too, might be a witness. "In Porto, when I teach, there is a boy who steals the chalk. Every day, the chalk is gone. I do not shout. I do not ask who. I say good morning to every child, one by one, at the door. The boy with the chalk cannot say good morning. He looks at his shoes." She ate the calisson. "A thief cannot say good morning."

Tobias looked at her.

"You were a teacher?"

She went a little pink, which happened to her more often now and which he had stopped pretending not to notice.

"Seven years. Primary school, in Gaia, across the river from Porto. Children of six and seven. I teach them to read." She looked out of the window. "Then the school closed. Not enough children, the Câmara said. They put them on a bus to a bigger school, and the bigger school had enough teachers already. So." She shrugged, the small, complete Portuguese shrug that contained a whole municipal budget. "I work for Fernanda in the restaurant. I carry plates. Then Inês telephones in March, at night, and I come to France and carry plates for a man in a lake house instead."

"You taught children to read."

"Is why the machine was stupid and I was not." She smiled at the window. "Every day for seven years I read handwriting worse than your typewriter. A boy called Rui made his letter *a* like a small dog lying down. I can read anything."

He thought of her at the kitchen table on the first morning, with the ruined pages laid out on every towel the house possessed, drying them one by one. He had thought it was patience. It had been a professional opinion.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"You did not ask." She looked at him then, directly, with her serious dark eyes. "Nobody asks the housekeeper what she was before. It is not a criticism. It is only true."

He found that he had nothing to say to that, which was becoming a pattern on trains, and also that he was ashamed, which was not new but was at least honest. He reached across the table and took the last calisson, and broke it carefully in two, and gave her the bigger half.

"What were you before?" he said.

She took the half. She held it for a moment.

"Later," she said. "When we are not going to meet a criminal. It is a long story, and it has no murders in it, and you will be bored."

"I won't be bored."

"No," she agreed, and ate the calisson. "You will not."

Marseille arrived all at once, the way it does, at the top of the great white staircase of the Gare Saint-Charles, with the whole city tipped out below them to the sea: roofs, cranes, a thousand washing lines, the gold Virgin on her hill above the old port, and beyond everything a flat bright light that was not quite blue and not quite silver and that Tobias, who was from Leeds, could only describe as expensive.

"It is like Porto," said Catarina, stopping at the top of the steps. "But it shouts."

The address on the contract was a street off the Canebière, near the market at Noailles, and they walked there down through streets that smelled in turn of cumin, fish, coffee, mint, diesel and something frying that Catarina insisted on stopping for and that turned out to be panisse, chickpea fritters, sold hot in a paper cone by a man who called her *ma belle* and Tobias *the cousin*. The building was tall and narrow, with a bakery on the ground floor and a row of brass bells beside a heavy green door. On the third bell, in a small engraved plate that had been polished more recently than the others, it said FONDATION VERRANE. Under it, on a strip of printed paper stuck on with tape, it said HALE BOOKS (MARSEILLE). Under that, on a second strip of tape, in biro, somebody had written PAS DE LIVRAISONS. No deliveries.

"Good morning," said Catarina to the bell, and pressed it.

Nothing happened. She pressed it again. Somewhere above them, a window opened, and a man leaned out. He was perhaps sixty, large, with a magnificent head of white hair swept back like a conductor's, a striped shirt open at the neck, and a cigarette, and he looked down at them with the expression of a man who has been interrupted in the middle of something important, such as lunch.

"C'est fermé," he called. "Le jeudi, c'est fermé."

"It is Wednesday," Catarina called back, in French.

The man considered this. He took the cigarette out of his mouth.

"Le mercredi aussi," he said.

Tobias stepped forward into the street, so that the man could see him clearly, and called up, in the most English voice he possessed, the voice of a man who has been Highly Commended and has told his mother:

"Good morning! I'm so sorry to bother you. My name is Lind. I rang last week, about the lighthouse."

The effect was remarkable. The man in the window went perfectly still. Then, very slowly, as if a tap had been turned somewhere inside him, the colour drained from his face, all the way from the magnificent hair to the open neck of the shirt.

"Fan de chichourle," he said, quite quietly, to nobody.

Then he closed the window. A minute later, the green door buzzed.

---

The office of the Verrane Foundation Award for Unpublished Fiction was a single room on the third floor, at the back, with a window onto a light well full of pigeons. It contained a desk, two chairs, a filing cabinet, a kettle, an ashtray in the shape of the Vieux-Port, eleven grey postal sacks, and a telephone switchboard of a type Tobias had last seen in a film about the Second World War, wired to a cassette deck on which a tape was going round and round. From the tape, very softly, came the murmur of a hundred velvet voices saying *where every writer is a winner*.

There were no other people. There had never been any other people. The hundred voices were all the same voice, recorded on a cassette and played behind the telephone like the sea in a shell, and the voice belonged to the man with the white hair, who stood in the middle of the room with his back to the pigeons and his hands spread in front of him, as if he were about to be shot, or applauded.

"Raymond Coste," he said, in an English that was ninety per cent Marseille and ten per cent old films. "Actor. I make the voices. Only the voices. I swear it on the head of my mother, who is alive, and who would kill me."

"We are not police," said Catarina.

"Then why are you here?"

"Because he won," said Catarina, pointing at Tobias. "A prize. For a very bad story. Highly Commended."

Raymond Coste looked at Tobias. He looked at Catarina. Then, to their great surprise, he sat down heavily in one of the two chairs, put his face in his large hands, and began to laugh, a deep, helpless, rumbling laugh that went on for some time and shook his shoulders and ended in a cough.

"The gull," he said, when he could. "The gull who is the keeper. And the lighthouse, his mother. Mon Dieu. I read it. I read it to my wife. She cried. She cried laughing, monsieur. Thirty years I am married, I never see her cry like this, not even at the funeral of her brother, who was a very funny man." He wiped his eyes. "Everybody who comes in, I read the first page. It is my only pleasure. Most are sad. Some are good. Yours, monsieur, is the worst thing I have ever read in my life, and I have been in a television series about a fisherman for eleven years."

"Thank you," said Tobias, who did not know what else to say.

"And then I put it in the sack," said Raymond, the laugh going out of his face, "with the others. And I write the letter. *Highly Commended.* The same letter. Every one. I print it in the morning, five hundred at a time." He gestured at the postal sacks, which, Tobias now saw, were not empty. They were full. They were full of envelopes, brown and white and manila, thousands of them, with stamps from everywhere, opened and stuffed back in. "Everyone is Highly Commended. Every one. Nine years."

The room was very quiet. The tape went round. *Where every writer is a winner.*

"Who reads them?" said Tobias. "The panel. The distinguished panel."

"There is no panel." Raymond said it simply, without drama, as a man states the price of fish. "There is me, and the kettle, and the bank. The money comes. The money goes. On Tuesday the Englishman with the Porsche comes for the cheques and the post from the bank, and he signs, and he does not sit down. Once in a year, a lawyer comes." He touched his own neck, just below the knot of an invisible tie. "Very chic. Very polite. A tie like a stain of wine. He looks at the sacks. He asks me, Raymond, does anybody complain? I say, monsieur, who complains about being commended? He says good, and he goes. He pays me in cash, in an envelope, at Christmas, with a box of calissons." Raymond looked at the paper bag in Catarina's hand, from which the smell of almonds was still faintly rising. "From Aix. Always from Aix."

Catarina put the bag behind her back.

"You knew it was a lie," said Tobias.

Raymond Coste looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the sacks.

"Monsieur," he said, "I am an actor. For forty years I tell lies on a stage and people pay me. This is the same. Only, there is no stage." He shrugged, and the shrug, unlike Catarina's, contained nothing at all. "I tell myself that. Every day I tell myself that. Then I read the letters. A woman in Australia writes, thank you, my husband is dead, I wrote this for him, now I know it is good. A man in Scotland, eighty-one, his first story. I put them in the sack. I write *Highly Commended*." He picked up a letter from the top of the nearest sack and held it out to Tobias without looking at it. "And in February a woman comes up those stairs. A woman with short hair and red glasses, very thin, very angry, very English. Not a writer. An editor. She stands where you are standing. She reads a letter from a sack, like you are going to do now. She does not shout. She cries, monsieur. Only a little. Then she asks me my name. She writes it in a little book. She says, Monsieur Coste, you are not the one I want, but you will have to tell the truth about the ones I want, when it is time. And I say yes. I say yes to her."

"Elise," said Tobias. He found that his voice had gone somewhere and came back without permission.

"I do not know her name. She did not say. She said, I will come back." Raymond looked at the window, at the pigeons. "She did not come back. In March I read in the newspaper there is an Englishwoman, an editor, drowned in a lake near Apt. There is a photograph. Short hair. Red glasses." He spread his hands again, the gesture of a man who is about to be shot, or applauded, and expects neither. "Since then, monsieur, I make the voices a little worse. Every week, a little worse. I hope somebody will hear."

"Someone did," said Catarina.

Raymond turned to look at her, and something in her face made him sit up a little straighter, the way the boy with the chalk had perhaps once sat up when she said good morning.

"The Englishman rang," she said, "and you put your hand on the telephone and said, *c'est encore l'Anglais*. To who? Who is in this room, when you say encore?"

The colour that had come back into Raymond's face went out of it again.

"Nobody," he said. "Nobody is in the room. I say it to the telephone." He looked at the switchboard. "The other telephone. On Tuesday, the Englishman with the Porsche telephones me from the car. He asks, any calls? I tell him. The funny Englishman rang again, with the lighthouse. He laughs. Then on Friday he telephones again and he does not laugh. He says, Raymond, what is the name of the funny Englishman? And I tell him." He swallowed. "I am sorry. I tell him Lind. And he says a word, in English, that I do not know, and he hangs up."

There was a silence in the little room. A pigeon landed on the windowsill outside and looked in at them with one orange eye, and decided it wanted nothing to do with any of it, and left.

"Monsieur Coste," said Tobias slowly. "If somebody asked you, properly, a magistrate, in a courtroom, with a policewoman who does not like her commandant, would you say what you've just said to us?"

Raymond Coste looked at the sacks for a long time. He looked at the tape going round. He looked at the ashtray in the shape of the old port, and at his own large hands.

"My mother," he said at last, "has told me since I am six years old that one day I will be in a courtroom." He stood up, and reached over, and pressed a button on the cassette deck, and the hundred velvet voices stopped in the middle of the word *winner*. In the silence the light well seemed suddenly very large. "She did not think I would be the witness."

They left with a carrier bag. In it were two hundred Highly Commended letters, chosen at random by Catarina from the top of eleven sacks, a printout of the Foundation's bank account details, which Raymond printed without being asked, and a cassette tape in a plastic case on which Raymond had written, in green felt-tip, *TOUTES LES VOIX. RAYMOND.* All the voices.

"For the Inspector," he said at the green door. "So she can hear that they are only me." He hesitated. "And please. If she can. Tell the woman with the red glasses I said yes."

Catarina took his hand in both of hers, and said good morning to him, though it was nearly two in the afternoon, and Raymond Coste, actor, sixty-one, who had been in a television series about a fisherman for eleven years, looked at his shoes.

---

They had time before the train. They walked down to the old port because Catarina wanted to see the sea, and because neither of them wanted to sit in a station with a carrier bag that seemed to have become heavier on the stairs. The fish market was over for the day. Gulls were cleaning up after it with the thoroughness of an accountant. Along the quay the restaurants were setting out their tables for the evening, and outside one of them, which had starched cloths and brass lamps and a blue lobster painted on its sign, Catarina stopped to read the menu.

She read it all the way down. At the bottom she stopped.

"A lobster," she said, "is one hundred and forty euros."

"It's a very good lobster, I expect."

"Is a lobster." She stood with her hands in her coat pockets, looking at the starched cloths through the glass. "My father catches lobsters. In Afurada. Eighteen years. He sells them to a man on the quay for the price of a lunch. The man sells them to a man in Lisbon. The man in Lisbon sells them to a man here." She turned away from the window. "Everybody is pink because of what they eat."

They went back up the great staircase to the station as the light turned gold over the roofs. The train was full of students and a family with a dog in a bag. Tobias and Catarina found two seats side by side, not opposite, which had not happened before, and sat in them with the carrier bag on Tobias's knees and the folder that said NOT ONLY PAPER on Catarina's, and somewhere after Gardanne, in the soft, rocking, orange dark, Catarina fell asleep against his shoulder.

He did not move for an hour and forty minutes.

At Aix she woke, and sat up, and looked at him, and saw that his arm had gone completely dead and that he had not moved it, and understood.

"Your arm," she said.

"It's fine."

"It is not fine. It is a dead arm."

"It's a very happy dead arm," said Tobias, and then heard himself, and went red to the ears, in the dark, on a train, at the age of forty-six.

Catarina looked at him for a long moment. Then she took the dead arm in both hands, and rubbed it, briskly, from the shoulder to the wrist, the way you would rub the arm of a child who has come in from the snow, until the blood came back into it in a thousand furious needles and he yelped. She did not let go when it had come back. She held his hand, quite firmly, in her lap, on top of the folder, for the rest of the way to the car.

"In Portuguese," she said, as they walked across the dark car park, "we say *braço dormente*. A sleeping arm. Not dead. Only sleeping."

"That's much better," said Tobias. "That's a much more hopeful language."

"Yes," said Catarina. "I have been telling you."

## Chapter 20: The Photograph

It was Catarina herself who told him in the end, and it took the whole of one long evening.

She sat him at the kitchen table, put the dictionaries between them, and laid a photograph beside them. A younger woman with her smile, standing on this same jetty in summer. Tobias, trying to be helpful, drew her on the back of an envelope. Catarina looked at the drawing for a long moment and said, "This is not a woman. This is a lamppost with an opinion."

"Inês," she said. "Minha irmã."

He had the name already, on an envelope under the Olivetti. He did not say so. A man does not spend a thing he was not given.

Word by painful word, with the dictionary and the drawings, the story came out in a mixture of English and Portuguese that neither of them could have spoken in September.

"My sister, she work here. Two year. Para o Hale. In March, she telephone me. Night. She is..." Catarina searched. "Assustada. Afraid. She say, I saw something. I cannot stay. Do not tell anyone. Then she is gone."

"She saw Elise," Tobias said slowly. "She saw what happened at the jetty."

Catarina nodded. Tears stood in her eyes and she did not wipe them.

"You came to find her. That's why you took the job."

"Sim." She laid her hand flat on the photograph. "I do not... confio. Trust. I do not trust this house."

He reached across the table and covered her hand with his. Neither of them moved for a long time.

"Then we will find her," he said.

She did not translate it. She did not need to. The sentence had already been spent, once, under a falling star. This one could wait its turn.

They worked in the study from then on, side by side.

Tobias rebuilt the manuscript chapter by chapter. Catarina sat opposite with her sister's old keys, her sister's old address book, and her own quiet sharp memory of every detail Inês had ever mentioned. She was the one who noticed that the lost chapters had a gap: the night of Elise's death, between ten and midnight, which the original manuscript had covered in a single page.

"This page. Where?"

"In the lake. It never came back."

"No." She frowned and drew something on the envelope. A small wooden cabinet with a false back. "Inês keep things here. Under the window seat."

He thought of the board he had seen, the dust disturbed in a clean rectangle, the cushion put back as if the cushion were the point. He did not say that either. He lifted the seat.

They found it at midnight. The board Catarina had lifted in October had shown her only dust. The false back was behind it, a second skin of wood that you could find only if you knew that your sister had once been very good at woodwork and very bad at trusting landlords. Inside, wrapped in oilskin, lay a notebook in Inês's handwriting and a small tape recorder. Inês had written, on the inside of the wood, DO NOT MOVE THIS. TRUST ME. They did not move it. They took what she had hidden, and they put the board back.

They had to borrow a cassette player from Madame Fournier's kitchen. It turned out to be built into a radio, and the first button Tobias pressed played Édith Piaf at full volume for four minutes, through the open window, over the lake, to the great alarm of a heron. The second button was the right one.

On the tape, Elise Marchetti's voice, clear and frightened:

Inês's first note on the inside cover explained it in four lines. Elise had rented the house in March. Elise had asked her to keep the little recorder running in the study, under the window seat, whenever Monsieur Hale came, because Elise was an editor and did not believe in anything that had not been written down. Then Elise's voice, clear and frightened:

"Victor, I know you took the Marchetti estate. I know the Verrane Award is a fraud and you've pocketed eleven thousand entry fees. I have the contracts and the bank statements. If you don't give the rights back and close the foundation, I'll take them to the press tomorrow."

A few seconds after she had finished, Hale's voice. Smooth. Almost kind.

"Come down to the jetty, Elise, and we'll talk about it."

Then a telephone rang, and Hale answered it, and put it on the speaker, as vain men do, so that the room could hear how important his callers were. A second voice, not Hale's. Cooler. A man who had never once raised it. "I'll be on the jetty. Don't let her bring the pages." Inês had written in the margin of the notebook, in a small furious hand: not the publisher. The one with the claret tie. He smiled. She went down.

Catarina was crying openly now, the recorder cupped in both hands. Tobias took it gently, set it on the desk, and did what he had wanted to do for weeks. He put his arms round her.

She stiffened. Then she let her forehead drop against his shoulder and said, in a voice too soft to hear, a word he did not need a dictionary for.

"Obrigada."

They did not play the tape again that night. They copied the margin onto a clean page, in Catarina's block capitals, and in the morning they carried the page and the tape over the wall and put them in Madame Fournier's biscuit tin, with her spare keys, which was the only place in the valley that had never once lost anything.

## Chapter 21: The Cup

The rain came after midnight, and Tobias went out in it.

He told himself he was checking the gate. The gate was locked. He stood at the end of the jetty anyway, because the cooler voice was still in the room, and a voice like that does not stay indoors. By the time he came in he was shaking. By morning the shake had become a fever.

Catarina put him to bed in the study with three blankets, a hot-water bottle and a bowl of her grandmother's soup, which smelled of garlic and ancient sorrow. She sat with him until he fell asleep, and then she tiptoed out. The lamp went down. The house creaked. And on the desk, in the dark, something cleared its throat.

Tobias opened one eye.

The Olivetti stood on the desk, very upright, its keys gleaming like a row of teeth. It was not a thing he had ever noticed having a face, but it had one now, in the arrangement of the carriage and the bell and the two round spools. It looked like a retired colonel who had been asked to wait in a draughty corridor.

"Good evening," it said. "I am a 1962 Olivetti Lettera 22 of the finest Italian manufacture, and I have been sat in the same position for five weeks. Do you know how many commas you leave out?"

"You're a typewriter," said Tobias. "I'm dreaming."

"Naturally. Nobody listens to a typewriter unless he has a temperature." The bell gave a small, offended ting. "I have typed every word you have written in this house. I have typed *she put the tray outside the door* forty-one times. I have typed *she did not look away* nine times. I have not once been asked to type what you intend to do about it."

On the bedside table the chipped cup rocked on its saucer. It was the one Catarina had handed him on the first day, with the crack along the rim, and it had a small, high, eager voice that spoke very fast.

"She went into the lake," said the cup. "For paper. *Your* paper. She talks to the toaster. She says good morning to the stove. She held your hand on the jetty and you said nothing for a week."

"I've said a great deal."

"You have said," said the Olivetti, "a great deal of rubbish, without a comma in it."

"My Portuguese is terrible," said Tobias.

"She does not want your Portuguese," said the cup.

"What does she want?"

The cup thought about it. It was the kind of silence in which, if you were a cup, you could hear all the soup in the world.

"She wants to be met," said the cup. "Halfway. On purpose."

Out in the garden the wind lifted, and in the long grass by the gate a very large white bird turned its head toward the lit window. Tobias lay in the blankets, shivering and warm, and in the half-light he saw for the first time how many small, tireless kindnesses there had been in the house since September: the trays, the notes, the stairs that did not creak when she came down in the night because she had learned which ones did. None of them had asked to be noticed. That, he thought, was rather the point of them, and rather the problem.

In the morning the fever had broken. The sun lay in pale squares on the quilt, and the study was perfectly tidy, and on the desk the Olivetti stood in its usual place with a sheet of paper in the roller. It was blank, apart from one line, in the neat, ungrammatical capitals he would have known anywhere.

THE SOUP IS HOT. (NOT IS THE SOUP.) I AM HAPPY YOU ARE BETTER.

Catarina came in with the tray. On it, steaming, was a mug of tea made properly for the first time: the pot warmed, the leaves left to stand, the milk in last. It was in the cup with the chip.

He took it, and held it in both hands, and looked at it for a long moment.

"It's a very good cup," said Catarina, a little shyly.

"Yes," said Tobias. "I think it has opinions."

She laughed, and sat on the edge of the bed, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. Outside, the clock in the hall struck eight. On the desk the Olivetti stayed discreetly silent. He did not tell her about the dream. A dream that has been explained is only a temperature. But he kept the sentence. Halfway, on purpose. He wrote it on the envelope under the typewriter, under the name.

## Chapter 22: The Road to Aix

Inês's notebook ended with an address in Aix-en-Provence: Maître Étienne Barbier, Notaire, rue Gaston de Saporta, 17h00. Beneath it, underlined twice: the originals.

"The originals of what?" said Tobias.

"Of everything he stole," said Catarina. "If a lawyer keeps the contracts, then the lawyer is the one who can open the door." She tapped the page. "It is an hour. We go tomorrow. We are there by five."

They left at ten. They were not there by five.

The first delay was a stall at the side of the road, run by a woman in a straw hat selling peaches out of a wheelbarrow. "We stop for one minute," said Catarina, who knew about peaches. They stopped for forty. The peaches were enormous and warm and tasted of a summer that had not yet finished with anyone, and the woman in the hat insisted that Tobias taste the apricots, and the plums, and a small yellow fruit nobody could name in any language, and by the time they drove off the back seat was a fruit bowl.

"This was a mistake," said Tobias, with juice running down his wrist. "We're going to be late."

"Paris can wait," said Catarina, with her mouth full.

"We're not going to Paris."

"Then the lawyer can wait."

The second delay was a goat farm, because there was a sign. The third was a ruined abbey on a hill, because Tobias remarked that it looked Romanesque, and Catarina said "Românico," and they both spent twenty minutes standing in an empty nave where the roof had fallen in and the sky had come to live, whispering because it seemed to demand it. The fourth was an olive mill, where an old man in a blue apron poured them a thimble of oil so green and peppery that Catarina coughed, wiped her eyes, and bought six litres.

"It is for the restaurant," she said defensively. "Fernanda will say it is too expensive. Then she will use all of it."

The fifth delay was a flat tyre.

It happened on a quiet road between two vineyards, in the full heat of the afternoon, with the cicadas screaming in the pines like a machine nobody could turn off. Tobias stood over the spare wheel with the expression of a man reading a document in Latin.

"I have never changed a tyre," he said.

"You are English. You have a man."

"I did have a man. He was called the AA."

"Afasta-te," said Catarina, rolling up her sleeves. She was four minutes with the jack and the spanner, glistening, singing a tune from the radio under her breath, and when it was done she wiped her hands on her skirt and looked at him, hot and happy and absurdly beautiful, with oil on her cheek.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," said Tobias. "I was thinking I should have married a mechanic."

"You are not married."

"No," said Tobias. "I was thinking it in advance."

She stood very still. A cicada, somewhere, chose that moment to stop. Then Catarina turned, very pink, picked up the spanner, put it in the boot with extraordinary care, and said to the air in Portuguese, quite clearly, "Meu Deus, ele disse isso a sério."

He understood every word.

They did not reach Aix until half past six. The plane trees along the Cours Mirabeau were already throwing their long shadows, the fountains were running gold, and the notary's house in the rue Gaston de Saporta was a tall honey-coloured building with its shutters closed and a small brass plate that said MAÎTRE BARBIER, NOTAIRE.

The door was ajar. Inside, the office had been taken to pieces. Drawers upended, files on the floor, a safe standing open and empty. A neighbour in a dressing gown told them in rapid French that two men in dark coats had come at a quarter to five. They were, she said, extremely loud for burglars. One had kept shouting "Gaston!", and the other had replied "Oui?", and the first had said, "Not you, the other Gaston!", and for a while it had gone on like that. They had left at five, taking a safe, an umbrella stand and, for reasons the neighbour could not explain, a stuffed owl. And that Maître Barbier, who was sixty-nine and had eleven weeks left before the law made every notary in France put down his pen at seventy, was at that moment in the hospital with a bruised arm and an extremely bad temper.

"At five," said Tobias slowly.

"We would have been there at five," said Catarina. She had gone very pale. "Exactly at five. Like the notebook says."

They stood in the ransacked office in the late honey light. Somewhere outside, an accordion began to play. Neither said what both were thinking. If they had been punctual, if they had been sensible, if they had not stopped for the peaches, and the goats, and the abbey, and the oil, and the tyre, and the three minutes in which a man had said something aloud that should have waited.

"Paris," said Catarina at last, in a small voice, "can wait."

"Yes," said Tobias. "Apparently, so can everything else."

On the way out they picked up the single object the thieves had overlooked: a calendar on the old man's desk, where a note in a spidery hand read: Marchetti originals. Safe deposit, Cours Mirabeau branch. Key with Mme Fournier.

"Madame Fournier," said Tobias.

"Odile," said Catarina. "Odile has the key."

They were halfway to the car when a long black saloon pulled up silently at the kerb, and a man got out. He was about fifty, immaculate, in a charcoal suit and a tie the colour of old claret, and he carried a furled black umbrella under a sky that had not held a cloud all day.

"Mr Lind," he said, in perfect, pleasant English. "I am so glad. I had hoped we might meet again."

"You left before the cheese," said Tobias, slowly. "At the Pemberton-Hayeses'."

"I never stay for the cheese. Maître Edouard Delacroix-Roux. I act for certain persons whose names you may know." He smiled. His teeth were extraordinary. "I wished to say, as a professional courtesy, how much I admire your novel."

"You've read it?"

"I have not read it. I have read a summary, which I understand is all that is left." He turned the umbrella slowly in his fingers. "It must be a great grief to lose two years of work. One would hate for anything similar to happen to the people one loves."

"Is that a threat?" said Catarina, in her very plainest English.

"A threat? Certainly not." Maître Delacroix-Roux looked at her with a tenderness so artificial it might have been varnished. "I was merely observing that the roads of Provence are most dangerous at dusk, mademoiselle. And that some people, for want of a decent education, cannot tell a landmark from a trap." He glanced up at the dark windows of the notary's house, then back to her. "Do give my regards to your sister. I understand she has been missing for some time. How very distressing."

He bowed, as one bows to a wedding guest, and got back into the car. It purred away down the Cours Mirabeau under the plane trees, and in the gutter behind it lay one fallen leaf, and the umbrella, which he had left behind on purpose.

Catarina picked it up. Tobias took it from her very gently and snapped it in two.

"I don't like him," said Tobias.

"No," said Catarina. "He is a man who has never once been in a boat."

The tie was the colour of the margin. It had been the colour of the margin across a plate of vol-au-vents in October, and he had admired it. He wrote that down before he started the car, and then, because Catarina was watching him write it, he wrote underneath: I should have known at the supper. She took the pen and added, in capitals: NOBODY KNOWS AT THE SUPPER. THAT IS WHY IT IS A SUPPER.

## Chapter 23: The Key

Madame Fournier did not look up from the secateurs.

"The key has been in the biscuit tin since March," she said. "Under your tape, now, and the letter about the rent, and a photograph of Lucien standing on one leg, which I am not going to explain. Your friend Elise gave it to me. She came over the wall on a Thursday with a jar of honey I did not need and asked me to keep one thing in this valley that Monsieur Hale did not know about. She said she was a very careful guest." Madame Fournier snipped a rose that did not need it. "She did not come back. I put the honey in the cellar. I have not been able to open it."

The bank in Aix took twenty minutes and no heroism. Brunner had not yet arrived. Mara, carrying the juge d'instruction's signature in a plastic sleeve as if it might blow away, signed. Tobias watched. Catarina held the peach bag, because the bank had no ceremony and a paper bag was honest. Inside the box, under a toy Porsche with a wheel missing, was a card in a child's hand: 1-9-6-4. Under that, the contracts Elise had meant to bring to the jetty, and a photocopy of a cover: a bird, wings spread, bending its neck. The codex itself was not there.

"Second box," said Mara, reading Inês's margin. "Marseille. A foundation. The one that likes to be photographed."

They did not stage it. They carried the contracts out in the bag that had held the peaches. In the car Catarina read the top page and did not say I told you. She said, "The woman in your chapter four should not slip."

"I've already taken it out."

"Then the book can stay in the house."

Captain Anselm Brunner arrived at the lake house at eight o'clock that evening, on foot, in the rain, with an umbrella held at the precise angle of a bayonet.

He was about thirty-five. He was six foot four. He had a close fair haircut, a face carved out of cheese and honesty, and a dark overcoat buttoned to the throat. He stood on the porch, dripped politely on the mat, and bowed from the waist, which no one had done to Tobias since he was a child at a pantomime.

"Good evening," he said, in English with a faint Swiss-German edge. "I am Anselm Brunner. I was for eleven years an officer of the Pontifical Swiss Guard, and I am now employed by the Vatican Apostolic Library, to look for things that have left it. I regret the hour. I regret the weather. I regret, in a more general sense, that I am here at all."

"The Swiss Guard," said Tobias.

"Yes."

"The ones with the... the striped trousers."

"Those are for ceremonies, and I no longer attend them." Brunner looked down at his coat, with faint sorrow. "A librarian is not permitted the halberd. I was given this." He lifted the umbrella. "It is very firm. It keeps off rain. That is the whole of its talent."

It was Catarina, behind Tobias in the doorway, who recovered first. She crossed herself, almost involuntarily, and said in a small voice, "Meu Deus. The Pope sends the police."

"Not the Pope," said Brunner gravely. "The Library. And not the police. I am a sort of polite enquirer. It is a new post. There are, at present, two of us, and the other one is in Malta."

They sat him in the kitchen. He declined tea, accepted water, declined the glass and drank from the tap cup with his back very straight. He then took from his coat an envelope, an old Latin letter, a photograph and a notebook, and laid them in a row on the table, in order of size.

"Two years ago," he said, "a woman named Valentina Marchetti died in Lyon, aged ninety-one. She was the mother of Elise Marchetti. In her estate was a book of hours, made in Avignon in 1347, when the popes lived there. The Marchetti Codex, it is called now, after the family who had it last. It belonged to the papal library at Avignon until the Revolution, when it left in a cart with a great many other things. We have been looking for it for two hundred and thirty years. We are not hasty."

"And it was in the estate," said Tobias.

"It was. And then it was with a publisher named Victor Hale. And now," said Brunner, looking at the photocopy from the peach bag, "I am told it is in a second box, in Marseille, in the name of a foundation that gave two million euros to be photographed with a cardinal."

He opened his own photograph. The same bird, wings spread, bending its long neck to pierce its own breast. Beneath it, three tiny chicks.

"A pelican," said Catarina softly.

"The pelican in her piety," said Brunner. "In the old iconography, the pelican feeds her young with her own blood. It is a figure of sacrifice. It is also, I understand, a figure of..." He frowned. "You have one here. In the garden."

"He escaped from a zoo," said Tobias. "He does not pay rent."

Brunner looked out of the window at the lawn, where in the rain, on a sun lounger left by the last tenants, a vast white bird sat motionless on one leg. A long silence stretched out. The former officer of the Swiss Guard, who had stood in the presence of cardinals without moving an eyelid, took out a clean white handkerchief and blew his nose.

"I am sorry," he said. "It has been a long journey."

Inspector Mara Quill eyed him across the kitchen table the way a cat eyes a different breed of cat.

"You've no jurisdiction here, Mr Brunner."

"None whatever, Inspector. I have no jurisdiction anywhere, except a very small country with a lovely garden." He met her eye without a flicker. "I wish only to ask where a book is. If it is found, I wish to carry it home. I do not wish to arrest anybody. I am not able."

"You'd be surprised how irregular I can be," said Mara, with a trace of respect.

"I have been told," said Brunner, "that the English are very good at it."

"Hale is a cashier," said Mara. She was not, strictly, on the case. She was on the juge's case, which was a different thing, and which existed because she had walked into a magistrate's office in Aix three weeks earlier with a rent ledger, a loose bolt in a jam jar and a scrapyard photograph, and had not walked out until it did. She put the Marseille photograph on the table, the one from the lobster restaurant: Hale, and the man with the closed-door face, and the hands buttering a roll. "This one decides. Armand Vial. The foundation is his. The cooler voice on the tape is not Hale's. Inês wrote the tie in the margin before any of us had a name for it."

"Delacroix," said Tobias. "We met him. He left the umbrella on purpose."

Brunner looked at the broken umbrella in the corner, which Catarina had not thrown away. "Then the book is a cherry," he said. "The money was the cake. I will go to Marseille for the cherry. You will do what you are permitted to do with the cake."

"She will mock your French," said Tobias, meaning Fournier, who had already mocked it on the step and given him the key anyway.

"I am Swiss," said Brunner, with a small, weary, dignified smile. "I have spent my whole life being mocked by the French. It is how they show affection."

He went to Marseille the next morning, by the slow train, with the umbrella. He did not come back for some time.

## Chapter 25: The Two Gastons

Hugo Pemberton-Hayes was nine, and large for his age in the head.

He had been left at the lake house for the night, because Nigel and Priscilla were at a technical rehearsal of Dick Whittington, and Chloe had a date with a roofer. Hugo had been delivered at six with a rucksack, a torch, a library copy of Poisonous Plants of the Mediterranean, and an expression of immense and unnatural calm.

"Your second book has a plot hole," he told Tobias, at supper. "The murderer couldn't have been in the library at ten, because he's on the stairs at nine fifty-eight. And the poison's wrong."

"I'll bear that in mind."

"You do that," said Hugo kindly, and went to bed with the torch.

At half past nine Tobias and Catarina went over the garden wall to Madame Fournier's kitchen, forty metres away, with the lit window of Hugo's room in plain sight, because Odile had telephoned to say that the biscuit tin had been moved an inch on its shelf and she wished to know by whom. They left Hugo asleep upstairs, or apparently asleep, which with Hugo is a different state. The pelican was on the porch. At ten past ten, a van with no lights pulled up at the bottom of the lane.

Two men got out. They wore dark coats, balaclavas and the stiff, hunted air of the professionally unlucky.

"Gaston," whispered the first one.

"Oui?"

"Not you. The other one."

"There is no other one, Gaston! It is only the two of us!"

The gate was locked. They climbed it, which is how a locked gate announces itself to the unlucky, and the first Gaston stepped onto a layer of marbles Hugo had laid that afternoon for reasons of his own.

What followed was a precise geometry of misfortune. Gaston One's legs went east. Gaston Two, reaching out to catch him, grasped the hall coat-stand, which toppled, and brought down two walking sticks and a brass gong that Madame Fournier had always said was a decoration. The sound rang through the valley. Both men arrived at the kitchen door in a tangle of coats.

"Gaston," said Gaston One. "I believe there is a child in the house."

Upstairs, a torch clicked on beneath a chin.

They did not find the stairs easily. Each step had been polished with olive oil from the six litres Catarina had bought at the mill, which Hugo said afterwards had been "a very good oil, actually, peppery." Gaston Two reached the landing on his knees. Gaston One reached it on his back, feet first, like a man arriving at a party by toboggan.

"The study!" hissed Gaston One. "The originals are in the study!"

The study door was not locked. That was the trap. It opened so easily that Gaston Two, who had put his whole shoulder into it, went straight across the room, stepped on a tray of cold custard that had been left with great care in front of the window seat, and went out through the open window with a long, thin, receding cry, and landed in the pond, which was not deep, and was very cold, and contained a heron.

From the window seat, in the dark, a small voice spoke.

"I'm nine," said Hugo. "My father's a solicitor. I know all about trespass."

Ten minutes later, when Tobias and Catarina came back along the lane, they found a white van abandoned, two men on the lawn, one of them dripping and one of them dusted all over with flour from a bag that had been balanced on the kitchen door, tangled together in the garden hose and a string of Christmas lights from the cellar, and a pelican standing on one chest with the air of a bird who had not been asked and did not intend to give a statement. In the porch sat Hugo, in his pyjamas, with a mug of cocoa.

"I rang the Inspector," said Hugo, not looking up. "She's on her way. I did my father's voice. She's very cross. Also I ate your biscuits."

The Gastons were arrested at midnight by an Inspector in her dressing gown. They did not resist. As they were led to the car, the first Gaston was heard to say, in a voice of broken wonder, "Gaston. I think we should go to New York."

"There are children in New York," said the second, and the car door closed on him.

## Chapter 26: The Far Shore

Hale came back on the Sunday night, uninvited, with his headlights off.

The gate was locked. He left the Porsche in the lane, and the sump scraped, and nobody laughed at it until the morning. Catarina put the kitchen light out and stood with her back to the door she had already bolted. Tobias picked up the telephone because she pointed at it. He asked for Mara by name. He did not explain the plot. He said the publisher was at the gate and the gate was shut and they would not open it.

Hale spoke through the wood, pleasant, and the pleasantness was the frightening part.

"Mr Lind. I thought we might talk about your novel. The jetty is rotten. I'd hate to lose another guest."

They did not answer. The pelican, on the lounger, did not come to the window. When the lights of Mara's car reached the lane, Hale was already backing out. The scrape was the only comedy.

"He will come again," said Catarina.

"Then we will not be on this side of the water," said Tobias.

Inês Vidal had been living since March in the old eel-fisher's hut half a kilometre down the outflow, where the pond narrowed into a reed channel and then gave itself up to the marsh, on land that belonged to a cousin of nobody who visited.

They rowed down the channel at dawn, with the tape in Tobias's coat and the notebook inside Catarina's blouse, because a bolted door is not a finding. The far shore smelled of tar and reeds. A black-and-white dog barked twice and then sat, as if the sitting were the point.

Inês came out onto the dock, thinner than in the photograph, with a lantern in one hand and a shotgun she did not raise. Behind her, a minute later, because Tobias had asked for her by name, came Inspector Quill.

"I rang her yesterday," Inês said, in careful English. "I did not trust the police until I met this one."

Catarina stood in the boat and did not get out of it. She had looked for her sister for seven months. She looked now as if she might hit her.

"Half a kilometre," she said, in Portuguese. "You were half a kilometre."

"I watched you peg his pages on a line for three weeks," said Inês, also in Portuguese, and then, because the Inspector was there and manners are manners, in English. "If I had come, they would have known where I was. They were watching the house. They were watching you." She looked at Tobias, briefly, as at a piece of evidence she had already filed. "And also you would have stopped looking at him, and I wanted to see how it ended."

"I did say," said Mara drily, "that quiet people listen."

"Gigi found the lane," said Inês, of the dog. "She does not like strangers. She makes exceptions for tins."

Gigi gave the Inspector a small, dignified nod, as one professional to another, and went back to the stick.

"You are the writer," said Inês, looking Tobias up and down. "I have read your book. The second one is better, but that is not saying much. Chapter four has a woman slip. She did not slip."

"She is very intelligent," Catarina said, in a tone that suggested this was a medical condition.

"I am very tired," said Inês. "Intelligent is how I got here."

Hale did not run far. He was still in the lane when Mara's second car came down it, because a Porsche that is too low for the lane cannot be heroic in it. He stood at the end of the jetty in his beautiful coat and watched the boat come in, and the tape recorder pass from Tobias's hands to Mara's.

"It was only paper," he said, almost to himself. "Contracts. Receipts. A few silly pages in the water."

"It was three dead people," said Mara. "Aubry. Giraud. Marchetti. You didn't do this for a house, Mr Hale. You haven't the imagination. Who was on the jetty?"

Hale's smile faltered. "I'm a cashier, Inspector. A very well-dressed cashier. If you think I'm the one who decides, you've read the wrong book."

"The margin has already read it," said Mara. "Claret tie. You can give me the name, or the tape can. The tape does not want a deal."

He gave her the name on the second telling. Maître Edouard Delacroix-Roux. The man who smiled. The man who had said he would be on the jetty. Armand Vial, he said, after that, because a cashier who has begun does not know where to stop. Chairman. Marseille. The foundation. The two million. The cooler voice was not Vial's either. Vial did not attend jetties. Vial sent a lawyer.

Catarina stepped onto the dock, soaked and shaking, and put an arm round her sister.

"Não é só papel," she said.

She said it again in English, slowly, so that everyone could hear.

"It is not. Only. Paper."

The tape was with Mara. The margin was with Mara. Hale had given the name. The house by the water had done what it was for.
