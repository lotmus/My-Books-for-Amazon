H1: Chapter 10. SPI, CAN, and USB
P: The OLED on the reader lab redraws its 128 × 64 pixels in about 23 ms over I2C, so the fastest animation it can show is about 43 frames per second, with the bus busy the whole time. Move the same display to SPI at 8 MHz and a frame takes about a millisecond. Same display, same pixels, twenty times faster: the bus decides. This chapter covers the three buses that take over when I2C is too slow, too short, or too local: SPI for speed on the board, CAN for robustness across a machine, and USB for the PC.
P: Where you meet this: Displays, SD cards, fast ADCs, radio chips such as the LoRa module, cars, robots, industrial controllers, and every board you plug into a laptop.
H2: 10.1 SPI: fast and simple
P: SPI uses four lines: a clock (SCK) driven by the controller, data out (MOSI, also called COPI or SDO), data in (MISO, also CIPO or SDI), and one chip select (CS) per device, active low. Every clock edge moves one bit in each direction at once, there are no addresses and no acknowledgments, and clock rates of 10 to 80 MHz are common. Its one complication is the mode: the clock polarity (CPOL) says whether the clock idles low or high, and the clock phase (CPHA) says whether data is sampled on the first or second edge. Mode 0, CPOL = 0 and CPHA = 0, is the most common; the data sheet's timing diagram decides.

H2: Worked Example 10.1: I2C or SPI for the OLED?
P: Problem. The SSD1306 OLED has a 1024-byte frame buffer (128 × 64 pixels, 1 bit each). Estimate the time to send a full frame over I2C at 400 kHz and over SPI at 8 MHz, and the CPU load of a 10 frames per second animation in each case if the CPU waits for the transfer.
P: Solution. Over I2C every byte costs 9 clocks, so 1024 bytes are 9216 clocks, 23.0 ms at 400 kHz, plus a few bytes of address and control: about 23 ms, or at most about 43 frames per second. Over SPI each byte is 8 clocks: 8192 clocks at 8 MHz is 1.02 ms. At 10 frames per second a waiting CPU spends 230 ms per second on I2C, 23 %, and 10 ms per second on SPI, 1 %. With DMA (Chapter 11) the SPI transfer costs almost no CPU at all. The SSD1306 accepts SPI clocks up to about 10 MHz; above that, bits are lost.
H2: 10.2 CAN: the robust bus
P: CAN was designed for cars and is now everywhere machines are: robots, e-bikes, industrial controls, and drones. Two wires carry a differential signal through a transceiver chip, so the bus tolerates meters of cable and electrical noise (the transceiver and termination are Books 2 and 7's). There are no addresses: each frame carries an 11-bit identifier that names the message, not the sender, and every node hears every frame. The identifier also sets priority, because when two nodes start together the lower identifier wins arbitration bit by bit without destroying either frame. Every frame carries a 15-bit CRC, and every node checks it and acknowledges it.
P: Firmware for CAN has three parts: the bit timing, the message filters (so a node handles only the identifiers it cares about), and the payload layout. Classic CAN carries up to 8 data bytes; CAN FD up to 64 and faster data phases. The ESP32-S3's CAN controller is called TWAI; STM32 parts have bxCAN or FDCAN; the RP2040 has none, but a Pico can use an external SPI CAN controller.
H2: Worked Example 10.2: CAN timing and bus load
P: Problem. A bus runs at 500 kbit/s. Find the bit time and a 16-time-quantum bit with an 87.5 % sample point. A classic frame with an 11-bit ID and 8 data bytes has 108 bits plus 3 bits of interframe space, and stuffing can add up to 24 bits. Find the frame time and the bus load of 100 such frames per second.
P: Solution. The bit time is 1/500 000 = 2 µs, so one quantum is 125 ns. A sample point at 87.5 % means 14 quanta before it (1 sync, 13 propagation and phase-1) and 2 after it (phase 2). The frame takes 111 to 135 bits, 222 to 270 µs. One hundred frames per second occupy 22 to 27 ms of every second: 2.2 to 2.7 % bus load. Designers keep total load well under about 50 %, so that high-priority frames never wait long behind others.

H2: 10.3 USB
P: USB connects a board to a PC with a protocol stack in both. A full-speed USB device such as the RP2040 or the ESP32-S3's USB port signals at 12 Mbit/s. Firmware chooses a device class, and the PC loads a matching driver without any installation: CDC-ACM for a virtual serial port, HID for keyboards, mice, and game controllers, MSC for a drive (the Pico's BOOTSEL drive is one), and MIDI or audio for instruments. The TinyUSB stack, used by the Pico SDK and ESP-IDF, implements all of them.
P: A CDC virtual serial port looks like a UART to the PC program, but the baud rate you set is ignored: data moves in USB packets at the bus speed. That is why a Pico prints faster over USB than over its UART, and why a C# or Python program that sets 115 200 baud still works if the firmware ignores it. Full-speed bulk transfers carry at most 19 packets of 64 bytes per 1 ms frame, 1.2 MB/s in theory; real throughput depends on the host and the firmware.
P: Key idea. Choose the bus by distance and speed: I2C for slow sensors on the same board, SPI for fast peripherals on the same board, UART for simple point-to-point links and modules, CAN for robust multi-node links across a machine, USB for the PC.
P: On the Job
P: Automotive and industrial firmware engineers live with CAN. A typical task: add a new message to a vehicle's CAN database, implement its packing in the node's firmware, filter it in two other nodes, and prove with a bus logger that load and latency stay within the system spec at the worst case, with every node transmitting.
P: AI Pair-Programming Note
P: Generated SPI code often uses the wrong mode, MSB-first where the device wants LSB-first, or a clock far above the device's maximum. Generated CAN code often leaves out the transceiver's standby pin, mixes up 11-bit and 29-bit identifiers, or packs structs directly. Check the mode and clock against the data sheet's timing diagram, and decode the bus with an analyzer.
BENCH: A CAN bus needs exactly two 120 Ω terminations, one at each end. Measure between CAN-H and CAN-L with the power off: 60 Ω is right, 120 Ω means one terminator is missing, and 40 Ω means there is one too many. Most "CAN doesn't work" reports end at that measurement.
