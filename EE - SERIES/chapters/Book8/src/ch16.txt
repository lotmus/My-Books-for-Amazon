H1: Chapter 16. Sensor Fusion: IMU Filters, Kalman, and SLAM Basics
P: Lay an IMU board flat on the bench, integrate the gyroscope, and watch the angle climb: half a degree per second, ten degrees after twenty seconds, while nothing moves. Read the accelerometer instead and the angle is right on average but jumps by two degrees from sample to sample, and by much more the moment the board is carried. Neither sensor alone can say how the board is tilted. Together, with a dozen lines of arithmetic, they can, to a fifth of a degree. That combination is sensor fusion, and it is how every drone, phone, and balancing robot knows which way is up.
P: Where you meet this: Drones, gimbals, balancing robots, phone orientation, vehicle dead reckoning between GPS fixes, robot vacuums and lawn mowers that map their surroundings, and any product that combines two imperfect sensors into one good estimate.
H2: 16.1 Two imperfect sensors
P: A MEMS gyroscope measures angular rate. Integrated over time it gives angle changes that are smooth and fast, but every small offset in the rate (the bias) integrates into a steadily growing error, the drift. An accelerometer measures acceleration including gravity; when the board is still, the direction of gravity gives the tilt directly, with no drift, but with noise, and any real acceleration of the board is mistaken for tilt. The gyro is good over short times and bad over long ones; the accelerometer is the opposite. Fusion takes the short-term information from one and the long-term information from the other. The MEMS physics of both sensors, and the noise densities in their datasheets, are Book 3's.
P: Book 5, Chapter 23.5 introduces sensor fusion as an estimation problem. This chapter focuses on its embedded implementation: reading an IMU, coding filters, and supplying sensor data to mapping software.
H2: 16.2 Reading the IMU
P: The MPU-6050 is the IMU on countless cheap breakout boards and a good first driver: I2C address 0x68 (0x69 with AD0 high), WHO_AM_I register 0x75 reading 0x68, and a reset state that is asleep (PWR_MGMT_1 at 0x6B), so the first write must wake it. Its fourteen data bytes from 0x3B hold three accelerometer axes, the temperature, and three gyro axes, each a big-endian signed 16-bit value. At the default ranges the accelerometer gives 16 384 counts per g and the gyro 131 counts per degree per second. It is an older part; for new designs, modern IMUs such as TDK's ICM-42688-P (WHO_AM_I 0x47 at register 0x75, power control in PWR_MGMT0 at 0x4E, gyro at 16.4 counts per degree per second on its ±2000 °/s range) offer lower noise and SPI at higher rates. The driver pattern is the same.
P: Listing 16.1 — A minimal MPU-6050 driver: identity check, wake-up, and one 14-byte burst read converted to g and degrees per second (MicroPython on the ESP32-S3).
P: # MicroPython: minimal MPU-6050 driver on the ESP32-S3 (I2C on GPIO 8/9).from machine import I2C, Pinimport struct, timeADDR = 0x68              # AD0 low; 0x69 with AD0 highWHO_AM_I, PWR_MGMT_1, ACCEL_XOUT_H = 0x75, 0x6B, 0x3BACC_LSB_PER_G = 16384    # +-2 g range (reset default)GYR_LSB_PER_DPS = 131    # +-250 deg/s range (reset default)i2c = I2C(0, scl=Pin(9), sda=Pin(8), freq=400_000)def init():    who = i2c.readfrom_mem(ADDR, WHO_AM_I, 1)[0]    if who != 0x68:        raise OSError("MPU-6050 not found, WHO_AM_I=0x%02X" % who)    i2c.writeto_mem(ADDR, PWR_MGMT_1, b"\x01")   # wake up, clock from X gyro PLL    time.sleep_ms(100)def read():    """Return (ax, ay, az) in g and (gx, gy, gz) in deg/s from one 14-byte burst."""    raw = i2c.readfrom_mem(ADDR, ACCEL_XOUT_H, 14)    ax, ay, az, _temp, gx, gy, gz = struct.unpack(">7h", raw)   # big-endian, signed    acc = tuple(v / ACC_LSB_PER_G for v in (ax, ay, az))    gyr = tuple(v / GYR_LSB_PER_DPS for v in (gx, gy, gz))    return acc, gyrinit()while True:    acc, gyr = read()    print("acc %.3f %.3f %.3f g  gyro %.2f %.2f %.2f dps" % (acc + gyr))    time.sleep_ms(100)
H2: Worked Example 16.1: Tilt from raw accelerometer counts
P: Problem. The board is still. The MPU-6050 reports ay = 2845 and az = 16 135 counts, ax = 0. Find the tilt about the x axis and check that the reading is plausible.
P: Solution. Tilt = atan2(ay, az) = atan2(2845, 16 135) = atan(0.1763) = 10.0°. Plausibility: the magnitude √(2845² + 16 135²) = 16 384 counts = 1.000 g, as it must be for a board at rest. A magnitude well away from 1 g means the board is accelerating and the accelerometer's tilt should be trusted less at that moment.
H2: 16.3 The complementary filter
P: The complementary filter blends the integrated gyro angle with the accelerometer angle at every sample: angle ← α (angle + ω Δt) + (1 − α) angle_acc. With α close to 1 the gyro dominates over short times and the accelerometer pulls the estimate back over long times. The crossover is set by the time constant τ = α Δt/(1 − α). It is one line of code, it runs on any chip, and on many hobby projects it is all the fusion needed.
EQ: angle ← α (angle + ω Δt) + (1 − α) angle_acc,    τ = α Δt/(1 − α)
H2: Worked Example 16.2: Choosing α and the price of gyro bias
P: Problem. The filter runs at 100 Hz with α = 0.98. The gyro has an uncorrected bias of 0.5 °/s. Find the time constant, the crossover frequency, and the steady error the bias causes with the board still.
P: Solution. τ = 0.98 × 0.01 s/0.02 = 0.49 s, crossover 1/(2π × 0.49 s) = 0.32 Hz. Setting the update to a fixed point with the board still: angle (1 − α) = α b Δt + (1 − α) truth, so the error is α b Δt/(1 − α) = b τ = 0.5 × 0.49 = 0.25°. Without fusion the same bias drifts 10° in 20 s; Listing 16.2 shows 9.94°. Measuring the bias at startup (average the gyro for a second while still) and subtracting it removes most of the 0.25°.
P: Listing 16.2 — Complementary filter and a two-state Kalman filter (angle and gyro bias) for one tilt axis, tested on simulated data with a 0.5 °/s gyro bias and 2° accelerometer noise (C, runs on the PC and on the ESP32-S3).
P: // Complementary filter and 1-D Kalman filter for one tilt axis, on simulated data.// Pure C, runs on the PC. Same code runs on the ESP32-S3 at 100 Hz.#include <math.h>#include <stdint.h>#include <stdio.h>#define DT 0.01f                         // 100 Hzstatic uint32_t seed = 12345;static float noise(void)                 // approximately Gaussian, sigma 1 (sum of 12 uniforms){    float s = 0.0f;    for (int i = 0; i < 12; i++) { seed = seed * 1664525u + 1013904223u; s += (seed >> 8) / 16777216.0f; }    return s - 6.0f;}typedef struct { float angle, bias, P[2][2]; } kalman_t;   // state: angle and gyro biasstatic float complementary(float *angle, float gyro_dps, float acc_deg, float alpha){    *angle = alpha * (*angle + gyro_dps * DT) + (1.0f - alpha) * acc_deg;    return *angle;}static float kalman_step(kalman_t *k, float gyro_dps, float acc_deg){    const float Q_angle = 0.001f, Q_bias = 0.003f, R = 4.0f;   // R = (2 deg)^2    k->angle += (gyro_dps - k->bias) * DT;                      // predict    k->P[0][0] += DT * (DT * k->P[1][1] - k->P[0][1] - k->P[1][0] + Q_angle);    k->P[0][1] -= DT * k->P[1][1];    k->P[1][0] -= DT * k->P[1][1];    k->P[1][1] += Q_bias * DT;    float S = k->P[0][0] + R;                                    // update    float K0 = k->P[0][0] / S, K1 = k->P[1][0] / S;    float y = acc_deg - k->angle;    k->angle += K0 * y;    k->bias  += K1 * y;    float P00 = k->P[0][0], P01 = k->P[0][1];    k->P[0][0] -= K0 * P00; k->P[0][1] -= K0 * P01;    k->P[1][0] -= K1 * P00; k->P[1][1] -= K1 * P01;    return k->angle;}int main(void){    const float truth = 10.0f, bias = 0.5f;      // true tilt (deg), gyro bias (deg/s)    float gyro_only = 0.0f, comp = 0.0f, e_acc = 0.0f, e_comp = 0.0f, e_kal = 0.0f;    kalman_t k = { 0 };    int n = 0;    for (int i = 1; i <= 2000; i++) {            // 20 s        float gyro = bias + 0.1f * noise();      // the board is still        float acc  = truth + 2.0f * noise();     // accelerometer tilt, sigma 2 deg        if (i == 1) { gyro_only = truth; comp = acc; k.angle = acc; }   // gyro starts from the true angle        gyro_only += gyro * DT;        complementary(&comp, gyro, acc, 0.98f);        kalman_step(&k, gyro, acc);        if (i > 1000) {                          // statistics over the last 10 s            e_acc  += (acc - truth) * (acc - truth);            e_comp += (comp - truth) * (comp - truth);            e_kal  += (k.angle - truth) * (k.angle - truth);            n++;        }    }    printf("gyro only after 20 s: %.2f deg (drift %.2f)\n", gyro_only, gyro_only - truth);    printf("rms error: accel %.2f, complementary %.2f, kalman %.2f deg\n",           sqrtf(e_acc / n), sqrtf(e_comp / n), sqrtf(e_kal / n));    printf("kalman bias estimate %.3f deg/s\n", k.bias);    return 0;}

H2: 16.4 The Kalman filter in one dimension
P: The Kalman filter makes the blending weight a calculation instead of a choice. It keeps an estimate and its variance P. Each step it predicts (the estimate moves with the gyro and the variance grows by the process noise Q), then corrects with the measurement, weighting it by the gain K = P/(P + R), where R is the measurement variance. A confident estimate (small P) and a noisy sensor (large R) give a small gain; the reverse gives a large one. Listing 16.2 adds the gyro bias as a second state, so the filter learns the bias by itself (it estimates 0.513 °/s against the true 0.5). The extended and unscented versions for full 3-D orientation follow the same predict-correct pattern; on the job you use a tested library or the sensor's own fusion engine (BNO085-class parts compute orientation on chip) and tune Q and R.
H2: Worked Example 16.3: Two Kalman steps by hand
P: Problem. The estimate is 10.0° with variance P = 1.0 deg². A measurement of 12.0° arrives with R = 4.0 deg² (σ = 2°). Update the estimate. Then the process adds Q = 0.1 deg², and a second measurement of 11.0° arrives. Update again.
P: Solution. Step 1: K = 1.0/(1.0 + 4.0) = 0.20. Estimate 10.0 + 0.20 × (12.0 − 10.0) = 10.40°. Variance (1 − 0.20) × 1.0 = 0.80. Step 2: predict P = 0.80 + 0.10 = 0.90; K = 0.90/4.90 = 0.184; estimate 10.40 + 0.184 × (11.0 − 10.40) = 10.51°; P = (1 − 0.184) × 0.90 = 0.73. The variance falls with every measurement until Q balances it, and the gain settles to a constant; a complementary filter is close to that steady-state Kalman filter with a fixed gain.
P: Common misconception. Tuning Q and R until the plot looks smooth. A small Q makes the output smooth and slow, so it lags real motion. Measure R from the sensor at rest (the variance of the accelerometer angle), choose Q from how fast the real quantity can change, and test with real motion, not only with the board lying still.
H2: 16.5 SLAM basics
P: A robot that maps a room and locates itself in that map at the same time is doing SLAM, simultaneous localization and mapping. The two halves depend on each other: the map is built from range readings taken at known poses, and the pose is found by matching new readings against the map. The mapping half can be shown on its own. An occupancy grid divides the floor into cells, each holding the log-odds that it is occupied. A range reading marks the cells along the beam as more likely free and the cell at the end as more likely occupied, by adding fixed log-odds increments. Repeated readings sharpen the map; a moving object fades out again.
P: Listing 16.3 — An occupancy grid with log-odds updates from ToF range readings at a known pose: the mapping half of SLAM (Python).
P: """Occupancy grid from range readings: the mapping half of SLAM, with known robot poses."""import mathCELL = 0.05                    # 5 cm cellsSIZE = 40                      # 2 m x 2 m mapL_HIT = math.log(0.7 / 0.3)    # +0.847: evidence that a cell is occupiedL_MISS = math.log(0.4 / 0.6)   # -0.405: evidence that a cell is freegrid = [[0.0] * SIZE for _ in range(SIZE)]   # log-odds, 0 = unknown (p = 0.5)def update(x, y, heading_deg, rng):    """Walk the beam from the robot at (x, y) meters; mark free cells and the hit cell."""    h = math.radians(heading_deg)    steps = int(rng / CELL)    for s in range(steps + 1):        cx = int((x + s * CELL * math.cos(h)) / CELL)        cy = int((y + s * CELL * math.sin(h)) / CELL)        if not (0 <= cx < SIZE and 0 <= cy < SIZE):            return        grid[cy][cx] += L_HIT if s == steps else L_MISSdef prob(l):    return 1.0 - 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(l))# Three readings from a ToF sensor, robot at (0.5 m, 1.0 m) facing a wall at x = 1.5 m.for _ in range(3):    update(0.5, 1.0, 0.0, 1.0)print("wall cell p = %.3f, free cell p = %.3f" % (prob(grid[20][30]), prob(grid[20][20])))
H2: Worked Example 16.4: Three readings of a wall
P: Problem. A ToF sensor with a hit probability model of 0.7 and a miss model of 0.4 reads the same wall three times. Find the occupancy probability of the wall cell and of a cell in front of it, starting from 0.5.
P: Solution. The hit increment is ln(0.7/0.3) = 0.847, the miss increment ln(0.4/0.6) = −0.405. After three readings the wall cell holds 2.54, so p = 1 − 1/(1 + e^2.54) = 0.927; the free cell holds −1.22, so p = 0.229. Listing 16.3 prints both. Adding log-odds rather than multiplying probabilities keeps the update to one addition per cell, which suits a microcontroller.

P: Full SLAM, with scan matching and loop closure, runs on the Pi 5 rather than on a microcontroller: ROS 2 packages such as slam_toolbox take a 2-D LiDAR scan and odometry and produce the map and pose. The firmware's part is to deliver clean, timestamped sensor data: wheel encoder counts, IMU angles from this chapter, and range scans (Chapter 20). Millimeter-wave radar is a third range source: TI's IWR6843 works at 60 to 64 GHz, and its 4 GHz sweep gives a range resolution of c/(2B) = 3 × 10⁸/(2 × 4 × 10⁹) = 3.75 cm, through smoke and darkness. Its point-cloud output looks to firmware like the LiDAR clouds of Chapter 20. The radar front end is Book 4's.
P: Key idea. Fusion trusts each sensor where it is good: the gyro over short times, the accelerometer, GPS, or range sensor over long times. A complementary filter does it with a fixed weight, a Kalman filter computes the weight from the variances.
P: AI Pair-Programming Note
P: Assistants write plausible fusion code with wrong units: gyro rates left in degrees per second while the angle is in radians, a Δt hard-coded to 0.01 while the loop runs at 50 Hz, or accelerometer axes swapped. Step 1 of the method fixes this: put the units, axis directions, and sample rate in the spec, and require a test on simulated data with a known answer, like Listing 16.2.
BENCH: Calibrate the gyro bias at every power-up: keep the board still for one second, average about a hundred samples per axis, and store the means. Then turn the board slowly through 90° on a protractor or against a square and check that the fused angle reads 90°. If it reads 87° or 93°, the scale or the axis mapping is wrong, not the filter.
