H1: Chapter 16. Gyroscopes, IMUs, and Dead Reckoning
P: A hobby rocket's flight computer must fire the parachute charge at apogee, the instant the rocket stops climbing. Integrate its accelerometer with a bias of only 50 mg, a twentieth of gravity, and it decides that moment about three-quarters of a second late. The same arithmetic explains why a phone's step counter drifts and why every robot fuses its IMU with something else.
P: Where you meet this: Every phone, drone flight controller, VR headset, and robot; the MPU-6050 and ICM-42688 breakout boards of hobby projects; rocket and model-aircraft altimeters; and the calibration screen that asks you to wave your phone in a figure eight.
P: Chapter 7 built the MEMS accelerometer from a spring and a mass. Its partner in every phone, drone, car, and robot is the MEMS gyroscope, which measures rotation rate, and together they form an inertial measurement unit, or IMU. This chapter explains how a vibrating structure senses rotation through the Coriolis force, computes how small the resulting motion is, and then shows what an IMU's errors do when its outputs are integrated to estimate orientation and position, which is the problem of dead reckoning. The numbers explain why every navigation system that uses MEMS sensors also needs something else, satellite positioning, cameras, or the robot's own legs and wheels, to correct them.
H2: 16.1 The Coriolis force
P: A MEMS gyroscope drives a proof mass into vibration along one axis at its resonant frequency, with amplitude x0 and angular frequency ωd, so that its velocity oscillates with peak value v = x0ωd. If the device rotates at rate Ω about an axis perpendicular to the motion, the mass experiences a Coriolis acceleration 2Ωv along the third axis, oscillating at the drive frequency. A second set of springs and electrodes senses that motion. The output is proportional to the rotation rate, and the design problem is to make the sensed motion measurable.
EQ: aC = 2 Ω v,    v = x0 ωd
H2: Worked Example 16.1
P: Problem. A gyroscope's mass vibrates with 5 µm amplitude at 20 kHz. Find the peak drive velocity and the Coriolis acceleration for a rotation of 1°/s. If the sense mode resonates at 21 kHz, how far does the mass move in the sense direction, using Chapter 7's x = a/ω0²?
P: Solution. v = 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 2π × 20,000 = 0.628 m/s. Ω = 1°/s = 0.01745 rad/s, so aC = 2 × 0.01745 × 0.628 = 0.0219 m/s², 2.2 milli-g. With the sense mode at 21 kHz, the displacement is 0.0219/(2π × 21,000)² = 1.26 × 10⁻¹² m, about 1.3 picometers, a hundredth of an atom's diameter. Gyroscope designers therefore operate the sense mode close to the drive frequency, use vacuum packaging for high Q, and read the capacitance with circuits resolving attofarads; the closer the two modes, the larger the signal and the more sensitive the device to their matching over temperature.
P: Key idea. A MEMS gyroscope senses rotation as a picometer-scale Coriolis motion of a vibrating mass. Its errors are tiny in each sample, but integration turns them into growing errors in angle and position, so inertial navigation always needs an external correction.
H2: 16.2 Error terms
P: An inertial sensor's specifications separate bias (the output at zero input), bias instability (slow bias wander), white noise, scale-factor error, and cross-axis sensitivity. White rate noise is commonly reported as angle random walk; white acceleration noise as velocity random walk. Allan deviation, plotted against averaging time, helps distinguish these behaviors in a datasheet. Consumer gyroscopes can have bias instability of several degrees per hour; tactical-grade MEMS units reach about 1 degree per hour, and navigation-grade instruments, usually optical, can be a hundred times lower. Section 16.5 translates sensor errors into position drift over time.
H2: Worked Example 16.2
P: Problem. An accelerometer has an uncorrected bias of 1 mg. Find the position error after 60 s and 600 s of double integration. A gyroscope has 1 °/h bias and 0.1 °/√h angle random walk; find the heading error from each after 4 h.
P: Solution. The bias is 9.81 × 10⁻³ m/s². After 60 s, x = ½ × 9.81 × 10⁻³ × 3600 = 17.7 m; after 600 s, 1766 m. The error grows with the square of time, so a MEMS IMU alone can dead-reckon only for seconds to tens of seconds. For the gyroscope, the bias gives 4° after 4 h and the random walk 0.1 × √4 = 0.2°. A tilt error is just as damaging: a 0.1° error in the estimated vertical puts 1.75 mg of gravity into the horizontal axes, which then integrates like a bias.
H2: 16.3 Fusion
P: Because inertial errors grow with time while satellite positions, camera features, wheel odometry, and leg kinematics have errors that do not, systems combine them. A Kalman filter predicts the state, position, velocity, and orientation, from the IMU at a high rate, and corrects it whenever another measurement arrives, estimating the IMU's biases along the way. The accelerometer's measurement of gravity corrects tilt; a magnetometer or satellite heading corrects yaw. In the robot of Chapter 29, the IMU feeds the fast balance loop at hundreds of hertz, while cameras and the learned model correct position at lower rates, the same two-speed structure that Section 29.1 finds in its control.
H2: 16.4 Packaging the IMU
P: The sensors of Chapter 7 and this chapter are packaging-sensitive in ways that logic chips are not. Stress from the board and package, through the expansion mismatch of Chapter 10, shifts the resonant frequencies and the bias, so IMUs are mounted with stress-isolating structures and calibrated over temperature. Vibration at the drive frequency couples into the output. Gyroscopes and resonators need vacuum or controlled-pressure cavities sealed at the wafer level. High-temperature navigation IMUs for drilling and engine monitoring, rated for 175 °C and above, need packages and solders chosen for those temperatures. A good IMU is as much a packaging product as a MEMS one.
H2: 16.5 How fast dead reckoning drifts
P: This section turns the sensor-level error terms from Section 16.2 into position error. After gravity is removed, a constant accelerometer bias b integrates twice, producing a position error of 1/2 b t^2. A constant gyroscope bias first creates an attitude error; that error misprojects gravity into horizontal acceleration, which then integrates twice. The resulting position error grows approximately with the cube of time. This explains why an unaided consumer IMU holds position only briefly and why practical systems fuse it with GNSS, wheel odometry, vision, or known walking constraints, as Section 16.3 describes.
EQ: position error ≈ ½ b t²

H2: Worked Example 16.3
P: Problem. A hobby rocket's motor burns out at 1.6 s, 100 m up and climbing straight at 120 m/s. Ignoring drag, find the time and height of apogee. The flight computer integrates an uncalibrated MPU-6050-class accelerometer, whose data sheet allows ±50 mg of zero-g offset, from launch, and its bias along the flight axis is 50 mg. How late does it detect apogee, and how far has the rocket fallen by then? How well could a BME280 barometer, with 0.2 Pa of rms pressure noise, about 1.7 cm of altitude, pin the same moment?
P: Solution. The coast lasts 120/9.81 = 12.2 s, so apogee comes at 13.8 s and 100 + 120²/(2 × 9.81) = 834 m. The bias b = 0.05 × 9.81 = 0.49 m/s² adds bt to the integrated velocity, 6.8 m/s by apogee. The integrated velocity reaches zero when g Δt = b(t_ap + Δt), so Δt = b t_ap/(g − b) = 0.49 × 13.8/9.32 = 0.73 s late, when the rocket has already fallen ½ × 9.81 × 0.73² = 2.6 m. Near apogee the height changes by ½gΔt², so 1.7 cm of barometer noise corresponds to ±√(2 × 0.017/9.81) = ±0.06 s. The barometer finds apogee ten times more sharply, but it is fooled by pressure transients at the port and by the motor phase, so flight computers use both: the accelerometer to detect launch and burnout, the barometer to time apogee, and a calibration such as Section 16.7's to shrink the bias.

H2: 16.6 The magnetometer and hard-iron error
P: A gyroscope's heading drifts without bound, and the cheapest absolute heading reference is a three-axis magnetometer measuring the Earth's field. Its horizontal component is only about 15 to 30 µT at mid-latitudes, and anything magnetic on the board or in the product adds to it. A permanent offset, from magnetized steel parts, speaker magnets, or steady currents in nearby traces, is called hard-iron error and shifts the measured field by a fixed vector; soft-iron error, from permeable material that bends the Earth's field, distorts the circle traced by a rotating sensor into an ellipse. Both are removed by calibration: rotating the product through many orientations and fitting the offset and the ellipse. Motor currents in a robot or battery currents in a phone change with operation and cannot be calibrated away, so the magnetometer is placed far from them and the fusion filter of Section 16.3 trusts it less when they are large.
EQ: heading error ≈ arctan(B_offset / H_horizontal)  (offset perpendicular to the field)
H2: Worked Example 16.4
P: Problem. The Earth's horizontal field is 20 µT. A trace 10 mm from the magnetometer carries a steady 1 A. Find the field it produces and the worst-case heading error if that field is perpendicular to the Earth's.
P: Solution. A long straight current produces B = µ₀I/(2πr) = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 1/(2π × 0.01) = 20 µT, as large as the Earth's horizontal field, giving a heading error of arctan(1) = 45°. At 100 mm the field falls to 2 µT and the error to arctan(0.1) = 5.7°, which is why power traces are kept well away from compasses.
H2: 16.7 Calibrating an IMU
P: A MEMS IMU leaves the factory trimmed, but soldering, board stress, and temperature move its offsets, which is why data sheets quote 'board-level' tolerances. The cheapest good calibration is the six-position test: hold the board still with each axis pointing up and then down, average each reading, and take half the sum as the offset and half the difference as the scale factor, since gravity reverses and the offset does not. The gyroscope's zero-rate offset is the average of a still recording. Because the offsets drift with temperature, the calibration is repeated at two or three temperatures, or the temperature sensor inside the IMU is logged and a slope is fitted. Book 8 turns these numbers into firmware with a complementary or Kalman filter.
EQ: offset = (up + down)/2,   scale = (up − down)/2,   σ_avg = noise density/√(2T)
H2: Worked Example 16.5
P: Problem. An ICM-42688-P on the reader lab's robot reads +1.0230 g with its Z axis up and −0.9830 g with it down. Its data sheet gives an accelerometer noise density of 70 µg/√Hz, a board-level zero-g tolerance of ±20 mg, a sensitivity tolerance of ±0.5 %, a zero-g change of ±0.15 mg/°C, a gyroscope zero-rate tolerance of ±0.5 °/s, and a gyroscope noise of 2.8 mdps/√Hz. Find the offset and scale, how long to average each position for a 50 µg standard error, the offset change over a 40 K temperature swing, and the gyroscope's offset error after a 10 s still recording.
P: Solution. The offset is (1.0230 − 0.9830)/2 = 0.0200 g, 20 mg, at the edge of the board-level tolerance, and the scale is (1.0230 + 0.9830)/2 = 1.0030, 0.30 % high, inside ±0.5 %. For σ = 50 µg, T = (70/50)²/2 = 0.98 s, so one second per position. Over 40 K the offset can move ±0.15 × 40 = ±6 mg, more than a hundred times the calibration's error, so a robot that warms up needs a temperature model. Uncorrected, the gyroscope's ±0.5 °/s would turn the heading by 30° a minute; a 10 s average leaves 2.8/√20 = 0.63 mdps, and the remaining error is set by temperature drift, not noise. With these corrections the 50 mg rocket of Worked Example 16.3 would carry a few milli-g instead, and detect apogee within tenths of a second.
P: Motion sensors, like every chip in this book, depend on their package. Chapter 17 opens the most demanding package of all, the high-bandwidth memory stack.
BENCH: Calibrate before you integrate: average the sensor at rest in six orientations to find each axis's bias and scale, and log the temperature with every sample, because the bias moves with it. Check the noise density and bias stability on the data sheet against the time your application must coast without correction. For a flight computer, cross-check the accelerometer with a barometer such as the BME280 rather than trusting either alone.
