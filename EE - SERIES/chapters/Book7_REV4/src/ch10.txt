H1: Chapter 10. Package Reliability: Expansion, Fatigue, and Electromigration
P: A board passes every test on the bench and fails in the field after a winter of nights in a cold car and days in the sun. The fault is a crack through one solder ball at the corner of a BGA, grown one temperature cycle at a time. Nothing failed suddenly; the joint was slowly worn out by expansion.
P: Where you meet this: Every automotive, aerospace, and outdoor product's qualification plan, every thermal-cycling and vibration test, every moisture-sensitivity label on a reel of parts, and every rocket or drone flight computer that must survive launch vibration.
P: A package that works on the day it is built must keep working through years of heating and cooling, current, humidity, and vibration. The commonest failures are mechanical: materials that expand by different amounts with temperature strain the joints between them, and the joints crack after enough cycles. The second family is electrical: high current density slowly moves metal atoms and opens wires and bumps. This chapter estimates both with the standard engineering models, and explains the design features, underfill, matched materials, and current limits, that keep the lifetimes long.
H2: 10.1 Thermal expansion mismatch
P: Silicon expands by about 2.6 parts per million per kelvin. A glass-epoxy board expands by roughly 14 to 17 ppm/K in its plane, copper by about 17, and organic package substrates by 10 to 17. When a silicon die is soldered directly to a board and the assembly heats by ΔT, the board tries to grow more than the die, and the solder joints between them are sheared. The shear strain is largest at the joints farthest from the neutral point, the center of the die, and is inversely proportional to the joint height.
EQ: Δγ ≈ Δα × ΔT × DNP / h
H2: 10.2 Solder fatigue
P: Each temperature cycle bends the joint back and forth, and solder, like any metal, fails after enough cycles of plastic strain. The Coffin–Manson relation, in the form Werner Engelmaier adapted for solder joints, estimates the number of cycles to failure from the cyclic shear strain. Its exponent depends on temperature and cycle time; a commonly used base value is c = −0.442, with a fatigue ductility coefficient εf = 0.325. Because the exponent is about −1/2.3, halving the strain multiplies the life by about five.
EQ: Nf ≈ ½ (Δγ / (2εf))^(1/c),   c ≈ −0.442,   εf ≈ 0.325

H2: Worked Example 10.1
P: Problem. A silicon die is flip-chip soldered directly to an FR-4 board with Δα = 14 ppm/K. Its farthest bump is 10 mm from the center and the joints are 0.1 mm high. Estimate the strain and cycles to failure for a 100 K swing. Repeat with the strain halved, and with an underfill that reduces the effective strain tenfold.
P: Solution. Δγ = 14 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 × 10/0.1 = 0.14. The simplified Engelmaier model gives about 16 cycles at this severe strain, 77 when the strain is halved, and about 2,950 when it is reduced tenfold. These are model outputs for the declared geometry and cycle, not typical field-life guarantees. Underfill is common in conventional flip-chip assemblies, but package and joint technology determine whether it is used; qualification data for the actual assembly still decide the lifetime.
P: This cycle count is a model estimate for the declared geometry and 100 K swing, not a typical life for assembled boards. A mission profile with two full temperature excursions a day for four years contains about 2,920 excursions, close to this estimate; field cycles are not automatically equivalent to the model cycle. A crack that reaches the joint can make the rail intermittent or open, causing a reset and a failed qualification run. The retry loop in Book 8 can record the interruption; it cannot restore the solder.
P: Key idea. Mismatched expansion turns every temperature cycle into a mechanical load on the joints. Strain grows with distance from the center and falls with joint height, and fatigue life rises steeply as the strain falls, so small reductions in mismatch buy large gains in life.
H2: 10.3 Warpage
P: The same mismatch bends the package. A large organic substrate carrying a stiff silicon interposer curls as it cools from its bonding temperature, and a package that is not flat at the solder-reflow temperature leaves some of its balls open or bridged. Warpage limits the size of advanced packages, and it is one reason glass and stiffened substrates are being adopted. Package and board designers specify the allowed warpage across the reflow temperature range, and the solder-ball height must accommodate it.
H2: 10.4 Electromigration
P: At high current density, the momentum of electrons pushes metal atoms along a conductor, thinning it at one end and piling atoms up at the other. Eventually a void opens the wire or bump. Black's equation describes the median time to failure in terms of the current density J, an exponent n near 2, and an activation energy Ea of about 0.5 to 0.9 eV, depending on the metal and the failure site. The temperature term makes electromigration a thermal problem as much as an electrical one.
EQ: MTTF = A J^(−n) exp(Ea / (kB T))
H2: Worked Example 10.2
P: Problem. With n = 2 and Ea = 0.7 eV, how much does the median life shorten when a bump's current density doubles, and when its temperature rises from 105 °C to 125 °C?
P: Solution. Doubling J shortens the life by 2² = 4. The temperature change shortens it by exp[(0.7/8.617 × 10⁻⁵)(1/378.15 − 1/398.15)] = 2.94. Together the two would cut the life by nearly twelve. The bump-current limit of Chapter 3 is therefore always quoted at a temperature, and a hot spot on a die can make its local bumps the life-limiting part of the whole package.
P: For a scale check, suppose a qualified structure has a measured 10,000-hour median life at 105 °C and current density J. With the same geometry and mechanism, doubling J and raising temperature to 125 °C gives about 10,000/(4 × 2.94) = 850 hours under Black-law scaling. The 10,000 hours is a declared baseline, not a general solder or interconnect lifetime; the prefactor must come from the actual process and test data.
H2: 10.5 Moisture and the popcorn failure
P: Plastic packages absorb moisture from the air. During reflow soldering, at peak temperatures near 250 °C, that moisture turns to steam within seconds, and the pressure can delaminate the mold compound from the die or lead frame, or crack the package outright, sometimes with an audible pop. IPC/JEDEC J-STD-020 classifies packages by moisture sensitivity level, MSL 1 to 6, according to how long they may sit in a factory at 30 °C and 60 % relative humidity before they must be soldered: unlimited for MSL 1, one year for MSL 2, 168 hours for MSL 3, 72 hours for MSL 4, and so on. J-STD-033 governs the dry packs, humidity indicator cards, and baking that reset the clock. Large BGAs and thin packages are usually MSL 3 or worse, which is why a production line tracks the floor life of every opened reel.
H2: Worked Example 10.3
P: Problem. A reel of MSL 3 parts is opened on Monday at 08:00 and the line will not reflow the last of them until the following Tuesday at 17:00. Are they within their floor life, and what must be done if not?
P: Solution. Monday 08:00 to the next Tuesday 17:00 is 8 days and 9 hours, 201 hours, beyond the 168-hour floor life of MSL 3. The remaining parts must be baked according to J-STD-033, typically for many hours at 125 °C for BGAs if the reel tolerates it, or at lower temperature for longer, before use; alternatively they can be returned to dry storage before the limit, which pauses or partially resets the clock.
H2: 10.6 Accelerated life tests and the Arrhenius factor
P: A product meant to last ten years cannot be tested for ten years, so reliability engineers run it hot and scale the result. For failure mechanisms driven by thermally activated chemistry, the rate rises with temperature as exp(−Ea/kBT), and the acceleration factor between a stress temperature Ts and a use temperature Tu is AF = exp[(Ea/kB)(1/Tu − 1/Ts)], with temperatures in kelvin. The standard high-temperature operating life test, HTOL, runs parts at 125 °C for 1000 hours under bias. The result depends strongly on the activation energy assumed, typically 0.7 eV for generic semiconductor mechanisms, and is meaningful only for mechanisms that the test actually activates. Solder fatigue, Section 10.2, needs temperature cycling instead, and moisture needs humidity tests, so a qualification plan combines several stresses rather than one.
EQ: AF = exp[(Ea / kB)(1/Tu − 1/Ts)],    kB = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K
H2: Worked Example 10.4
P: Problem. A 1000 hour HTOL test runs at 125 °C. With Ea = 0.7 eV, what use time at 55 °C does it represent?
P: Solution. Tu = 328.15 K and Ts = 398.15 K, so 1/Tu − 1/Ts = 5.36 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. Ea/kB = 0.7/(8.617 × 10⁻⁵) = 8124 K. AF = exp(8124 × 5.36 × 10⁻⁴) = exp(4.35) = 78. The test represents about 78,000 hours, close to nine years at 55 °C, for the mechanisms with that activation energy.
H2: 10.7 Vibration: the flight computer on a rocket
P: Launch vibration is a different kind of stress from temperature cycling: a board bends at its own resonance thousands of times a second for a few seconds, and the corner joints of a large package take the strain. Random vibration is specified as an acceleration spectral density in g²/Hz. Miles' equation gives the board's rms response at its first resonance fn with quality factor Q, Grms = √(π/2 · fn Q · PSD), and Steinberg's rules estimate the board's deflection and the deflection a component can survive about twenty million cycles of. Hobby and student rocketry boards meet these numbers too, and a shaker test of the finished flight computer is the honest check.
EQ: Grms = √(π/2 · fn · Q · PSD),   Q ≈ √fn,   Z(3σ) ≈ 3 × 9.8 Grms/fn² in
H2: Worked Example 10.5
P: Problem. A rocket flight computer is an 80 × 50 mm board, 1.6 mm thick, of 25 g with its parts, supported on all four edges, with an 18 mm BGA at its center. Take E = 18.6 GPa and ν = 0.12 for the laminate. Find the first resonance, the rms response to a declared 0.04 g²/Hz input, the 3σ deflection, and Steinberg's allowable deflection for the BGA (c = 1.75).
P: Solution. The plate stiffness is D = Et³/[12(1 − ν²)] = 18.6 × 10⁹ × (1.6 × 10⁻³)³/(12 × 0.986) = 6.44 N·m, and the mass per area 0.025/0.004 = 6.25 kg/m². A simply supported plate resonates at fn = (π/2)√(D/ρs)(1/a² + 1/b²) = 1.571 × 1.015 × (156 + 400) = 887 Hz. Steinberg's Q ≈ √fn = 29.8, so Grms = √(1.571 × 887 × 29.8 × 0.04) = 40.7 g. The 1σ deflection is 9.8 × 40.7/887² = 5.07 × 10⁻⁴ in, so 3σ is 0.039 mm. Steinberg's allowable is Z = 0.00022 B/(c t r √L) = 0.00022 × 3.15/(1.75 × 0.063 × 1 × √0.71) = 0.0075 in, 0.19 mm, with B = 3.15 in the board edge, t = 0.063 in, L = 0.71 in the package length, and r = 1 at the center. The margin is a factor of 4.9. Moving the board's supports inward or adding a center standoff raises fn and shrinks the deflection as 1/fn^1.5; heavy parts such as the battery belong near the supports.
P: Moisture, expansion, fatigue, and electromigration set the package's life. Chapter 11 returns to its electrical role and to the network of capacitors that keeps the supply quiet.
BENCH: Find the joint with the largest distance from the package, estimate its strain from Δα ΔT DNP/h, and compare the result with the real product profile. A fatigue estimate is a planning number; the qualification test is where the assembly gets to answer back. Bake moisture-sensitive parts whose floor life has expired before reflow. For a flight computer, check the board's first resonance against the vibration spectrum, and keep heavy parts and large BGAs away from the board's center, where it flexes most.
