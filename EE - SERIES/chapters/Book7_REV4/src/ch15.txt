H1: Chapter 15. Optical Interconnect and Co-Packaged Optics
P: Fiber carries light for kilometers with less loss than copper has in a meter of board at the same speed, but every time the signal crosses from electrons to photons and back, it pays in watts and decibels. In 2026 the industry is moving that crossing from a pluggable module at the front panel into the package itself.
P: Where you meet this: Every data-center switch and AI cluster, every pluggable transceiver, every co-packaged optics announcement, and the 940 nm laser in the VL53L1X time-of-flight sensor that a hobby robot uses to measure distance.
P: Copper wins on short links, within a package or across a board, and loses on long ones. At 200 Gbit/s per lane, a passive copper cable reaches only a meter or two before its loss exceeds the budget of Chapter 14, while an optical fiber carries the same signal kilometers with less than a decibel of loss. The AI clusters of 2026 connect tens of thousands of accelerators, and most of those connections are optical. Optics was named in Chapter 1 as one of the scarce inputs of the build-out, and its power is now a significant fraction of a cluster's. This chapter computes the energy cost of optical links, works an optical power budget, and explains why the industry is moving the optics from pluggable modules on the front panel into the package itself.
H2: 15.1 Copper and fiber
P: A copper channel's loss grows with frequency and length, as Chapter 14 showed. A standard single-mode fiber loses about 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm and less at 1550 nm, almost independent of the data rate over the distances inside a data center. Multimode fiber with 850 nm vertical-cavity lasers is cheaper for reaches up to about 100 m. The trade is the conversion: each optical link needs a laser or modulator, a photodetector, and the driver, amplifier, and signal-processing circuits around them, at both ends, and those cost power, money, and reliability.
H2: 15.2 Energy per bit
P: The power of an optical module divided by its data rate gives its energy per bit. Representative 2026 pluggable modules draw roughly 15 W for 800 Gbit/s and 25 W for 1.6 Tbit/s, about 19 and 16 pJ/bit; these are approximate figures that vary by vendor and reach. Much of that power goes not into the optics but into the electrical link from the switch or accelerator chip to the module's front-panel cage, the retiming and equalization of Chapter 14 at each end of a long board trace. Co-packaged optics place the optical engines on the same package as the switch or accelerator, replacing the long electrical path with a millimeter-scale connection, and target about 5 pJ/bit.
EQ: Energy per bit = Pmodule / data rate
H2: Worked Example 15.1
P: Problem. A cluster has 100,000 accelerators, each with a declared 16 optical transceivers of 1.6 Tbit/s once both ends of each link are counted. Find the optical power at 25 W per module and with co-packaged optics at 5 pJ/bit, and the annual energy saved.
P: Solution. There are 1.6 million transceivers. At 25 W each they draw 40 MW. At 5 pJ/bit, each 1.6 Tbit/s engine draws 1.6 × 10¹² × 5 × 10⁻¹² = 8 W, and the total is 12.8 MW, a saving of 27.2 MW, or about 238,000 MWh per year, before the cooling the saved power would have needed. By Book 6's waste arithmetic, those megawatts are a power plant.
P: Key idea. Inside a data center, the cost of an optical link is mostly the electronics around it. Moving the optics closer to the chip shortens the electrical path, and the energy per bit falls by a factor of two to three.
H2: 15.3 The optical power budget
P: An optical link must deliver enough power to the receiver to meet its sensitivity, the minimum average power for the target error rate, with margin for aging and temperature. The budget subtracts every loss from the transmitter's launch power: fiber attenuation, each connector, and, for co-packaged optics, the coupling between the silicon photonic chip and the fiber, which is often the largest single loss. Penalties for dispersion, reflections, and the PAM4 signaling of Chapter 14 are added as further decibels.
EQ: margin = Ptx − Σ losses − Psens

H2: Worked Example 15.2
P: Problem. A transmitter launches 0 dBm. The link has two connectors at 0.5 dB each, 2 km of single-mode fiber at 0.35 dB/km, and 3 dB of chip-to-fiber coupling loss. The receiver's sensitivity is −8 dBm. Find the received power and the margin. How long could the fiber be for a 1 dB margin?
P: Solution. The losses are 1.0 + 0.7 + 3.0 = 4.7 dB, so the received power is −4.7 dBm and the margin 3.3 dB. For a 1 dB margin the fiber may add 2.3 dB more, 6.6 km more, for about 8.6 km in total, though dispersion penalties, not loss, usually limit such a link first.
H2: 15.4 Co-packaged optics in practice
P: Co-packaged optics moves optical engines beside the switch or accelerator die, shortening high-speed electrical paths but concentrating new integration risks at the package. Fiber attachment needs sub-micrometer alignment and must survive reflow and the thermal cycling of Chapter 10. The optical engines sit beside a die dissipating hundreds of watts, while testing, repair, and yield become harder when an engine is bonded to an expensive switch. External laser-source arrangements address one part of this thermal and serviceability problem; Section 15.6 covers that subsystem. In 2026, co-packaged switch products were entering deployment, and the same techniques were being prepared for accelerators.
H2: 15.5 Why co-packaged optics saves power
P: A pluggable optical module sits at the front panel, and the switch chip must drive each lane across 20 to 30 cm of board to reach it, through a long-reach SerDes with heavy equalization and often a retimer in the module. Co-packaged optics moves the optical engine onto the switch package, so the electrical path shrinks to millimeters and the SerDes can be a simple short-reach link. Published system figures put pluggable modules with their host SerDes at roughly 15 pJ per bit in 2026 and co-packaged designs near 5 pJ/bit. At the 51.2 and 102.4 Tbit/s of current switch chips, that difference is hundreds of watts per switch. The obstacles are serviceability, since a failed laser can no longer be swapped from the front panel, which is why designs keep the lasers in a replaceable external light source, and the thermal coupling of temperature-sensitive optics to a hot switch die.
H2: Worked Example 15.3
P: Problem. A 51.2 Tbit/s switch uses optical I/O at 15 pJ/bit with pluggables or 5 pJ/bit with co-packaged optics. Find the I/O power in each case and the saving across 1000 switches.
P: Solution. 51.2 × 10¹² × 15 × 10⁻¹² = 768 W with pluggables; 256 W co-packaged. The 512 W saved per switch is 512 kW across 1000 switches, before cooling, about a megawatt-scale saving in a large AI cluster.
H2: 15.6 The external laser source
P: An external laser source keeps the lasers in a cool, replaceable pluggable module at the front panel and sends continuous light through fibers to modulators in the package. One laser can serve several lanes, improving serviceability and reducing heat near the optical engine. The added fibers and connectors introduce loss that must be made up with higher output power. With laser wall-plug efficiency often 10 to 20 percent including drive and temperature control, that extra optical power can cost several electrical watts.
EQ: P_electrical = N × P_lane / (η_coupling × η_wall-plug)
H2: Worked Example 15.4
P: Problem. An external laser source feeds 16 lanes, each needing 20 mW of light at the modulator. The path from the laser to the modulator loses 3 dB, and the laser's wall-plug efficiency is 15 %. Find the electrical power, and the energy per bit if each lane carries 200 Gbit/s.
P: Solution. The laser must emit 16 × 20 mW / 0.5 = 640 mW of light, and at 15 % efficiency it draws 640/0.15 = 4.3 W. The lanes carry 3.2 Tbit/s, so the laser costs 4.3/3.2 × 10¹² = 1.3 pJ/bit, about a quarter of a 5 pJ/bit co-packaged budget, which is why every decibel of coupling loss matters.
H2: 15.7 Laser safety and testing optical modules
P: Every product with a laser is classified under IEC 60825-1, whose classes run from Class 1, safe under all reasonably foreseeable use, through 1M, 2, 2M, 3R, and 3B to Class 4. The class depends on the accessible emission at the product's exposed aperture against limits that vary with wavelength, exposure time, and the apparent size of the source, so the same diode can be Class 1 behind one lens and Class 3B behind another. Pluggable transceivers, the dot projector of a phone's face recognition, and ST's VL53L1X time-of-flight sensor with its 940 nm VCSEL are designed as Class 1. That holds only as built: changing the driver current, the optics, or the duty cycle voids the classification. Near-infrared light is invisible and the eye does not blink at it, which is why the rule is to never look into an aperture or a fiber end.
P: A module's optical test measures the average power and the optical modulation amplitude, OMA = P1 − P0; the extinction ratio, ER = 10 log₁₀(P1/P0); the eye, against a mask for NRZ or by the TDECQ penalty for PAM4; the wavelength; and the receiver's sensitivity with a stressed eye. A calibrated optical power meter set to the right wavelength and clean connectors come first.
H2: Worked Example 15.5
P: Problem. A transmitter sends P1 = 1.2 mW for a one and P0 = 0.25 mW for a zero. Find the extinction ratio, the average power, and the OMA in dBm. If aging lowers the ER to 3 dB while the average power control keeps the average constant, how much OMA is lost?
P: Solution. ER = 10 log₁₀(1.2/0.25) = 6.8 dB. The average is 0.725 mW, −1.40 dBm, and the OMA is 0.95 mW, −0.22 dBm. With an ER of 3 dB at the same average, P1 = 2P0 and P1 + P0 = 1.45 mW, so P0 = 0.483 mW and P1 = 0.967 mW, an OMA of 0.483 mW, −3.16 dBm. The receiver sees 2.9 dB less signal although the power meter reads exactly the same, which is why a module is tested for OMA and ER, not average power alone.
P: Optics carries the bits between packages. Chapter 16 returns to sensors, with the motion sensors that let a device know where it is.
BENCH: Write the optical budget like an RF link: launch power minus connector, fiber, and coupling losses against the receiver's sensitivity, with margin. Clean every connector before you measure; a fingerprint on a ferrule costs more than a kilometer of fiber. Never look into a fiber or a laser aperture, even one that seems dark; infrared is invisible.
