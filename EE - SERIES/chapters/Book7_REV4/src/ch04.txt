H1: Chapter 4. Board and Package Substrate Materials: FR-4, ABF, Ceramics, and Glass
P: Two boards look identical: green, 1.6 mm thick, eight layers. One carries 28 GHz radar signals to its antennas and loses more than two-thirds of its power in five centimeters; the other loses a sixth as many decibels. The difference is a laminate you cannot see, chosen by a line on the fabrication drawing. Below the board, the package substrate makes the same choice again at ten times finer lines.
P: Where you meet this: Every fabrication drawing's material call-out, every laminate data sheet's Dk and Df table, every 77 GHz radar module, and every package that sits on ABF, LTCC, ceramic, or glass, including the ESP32-S3 and LoRa modules of the reader lab.
P: A printed circuit board is a stack of copper foils separated by dielectric laminate, and the laminate determines how fast signals travel, how much they are attenuated, how well the board handles heat, and how finely it can be built. FR-4, woven glass in epoxy resin, is the default material of electronics, cheap and well understood. This chapter shows when it stops being good enough and what to buy instead. The rule is simple: use FR-4 unless you have a reason, and the reasons are loss, stability of the dielectric constant, heat, or density.
H2: 4.1 Dielectric loss
P: A laminate is described by its relative permittivity εr, often called Dk, and its loss tangent tan δ, or Df. A wave on a line fully embedded in the dielectric, a stripline, loses power in the dielectric at a rate proportional to frequency, to √εr, and to tan δ. Because the loss grows linearly with frequency, a material that is fine at 1 GHz can be unusable at 28 GHz.
EQ: αd ≈ 8.686 π f √εr tan δ / c   dB/m   (stripline)
H2: Worked Example 4.1
P: Problem. Compare the dielectric loss at 10 GHz of a standard FR-4 with εr = 4.5 and tan δ = 0.020, and a low-loss hydrocarbon-ceramic laminate with εr = 3.55 and tan δ = 0.0037. Repeat at 28 GHz.
P: Solution. For FR-4, αd = 8.686 × π × 10¹⁰ × 2.12 × 0.020/2.998 × 10⁸ = 38.6 dB/m, or 0.98 dB per inch. For the low-loss laminate, 6.3 dB/m, or 0.16 dB per inch. At 28 GHz the figures are 108 and 17.8 dB/m. A 5 cm feed line at 28 GHz loses 5.4 dB in FR-4, leaving only 29% of the transmitter's power at the antenna, and 0.9 dB in the low-loss material. Book 4's reference laminate, with εr = 3.55, is this class of material.
H2: 4.2 Datasheet values and real boards
P: A laminate's Dk and Df depend on frequency, temperature, humidity, and the ratio of glass to resin in a particular construction, and they are measured by different methods that do not always agree. A material sold as FR-4 by two mills can differ in Df by a factor of two. The designer must therefore specify Dk and Df at the frequency of interest, in the actual construction, and ask the fabricator to confirm them. The copper matters too. At 10 GHz the skin depth is only 0.66 µm, so the roughness of copper foil, which is treated to adhere to the resin and may have features of 1 to 5 µm, lengthens the current path and can add as much loss as the dielectric. Smooth, very-low-profile foils are part of every low-loss specification.
P: For scale, Rogers' RO4003C data sheet gives a dielectric-constant temperature coefficient of +40 ppm/°C from −50 to 150 °C, in-plane thermal expansion of 11 to 14 ppm/°C and 46 ppm/°C through thickness, and 0.06% water absorption after 48 hours of immersion at 50 °C. Those are formulation- and test-specific values, not generic FR-4 coefficients; the water-absorption figure is not a slope per percentage point of relative humidity. A published SAC305 data set reports CTE from about 21 to 25 ppm/°C over its modeled temperature range, so a joint's strain depends on alloy, geometry, temperature swing, dwell, and board construction. Humidity-related joint and surface failures depend on bias, contamination, and moisture films; use the actual laminate data and temperature-humidity-bias qualification rather than a universal humidity coefficient.
P: Key idea. FR-4 is the default; buy better for loss, Dk stability, heat, or density. Specify Dk and Df at the frequency you care about, in the construction you will build, because the material's name is not a specification.
H2: 4.3 Glass weave and skew
P: In woven-glass laminates, glass has εr near 6 and the resin near 3, so a trace that runs along a glass bundle sees a higher effective permittivity than one over a resin-rich gap. In a differential pair, the two lines can then travel at different speeds, and the skew between them converts the differential signal partly into common mode, which radiates and closes the eye. Spread-glass and flat-glass weaves, angled routing, and low-Dk glass reduce the effect.
H2: Worked Example 4.2
P: Problem. The two lines of a differential pair see effective permittivities of 3.9 and 4.2 over 5 cm. Find the skew, and compare it with the unit interval of a 56 Gbaud link.
P: Solution. The delay difference is (√4.2 − √3.9) × 0.05/2.998 × 10⁸ = 12.4 ps. The unit interval at 56 Gbaud is 1/56 × 10⁹ = 17.9 ps, so the skew is 70% of a symbol, enough to close the eye of a 112 Gbit/s PAM4 link. At 10 Gbaud, with a 100 ps unit interval, the same skew would be tolerable.
H2: 4.4 Heat, density, and hybrids
P: FR-4's glass transition temperature, the point where the resin softens and its expansion rises sharply, is typically 130 to 180 °C, and repeated lead-free soldering near 250 °C stresses vias through the board's thickness. High-Tg and low-expansion materials are chosen for thick boards, many reflow cycles, and large packages. Fine-line, high-density interconnect boards use thin dielectrics and laser-drilled microvias. Many products use hybrid stack-ups: a low-loss laminate on the RF or high-speed layers bonded to FR-4 for the rest, which buys performance only where it is needed. Mixing materials requires care with expansion and lamination, and the fabricator must agree to it.
H2: 4.5 How much copper roughness costs
P: At high frequency the current crowds into the outermost skin depth of the conductor, 0.39 µm at 28 GHz, and if the copper surface has peaks and valleys taller than that, the current must follow them. The Hammerstad correction multiplies the smooth-conductor loss by 1 + (2/π) arctan[1.4(Δ/δ)²], where Δ is the RMS roughness, so the factor approaches 2 once the roughness is a few skin depths. More accurate models, such as Huray's snowball model, treat the nodules of electrodeposited foil as spheres, but the conclusion is the same. Board vendors therefore sell grades: standard foil with several micrometers of roughness, very-low-profile (VLP) foil near 1 µm, and hyper-very-low-profile (HVLP) foil of a few tenths of a micrometer, at progressively higher cost and with adhesion that the laminate chemistry must restore.
EQ: K_rough = 1 + (2/π) arctan[1.4 (Δ/δ)²]

H2: Worked Example 4.3
P: Problem. Find the roughness factor at 28 GHz for VLP foil with Δ = 1.0 µm and HVLP foil with Δ = 0.3 µm.
P: Solution. δ = 66/√(28 × 10⁹) mm = 0.394 µm. For VLP, (1.0/0.394)² = 6.44, and K = 1 + (2/π) arctan(9.0) = 1.93. For HVLP, (0.3/0.394)² = 0.58, and K = 1 + (2/π) arctan(0.81) = 1.43. The conductor loss of the HVLP trace is 26 % lower, a saving that can be worth several decibels on a long 112 Gbit/s channel.
H2: 4.6 Package substrates: the small boards between the chip and the board
P: Every package that holds more than a single die on a lead frame is built on a substrate, a small, fine-lined circuit board between the chip and the main board. Its job is to fan the chip's connections out from the chip's pitch to the board's, and in 2026 it does far more: it carries filters, antennas, passives, power delivery, heat paths, and sometimes light. The line widths fall into three rough bands. Wiring on the chip runs from tens of nanometers to a few micrometers; package substrates run from a few micrometers to a few tens; boards from about 40 µm for HDI to 150 µm for standard work. The ranges overlap and move every year, but the three orders of magnitude between the transistor's wiring and the board are why the substrate exists.

P: The reader lab's ESP32-S3 module is a small example. Under its metal can sit the ESP32-S3 chip, a quad-SPI flash (and PSRAM in some versions), a 40 MHz crystal, the radio's matching network, and a printed antenna, all on a small multilayer board that is itself the module's substrate. The can is part of the design: it shields the radio and the clock, and it is part of what the module's radio certification covered. A LoRa module such as an SX1276-based RFM95W is built the same way.
H2: 4.7 Organic substrates: ABF and BT
P: Ajinomoto Build-up Film, ABF, is the dominant substrate material for processors, GPUs, and AI accelerators. Thin films of epoxy-based dielectric are laminated on both sides of a glass-reinforced core, and copper lines are built up layer by layer by a semi-additive process with laser-drilled microvias, reaching 5 µm lines and spaces in volume. Ajinomoto lists its low-loss GL102 grade at εr = 3.3 and tan δ = 0.0044 at 5.8 GHz, with an in-plane expansion of 20 ppm/K below its glass transition. BT resin, bismaleimide-triazine, is the older, cheaper laminate for memory packages, RF modules, and consumer chips, with coarser lines. A shortage of ABF substrate capacity limited processor supply in 2021 and 2022, and the substrate is now planned as early as the wafer.
H2: 4.8 Ceramic and thin-film substrates
P: Ceramics trade cost for stability, low loss, hermeticity, and heat. Low-temperature co-fired ceramic, LTCC, is a stack of glass-ceramic tapes, each punched with vias and screen-printed with silver or gold, laminated and fired together near 850 °C. Because every layer is fired at once, inductors, capacitors, filters, baluns, and striplines can be buried inside, which is why LTCC carries GPS and Wi-Fi front ends, phone RF modules, and radar parts. High-temperature co-fired ceramic, HTCC, fires alumina near 1600 °C with tungsten or molybdenum conductors; it is hermetic and rugged and serves aerospace, military, and sensor packages. Plain 99.6 % alumina, εr ≈ 9.9 with very low loss, is the classic substrate for thin-film microwave hybrids and power amplifiers. Aluminum nitride conducts heat about six times better than alumina, near 170 W/(m·K), with an expansion of about 4.6 ppm/K that is close to silicon's and silicon carbide's, so it carries GaN amplifiers, laser diodes, and the power modules of Book 6. Thin-film processing, sputtered and plated metal patterned like a chip, puts lines of about 10 µm on alumina, AlN, or quartz for high-Q filters and space hardware.

H2: Worked Example 4.4
P: Problem. A 50 Ω microstrip carries 77 GHz radar signals over a 0.127 mm substrate to an antenna. Compare FR-4 (εr = 4.4, tan δ = 0.020), Corning EAGLE XG display glass (5.11, 0.013 at 70 GHz), Rogers RO3003 (3.00, 0.0010), and DuPont 9K7 LTCC (7.1, 0.0010). Find each line's width and its loss per centimeter with smooth copper, and the loss of a 3 cm feed.
P: Solution. The 50 Ω widths from the Hammerstad equations are 0.243, 0.217, 0.319, and 0.166 mm, with effective permittivities 3.33, 3.78, 2.42, and 5.01. The free-space wavelength is 3.89 mm. Dielectric loss is αd = 27.3 εr(εeff − 1) tan δ/[√εeff (εr − 1) λ₀]; for FR-4, 27.3 × 4.4 × 2.33 × 0.020/(1.825 × 3.4 × 3.89 mm) = 2.32 dB/cm. Copper's surface resistance at 77 GHz is Rs = √(πfµ₀ρ) = 0.072 Ω, and the conductor loss αc ≈ 8.686 Rs/(Z₀w) is 0.52 dB/cm on the FR-4 line. The totals are 2.83 dB/cm for FR-4, 2.20 for the display glass, 0.49 for RO3003, and 0.90 for the LTCC, whose narrow line makes conductor loss dominate. A 3 cm feed loses 8.5 dB on FR-4, 86 % of the power, against 1.5 dB on RO3003. Real copper is rough, so the conductor loss grows by the Hammerstad factor of Section 4.5. Radar boards therefore put the antenna and its feeds on a thin low-loss outer layer and use FR-4 inside, and 'glass' is a low-loss choice only in grades made for RF.

Gray is the smooth-copper conductor loss, color the dielectric loss. | Stacked bars of loss in decibels per centimeter for four substrates; the FR-4 bar is tallest at 2.83 and mostly red dielectric loss, the RO3003 bar shortest at 0.49.
H2: Worked Example 4.5
P: Problem. A GaN amplifier die of 1 × 2 mm dissipates 10 W into a 0.635 mm substrate on a cold base. Estimate the temperature rise across the substrate for aluminum nitride (k = 170 W/(m·K)), alumina (26), DuPont 9K7 LTCC (4.6), and FR-4 (0.3), with heat spreading at 45° through the thickness.
P: Solution. At 45° the footprint grows by the thickness on every side, from 1 × 2 mm to 2.27 × 3.27 mm. The geometric mean of the two areas is √(2 × 7.42) = 3.85 mm², so θ = t/(kA) = 0.635 × 10⁻³/(k × 3.85 × 10⁻⁶). AlN gives 0.97 K/W, a rise of 9.7 K; alumina 6.34 K/W, 63 K; LTCC 35.8 K/W, 358 K; FR-4 549 K/W, several thousand kelvin, which means it cannot be done. The same die on FR-4 needs a filled thermal-via field or an embedded copper coin; solid copper (k ≈ 400) would give 0.41 K/W. The die attach and the base add their own resistances, so these are the substrate's share only, and the reason GaN power amplifiers sit on AlN, copper, or composite carriers.

H2: 4.9 Interposers, fan-out, and passives inside the substrate
P: An interposer is a wiring layer between the dies and the substrate. Silicon interposers, made on older wafer lines, give sub-micrometer to 2 µm lines and through-silicon vias, and they carry the HBM stacks beside AI accelerators; their size is limited and their cost is high. Glass interposers and glass-core substrates are flatter, stable in size, available as large panels, and in low-loss grades better for millimeter waves; many in the industry expect glass to take over part of silicon's role, though that is an outlook, not a settled result. Fan-out wafer-level packaging, FOWLP, removes the substrate altogether: the dies are embedded in mold compound and the wiring is built directly on them, so the package is its own wiring board. Phones use it for processors, PMICs, and RF parts, and its embedded-wafer-level form, eWLB, carries automotive radar transceivers.
P: Passives are moving inside as well. Embedded-component substrates bury resistors, capacitors, and inductors in their layers. Integrated passive devices, IPDs, put capacitors, inductors, baluns, and matching networks on a small silicon or glass die that replaces dozens of discrete parts in a phone's front end. Thin-film lithium niobate, TFLN, is becoming a platform of its own for modulators, acoustic filters, and optical waveguides, and photonic interposers carry light in waveguides inside the substrate to the co-packaged optics of Chapter 15. Book 3 explains the piezoelectric and optical materials; Book 4 designs the filters and lines that live in them.
H2: Worked Example 4.6
P: Problem. An LTCC front-end module needs a 10 nH inductor. Estimate the inductance of a buried three-turn square spiral with an outer size of 1.0 mm and an inner size of 0.5 mm, using the modified Wheeler formula L = 2.34 µ₀ n² d_avg/(1 + 2.75ρ), and compare its surface footprint with a discrete 0402 inductor.
P: Solution. d_avg = (1.0 + 0.5)/2 = 0.75 mm and the fill ratio ρ = (1.0 − 0.5)/(1.0 + 0.5) = 0.333. L = 2.34 × 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 9 × 0.75 × 10⁻³/(1 + 0.917) = 1.99 × 10⁻⁸/1.917 = 10.4 nH. The spiral occupies about 1 mm² on an inner layer and none of the surface, while a 0402 part is 1.0 × 0.5 mm plus its pads, about 1.5 mm² of the top surface where the dies and filters must go. The buried coil's value depends on the tape's shrinkage and the printing, typically within a few percent after tuning, and its Q is lower than a wire-wound part's; for a matching network that is usually a good trade.
H2: 4.10 Which substrate for which product
P: Real products mix these materials. A phone's RF front-end module typically sits on a BT or LTCC base and holds an RF SOI switch, BAW or XBAR filters, and a GaAs HBT power amplifier, with IPDs for matching. A 77 GHz radar uses a low-loss laminate such as a PTFE-ceramic of the RO3003 class for its antennas, an eWLB or antenna-in-package transceiver, and alumina, AlN, or LTCC in some modules. An AI processor stacks the die and HBM on a silicon interposer over an ABF substrate. A photonic engine puts silicon photonics on a silicon or glass interposer beside the switch chip. The trend in all of them is the same: the substrate is turning from a wiring board into a functional layer that is nearly as sophisticated as the chips on it.
P: Looking ahead, and as the author's outlook rather than a ranking: ABF build-up, silicon and glass interposers, LTCC for RF, AlN, embedded passives, fan-out, thin-film microwave substrates, TFLN, and photonic interposers are the substrate technologies to watch.
P: Key idea. The package substrate is a board with lines ten times finer than the main board's, and its material is chosen by the same four questions: loss at the frequency of use, heat, expansion against the die, and how fine it can be built.
P: The board's and the package's materials set the loss per inch. Layout, Chapter 5, decides how the signals and their return currents use them.
BENCH: Specify the laminate by its Dk and Df at your frequency, in the construction you will build, and ask the fabricator for a stack-up with those values. For anything above about 10 GHz, also specify the copper foil's roughness. For a package or module substrate, ask the package house for its design rules and its measured loss, not only the material name.
P: On the Job
P: Package and substrate engineers sit between three teams: the chip designers who own the bump map, the board designers who own the ball map, and the package house that builds the substrate. Their tools are Cadence Allegro Package Designer or Siemens Xpedition Package Integrator, a 3D field solver such as Ansys HFSS or Cadence Clarity for the package's S-parameters, and warpage and thermal simulation. A typical task: route a 3000-ball flip-chip substrate for a new SoC, deliver its power and signal models to the chip and board teams, and negotiate with the substrate supplier about layer count, line and space, and the warpage at reflow temperature, which decides whether the corner balls will solder at all.
