H1: Chapter 14. Loss, Equalization, and the Eye
P: In 2026 a single serial lane carries 200 Gbit/s, and each symbol lasts about 9 ps, the time light takes to cross a fingernail. By the end of a board trace the symbol is smeared over its neighbors, and the receiver must rebuild it from an eye that looks closed. That it works at all is the result of a careful budget.
P: Where you meet this: Every PCIe, Ethernet, and HBM link; every FPGA's high-speed transceiver; every SFP or QSFP cage on a network card; and every eye diagram on a scope or in a simulation.
P: A serial link at 100 Gbit/s or more per lane sends symbols lasting less than 20 ps across centimeters of board, through packages, vias, and connectors. By the time a symbol arrives, the channel has attenuated its high-frequency content by tens of decibels and smeared it into its neighbors. The receiver recovers it with equalization and, when that is not enough, with forward error correction. This chapter puts numbers on the channel's loss, shows why four-level signaling pays a noise penalty, explains what the equalizers do, and assembles a loss budget that sets how far a lane can reach on a given laminate.
H2: 14.1 Baud rate, Nyquist frequency, and PAM4
P: A link sending symbols at a rate Rs has a Nyquist frequency of Rs/2, and its loss is usually specified there. Non-return-to-zero signaling, NRZ, carries one bit per symbol on two levels. Four-level pulse-amplitude modulation, PAM4, carries two bits per symbol on four levels, so it needs only half the symbol rate and half the Nyquist frequency for the same bit rate, where the channel's loss is much lower. The price is that its three eyes are each one-third as tall as an NRZ eye at the same swing, a penalty of 20 log₁₀3 = 9.5 dB in signal-to-noise ratio. Links from 50 Gbit/s per lane upward use PAM4, because at those rates the loss saved by halving the Nyquist frequency is worth more than 9.5 dB.
EQ: fNyquist = Rs / 2,    PAM4 penalty = 20 log₁₀ 3 = 9.5 dB
H2: Worked Example 14.1
P: Problem. A 106.25 Gbit/s Ethernet lane uses PAM4. Find its symbol rate, unit interval, and Nyquist frequency. What would the Nyquist frequency be with NRZ?
P: Solution. Two bits per symbol give 53.125 Gbaud, a unit interval of 18.8 ps, and a Nyquist frequency of 26.6 GHz. NRZ would need 106.25 Gbaud and a Nyquist frequency of 53.1 GHz, where the board's dielectric loss, which Chapter 4 showed grows linearly with frequency, would be twice as large in decibels, along with larger conductor, via, and connector losses.
H2: 14.2 The loss budget
P: A link standard specifies the total insertion loss allowed between transmitter and receiver at the Nyquist frequency. The budget is shared by the transmitter and receiver packages, the vias, connectors, and the board traces. The board's share sets the reach. On the low-loss laminates of Chapter 4, with εr = 3.4 and tan δ = 0.002, the dielectric loss at 28 GHz is only 9.4 dB/m, and the conductor loss, increased by copper roughness, is typically larger; a declared total of 0.9 dB per inch is a reasonable figure for a good stripline at that frequency. Standard FR-4 at the same frequency, with 108 dB/m of dielectric loss alone, would lose about 3.4 dB per inch.
H2: Worked Example 14.2
P: Problem. A channel has a 28 dB budget at 26.6 GHz. Packages, vias, and connectors take 8 dB. Find the trace reach on a low-loss laminate at a declared 0.9 dB per inch, and on FR-4 at 3.4 dB per inch.
P: Solution. The traces may lose 20 dB. On the low-loss laminate that is 20/0.9 = 22 inches, 56 cm, enough to cross a large switch board. On FR-4 it is 20/3.4 = 5.9 inches, 15 cm. That difference is why high-speed switch and accelerator boards use premium laminates, and why at 200 Gbit/s per lane designers increasingly route over twinaxial cables or move the optics into the package, as Chapter 15 describes.
P: Key idea. A high-speed link is designed to a loss budget at its Nyquist frequency. PAM4 halves the frequency at a 9.5 dB cost in noise, equalizers recover what loss removes, and forward error correction lets the link run at a raw error rate that would once have been unacceptable.
H2: 14.3 Equalization
P: Three equalizers work together. The transmitter's feed-forward equalizer is a short finite-impulse-response filter that pre-emphasizes transitions, boosting high frequencies before the channel attenuates them. The receiver's continuous-time linear equalizer is an analog filter with a peak near the Nyquist frequency, typically 10 to 20 dB, which restores the high frequencies but amplifies noise and crosstalk along with them. The decision-feedback equalizer subtracts the interference that already-decided symbols leave in the current one; because it uses decisions rather than the noisy signal, it removes interference without amplifying noise, but an error can propagate through it. Links at 100 Gbit/s and above usually digitize the received signal with a converter and perform much of the equalization digitally.
H2: 14.4 Error rates and forward error correction
P: For a binary decision with Gaussian noise, the error rate is Q(eye/2σ) in terms of the eye opening and the noise. An error rate of 10⁻¹² requires Q = 7.03, and 10⁻¹⁵ requires 7.94. Older links were specified to 10⁻¹² without correction. Modern Ethernet lanes use a Reed–Solomon forward error-correcting code, called KP4, that accepts a raw bit error rate of about 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ and corrects it to well below 10⁻¹², at a cost of some latency. A raw rate of 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ needs only Q = 3.49, so the code is worth 20 log₁₀(7.03/3.49) = 6.1 dB of signal-to-noise ratio against an uncoded 10⁻¹² target, most of the PAM4 penalty.

H2: Worked Example 14.3
P: Problem. A PAM4 receiver sees an eye 30 mV high with 4 mV rms of noise. Find Q and the approximate raw bit error rate. Would it meet the KP4 threshold?
P: Solution. Q = 30/(2 × 4) = 3.75, so the error rate per decision boundary is Q(3.75) = 8.8 × 10⁻⁵. With three boundaries, Gray coding, and two bits per symbol, the bit error rate is about 0.75 × 2 × 8.8 × 10⁻⁵/2 = 6.6 × 10⁻⁵, below the 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ threshold, so the link would work with the code. Without the code it would fail a 10⁻¹² target by a wide margin.
H2: 14.5 Channel operating margin
P: An eye diagram is an intuitive picture but a poor specification, because it depends on how long the simulation ran and which patterns it saw. The IEEE 802.3 standards for 100 and 200 Gbit/s per lane therefore define channel compliance by a single number, the channel operating margin, COM. A reference transmitter, receiver, and equalizer are applied to the channel's measured S-parameters; the tool computes the signal amplitude at the decision point and the statistical distribution of every impairment, residual intersymbol interference, crosstalk, jitter, and noise, at the target error rate, and reports their ratio in decibels. A channel passes if COM is at least 3 dB. Because the reference equalizer is fixed, COM measures the channel, not the cleverness of a particular SerDes, and both board designers and silicon vendors can test against it independently.
EQ: COM = 20 log₁₀(A_signal / A_noise)   ≥ 3 dB
H2: Worked Example 14.4
P: Problem. A channel's computed signal amplitude at the slicer is 30 mV and the combined noise and interference amplitude at the target error rate is 20 mV. Find COM, and say whether the channel passes.
P: Solution. COM = 20 log₁₀(30/20) = 3.5 dB, a pass with 0.5 dB to spare. Shortening the backplane's longest trace by an inch or moving to a lower-loss laminate are the usual ways to buy more.
H2: 14.6 Jitter and the timing budget
P: Noise closes the eye vertically; jitter closes it horizontally. Jitter is the deviation of each transition from its ideal time, and it is split into two kinds. Random jitter, RJ, comes from thermal and phase noise, is Gaussian, and is specified by its rms value; because a Gaussian has no bound, its contribution grows with the number of bits observed. Deterministic jitter, DJ, comes from bandwidth limits, reflections, crosstalk, and duty-cycle distortion, and is bounded. The dual-Dirac model combines them at a target bit error ratio as TJ = DJ + 2Q·RJ, where Q is the number of standard deviations that corresponds to that error ratio: 7.03 for 10⁻¹² and 4.75 for 10⁻⁶. Forward error correction, Section 14.4, lets PAM4 links run at raw error ratios near 10⁻⁵ or 10⁻⁶, which shrinks the random-jitter allowance substantially.
EQ: TJ(BER) = DJ + 2 Q(BER) RJ,    Q(10⁻¹²) = 7.03,    Q(10⁻⁶) = 4.75
H2: Worked Example 14.5
P: Problem. A 106.25 Gbit/s PAM4 lane runs at 53.125 GBd. Its transmitter has RJ = 0.3 ps rms and DJ = 3 ps. Find the unit interval, and the total jitter at error ratios of 10⁻¹² and 10⁻⁶ as fractions of the unit interval.
P: Solution. UI = 1/53.125 GHz = 18.8 ps. At 10⁻¹², TJ = 3 + 2 × 7.03 × 0.3 = 7.2 ps, 38 % of the UI. At 10⁻⁶, TJ = 3 + 2 × 4.75 × 0.3 = 5.9 ps, 31 %. The FEC's tolerance of a higher raw error ratio returns about 1.4 ps of timing margin.
H2: 14.7 Laying out an FPGA transceiver channel
P: An FPGA's serial transceivers are the high-speed links most engineers lay out themselves. The vendor gives the transceiver's equalization range, the package's loss, and the pin map; the board designer owns everything from the ball to the connector. The habits are this chapter's and Chapter 5's in one place: route each pair as a stripline next to a solid plane, keep it out of the glass-weave trouble of Section 4.3, put the AC-coupling capacitors in 0201 size with the plane cut out under their pads to lift the pad capacitance, back-drill or blind the vias so that no stub resonates near the Nyquist frequency, and simulate the whole channel with the vendor's models and the fabricator's stack-up before ordering boards.
H2: Worked Example 14.6
P: Problem. An FPGA drives a 25.78125 Gbit/s NRZ lane to an SFP28 cage. Suppose the transceiver tolerates 20 dB of loss at Nyquist and the package, via fields, and connector take 4 dB. With 0.5 dB/in of conductor loss, find the longest stripline on FR-4 (εr = 4.5, tan δ = 0.020) and on a low-loss laminate (3.4, 0.002).
P: Solution. Nyquist is 25.78125/2 = 12.89 GHz. FR-4's dielectric loss there is 8.686π × 12.89 × 10⁹ × 2.12 × 0.020/(2.998 × 10⁸) = 49.8 dB/m, 1.26 dB/in, so the total is 1.76 dB/in and 16 dB of budget reaches 9.1 in. The low-loss laminate's dielectric loss is 0.11 dB/in, a total of 0.61 dB/in and a reach of 26 in. An FPGA board whose cage sits more than about 20 cm from the FPGA therefore needs the better laminate, a retimer, or a shorter route, and the decision is made at the stack-up, before routing.
P: Electrical channels lose more per inch at every generation. Chapter 15 asks when light should replace them.
BENCH: Write the loss budget first: total allowed loss minus packages, connectors, and margin, divided by the loss per inch, gives the reach. Then decide the laminate. Use the FPGA vendor's channel analysis tools with the real stack-up and back-drill or avoid via stubs on every lane above about 10 Gbit/s.
P: On the Job
P: Hardware engineers who design FPGA and network boards spend much of their time on this chapter. Their tools are the FPGA vendor's transceiver wizard and IBIS-AMI models, a channel simulator such as Keysight ADS or Ansys SIwave, the fabricator's stack-up tool, and a high-bandwidth scope or bit-error-rate tester in the lab. A typical task: lay out a board with an AMD (Xilinx) or Altera FPGA driving four SFP28 or QSFP cages, simulate each lane's loss and crosstalk against the transceiver's equalization, and then bring the board up by running the FPGA's built-in eye scan on every lane before handing it to the firmware team.
