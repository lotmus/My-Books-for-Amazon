H1: Chapter 26. Conducted Emissions and the LISN
P: Below 30 MHz, conducted emissions on a mains lead are often the dominant compliance concern, though cables and structures can also radiate. The test measures disturbance voltage using a defined network and procedure; a few millivolts can matter, depending on frequency, detector, and limit.
P: Where you meet this: Every power supply's EMI filter, every product's mains inlet with its X and Y capacitors and common-mode choke, every touch-current test, and every medical device whose isolation barrier protects a patient.
P: Below 30 MHz, a product's wavelengths are too long for its own structure to radiate much, and interference travels instead along its power cord into the building's wiring, which then radiates. Conducted-emission limits therefore apply to the voltage the product puts back onto the mains from 150 kHz to 30 MHz. The switching converters of Book 6 are the usual offenders. This chapter explains the line impedance stabilization network that makes the measurement repeatable, separates differential and common-mode noise, and sizes a filter.
H2: 26.1 The LISN
P: The mains impedance at radio frequencies varies from building to building, so a measurement made at an outlet would not be repeatable. The line impedance stabilization network, LISN, of CISPR 16-1-2 sits between the mains and the product. It passes 50 or 60 Hz power through series inductors of 50 µH, blocks mains-side noise, and presents the product with a defined 50 Ω impedance on each line at radio frequencies, across which the receiver measures the noise voltage. The limits in CISPR 32 Class B are 66 to 56 dBµV quasi-peak from 150 to 500 kHz, falling with the logarithm of frequency, 56 dBµV quasi-peak and 46 dBµV average from 0.5 to 5 MHz, and 60 and 50 dBµV from 5 to 30 MHz.
EQ: VLISN (dBµV) = 20 log₁₀(Inoise × 50 Ω / 1 µV)
H2: 26.2 Differential and common-mode noise
P: Differential-mode noise is the converter's switching current drawn between line and neutral; its fundamental is at the switching frequency, and it is filtered by capacitors across the line, X capacitors, and by series inductance. Common-mode noise flows from both lines to protective earth through stray capacitance, above all from the switching node to the heat sink, and returns through the LISN's two 50 Ω resistors in parallel, 25 Ω. It is filtered by capacitors from each line to earth, Y capacitors, whose value is limited by touch-current safety rules, and by a common-mode choke. A current probe on the two lines together measures common mode; the LISN reading contains both.
H2: Worked Example 26.1
P: Problem. A power factor stage switches 400 V in 20 ns, and the switching node couples to the grounded heat sink through 20 pF. Find the common-mode current during each edge, and the voltage it would develop across the LISN's 25 Ω if unfiltered.
P: Solution. The ideal displacement-current estimate is I = C dv/dt = 20 pF x 400 V/20 ns = 0.40 A during the edge. Multiplying by the LISN's 25 ohm common-mode equivalent gives 10 V only in this simplified lumped model. It is not a predicted CISPR receiver reading: the real edge spectrum, coupling path, parasitics, LISN network, receiver bandwidth, and detector all matter. Do not compare this transient peak directly with a conducted-emissions limit. The calculation identifies a mechanism worth investigating; it does not certify a failure or a pass. The calculator, as ever, has not been appointed the receiver.
P: Key idea. Conducted emissions are measured as a voltage across the LISN's defined 50 Ω. Differential-mode noise is the converter's ripple current; common-mode noise is dv/dt driving stray capacitance to earth. Each has its own filter components.
H2: 26.3 Sizing a differential-mode filter
P: The filter needed is the difference between the unfiltered noise and the limit, plus a margin. A single LC stage above its corner frequency f0 attenuates at 40 dB per decade, so the corner must lie at f0 = fs/10^(A/40) for an attenuation A at the switching frequency fs. The inductor and capacitor follow from f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)). Because the limits begin at 150 kHz, a converter switching below that frequency has its first measured harmonic above it, which is one reason many offline converters switch near 65 or 100 kHz.
EQ: f0 = fs / 10^(A/40),   f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))

H2: Worked Example 26.2
P: Problem. A 500 kHz converter puts 50 mV of fundamental ripple on the LISN. The quasi-peak limit there is 56 dBµV. With 6 dB of margin, how much attenuation is needed, where must an LC filter's corner be, and what capacitance goes with a 4.7 µH inductor?
P: Solution. 50 mV is 94.0 dBµV, so the filter must give 94.0 − 56 + 6 = 44.0 dB at 500 kHz. The corner is 500 kHz/10^(44/40) = 39.7 kHz, and C = 1/((2π × 39.7 kHz)² × 4.7 µH) = 3.42 µF. The filter's own resonance must then be damped, or it can oscillate with the converter's negative input resistance, the problem Middlebrook described and Book 6's control loop must respect.
H2: 26.4 Layout of the filter
P: A filter's attenuation in the datasheet assumes that its input and output are isolated from each other. On a real board, a filter placed in the middle of the converter, with its input trace running past the switching node, is bypassed by stray capacitance and mutual inductance, and its attenuation at 10 MHz can collapse by 20 dB or more. Filters belong at the power entry, with the noisy and clean sides physically separated and the Y capacitors returned directly to chassis. The same rule applies to I/O filters for radiated emissions: a filter that is not at the boundary becomes part of the antenna.
H2: 26.5 Y-capacitor current estimates and safety limits
P: A Y capacitor connected across an isolation boundary can contribute to touch current. For a sinusoidal source and ideal capacitor, I = 2 pi f C V estimates the current magnitude; it is a screening calculation, not a safety measurement. Do not infer a universal permissible current from the ES1 energy-source class in IEC 62368-1 or carry limits over from withdrawn IEC 60950-1. The applicable product standard, equipment class, protective-earth arrangement, normal and single-fault conditions, frequency weighting, and prescribed measuring network determine the limit and test. IEC 60990:2016 describes touch-current measurement methods but explicitly does not set limit values. Use the applicable adopted edition and have the safety design reviewed before fixing Y-capacitance. The choke can supply additional common-mode attenuation, but it does not remove the need to verify touch current.
EQ: I_touch = 2π f C V
H2: Worked Example 26.3
P: Problem. Estimate the ideal sinusoidal current through a 4.7 nF capacitor at 230 V, 50 Hz, and at 264 V, 60 Hz. For a purely mathematical exercise, what capacitance corresponds to 0.25 mA under the latter assumptions? This chosen number is not a universal regulatory limit.
P: Solution. At 230 V, 50 Hz, the ideal estimate is I = 2 pi x 50 x 4.7 nF x 230 V = 0.34 mA. At 264 V, 60 Hz, it is 0.47 mA. Solving the exercise's assumed 0.25 mA value gives C = 0.25 mA / (2 pi x 60 x 264 V) = 2.5 nF. These figures ignore the prescribed touch-current measuring network, waveform, fault configuration, and component tolerances; they cannot establish product compliance or select a safe component by themselves.
H2: 26.6 Sizing the common-mode choke
P: Once the applicable safety review has established which Y-capacitance values are permitted, the common-mode choke can supply the remaining common-mode attenuation. For an ideal common-mode model the two capacitors act in parallel, from both lines together to earth, and the choke’s common-mode inductance forms a second-order low-pass filter with them, falling at 40 dB per decade above its corner. The choke is wound so that line and neutral currents, equal and opposite, cancel their flux in the core, while common-mode current flowing the same way in both windings sees the full inductance. High-permeability ferrite cores can provide millihenries in a small part, but permeability and inductance fall at high frequency, and inter-winding capacitance eventually bypasses the choke. Practical filters therefore need measured insertion loss and attention to damping and parasitics.
EQ: f₀ = 1 / (2π √(L_CM × 2C_Y))
H2: Worked Example 26.4
P: Problem. For an idealized common-mode filter model, use two 2.2 nF capacitors in parallel and target a 50 kHz corner. Find the required inductance and the ideal second-order attenuation at 500 kHz. Treat these capacitances as model inputs only; the calculation does not show that they are allowed or safe in a product.
P: Solution. In the ideal filter model, the two capacitances sum to 4.4 nF. L = 1/((2 pi x 50 x 10^3)^2 x 4.4 x 10^-9) = 2.3 mH. At 500 kHz, one decade above the corner, an ideal second-order filter gives about 40 dB attenuation. This ignores source and load impedance, component loss, choke parasitics, layout, and the actual line/earth topology; measure the assembled filter and assess safety separately.
H2: 26.7 Isolation barriers: patients, optocouplers, and creepage
P: Some circuits must be separated from the mains, or from each other, by an insulation barrier: a person touching the low-voltage side, or a patient wired to a sensor, must be protected even if one part fails. The barrier is crossed by transformers, optocouplers, and digital isolators, and its capacitance and its distances on the board are both specified. The capacitance sets the leakage current; the distances set whether the barrier survives the voltages it will see.
H2: Worked Example 26.5
P: Problem. In an idealized model, an EEG headband's isolated DC-DC converter contributes 10 pF and its digital isolator 2 pF across a barrier. Estimate the 60 Hz displacement current for 264 V across 12 pF. Explain why this calculation cannot establish patient-current compliance under IEC 60601-1.
P: Solution. The ideal sinusoidal estimate is I = 2 pi x 60 x 12 pF x 264 V = 1.19 uA. That value is not a patient-leakage test result and cannot be compared directly with an IEC 60601-1 limit. Patient-current measurements use prescribed networks and operating/fault configurations; the allowed value depends on the applied-part classification and relevant clauses. Means of patient protection, insulation tests, creepage, and clearance depend on the design and applicable collateral or particular standards. No single voltage or distance follows from this capacitance calculation. This example illustrates coupling only; it does not establish that a circuit is safe to connect to a person.
P: Safety: anything connected to a person
P: A hobby EEG, ECG, or EMG build is not a medical device and must never be connected to a mains-powered computer, charger, or scope while its electrodes are on someone. Run it from a battery and send the data by Bluetooth Low Energy or Wi-Fi, or through a certified isolated USB link. Never use such a device on a person with a pacemaker or implanted electronics, and make no medical claims from its readings.
P: Where a signal must cross from the mains to the user's side, an optocoupler or digital isolator carries it across a gap that a person's safety depends on. Two distances define the gap on the board: clearance, the shortest path through air, set by the transient overvoltages that can jump it, and creepage, the shortest path along the surface, set by the working voltage, the pollution degree, and the tracking resistance of the insulating material. The insulation coordination tables of IEC 60664-1, which product standards such as IEC 62368-1 build on, give the minimums; reinforced insulation, the single barrier allowed between mains and a touchable circuit, needs twice the creepage of basic insulation and the clearance of the next higher impulse voltage. A slot milled through the board under the part lengthens the creepage path when the pads are too close.
H2: Worked Example 26.6
P: Problem. A flyback supply for 230 V mains sends its feedback across the barrier through a PC817 optocoupler, a 4-pin DIP whose data sheet gives 5.0 kV rms of isolation. The board is FR-4 of material group IIIa, in pollution degree 2, overvoltage category II. Using IEC 60664-1, find the creepage and clearance needed for reinforced insulation at a 250 V working voltage, and check the board between the DIP's two rows of pads, 7.62 mm apart center to center with 1.8 mm pads.
P: Solution. For 250 V, pollution degree 2, and material group III, basic insulation needs 2.5 mm of creepage, so reinforced insulation needs 5.0 mm. Overvoltage category II at 230 V means a 2.5 kV impulse, needing 1.5 mm of clearance for basic insulation; reinforced uses the next step, 4 kV, and 3.0 mm. Between the pad rows the surface distance is 7.62 − 1.8 = 5.8 mm, which meets both. Any trace, via, or copper pour on the board that comes closer than 5.0 mm to the other side must be removed, and the transformer and the Y capacitor across the barrier must meet the same distances. The product standard has the final word and may add requirements, so the safety engineer checks the layout against it before the design is released.
P: Emissions are what a product does to its surroundings. Chapter 27 turns the question around: what the surroundings do to the product.
BENCH: Measure with a LISN, and separate the differential and common-mode parts with a noise separator if you have one. Size the Y capacitors from the touch-current or patient-leakage budget first, then let the common-mode choke supply the rest. For anything connected to a person, power it from a battery during development and never connect a mains-powered scope ground to a patient circuit.
