H1: Chapter 27. Immunity, and the Second Report for Radios
P: A finger touches a USB connector after a walk across a carpet. For a nanosecond, 30 amperes flow into the product. Whether it keeps working depends on a diode the size of a grain of rice and on how many millimeters of trace lie between it and the connector.
P: Where you meet this: Every USB-C port, every sensor cable, every TVS diode array on a data line, every surge suppressor, and every smart thermostat that must keep running when the furnace relay clicks next to it.
P: Emissions are half of EMC. The other half is immunity: the product must keep working when exposed to the disturbances of its environment, from a static spark off a finger to a lightning surge on the mains and the field of a nearby transmitter. The IEC 61000-4 family defines the test methods, and product standards such as CISPR 35 set the levels and the pass criteria. A product that contains a radio transmitter needs more: a separate radio-conformance report that proves its transmitter meets spectrum rules. This chapter covers the main immunity tests with their numbers and draws the line between the EMC report and the radio report.
H2: 27.1 Electrostatic discharge
P: IEC 61000-4-2 models a charged human touching the product with a 150 pF capacitor discharged through 330 Ω. In contact discharge the generator's current reaches its first peak in about 0.8 ns, at about 3.75 A per kilovolt, followed by a slower tail. Test levels for contact discharge are 2, 4, 6, and 8 kV, and for air discharge 2, 4, 8, and 15 kV; many product standards ask for 4 kV contact and 8 kV air. A nanosecond edge of tens of amperes means that the inductance of every path matters: the discharge current must be steered to chassis or through a clamp to the ground plane, never through the logic.
EQ: Ipeak ≈ 3.75 A/kV × V,   V = L dI/dt
H2: Worked Example 27.1
P: Problem. Find the first-peak current of a 4 kV contact discharge, and the voltage it develops across 10 nH of trace if it rises in 1 ns.
P: Solution. Ipeak = 3.75 × 4 = 15 A. V = 10 nH × 15 A/1 ns = 150 V across a few centimeters of trace. A clamp diode placed at the connector, with a short, wide path to the plane, keeps most of that voltage away from the chip; the same clamp placed a few centimeters further in is nearly useless.
H2: 27.2 Fast transients and surge
P: IEC 61000-4-4, the electrical fast transient test, injects bursts of 5/50 ns pulses, repeated at 5 kHz or 100 kHz, onto power and signal lines, imitating the arcing of relay contacts and switches. IEC 61000-4-5, the surge test, imitates lightning and power switching: the combination-wave generator produces a 1.2/50 µs voltage into an open circuit and an 8/20 µs current into a short, with an effective source impedance of 2 Ω for line-to-line tests. A 1 kV surge can therefore drive 500 A into a short; metal-oxide varistors, gas discharge tubes, and TVS diodes must absorb it without letting the voltage at the protected circuit exceed its rating.
EQ: Isurge ≈ Vsurge / 2 Ω  (line to line)
H2: Worked Example 27.2
P: Problem. A 2 kV line-to-line surge is applied to a power input protected by a varistor that clamps at 600 V. Estimate the current and the energy the varistor absorbs, approximating the current as an 8/20 µs pulse with an effective duration of 20 µs.
P: Solution. The current is about (2000 − 600)/2 = 700 A. The energy is roughly 600 V × 700 A × 20 µs = 8.4 J, within the rating of a medium-sized varistor. The capacitor or converter behind it must tolerate 600 V for those microseconds, which sets its own voltage rating.
P: Key idea. Immunity tests inject defined disturbances, ESD, fast bursts, surges, and RF fields, and the product must keep working to a stated criterion. Every injected current must have a short, intended path to chassis or a clamp.
H2: 27.3 Radiated and conducted RF immunity
P: IEC 61000-4-3 exposes the product to a uniform field, typically 3 V/m or 10 V/m, swept from 80 MHz to 6 GHz in a chamber with absorber on the floor, amplitude-modulated 80% at 1 kHz. The modulation raises the peak field by a factor of 1.8 and imitates the audio-rate envelope that disturbs analog circuits. Below 80 MHz, where wavelengths are too long for chamber exposure, IEC 61000-4-6 injects the RF into the cables through coupling-decoupling networks, at 3 V or 10 V of source voltage. Rectification of RF in the junctions of op-amps, Book 2's devices, is the usual failure mechanism, and small capacitors at the inputs are the usual cure.
H2: Worked Example 27.3
P: Problem. A product is tested at 10 V/m with 80% AM. Find the peak field.
P: Solution. With 80% modulation the envelope's peak is 1.8 times the unmodulated level, so the peak field is 18 V/m. Pass criteria are written against the product's function: criterion A, normal operation during the test; B, temporary degradation with self-recovery; C, loss of function that the user must restore.
H2: 27.4 The second report: radio conformance
P: A product with a transmitter is also a radio, and the radio rules are a separate legal requirement. In the European Union the Radio Equipment Directive requires conformance in three areas: health and safety, including RF exposure; EMC, usually shown with ETSI EN 301 489; and efficient use of spectrum, shown with radio standards such as EN 300 328 for 2.4 GHz wideband transmitters. In the United States, the digital circuitry falls under Part 15 Subpart B, while the transmitter is certified under Subpart C or another rule part, through a Telecommunication Certification Body, with its own RF-exposure evaluation of the kind Book 4's Chapter 33 describes. The EMC report and the radio report are different documents, from different test setups, and a product needs both.
H2: 27.5 Choosing a TVS diode for a signal line
P: Surges on signal and data lines are applied through a larger coupling impedance than on mains lines. For unshielded lines IEC 61000-4-5 adds 40 Ω to the generator's 2 Ω, so the current is far smaller than on a power input, but the protected circuit is also far more fragile. A TVS diode conducts above its breakdown voltage V_BR and behaves, once conducting, roughly like a voltage source V_BR in series with a dynamic resistance R_dyn. The clamping voltage is then V_C = V_BR + I_PP R_dyn, and that voltage, not the breakdown voltage, is what the protected pin sees. Choosing a TVS means meeting four conditions at once: its stand-off voltage must exceed the line's highest working voltage so it does not leak in normal use; its clamping voltage at the surge current must stay below the protected part's absolute maximum; its peak pulse power rating for an 8/20 µs pulse must exceed V_C × I_PP; and its capacitance must be small enough not to load the signal, which for multi-gigabit lines means a fraction of a picofarad and a different class of device.
EQ: I_PP ≈ (V_surge − V_C) / 42 Ω,    V_C = V_BR + I_PP R_dyn,    P_PP = V_C I_PP

H2: Worked Example 27.4
P: Problem. A 24 V industrial input is protected by a TVS with V_BR = 30 V and R_dyn = 0.5 Ω. A 1 kV surge is applied through 42 Ω. Estimate the surge current, the clamping voltage, and the peak power the TVS must handle.
P: Solution. Taking V_C near 41 V, I_PP ≈ (1000 − 41)/42 = 22.8 A. Then V_C = 30 + 22.8 × 0.5 = 41.4 V, consistent with the estimate. The peak power is 41.4 × 22.8 = 944 W, so the TVS needs an 8/20 µs rating of at least 1 kW, and the input circuit behind it must tolerate about 42 V for 20 µs.
H2: Worked Example 27.5
P: Problem. The series' smart thermostat has a USB-C service port at 5 V and an ESP32-S3 Wi-Fi radio module with an FCC grant, installed under the grant's conditions. A TVS on VBUS has V_BR = 6.0 V and R_dyn = 0.3 Ω (example figures). Find its clamping voltage at the first peak of an 8 kV contact discharge, say what the USB 2.0 data lines need, and list what the module's grant does not cover.
P: Solution. An 8 kV contact discharge peaks near 3.75 × 8 = 30 A, so V_C = 6.0 + 30 × 0.3 = 15 V, which the regulator behind it must tolerate for nanoseconds, and only if the TVS's ground via is short. D+ and D− at 480 Mbit/s need an array with under about 1 pF per line, or the eye closes. The module's grant covers its radio, used as the grant allows, with its own antenna or an approved one and the 'Contains FCC ID' label. The thermostat itself still needs Part 15 Subpart B for its digital circuitry and display, its own ESD and immunity tests, and in the EU an assessment of the finished product under the Radio Equipment Directive, for which the module's reports can be reused.
H2: Worked Example 27.6
P: Problem. The reader lab's BME280 is moved to the end of a 0.5 m cable for outdoor readings, on I²C at 400 kHz from 3.3 V. Fast-mode I²C allows a 300 ns rise time, given by tr = 0.8473 R C for a resistive pull-up, and a 3 mA sink current at 0.4 V. With 4.7 kΩ pull-ups, cable capacitance of 50 pF/m, and 10 pF of pins, how much TVS capacitance can each line take? What changes with 2.2 kΩ?
P: Solution. With 4.7 kΩ, C ≤ 300 ns/(0.8473 × 4700) = 75 pF. The cable takes 25 pF and the pins 10 pF, leaving 40 pF for the TVS, so a low-capacitance array of a few picofarads fits easily, while a generic 100 pF TVS would break the bus. With 2.2 kΩ the bus may carry 161 pF, which leaves room for a longer cable; the pull-up current is then 3.3/2.2 kΩ = 1.5 mA, within the 3 mA sink limit, which allows pull-ups down to (3.3 − 0.4)/3 mA = 967 Ω. A cable that leaves the enclosure also needs the clamp at the connector, as Section 27.1 explains.
P: Immunity tests and emission tests are both measurements against limits, and every measurement is made on a few units. Chapter 28 asks how those few units speak for production, and how the simulation flow and the day in the lab fit together.
BENCH: Put the clamp at the connector, with its ground going straight to the chassis or return plane through vias, before the signal reaches anything else. Choose the TVS by its capacitance first on fast lines and by its clamping voltage at the test current on power lines. Test with an ESD gun on a ground plane exactly as the standard describes; a hand spark proves nothing.
P: On the Job
P: The series' professional reference product, a smart thermostat, meets this chapter at its connector, its relays, and its radio. An EMC engineer at a thermostat company uses an ESD gun, a burst and surge generator, a TEM cell or chamber, and the radio module maker's certification documents. A typical task: the thermostat resets when an ESD gun hits its USB-C port; the engineer finds that the TVS's ground runs through a long trace, moves it beside the connector with two vias to the plane, re-runs the test at 8 kV, and documents the fix for the certification lab. Learn the mechanisms here; at work you buy the TVS array and the pre-certified module and spend your time on where they sit.
