H1: Chapter 16 — Algebra for Circuit Analysis
P: Most circuit problems are not hard because of physics. They are hard because someone solved for the wrong variable on line two. An engineer who can rearrange a formula quickly and check it with units will beat a slower one with a fancier simulator on most days.
P: Where you meet this: Choosing a resistor for a target gain, solving a divider for an unknown, picking a time constant, and checking a simulator's output.
P: Circuit analysis produces equations, and equations must be rearranged. The algebra involved is not difficult, but a few specific manipulations recur so often in electrical engineering that they are worth practicing until they are automatic — because an engineer who has to stop and think about them loses the thread of the circuit while doing so.
H2: 16.1 Rearranging for Any Variable
P: Ohm's law, V = IR, is three formulas in one. Solving for each in turn gives I = V/R and R = V/I. Every constitutive relation in this series works the same way, and you should be able to move between forms without hesitation.
P: The general procedure is to isolate the target variable by applying inverse operations to both sides, working outward from the target. What makes engineering formulas occasionally awkward is that the target often appears inside a reciprocal, inside a square root, or in more than one place at once.
WE: Worked Example 16.1
P: The parallel resistance of two resistors is 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂. Given R and R₁, solve for R₂.
P: Start by isolating the term containing R₂:
P: 1/R₂ = 1/R − 1/R₁
P: Combine the right-hand side over a common denominator:
P: 1/R₂ = (R₁ − R)/(R·R₁)
P: Now invert both sides — and this is the step people rush:
P: R₂ = (R·R₁)/(R₁ − R)
P: Inverting both sides of an equation is legitimate provided neither side is zero, but note that you must invert each side as a whole. A frequent error is to invert term by term, turning 1/R − 1/R₁ into R − R₁, which is wrong. The reciprocal of a difference is not the difference of the reciprocals.
P: Check the result: if R₁ = R, the denominator vanishes and R₂ is infinite, which is correct — if the parallel combination already equals one of the resistors, the other must be an open circuit.
MIS: The reciprocal of a sum is not the sum of the reciprocals. 1/(a + b) ≠ 1/a + 1/b. This is the single most common algebraic error in parallel-impedance work, and it is worth testing yourself on numbers once: 1/(2 + 2) = 0.25, while 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. The two are not close. The parallel-resistance formula is stated as 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ precisely because it is the reciprocals that add, not the resistances.
H2: 16.2 The Product-Over-Sum Shortcut
P: For exactly two resistors in parallel, the formula above rearranges to a form worth memorizing:
EQ: R = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)
P: This is faster than the reciprocal form for hand calculation, and it generalizes to any two impedances in parallel, which is why it recurs constantly in later books. Its limitation is that it applies to exactly two elements. For three or more you must either use the reciprocal form or apply product-over-sum repeatedly, combining two at a time.
P: Two special cases are worth having at your fingertips. Two equal resistors in parallel give half the value. A resistor in parallel with one ten times larger gives approximately 91% of the smaller value — near enough to the smaller value that in a first-pass estimate the larger one can often be ignored entirely.
H2: 16.3 Ratios, Dividers, and Fractions
P: The voltage divider is the most-used formula in electronics, and it is pure algebra:
EQ: V_out = V_in · R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
P: What makes it worth dwelling on is the structure: the output is the input scaled by a dimensionless ratio between zero and one. Recognizing this shape lets you read a divider off a schematic without writing anything down. If the two resistors are equal, the output is half the input. If R₂ is nine times R₁, the output is nine-tenths of the input.
P: The current divider has the complementary structure, and the thing to notice is that the resistances swap:
EQ: I₁ = I_total · R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
P: Current through R₁ is proportional to the other resistance. This is not a typographical accident — current preferentially takes the path of lower resistance, so the branch current is larger when its own resistance is smaller, which requires the opposite resistance in the numerator.
KEY: Voltage divides in proportion to resistance; current divides in inverse proportion. The voltage-divider numerator is the resistance you are measuring across. The current-divider numerator is the other resistance. Mixing these up is the most common error in first-year circuit analysis, and the check is always the limiting case: as one resistance goes to zero, does the formula give what physics demands?
H2: 16.4 Quadratics and Where They Come From
P: Quadratic equations appear in circuit work whenever power is involved, since power is proportional to the square of voltage or current, and whenever a second-order system's characteristic equation is written down. The solution is the familiar formula:
EQ: x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)] / 2a
P: The discriminant b² − 4ac carries the physical meaning. Positive discriminant gives two distinct real roots — in a second-order circuit, the overdamped case. Zero gives one repeated root, the critically damped case. Negative gives two complex conjugate roots, the underdamped case, which oscillates. Chapters 21 and 22 make the last of these tractable; for now, note only that a negative discriminant is not an error but a signal that the system rings.

H2: 16.5 Approximations Worth Knowing
P: Engineers approximate constantly, and two approximations do most of the work. The first is the binomial approximation: for small x,
EQ: (1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nx
P: So √(1 + x) ≈ 1 + x/2 and 1/(1 + x) ≈ 1 − x when x is small. "Small" here means roughly below 0.1, where the error stays under about 1%.
P: The second is the observation that when two quantities differ greatly in size, the smaller can often be dropped. In R₁ + R₂ with R₂ a hundred times R₁, dropping R₁ costs 1%. Whether 1% matters depends on the tolerance of the components, which is exactly the kind of judgment Chapter 15 was preparing you to make: there is no point computing to 0.01% with 5% resistors.
BENCH: Before trusting a rearranged formula, test it with numbers you can do in your head: set R1 = R2 and see whether a divider gives exactly half. Limits are cheap tests too. What happens when a resistor goes to zero, or to infinity? If the answer is absurd, the algebra is wrong.
H3: Practice Problems
LI: Solve V = IR for R, and evaluate for V = 3.3 V and I = 150 µA.
LI: Three resistors of 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ, and 3 kΩ are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent resistance, first with the reciprocal formula and then by applying product-over-sum twice, and confirm the two agree.
LI: A voltage divider must produce 2.5 V from a 9 V supply. If R₂ = 10 kΩ, find R₁.
LI: Use the binomial approximation to estimate √(1.06) and 1/0.98, then compare each with the exact value and state the percentage error.
