H1: Chapter 3 — The Electric Field
P: A thundercloud a kilometer overhead can make the hair on your arms stand up. Nothing touches you; the cloud reaches you through its electric field. Engineers worry about the same thing on a far smaller scale, such as the field across a transistor's gate insulator, only a few nanometers thick.
P: Where you meet this: Capacitive touchscreens, the voltage rating of every capacitor, creepage and clearance spacing on mains boards, and the rounded edges on high-voltage hardware.
H2: 3.1 Why Introduce a Field at All
P: Rub a balloon on your hair, as in Chapter 1, and bring it near a second balloon charged the same way: they push apart, and Coulomb's law from Chapter 2 says exactly how hard. Bring in a third balloon. A fourth. Coulomb's law still handles every pair, one at a time — but nothing in it describes the empty patch of air in the middle of the room, the spot where no balloon happens to be floating yet. Ask what force acts there and the question has no subject: a force is something one charged object exerts on another, and an empty point in space is not an object.
P: So the question has to change. Not “what force acts on this balloon,” which only makes sense once a balloon is actually there, but “what is the condition of that empty spot, ready and waiting, before any balloon arrives to feel it?” Coulomb's law cannot answer that on its own terms: it names two charges and falls silent about everywhere else. Whatever answer works has to live at points that, for the moment, hold no charge at all.
P: The way around it looks almost too simple to be the whole story, and turns out to be exactly that. Stop asking the empty point to supply a force on its own. Ask it instead for a readiness — a number saying how hard a charge would be pushed if one were placed there, without yet committing to how big that charge is. Bring a small test charge to the point, measure the force it feels, and divide the force by the size of the charge.
P: Try it with an ever-smaller test charge, near a fixed source charge of +8.0 nC, 5.0 cm away. Shrink the test charge by a factor of ten at each step — 1.0 nC, then 0.10 nC, then 0.010 nC, then 0.0010 nC — and the force it feels shrinks by that same factor of ten each time: about 2.9×10⁻⁵ N, then 2.9×10⁻⁶ N, then 2.9×10⁻⁷ N, then 2.9×10⁻⁸ N. Divide each force by its own test charge, though, and the factor of ten cancels every time: all four pairs give the same quotient, about 2.9×10⁴ N/C.
P: Notice what that quotient never does: it never depends on which test charge produced it. Whatever fixes that number is a property of the point itself — 5.0 cm from that particular source charge — a readiness sitting there whether or not a test charge happens to be at that point to notice it.
P: This is more than philosophical tidiness. The field concept is what lets electromagnetism handle radiation, time delay, and energy storage in empty space — none of which fit comfortably into an “action at a distance” picture. If a source charge is suddenly moved, a distant test charge does not respond instantaneously; the change in the field propagates outward at the speed of light (Chapter 12), and until it arrives, the test charge continues to respond to where the source charge used to be. Coulomb's law alone, treated as a direct force between two charges, has no room in it for this kind of delay; the field, treated as a physically real thing occupying the space between them, does. By the time you reach the RF and antenna volumes of this series, the field will have become the primary object of study, with charges relegated to being merely its sources.
H2: 3.2 Defining the Electric Field
P: That readiness has a name: the electric field. It is exactly what the previous section's quotient was already computing, defined operationally: place a small positive “test charge” q₀ at a point, measure the force F on it, and divide by q₀.
EQ: E = F / q0
P: The field is a vector, pointing in the direction of the force that would be felt by a positive test charge. Its units are newtons per coulomb (N/C), equivalently volts per meter (V/m) once potential is introduced in Chapter 5. The test charge is taken to be small enough that it does not itself disturb the charge distribution being measured — a limiting idealization, since strictly any nonzero q₀ will push the source charges around a little if they are free to move.
P: From Coulomb's law, the field of a single point charge q at distance r is:
EQ: E = kq / r2
P: directed radially outward from a positive source charge, and radially inward toward a negative one.
H2: 3.3 Superposition of Fields
P: Just as forces from multiple charges add as vectors, so do the fields they produce. The net field at any point is the vector sum of the fields each source charge would produce there individually. This is the field-picture restatement of the same superposition principle from Chapter 2, and it is what makes the field concept practically useful: once you know how to add vectors, you can find the field of any charge distribution, however complicated, by summing (or integrating) the contributions of its pieces.
H2: 3.4 Field Lines
P: Field lines are a visualization tool, not a physical object: a diagram of curves drawn so that the tangent to the curve at any point gives the field direction there, and the density of lines (how close together they are drawn) represents field strength. Field lines originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges (or run off to infinity if there is a net charge imbalance in the region). They never cross, because the field at any point has one and only one direction.
FIG: fig3_1.png | Figure 3.1 — Electric field lines run from positive charge to negative charge. | Field lines leave the positive charge on the left and curve into the negative charge on the right; arrowheads point from plus to minus.
KEY: Field line density encodes field strength. Wherever field lines bunch closer together, the field is stronger; wherever they spread apart, it is weaker. This is exactly the intuition Gauss's law makes quantitative in the next chapter.
H2: 3.5 The Field of a Dipole
P: An electric dipole — two equal and opposite charges +q and −q separated by a small distance d — is worth working through explicitly because dipole fields reappear constantly: in molecular physics, in antenna theory, and as the building block for understanding how insulating (dielectric) materials respond to fields, which matters directly for capacitors in Chapter 6. The dipole is characterized by its dipole moment, a vector p of magnitude p = qd, pointing from the negative charge to the positive one. Far from the dipole (at distances much larger than d), the field falls off as 1/r³ rather than 1/r² — faster than a single point charge, because the +q and −q fields nearly cancel except for the small asymmetry due to their separation.
P: A dipole placed in an external, uniform electric field feels no net force (the forces on +q and −q are equal and opposite, since both charges sit in the same external field), but it does feel a net torque, which acts to rotate the dipole until p aligns with the external field. This torque-without-net-force behavior is exactly how a compass needle responds to Earth's magnetic field, and it is the mechanism by which an external field polarizes and aligns the molecular dipoles inside a dielectric material — the microscopic picture behind the macroscopic dielectric constant introduced in Chapter 6.
WE: Worked Example 3.1
PROB: Find the electric field 5.0 cm from a point charge of +8.0 nC.
SOL: E = kq/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(8.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.050)² ≈ 2.9 × 10⁴ N/C, directed radially away from the charge.
WE: Worked Example 3.2
PROB: A +4.0 nC charge sits at the origin and a −4.0 nC charge sits at x = 6.0 cm. Find the net electric field at the point x = 3.0 cm (the midpoint between them).
SOL: Field from the +4.0 nC charge, 3.0 cm away, points in the +x direction (away from a positive charge): E₁ = kq/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.030)² ≈ 3.996×10⁴ N/C. Field from the −4.0 nC charge, also 3.0 cm away, points in the +x direction as well (toward a negative charge, and the negative charge is further along +x, so the field at the midpoint points toward it): E₂ ≈ 3.996×10⁴ N/C, same direction. Because the two fields point the same way at the midpoint of a dipole (this is a general feature of dipole fields directly between the charges), they add rather than cancel: E_net ≈ 7.99×10⁴ N/C in the +x direction.
MIS: It is a common first guess that the field exactly midway between a +q and a −q charge of equal magnitude should be zero, by some vague appeal to symmetry. The worked example above shows this is exactly backward for points between the charges: the two fields point the same direction there and add. The field never actually vanishes anywhere off to the sides, for an ideal dipole of exactly equal and opposite charge: the two individual field vectors on the perpendicular bisector never point in exactly opposite directions, so they never cancel at any finite distance — the field there only grows weaker, approaching zero as the distance grows without bound.
BENCH: Dry air breaks down at roughly 3 kV per millimeter between smooth electrodes, and at much less near sharp points, where the field crowds together. On a mains or high-voltage board, solder spikes and pointed copper corners are where arcing starts. Space conductors to the safety standard's creepage and clearance values, never to your own estimate, and remember that dirt and humidity eat into the margin.
