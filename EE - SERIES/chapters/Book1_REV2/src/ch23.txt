H1: Chapter 23 — Phasors and Impedance
P: A phasor is a sort of cheat code. Instead of solving a differential equation for every AC circuit, you freeze the rotation, do ordinary algebra with complex numbers, and switch time back on at the end. It works for any linear circuit at a single frequency, which covers a large share of everyday analysis.
P: Where you meet this: AC circuit analysis, power systems, filter design, and impedance-versus-frequency curves on component datasheets.
P: This chapter assembles everything the book has built. Units gave us dimensional discipline, algebra gave us rearrangement, trigonometry gave us the sinusoid, complex numbers gave us two-quantity arithmetic, and Euler's formula connected rotation to oscillation. The phasor is what they combine into, and it is the single most powerful labor-saving device in circuit analysis.
H2: 23.1 The Central Observation
P: Put a resistor and an inductor in series, drive the pair with a sinusoidal voltage source, and every voltage and current anywhere in that little circuit turns out to be a sinusoid too, all at the exact same frequency the source is providing — nothing in an ordinary resistor or inductor can manufacture a new frequency out of nothing. Write out each of those signals in full, though, as an explicit function of time, and the identical oscillating factor — cos(ωt), or its complex cousin e^(jωt) — reappears in the source voltage, the resistor’s voltage, the inductor’s voltage, and the current, term after term, equation after equation, purely because every one of them is being driven at that one shared frequency.
P: The useful question in a circuit like this is never what the oscillation itself looks like — that part is settled, and identical everywhere in the circuit. It is how each signal’s size and timing compare to the others, once that shared oscillation is set aside. Carrying the same repeated factor through every step of a derivation adds real clutter, and real chances for error, for information that never actually distinguishes one quantity from another.
P: In a linear circuit driven by a sinusoid at a single frequency, every voltage and every current in the circuit is a sinusoid at that same frequency. The frequency never changes. Only amplitude and phase differ from point to point.
P: The phasor method stops carrying that shared factor. Agree once, for the whole circuit, on the frequency, set it aside, and track only each signal’s amplitude and phase — which, as Chapter 21 established, is exactly what a complex number is built to hold. Only at the very end, if an explicit time-domain waveform is actually wanted back, is the shared oscillating factor reattached.
H2: 23.2 Defining the Phasor
P: Given a sinusoid v(t) = V_m·cos(ωt + φ), the corresponding phasor is the complex number
EQ: V = (V_m/√2)∠φ = (V_m/√2)·e^(jφ)
P: The time dependence has been dropped entirely. To recover the real signal, multiply by e^(jωt), take the real part, and scale by √2 to undo the RMS normalization: v(t) = √2·Re{V·e^(jωt)}.
P: Two conventions must be fixed and then adhered to. First, this series uses cosine as the reference — a phasor angle of zero means a cosine, not a sine. A sine must be converted first, using sin θ = cos(θ − 90°). Second, phasor magnitudes in this series are RMS values rather than peak values, since that makes power calculations come out without stray factors of two. Other texts use peak amplitudes; neither is wrong, but mixing them within one problem produces answers off by √2.
MIS: A phasor is not a function of time, and writing V(t) for a phasor is a category error. The whole point of the phasor is that time has been factored out. Nor is a phasor a vector in physical space — the "arrow" of the complex plane points in a mathematical direction, not a spatial one. A phasor diagram showing a current 90° behind a voltage is saying something about timing, not geometry.
H2: 23.3 Impedance
P: With signals represented as phasors, the relationship between voltage and current across any passive component becomes a simple ratio, and that ratio is called impedance:
EQ: Z = V/I
P: Impedance is a complex number measured in ohms. Its real part is resistance, which dissipates power; its imaginary part is reactance, which stores and returns energy without dissipating it. Deriving the three basic impedances is now a short exercise, using the result from Chapter 22 that differentiation becomes multiplication by jω.
FIG: fig23_1.png | Figure 23.1 — A phasor diagram and its corresponding impedance triangle. | A phasor diagram sits beside a right-triangle impedance diagram, linking voltage phase to resistance and reactance.
P: For a resistor, v = iR with no derivative involved, so
EQ: Z_R = R
P: For an inductor, v = L·di/dt. Replacing the derivative with jω gives V = jωL·I, so
EQ: Z_L = jωL
P: For a capacitor, i = C·dv/dt, so I = jωC·V, and inverting,
EQ: Z_C = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC)
P: Three differential relationships have become three algebraic ones. Read the signs: inductive reactance is positive imaginary, capacitive reactance is negative imaginary, and the opposite signs are why they cancel at resonance.
KEY: Impedance turns AC circuit analysis into DC circuit analysis with complex numbers. Series impedances add. Parallel impedances combine by product-over-sum. Voltage dividers, current dividers, Thevenin equivalents — every technique from resistive circuit analysis carries over unchanged, with R replaced by Z and real arithmetic replaced by complex arithmetic. Nothing new needs to be learned; the old methods work.
H2: 23.4 Reading Phase From Impedance
P: The angle of the impedance is the phase by which voltage leads current. For a pure resistor the impedance is real, its angle is zero, and voltage and current are in phase. For a pure inductor, Z = jωL has angle +90°, so voltage leads current by a quarter cycle. For a pure capacitor, angle −90°, so voltage lags current.
P: A common mnemonic is ELI for an inductor (L), where voltage E leads current I, and ICE for a capacitor (C), where current I leads voltage E.
P: Real components are never pure, and the impedance angle tells you how far from ideal a component is. A capacitor whose measured impedance angle is −88° rather than −90° has a small resistive component, and that two-degree discrepancy is precisely the loss mechanism quantified later in the series as equivalent series resistance.
WE: Worked Example 23.1
P: A 1 kΩ resistor is in series with a 0.1 µF capacitor across a 10 V RMS, 1 kHz supply. Find the total impedance, the current, and the voltage across each component.
P: First the angular frequency: ω = 2πf = 2π(1000) = 6283 rad/s.
P: Capacitive impedance: Z_C = −j/(ωC) = −j/(6283 × 0.1 × 10⁻⁶) = −j/(6.283 × 10⁻⁴) = −j1592 Ω.
P: Total impedance in series is the sum: Z = 1000 − j1592 Ω.
P: In polar form: |Z| = √(1000² + 1592²) = √(10⁶ + 2.534 × 10⁶) = √(3.534 × 10⁶) = 1880 Ω.
P: The angle: θ = arctan(−1592/1000) = −57.9°. The real part is positive, so we are in the fourth quadrant and no 180° adjustment is needed.
P: Current: I = V/Z = 10∠0° / 1880∠−57.9° = 5.32∠+57.9° mA. Dividing means dividing magnitudes and subtracting angles, so the current leads the supply voltage by 57.9° — as expected for a circuit that is predominantly capacitive.
P: Voltage across the resistor: V_R = I·R = (5.32 mA)(1000 Ω) = 5.32 V, in phase with the current, so at +57.9°.
P: Voltage across the capacitor: V_C = I·Z_C = (5.32 mA)(1592 Ω) = 8.47 V, at an angle of 57.9° − 90° = −32.1°.
P: Now the check that catches nearly every error in this kind of problem. The two component voltages are 5.32 V and 8.47 V, which sum arithmetically to 13.79 V — considerably more than the 10 V supply. That is not a violation of Kirchhoff's voltage law. The voltages are 90° apart in phase, so they add as phasors, not as numbers: √(5.32² + 8.47²) = √(28.3 + 71.7) = √100 = 10.0 V. Exactly the supply voltage.
MIS: It looks impossible for the voltages across two series components to exceed the supply voltage, and in a DC circuit it would be. In AC circuits it is routine, because component voltages add as phasors rather than as plain numbers. In a resonant circuit the effect becomes dramatic: the voltage across the inductor and the capacitor individually can exceed the supply by a factor equal to the Q of the circuit, sometimes by hundreds of times. Those voltages are entirely real and will destroy components rated only for the supply voltage. They cancel each other in the sum precisely because they are 180° apart.
H2: 23.5 Admittance, and When to Use It
P: The reciprocal of impedance is admittance, Y = 1/Z, measured in siemens. Its real part is conductance and its imaginary part is susceptance.
P: Admittance exists for the same reason conductance does: for parallel combinations, admittances add, whereas impedances require the reciprocal formula. In a circuit with many parallel branches, working in admittance turns a nested reciprocal expression into a plain sum. Nodal analysis, which is formulated around parallel connections at each node, is therefore naturally written in admittances — the conductance matrix of Chapter 17 becomes an admittance matrix, and every technique carries straight over.
BENCH: Real components are never pure. A capacitor has series resistance (ESR) and series inductance (ESL), and above its self-resonant frequency it behaves like an inductor. That is why one large decoupling capacitor does little at hundreds of megahertz, and why datasheets give impedance-versus-frequency curves rather than just a value.
