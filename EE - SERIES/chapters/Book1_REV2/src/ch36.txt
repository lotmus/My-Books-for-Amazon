H1: Chapter 36 — Resonance, Frequency Response, and Transformers
P: An old radio picked one station out of dozens with a single knob: a coil and a variable capacitor resonating at one frequency and ignoring the rest. Your phone still does a version of the same thing, and the charger on your desk relies on the transformer that closes this chapter.
P: Where you meet this: Radio tuning and filters, LC and crystal oscillators, wireless-charging coils tuned to resonance, and the transformers in chargers and power supplies.
H2: 36.1 The Series RLC Circuit
P: Push a playground swing at random moments and it barely moves, no matter how hard you push — sometimes a push fights the swing's own motion, sometimes it helps, and the two roughly cancel out over time. Push it instead at exactly the swing's own natural rhythm, small push after small push, each one perfectly timed, and the swing climbs higher and higher, far beyond what any single push could produce alone. An RLC circuit has a natural rhythm of its own too, set by its inductor and capacitor, and driving it at exactly that frequency produces the same kind of buildup — not in how high something swings, but in how large a voltage appears across the inductor or capacitor individually, compared to the source driving the whole circuit.
P: A series combination of a resistor, inductor, and capacitor, driven by a sinusoidal source, has total impedance:
EQ: Z = R + jωL + 1/(jωC) = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC))
P: The imaginary part — the net reactance — depends on frequency in an interesting way: the inductor's reactance ωL grows with frequency while the capacitor's reactance 1/(ωC) shrinks with frequency, so at one particular frequency, the resonant frequency, the two exactly cancel, leaving a purely real impedance equal to R alone.
H2: 36.2 Resonant Frequency
P: Setting ωL = 1/(ωC) and solving for ω gives the resonant angular frequency:
EQ: ω0 = 1/√(LC)
P: At resonance, a series RLC circuit's impedance is at its minimum possible value (equal to R alone, with the reactive parts canceling), so current is at its maximum for a given source voltage, and — since the inductor's and capacitor's voltages are equal in magnitude but opposite in phase at this exact frequency — the voltage across either one individually can substantially exceed the source voltage itself, an effect exploited deliberately in radio tuning circuits, where a resonant circuit is used to select one specific broadcast frequency out of many present simultaneously on an antenna. Worked Example 36.2 makes the size concrete: with R = 5.0 Ω, L = 10 mH, C = 100 nF, and a 1.0 V RMS source at resonance, Q ≈ 63.2, so the ideal inductor and capacitor voltages are each about 63 V RMS, equal and opposite. Component voltage can exceed the source without violating KVL; the two reactive phasors cancel.
FIG: fig36_1.png | Figure 36.1 — Resonance curves at three values of Q, the quality factor defined in §36.3: a higher Q gives a sharper, narrower peak. | Three resonance curves share the same center frequency; the higher-Q curve has the tallest and narrowest peak.
KEY: Resonance is the frequency at which inductive and capacitive reactance precisely cancel. Below resonance, a series RLC circuit's net reactance is capacitive (current leads voltage); above resonance, it is inductive (current lags voltage); exactly at resonance, the circuit is purely resistive, current and voltage are in phase, and the power factor is 1.
H2: 36.3 Bandwidth and Quality Factor
P: A resonant circuit does not respond only at exactly ω₀ — it responds over a range of frequencies around ω₀, with the response falling off on either side. The bandwidth (the width of the frequency range over which the circuit's response stays within a standard threshold of its peak value) and the quality factor Q, which measures how sharply peaked the resonance is (a high-Q circuit has a narrow, sharply peaked response; a low-Q circuit has a broad, gentle one), are related by:
EQ: Q = ω0L / R = 1 / (ω0RC) ,   Bandwidth = ω0/Q
P: A high-Q circuit — achieved with a small resistance relative to the inductor's reactance at resonance — is desirable in a radio tuner precisely because it rejects nearby, unwanted frequencies sharply while passing the desired station strongly; the same quality factor, and much of this same frequency-response machinery, reappears throughout this series' RF and microwave volumes, where filters and resonant cavities are central design elements rather than incidental circuit behaviors.

H2: 36.4 Mutual Inductance and the Transformer
P: Two coils placed near each other share magnetic flux, so that a changing current in one coil (Chapter 11) induces a voltage in the other. This coupling is quantified by the mutual inductance M, defined so that a changing current in one coil induces an EMF in the other, ε2 = −M dI1/dt (and, symmetrically, ε1 = −M dI2/dt), measured, like self-inductance, in henries (H). Mutual inductance is the physical basis of the transformer, one of the most important devices in all of electrical power engineering. An ideal transformer, with N₁ turns on its primary winding and N₂ turns on its secondary, relates the two windings' voltages and currents by the turns ratio:
FIG: fig36_3.png | Figure 36.3 — An ideal transformer: voltage and current scale with the turns ratio, in opposite directions. | An ideal transformer has primary and secondary coils on a shared core, with turns, voltage, and current ratios labeled.
EQ: V2/V1 = N2/N1 ,     I2/I1 = N1/N2
P: Notice that voltage and current transform in opposite ratios — a step-up transformer (more secondary turns than primary) increases voltage but correspondingly decreases current by the same factor, and vice versa for a step-down transformer, so that an ideal transformer's input and output apparent power (Chapter 35) are exactly equal: transformers change the voltage-to-current ratio of the power delivered, but (idealized, with no internal losses) never change the total power itself. This single fact — trading voltage for current, or current for voltage, at essentially no cost — is the entire reason long-distance power transmission is done at very high voltage (minimizing current, and therefore minimizing I²R transmission losses over the wires) before being stepped back down to safer, more usable voltages near the point of consumption, a subject this series returns to fully in its power-systems volume.
H2: 36.5 Simple Filters: Low-Pass and High-Pass RC Circuits
P: A single resistor and capacitor, arranged one way, form the simplest possible low-pass filter; arranged the other way, they form the simplest possible high-pass filter — and both are a direct, practical application of the frequency-dependent impedance introduced in Chapter 23. Take a series RC circuit driven by a sinusoidal source, and consider the output taken across the capacitor. By the voltage-divider relationship (Chapter 34, generalized to impedances):
EQ: Vout/Vin = ZC/(R + ZC) = 1/(1 + jωRC)
P: At low frequencies (ω small), Z_C = 1/(jωC) is large compared to R, so nearly all the input voltage appears across the capacitor — the circuit passes low frequencies through to the output largely unattenuated. At high frequencies, Z_C shrinks toward zero, and the output voltage shrinks with it — high frequencies are attenuated. This is a low-pass filter, and the frequency at which the output has fallen to 1/√2 (about 70.7%) of the input — the conventional definition of a filter's cutoff frequency — occurs exactly where |Z_C| = R, which works out to:
EQ: ωc = 1/(RC)     (equivalently fc = 1/(2πRC))
P: Taking the output across the resistor instead of the capacitor, in the same series RC circuit, gives the complementary behavior: at low frequencies, nearly all the voltage drops across the now-large capacitor impedance, leaving little across the resistor; at high frequencies, the capacitor's impedance shrinks and the resistor's share of the voltage grows toward the full input. This is a high-pass filter, with exactly the same cutoff frequency ω_c = 1/(RC), measured across the other element.
KEY: The same RC circuit is a low-pass or a high-pass filter depending only on which element's voltage you take as the output. Both share the identical cutoff frequency ω_c = 1/(RC) — the same time constant from Chapter 33's transient analysis reappears here as a frequency-domain quantity, which is not a coincidence: a circuit's time-domain settling behavior and its frequency-domain filtering behavior are two views of the exact same underlying impedance relationships.
WE: Worked Example 36.1
PROB: A low-pass RC filter is built with R = 1.0 kΩ and C = 100 nF. Find its cutoff frequency in Hz, and determine whether a 500 Hz signal is passed or substantially attenuated.
SOL: ω_c = 1/(RC) = 1/[(1.0×10³)(100×10⁻⁹)] = 1/(1.0×10⁻⁴) = 1.0×10⁴ rad/s. Converting: f_c = ω_c/(2π) ≈ 1592 Hz, about 1.6 kHz. Since 500 Hz is well below this cutoff frequency, it falls within the filter's passband and is passed through with little attenuation — only frequencies well above roughly 1.6 kHz would be substantially attenuated by this particular filter.
WE: Worked Example 36.2
PROB: A series RLC circuit has L = 10 mH, C = 100 nF, and R = 5.0 Ω. Find the resonant frequency (in Hz) and the quality factor.
SOL: ω₀ = 1/√(LC) = 1/√[(10×10⁻³)(100×10⁻⁹)] = 1/√(1.0×10⁻⁹) = 1/(3.1623×10⁻⁵) ≈ 3.1623×10⁴ rad/s. Converting to ordinary frequency: f₀ = ω₀/(2π) ≈ 5033 Hz, about 5.0 kHz. Quality factor: Q = ω₀L/R = (3.16×10⁴)(10×10⁻³)/5.0 ≈ 63.2 — a fairly high-Q, sharply resonant circuit.

WE: Worked Example 36.3
PROB: An ideal transformer has 1000 turns on its primary and 100 turns on its secondary, with 240 V RMS applied to the primary, delivering 5.0 A RMS to a load on the secondary. Find the secondary voltage and the primary current.
SOL: Turns ratio N₂/N₁ = 100/1000 = 0.10, a step-down transformer. Secondary voltage: V₂ = V₁ × (N₂/N₁) = 240 × 0.10 = 24 V. For the current, the ratio inverts: I₁ = I₂ × (N₂/N₁) = 5.0 × 0.10 = 0.50 A. Check via apparent power: primary S = V₁I₁ = 240 × 0.50 = 120 VA; secondary S = V₂I₂ = 24 × 5.0 = 120 VA — equal, as required for an ideal (lossless) transformer.
MIS: It is a common first-pass error to assume a step-down transformer, since it reduces voltage, must therefore also reduce the power available — as though “stepping down” meant losing capability. An ideal transformer changes voltage and current in exactly inverse proportion and delivers the same apparent power out as goes in; a step-down transformer trades a smaller voltage for a larger available current at the same total power, which is exactly the behavior wanted at the receiving end of a power line, where a lower, safer voltage is needed but the same total power must still be delivered to the load.
BENCH: High-Q resonant circuits are touchy. Component tolerance moves the resonant frequency (a 5% change in capacitance shifts f₀ by about 2.5%), and even the 10 pF or so of a standard 10× scope probe can detune a small LC tank. Measure resonant circuits with low-capacitance probes, or couple to them loosely.
