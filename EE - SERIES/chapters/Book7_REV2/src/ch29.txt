PART: Part III. Where the Series Meets
H1: Chapter 29. The Robot: Where the Series Meets
P: A humanoid robot balances on one foot while its arms reach for a cup, and its motor drives switch 48 V in nanoseconds a few centimeters from a camera and an IMU. Every book in this series is inside it at once, and the hardest problems are where those books meet. It does not care which volume supplied the fix.
P: Where you meet this: Every humanoid and mobile manipulator, every warehouse robot, every drone and rover, and the reader lab's ESP32-S3 robot, which meets the same boundary problems at a smaller scale.
P: A mobile robot, and in 2026 particularly the humanoid and the mobile manipulator, is one product that draws on all nine books. Book 1 supplies the foundations; Book 2, circuits and control; Book 3, device behavior; Book 4, RF hardware; Book 5, communications; Book 6, power and energy; this book, packaging, layout, and EMC; Book 8, firmware; and Book 9, measurement. A robot uses them together: its battery feeds motor converters, feedback loops stabilize its joints, MEMS sensors report motion, radios carry data, and the package must keep heat and switching noise from disrupting the computer. This chapter focuses on those boundaries rather than repeating each volume's full treatment. It uses a few numbers to show how the subjects meet. The robot here is a worked design thread, not a report of a tested prototype: each number is an assumption to check against a chosen motor, board, enclosure, and test plan. If the camera link drops frames only when a motor accelerates, the useful diagnosis is at the boundary: Book 6’s current transient, Book 7’s return path and package, and Book 8’s dropped-frame log have to agree on the same event.
H2: 29.1 Two loops at two speeds
P: The control of a modern robot has two layers. At the top, a learned model takes in camera images, other sensor data, and an instruction in language, and produces a short sequence of target motions; such vision-language-action models, of which Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics and NVIDIA's GR00T families were prominent examples by 2026, typically run at a few to a few tens of updates per second on the robot's computer. Below them, a classical tracking controller, the kind Book 2 designs, runs at hundreds of hertz to a few kilohertz, holding each joint on the commanded trajectory and rejecting disturbances. A model ahead of a mechanism: the learned layer chooses what to do, and the feedback layer makes the mechanism do it.
H2: Worked Example 29.1
P: Problem. A model produces action chunks at 10 Hz, and Book 2's tracking loop runs at 200 Hz. Find the tracker's period, the number of tracker updates per chunk, and the latency budget for one tracker cycle if sensing, computation, and actuation must share it equally.
P: Solution. The tracker's period is 1/200 = 5 ms, and each 100 ms chunk spans 20 tracker updates, so the tracker interpolates the model's targets and corrects errors between them. Divided equally among sensing, computation, and actuation, each gets about 1.7 ms. The model can be slow because the tracker is fast; if the model ran the joints directly at 10 Hz, any disturbance would go uncorrected for 100 ms, long enough for a walking robot to fall.
P: Key idea. A modern robot is a model ahead of a mechanism. The learned layer runs slowly and decides; the feedback layer runs fast and holds, and the boundary between them is a data rate and a latency, not a philosophy.
H2: 29.2 Power
P: A robot's power budget couples the mechanics, the converters, and the battery. A joint that delivers torque τ at angular speed ω produces mechanical power τω, and the motor and its drive, Book 6's converters, lose some of it. Robots commonly use a 48 V battery bus, high enough to keep currents moderate and low enough to stay within extra-low-voltage safety rules.
EQ: Pmech = τ ω,    Ibus = Pmech / (η Vbus),    runtime = Ebattery / Paverage
H2: Worked Example 29.2
P: Problem. A hip joint delivers 100 N·m at 2 rad/s through a motor and drive that are 85% efficient from a 48 V bus. Find the mechanical power and bus current. A 2 kWh battery supplies an average of 500 W. Find the runtime.
P: Solution. Pmech = 100 × 2 = 200 W, the electrical input is 200/0.85 = 235 W, and the bus current is 235/48 = 4.9 A. Peaks in a fast motion may be several times higher, which is what sizes the drive and its wiring. The runtime is 2000/500 = 4 hours. The onboard computer may draw 100 W or more of that 500 W, so the bandwidth-per-watt arithmetic of Chapter 1 sets a robot's runtime as directly as its motors do.
H2: 29.3 Data
P: Cameras dominate a robot's raw data. An uncompressed 1920 × 1080 color stream at 30 frames per second, with 3 bytes per pixel, is 186.6 MB/s, and two such cameras produce 373 MB/s, before depth sensors, microphones, and joint encoders. That data must reach the computer over short, robust links in a chassis full of switching motor drives, a demanding environment for signal integrity and EMC (Chapter 5 and Part II). Much of it is reduced at the sensor or in a dedicated processor before the model sees it, for the same energy-per-bit reasons that put HBM on the accelerator's package.
H2: 29.4 The motor drive as a noise source
P: The boundary problems of a robot are concrete. Each joint's motor drive switches the 48 V bus at tens or hundreds of kilohertz, and with gallium nitride transistors the edges take a few nanoseconds, a dv/dt near 10 kV/µs. Every edge charges the capacitance from the motor winding and its leads to the metal frame of the limb, and that common-mode current, I = C dv/dt, must return to the drive. If its return path runs through the frame, it passes the camera, the encoder, and the joint's data link, and their grounds shift by the current times the impedance of the shared path. The remedies are those of Chapters 22 and 26 applied inside the body: slow the edges where switching loss allows, return the common-mode current locally with a capacitor from the frame to the drive's return, put a common-mode choke or shielded leads on the motor cable with the shield bonded at both ends, and keep the data links differential, balanced, and away from the motor leads.
EQ: I_CM = C dv/dt

H2: Worked Example 29.3
P: Problem. A GaN motor drive switches 48 V in 5 ns, and the motor and its leads have 1 nF to the frame. Find the dv/dt and the common-mode current. What ground shift does that current produce across 20 mΩ of shared frame return, and how much does slowing the edge to 20 ns help?
P: Solution. dv/dt = 48 V / 5 ns = 9.6 kV/µs, so I_CM = 1 nF × 9.6 kV/µs = 9.6 A during each edge. Across 20 mΩ that is 0.19 V of ground shift between the camera and the computer, which a differential link rejects only within its common-mode range and only if the link is well balanced. A 20 ns edge gives 2.4 kV/µs and 2.4 A, 20 log₁₀(9.6/2.4) = 12.0 dB less, at the cost of more switching loss in the drive.
H2: 29.5 The boundaries
P: The difficult problems in such a system are seldom inside one book. A motor drive's switching edges couple into a camera link; the battery's current spikes disturb the computer's supply; the computer's heat must leave a sealed body; the radio's antenna must work beside a metal limb; and the learned model's latency must fit inside the controller's stability margin. Each is a boundary between two of this series' books, and each is solved by engineers who understand both sides. That, finally, is the reason for writing the series as a set: the subjects are separate on the shelf and joined in the product.
BENCH: Draw the robot's current loops, not only its block diagram: where each motor's common-mode current returns, which ground a camera's link shares with it, and where the battery's surge goes. Slow the edges where the losses allow, return noise locally, and keep data links differential.
P: On the Job
P: Robotics hardware engineers integrate exactly the boundaries of this chapter. Their tools are a scope with differential and current probes, an EMC pre-scan setup, a thermal camera, and the robot's own logs. A typical task: a humanoid's camera link drops frames whenever the knee motors accelerate. The engineer measures the common-mode current on the motor leads, finds it returning through the leg's frame past the camera connector, adds a local return capacitor at the drive and a choke on the motor cable, and shows in the logs that the frame drops are gone.
