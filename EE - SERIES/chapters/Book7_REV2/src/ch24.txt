H1: Chapter 24. Detectors and the Number You Quoted
P: The same signal reads 52 dBµV on one instrument and 46 on another, and both are right. One used a peak detector, the other a quasi-peak detector with a 120 kHz bandwidth. An emissions number is meaningless without the detector and the bandwidth that produced it.
P: Where you meet this: Every spectrum analyzer's RBW and detector menu, every EMC receiver, every spread-spectrum clock option in a microcontroller's clock tree, and every FCC report's 'peak' and 'average' columns above 1 GHz.
P: A spectrum analyzer and an EMC receiver can show the same signal at different levels, and both can be right. The difference lies in the detector and the bandwidth. A limit is meaningful only with its detector: CISPR's quasi-peak and average limits, and FCC's quasi-peak limits below 1 GHz and average and peak limits above it. This chapter explains the detectors, the resolution bandwidths that CISPR 16 fixes for each frequency range, and the large difference they make for impulsive and spread-spectrum signals.
H2: 24.1 Bandwidths
P: CISPR 16-1-1 fixes the receiver's bandwidth by band: 200 Hz from 9 to 150 kHz, 9 kHz from 150 kHz to 30 MHz, 120 kHz from 30 MHz to 1 GHz, and 1 MHz above 1 GHz, each defined at its 6 dB points. A narrowband signal, a single harmonic of a clock, reads the same in any bandwidth wider than itself. A broadband signal, noise or a fast train of pulses, reads higher in a wider bandwidth, because more of its energy falls inside. Using the wrong bandwidth on a spectrum analyzer is the most common reason pre-compliance results disagree with the lab's.
H2: 24.2 Peak, quasi-peak, and average
P: A peak scan is often used to find candidate frequencies quickly, followed by measurements with the detector required by the product standard. Under FCC Section 15.35, peak measurement at the same bandwidth may be used as an alternative to quasi-peak below 1 GHz where that provision applies. That FCC option does not automatically replace a CISPR product standard's prescribed detector. The report must state what was measured and under which rule.
P: Key idea. A field-strength result is incomplete without its detector, bandwidth, dwell, and governing standard. Continuous signals may read similarly on different detectors; impulsive signals can differ substantially. A peak pre-scan helps locate candidates, but the final detector is selected from the applicable standard, including any explicit alternative it permits. In the opening example, 46 dBµV quasi-peak and 52 dBµV peak are different results; against a 49 dBµV quasi-peak limit, the prescribed reading passes by 3 dB while substituting the peak value would appear to fail by 3 dB. Substituting the lower quasi-peak value for a separate peak limit can instead create a false pass.
H2: 24.3 Spread-spectrum clocking
P: A spread-spectrum clock modulates its frequency slowly, typically by 0.5% to 2%, at a rate of about 30 kHz. Each harmonic is smeared across a band proportional to its frequency: the nth harmonic spreads over n times the clock's deviation. When that band is wider than the receiver's bandwidth, the receiver captures only part of the energy at any moment, and the reading falls by about 10 log₁₀ of the ratio of the spread width to the bandwidth. The total radiated power is unchanged; only its density at any one frequency falls. Interfaces with tight timing, such as some serial links, tolerate only small spreads.
EQ: reduction ≈ 10 log₁₀(Δfspread / RBW)   (when Δfspread > RBW)

H2: Worked Example 24.1
P: Problem. A 100 MHz clock is spread downward by 0.5%. Estimate the reduction of its fundamental and of its third harmonic in a 120 kHz bandwidth.
P: Solution. The fundamental spreads over 0.5% of 100 MHz = 500 kHz, so the reduction is 10 log₁₀(500/120) = 6.2 dB. The third harmonic at 300 MHz spreads over 1.5 MHz, and the reduction is 10 log₁₀(1500/120) = 11.0 dB. Higher harmonics benefit more, which suits the frequencies where Chapter 21 showed radiation is strongest.
H2: 24.4 Scanning in practice
P: A full radiated scan from 30 MHz to 1 GHz in 120 kHz steps has thousands of frequencies, each to be measured at every antenna height, polarization, and turntable angle. Labs therefore make a fast preliminary peak scan, often with the equipment rotated and the antenna moved continuously, identify the frequencies within a chosen margin of the limit, typically 6 dB, and then maximize and measure each one with the specified detector. The time-domain scanning receivers allowed by CISPR 16 since 2010 capture many frequencies at once with FFT processing and have shortened scans greatly.
H2: 24.5 Scan time, step size, and the FFT receiver
P: A stepped receiver measures one frequency at a time, and CISPR 16 requires the step to be no more than half the resolution bandwidth so that no narrowband emission falls between steps. The number of steps is the span divided by the step, and the time is the number of steps times the dwell at each. The quasi-peak detector needs a dwell of about a second to settle, so a quasi-peak scan of the whole band at every antenna position would take hours, which is why labs scan with the peak detector first and apply quasi-peak only at the suspect frequencies. A time-domain or FFT-based receiver digitizes a wide segment at once and computes all the bins in that segment in parallel, applying the CISPR weighting digitally. It replaces thousands of dwells with a few dozen segment captures. It also catches intermittent emissions, a burst from a radio or a motor start, that a stepped scan would see only if the burst happened to coincide with the step.
EQ: N_steps = span / step,   step ≤ RBW / 2,   T_scan = N_steps × dwell
H2: Worked Example 24.2
P: Problem. Find the number of steps and the quasi-peak scan time for 30 MHz to 1 GHz with a 120 kHz bandwidth and a 1 s dwell. Compare an FFT receiver that captures 20 MHz segments with a 1 s dwell each.
P: Solution. The step is 60 kHz, so N = 970 MHz / 60 kHz = 16,167 steps, and the scan takes 16,167 s, about 4.5 hours, for one antenna height and turntable angle. The FFT receiver needs 970/20 = 48.5, so 49 segments, about 49 s, a speedup of more than 300 times. The difference is what makes full quasi-peak final scans practical.
H2: 24.6 Pulsed emissions: why the peak and average limits differ by 20 dB
P: Many emissions are not steady. A radio transmitting in bursts, an intermittent motor drive, or a processor with varying activity produces a signal present for only part of the observation time. For an ideal rectangular voltage envelope with duty factor d, the time-average envelope is d times the burst amplitude, a reduction of 20 log10(1/d) dB. This is a teaching estimate, not a substitute for the detector response, dwell, pulse-weighting rules, or measurement procedure required by the applicable standard. FCC peak and average limits above 1 GHz are separate compliance limits; their 20 dB separation does not create a universal duty-cycle pass rule. A scan that misses bursts can under-report them, so the operating mode and measurement timing must be documented.
EQ: E_avg = E_peak − 20 log₁₀(1/d)  (envelope average)
H2: Worked Example 24.3
P: Problem. A spurious emission at 4.8 GHz from a bursting radio is on 5 % of the time and reads 70 dBµV/m peak at 3 m. Estimate its average reading and the margins to the FCC limits of 74 dBµV/m peak and 54 dBµV/m average.
P: Solution. 20 log₁₀(1/0.05) = 26 dB, so the average is about 44 dBµV/m. The peak margin is 4 dB and the average margin 10 dB, so the peak limit governs this low-duty emission.
