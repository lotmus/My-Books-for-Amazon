PART: Part I. Carriers and Junctions
H1: Chapter 1. A Crystal That Can Be Talked Into Conducting
P: Window glass and a processor die both contain silicon. One of them sits in a wall and refuses to carry a current you would bother to measure. The other switches billions of times a second because a small voltage was allowed to decide how many charges are free to move. The difference is not a mystery metal. It is how pure the crystal is, and which few atoms you deliberately put in.
H2: 1.1 Three ways a solid treats its electrons
P: A useful picture, good enough for every circuit in this series, sorts solids by the energy gap between the last filled band of electron states and the next empty one. In a metal the bands overlap, so a tiny electric field finds electrons already free to drift. In a good insulator the gap is several electron-volts wide, and ordinary voltages do not lift electrons across it. Silicon sits in between. Its gap is about 1.12 eV at room temperature. Germanium is narrower, about 0.66 eV, which is why a germanium junction leaks more when the board gets warm. Gallium arsenide is wider, about 1.42 eV. Silicon carbide, used where the voltage and the heat are both high, is wider still, a few electron-volts depending on the crystal form. Silicon won the ordinary chip not because its gap is magical, but because the industry learned to grow it as a nearly perfect crystal and to put an excellent insulator, silicon dioxide, on top of it.
H2: 1.2 Intrinsic means almost empty
P: In a perfect silicon crystal at absolute zero, every bond is full and no charge moves. At room temperature a few electrons have enough thermal energy to break a bond. Each broken bond leaves an electron in the conduction band and a hole, a missing bond, in the valence band. They are created in pairs, so in pure material their concentrations are equal. That common value is ni, and this book takes it as 1.0×10^10 cm⁻³.
P: A cubic centimeter of silicon holds about 5×10^22 atoms. Ten billion free electrons in that same volume is a very small fraction. Intrinsic silicon is a poor conductor. The whole point of doping, in the next chapter, is to raise one of those two populations by many orders of magnitude while the other falls.
H2: 1.3 Conductivity is charge times speed per field
P: Drift current density is conductivity times electric field. For a semiconductor that still has both electrons and holes,
EQ: σ = q (n μn + p μp)
P: where q is the elementary charge, n and p are the electron and hole concentrations, and μn and μp are the mobilities. Resistivity is 1/σ. In doped material one term usually swamps the other, which is why a resistor formula and a doped-silicon formula can look alike: both say "lots of carriers, easy current."
KEY: Key idea. Pure silicon at room temperature has very few free charges. Doping, not the word "semiconductor" by itself, is what makes a useful current.
H2: Worked Example 1.1
P: A piece of n-type silicon is doped so that n = 1.0×10^16 cm⁻³. The hole population has already fallen to the value required by the mass-action law in Chapter 2, p = ni²/n = 1.0e+04 cm⁻³. Using this book's μn, find the resistivity. The hole term is negligible next to the electron term.
P: σ = q n μn = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(1.0×10^16)(1000) = 1.602 (Ω·cm)⁻¹. ρ = 1/σ = 0.624 Ω·cm. A metal is a million times more conductive. This doped slice is already a practical semiconductor, and it is still nothing like copper.
H2: 1.4 The diamond lattice and the count of atoms
P: Silicon crystallizes in the diamond structure. Every atom sits at the center of a tetrahedron formed by four neighbors, and it shares one pair of electrons with each of them: the four covalent bonds that the bond picture of Section 1.2 breaks and repairs. The repeating cube of the structure, the unit cell, has an edge a = 0.5431 nm and holds the equivalent of eight atoms. The count in Section 1.2 comes straight from that cube.
EQ: N = 8 / a³ = 8 / (5.431×10⁻⁸ cm)³ = 4.99×10²² atoms per cm³
P: Two practical facts follow from the lattice. First, the crystal is not the same in every direction. Wafers are cut along chosen planes, labeled with Miller indices such as (100) and (110), and the choice matters: electron mobility and the quality of the oxide interface are best on (100), which is why nearly all CMOS starts on (100) wafers, while hole mobility is higher on (110), a fact that FinFET designers exploit by orienting the fin sidewalls. Second, the spacing between atoms sets how much room a foreign atom has. Phosphorus and boron fit on silicon sites with modest strain, which is part of why they became the standard dopants of Chapter 2.
H2: 1.5 Bands, the Fermi level, and where ni comes from
P: The band picture of Section 1.1 can be made quantitative with two ideas. The first is a density of available states. Near the bottom of the conduction band, the crystal offers a certain number of places for electrons per unit volume per unit energy; lumped into one number at room temperature, this is the effective density of states Nc, about 2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for silicon. The valence band has its own, Nv, about 1.04×10¹⁹ cm⁻³. The second idea is the Fermi level EF, the energy at which a state has an even chance of being occupied. The occupation falls off exponentially above it, by a factor of e for every kT of energy, where kT is 25.9 meV at 300 K, the same number as this book's VT expressed in electron-volts.
EQ: n = Nc e^(−(Ec − EF)/kT),    p = Nv e^(−(EF − Ev)/kT)
P: Multiply the two expressions and the Fermi level cancels. What remains depends only on the crystal and the temperature:
EQ: n p = Nc Nv e^(−Eg/kT) = ni²,    ni = √(Nc Nv) e^(−Eg/(2kT))
P: That one line is the mass-action law Chapter 2 will lean on, and it explains the orientation numbers of Section 1.1. With Eg = 1.12 eV and kT = 0.02585 eV, the exponent is −21.66, and the formula gives ni ≈ 6.7×10⁹ cm⁻³. Careful measurements put silicon at about 1.0×10¹⁰ cm⁻³; the difference comes from the exact effective masses and the temperature dependence of the gap. This book keeps the round value, as declared in the front matter, because a hand number that everyone shares is worth more than a third significant figure that no two textbooks agree on.
P: In intrinsic material n = p, and the Fermi level sits almost exactly in the middle of the gap. Call that energy Ei. Doping moves EF up toward the conduction band for n-type material and down toward the valence band for p-type, by kT times the natural logarithm of how far the majority concentration exceeds ni. For n = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ the shift is 0.026 × ln(10⁶) = 0.359 eV above midgap. This energy offset is the quantity that will reappear, in volts, as half of the built-in voltage of Chapter 4.
H2: 1.6 Temperature is the enemy of the minority
P: The exponential in ni makes it violently temperature dependent. Differentiating ln ni with the gap held fixed gives a fractional change of 1.5/T + Eg/(2kT²) per kelvin, which at 300 K is 0.077 per kelvin. Silicon's ni therefore doubles roughly every 9 °C. Between a 27 °C bench and a 100 °C engine bay, ni rises by a factor of about 96, and ni² by more than 9,000.
P: Two design consequences follow, and both return later. Every leakage current that is set by minority carriers, the reverse current of a diode, the off-current of a power device, the dark current of a photodiode, scales with ni² or with ni and therefore grows by orders of magnitude across a commercial temperature range. And at a high enough temperature the thermally generated carriers outnumber the dopants, and the material forgets that it was doped. With this book's hand model, ni reaches 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ at about 580 K and 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at about 710 K. A lightly doped silicon drift region in a power device stops behaving as designed long before that, which is the physical reason silicon power devices are rated to 150 or 175 °C and why the wide-gap materials of Chapters 7 and 19, whose ni is many orders of magnitude smaller, can run hotter.
KEY: Key idea. ni is set by the gap and the temperature through e^(−Eg/2kT). It doubles about every 9 °C in silicon, and every minority-carrier leakage in this book inherits that temperature dependence.
H2: Worked Example 1.2
P: A silicon diode's reverse leakage is dominated by a term proportional to ni². At 25 °C it leaks 1.0 nA. Estimate the leakage at 125 °C with the hand model ni ∝ T^1.5 e^(−Eg/(2kT)), Eg = 1.12 eV held fixed.
P: The ratio of ni between 398 K and 298 K is (398/298)^1.5 × exp[(1.12/(2 × 8.617×10⁻⁵)) × (1/298 − 1/398)] = 1.544 × exp(6499 × 8.43×10⁻⁴) = 1.544 × exp(5.48) = 1.544 × 240 = 370. The leakage scales as the square, about 1.4×10⁵, so the 1.0 nA becomes roughly 140 µA. Datasheets for small-signal silicon diodes show the same story: a leakage that is negligible at room temperature becomes a design input at the top of the rated range.
H2: Worked Example 1.3
P: Compare silicon with 4H silicon carbide (Eg ≈ 3.26 eV, and take √(Nc Nv) ≈ 2×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for a hand estimate) at 300 K. How many orders of magnitude smaller is ni in SiC?
P: For SiC, ni ≈ 2×10¹⁹ × e^(−3.26/0.0517) = 2×10¹⁹ × e^(−63.1) = 2×10¹⁹ × 4.1×10⁻²⁸ ≈ 8×10⁻⁹ cm⁻³. That is about eighteen orders of magnitude below silicon's 10¹⁰ cm⁻³; in a cubic centimeter of SiC at room temperature, thermally generated pairs essentially do not exist. This is why SiC leakage is set by defects and surfaces, not by intrinsic generation, and why a SiC device can tolerate a junction temperature at which silicon would be intrinsic.
H2: 1.7 What the chapter leaves on the bench
P: A crystal of silicon, then, is a lattice of fifty thousand billion billion atoms per cubic centimeter in which a few billion bonds are broken at any moment, a number set by the gap and violently sensitive to temperature. On its own it is a poor conductor and a poor insulator. What makes it the material of the century is that one population of carriers can be raised by a factor of a million on purpose, in a pattern drawn by lithography, while the other falls. That deliberate pinch of foreign atoms is the subject of the next chapter.
