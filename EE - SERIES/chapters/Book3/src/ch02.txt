H1: Chapter 2. Doping, and the Partner That Gets Scarce
P: Soup tastes salty after a pinch of salt, not after you have replaced the water. A semiconductor is the same kind of trick. You leave the crystal almost entirely silicon, and you swap in about one foreign atom per million, or per billion. That pinch decides whether the free charges are electrons or holes, and about how many of them there are.
H2: 2.1 Donors and acceptors
P: A column-V atom such as phosphorus has one more valence electron than silicon. Sitting on a silicon site, it completes the four bonds and has one electron left, loosely held. A little thermal energy frees that electron into the conduction band. The phosphorus atom, now positive, stays put. It donated an electron. Call its concentration ND. The material is n-type, and the electrons are the majority carriers.
P: A column-III atom such as boron has one valence electron too few. It completes three bonds and borrows an electron from a neighboring bond, leaving a hole that can hop. The boron atom, now negative, stays put. Call its concentration NA. The material is p-type, and the holes are the majority carriers.
H2: 2.2 Mass action
P: In equilibrium, generation and recombination balance. The product of the two carrier concentrations is fixed by the crystal and the temperature, not by how you doped it:
EQ: n p = ni²
P: Add donors and you raise n. The product cannot rise with it, so p falls. Add acceptors and the mirror happens. This is why a heavily doped n-type region is full of electrons and starved of holes. The starved population is still real, and it will matter as soon as a junction sweeps those minorities across a boundary.
H2: 2.3 Charge neutrality, the short form
P: A lump of semiconductor on the bench is not charged up like a capacitor plate. In the bulk, away from surfaces,
EQ: n + NA = p + ND
P: for ionized donors and acceptors, which is the right assumption at room temperature for ordinary silicon doping. If donors dominate and ND is far above ni, then n ≈ ND and p ≈ ni²/ND. For the 10^16 cm⁻³ example, p falls to 1.0e+04 cm⁻³, six decades below the majority population. The same arithmetic with acceptors swaps the names.
KEY: Key idea. Doping sets the majority. Mass action sets the minority. You do not choose them independently.
H2: Worked Example 2.1
P: Boron is added to silicon at NA = 1.0×10^17 cm⁻³. Donors are negligible. Find the majority and minority concentrations.
P: Holes are the majority: p ≈ NA = 1.0×10^17 cm⁻³. Electrons are the minority: n = ni²/NA = 1.0e+03 cm⁻³. There is one free electron for every ten million holes.
H2: 2.4 How loosely the extra electron is held
P: The phrase "a little thermal energy frees that electron" in Section 2.1 can be put in numbers. The fifth electron of a phosphorus atom is bound to its ion roughly the way the electron of a hydrogen atom is bound to its proton, except that the silicon around it screens the attraction by its relative permittivity of 11.7 and the electron moves with a reduced effective mass. Both effects weaken the bond enormously. Hydrogen's 13.6 eV shrinks to a few tens of millielectron-volts: about 45 meV for phosphorus, 54 meV for arsenic, and 45 meV for boron's hole. Compared with kT = 26 meV at room temperature, those energies are small, and nearly every dopant is ionized. That is the assumption behind n ≈ ND in Section 2.3.
P: Cool the crystal and the assumption fails. Below roughly 100 K the electrons begin to fall back onto their donors, a regime called freeze-out, and the carrier concentration drops below ND. Heat the crystal far enough and the opposite happens: ni climbs past the doping, as Section 1.6 showed, and the material turns intrinsic. Between the two lies the extrinsic range, where n ≈ ND holds within a few percent. For silicon doped near 10¹⁵ to 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ it spans about −150 °C to well above +200 °C, comfortably wider than any rated junction temperature. Cryogenic electronics for quantum computers and space instruments, which run at a few kelvin, must use device models that include freeze-out; ordinary circuit design never meets it.
P: Some atoms are deliberately chosen because they are not shallow. Gold and platinum put levels near the middle of the gap, where they make poor dopants and excellent recombination centers. Power diodes and thyristors are sometimes gold- or platinum-doped, or irradiated with electrons, to shorten the minority-carrier lifetime and speed up the reverse recovery that Chapter 6 describes, at the price of more leakage.
H2: 2.5 Compensation and the counter-doped region
P: Real devices are built by doping the same silicon more than once. A p-type well is implanted into an n-type wafer; an n+ source is implanted into that well. Each region contains both kinds of dopant, and what counts is the difference. If donors exceed acceptors, the region is n-type with n ≈ ND − NA; if acceptors win, p ≈ NA − ND. This is compensation, and it is how a planar process draws junctions: wherever the two concentrations cross, the net doping passes through zero and a pn junction forms.
P: Compensation has a cost that the net-doping formula hides. Every ionized dopant scatters carriers, whether it is a donor or an acceptor. A region with ND = 1.1×10¹⁷ and NA = 1.0×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ has the carrier concentration of a 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ region but the impurity scattering of a 2×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ region, so its mobility, and therefore its conductivity, is noticeably worse than the net doping alone suggests. Chapter 3 gives the mobility numbers.
H2: 2.6 Measuring what you put in
P: Two bench measurements tell a process engineer whether the doping came out right. The first is the four-point probe. Four needles in a line touch the wafer; a known current I flows between the outer pair, and the inner pair reads a voltage V without drawing current, so contact resistance drops out. For a thin doped layer on an insulating or oppositely doped substrate, the result is the sheet resistance,
EQ: Rs = (π / ln 2) × V / I = 4.532 V / I    (ohms per square),    Rs = ρ / t
P: where t is the layer thickness. Sheet resistance is the natural unit for a doped layer because a square of any size has the same resistance edge to edge: a resistor drawn as a strip ten squares long and one square wide has 10 Rs regardless of its absolute dimensions. Every diffused resistor, polysilicon resistor, and silicide layer in a process design kit, Chapter 22, is specified this way.
P: The second measurement is the Hall effect, and it answers a question the four-point probe cannot: which carrier is the majority, and how many are there? A bar carries current I along its length in a magnetic field B perpendicular to its face. The Lorentz force pushes the moving carriers toward one side until the charge that piles up there balances the force. The resulting Hall voltage across the width is
EQ: VH = I B / (q n t)
P: for a bar of thickness t along the field. Its sign reveals the carrier type, because holes and electrons moving in opposite directions are pushed to the same side and charge it oppositely. Its magnitude gives n directly, without needing to know the mobility. Combining VH with a resistivity measurement then yields the mobility as well, which is how the mobility tables used in this book were compiled.
KEY: Key idea. Dopants are shallow, so nearly all are ionized at room temperature. What sets a region's type is the net doping, but every dopant scatters. Sheet resistance and the Hall voltage are how the doping is checked.
H2: Worked Example 2.2
P: A Hall bar of n-type silicon is 0.5 mm thick. With I = 1.0 mA and B = 0.50 T, the Hall voltage is 2.5 mV. Find the electron concentration.
P: In SI units, n = I B / (q VH t) = (1.0×10⁻³)(0.50) / [(1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(2.5×10⁻³)(5.0×10⁻⁴)] = 2.5×10²¹ m⁻³ = 2.5×10¹⁵ cm⁻³. A lightly doped wafer gives millivolts, easy to measure; a heavily doped layer gives microvolts, which is why Hall measurements on thin implanted layers use thin samples and careful offset cancellation.
H2: Worked Example 2.3
P: A four-point probe on an implanted n+ layer 0.5 µm deep reads V/I = 22.0 Ω. Find the sheet resistance, the average resistivity of the layer, and the number of squares needed for a 10 kΩ resistor.
P: Rs = 4.532 × 22.0 = 99.7 Ω per square, call it 100 Ω/sq. The average resistivity is Rs t = 100 × 0.5×10⁻⁴ cm = 5.0×10⁻³ Ω·cm. A 10 kΩ resistor needs 10,000/100 = 100 squares, for example a strip 2 µm wide and 200 µm long, usually folded into a serpentine. Real layouts add a correction for the contact heads and the corners of the serpentine.
P: The chapter's ledger is now complete. Doping chooses the majority and fixes the minority, compensation lets a process draw one region inside another, and two simple measurements confirm the result. The next chapter sets those carriers in motion.
