H1: Chapter 14. The Noise a Device Makes
P: Book 2 treated op-amp noise as two numbers from a datasheet, a voltage density en and a current density in, and combined them with the thermal noise of the resistors around the amplifier. This chapter derives those numbers from the transistors. Three mechanisms account for nearly all of it: thermal noise in anything that has resistance, shot noise in any current that crosses a barrier, and flicker noise from charges trapped and released at imperfections. Each has a formula, and the formulas explain why bipolar and CMOS amplifiers have such different noise specifications.
H2: 14.1 Thermal and shot noise
P: Any resistance R at absolute temperature T generates a random voltage with a flat power spectral density 4kTR, in V²/Hz. Book 2 fixed the reference case: 100 kΩ at 290 K has 40 nV/√Hz. This part uses the same 290 K for noise, where 4kT = 1.60 × 10⁻²⁰ J, so that the numbers carry over directly; the 26 mV thermal voltage remains the hand value for device equations.
EQ: vn = √(4kTR),    in = √(2qI)
P: Shot noise arises because current crossing a junction is a stream of individual charges arriving independently. The current fluctuates about its mean with a flat density 2qI, in A²/Hz. A forward-biased diode, the collector current of a bipolar transistor, and the base current all carry full shot noise. A plain resistor does not: its electrons drift collectively and it has only thermal noise.
H2: Worked Example 14.1
P: Find the shot-noise current density of a 1 mA collector current and of the 10 µA base current that accompanies it at β = 100.
P: √(2 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10⁻³) = 17.9 pA/√Hz for the collector current, and √(2 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10⁻⁵) = 1.79 pA/√Hz for the base current. Over a 10 kHz bandwidth the collector figure integrates to 17.9 pA × √10,000 = 1.79 nA rms, roughly two parts per million of the current.
H2: 14.2 The bipolar transistor's input noise
P: Referred to the input, the collector's shot noise becomes a voltage: divide the current noise by gm. Because gm = IC/VT, the result falls as the square root of the collector current. The base current's shot noise flows in the source resistance and appears as the input current noise. The base spreading resistance rb adds its own thermal noise in series with the input, which matters for very low source resistances.
EQ: en = √(2qIC)/gm = VT √(2q/IC),    in = √(2q IB)
H2: Worked Example 14.2
P: Find en and in for the declared transistor at IC = 1 mA, ignoring rb. Find the source resistance for which the two contributions are equal, and the total noise with a 1 kΩ source.
P: en = 26 mV × √(2 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹/10⁻³) = 0.47 nV/√Hz. in = 1.79 pA/√Hz from Worked Example 14.1. The two are equal when the source resistance is en/in = 0.465 nV/1.79 pA = 260 Ω, which is rπ/√β = 2.60 kΩ/10. With a 1 kΩ source, the source's own thermal noise is √(1.60 × 10⁻²⁰ × 1000) = 4.0 nV/√Hz, and adding en² and (in × 1 kΩ)² gives √(4.0² + 0.47² + 1.79²) = 4.4 nV/√Hz. The transistor adds only 10% to the noise of the source.
P: The optimum source resistance rπ/√β can be moved by choosing the collector current. Lowering IC raises en and lowers in, in proportion, so high-impedance sources call for low-current input stages and low-impedance sources such as moving-coil microphones or strain-gauge bridges call for high-current ones, sometimes several transistors in parallel to lower rb as well.
H2: 14.3 The MOSFET's channel noise
P: The channel of a MOSFET in saturation is a resistor-like region whose thermal noise appears as a drain current density 4kTγgm, where γ is 2/3 for a long-channel device and somewhat larger for short channels. Referred to the gate, the voltage noise is set by gm alone. The gate draws no steady current, so in at low frequency is only the shot noise of the gate leakage, femtoamperes per root hertz, which is why CMOS-input amplifiers are the choice for high-impedance sources such as photodiodes and piezoelectric sensors.
EQ: en² = 4kTγ/gm   (V²/Hz)
H2: Worked Example 14.3
P: The MOSFET of Chapter 10's Worked Example 10.2 has gm = 0.40 mS. Find its gate-referred thermal noise with γ = 2/3, and the gm needed to match the bipolar transistor's 0.47 nV/√Hz.
P: en = √(1.60 × 10⁻²⁰ × 0.667/0.40 × 10⁻³) = 5.2 nV/√Hz. To reach 0.47 nV/√Hz, gm must grow by (5.17/0.465)² ≈ 124 to about 49 mS, which at a gm/ID of 10 V⁻¹ means 4.9 mA, more than the bipolar transistor's 1 mA. A bipolar transistor reaches low voltage noise with less current because its gm/ID of 38.5 V⁻¹ is the highest of any device.
H2: 14.4 Flicker noise
P: At low frequencies a third mechanism dominates. Charges trapped and released at defects, most of all at the MOSFET's oxide interface, modulate the current with a density that rises as 1/f. The frequency at which flicker noise equals the white noise is the corner frequency fc. In a voltage-density plot the flicker part rises as 1/√f, so for f below fc the total density is en,white √(1 + fc/f). MOSFET flicker noise is inversely proportional to gate area, so large input devices are quieter; PMOS devices typically have lower flicker noise than NMOS because their carriers flow slightly away from the interface. Bipolar transistors, whose current flows in the bulk, have corners of a few hertz to a few hundred hertz, against kilohertz to megahertz for small MOSFETs.
H2: Worked Example 14.4
P: A CMOS amplifier has a white voltage noise of 10 nV/√Hz and a flicker corner of 1 kHz. Find the noise density at 10 Hz, and the corner if the input devices are made four times larger in area at the same gm.
P: At 10 Hz, en = 10 × √(1 + 1000/10) = 10 × √101 = 100 nV/√Hz, ten times the white value. Flicker power is inversely proportional to area, so four times the area cuts the flicker power by four and moves the corner to 250 Hz; at 10 Hz the density becomes 10 × √26 = 51 nV/√Hz. Chopper-stabilized amplifiers move the signal above the corner before amplifying it and avoid flicker noise almost entirely.
KEY: Key idea. Voltage noise is set by transconductance, current noise by the input bias current, and flicker noise by area and interface quality. A bipolar input wins on voltage noise per unit current; a CMOS input wins on current noise by orders of magnitude.
H2: 14.5 The differential pair and the op-amp
P: An op-amp's input stage is a differential pair, and both transistors contribute uncorrelated noise. The input-referred voltage noise is therefore √2 times that of one transistor, before the load mirror adds its own share referred back through the ratio of its gm to the input gm. Designers keep the mirror's gm small relative to the input pair's for exactly this reason. Popcorn noise, abrupt switching between two current levels caused by a single trap, appears in some devices as a random telegraph signal; it is a process-quality issue, screened in production, not something a circuit can design away.
H2: 14.6 Matching the amplifier to the source
P: Section 14.5 described an op-amp's noise as a voltage density en and a current density in at its input. Which one matters depends on the source resistance Rs. The current noise flows through Rs and becomes a voltage in Rs; the source's own thermal noise is √(4kTRs). Adding the three uncorrelated terms in power,
EQ: etotal = √( en² + (in Rs)² + 4kTRs )
P: For small Rs the en term dominates; for large Rs the in Rs term dominates; in between lies the optimum source resistance Ropt = en/in, at which the amplifier adds the least noise relative to the source. A bipolar input, with low en and higher in, has a low Ropt; a FET input, with in in the femtoamps, has an enormous one. The choice of amplifier is therefore a choice made by the source, not a ranking of data sheets.
P: Two audio op-amps illustrate it. The NE5532 has en ≈ 5 nV/√Hz and in ≈ 0.7 pA/√Hz at 1 kHz, so Ropt ≈ 7 kΩ. The OPA1612 has en ≈ 1.1 nV/√Hz and in ≈ 1.7 pA/√Hz, so Ropt ≈ 650 Ω. For a dynamic microphone of about 200 Ω, the OPA1612 is far better; for a 10 kΩ source, the difference nearly vanishes, and for a 1 MΩ piezoelectric pickup both lose to a FET-input part whose current noise is a thousand times lower. The noise figure, the ratio in decibels of the total noise to the source's own thermal noise, puts a single number on the comparison and is the quantity Book 4 cascades through a receiver.
KEY: Key idea. Total input noise is en, in Rs, and the source's thermal noise added in power. The best amplifier for a source has Ropt = en/in near Rs.
H2: Worked Example 14.1
P: A 200 Ω microphone feeds either an NE5532 or an OPA1612. Using 4kT = 1.60×10⁻²⁰ J, find the total input noise density and the noise figure at 1 kHz for each.
P: The source's thermal noise is √(1.60×10⁻²⁰ × 200) = 1.79 nV/√Hz. NE5532: in Rs = 0.14 nV/√Hz, negligible, and etotal = √(5² + 0.14² + 1.79²) = 5.31 nV/√Hz, a noise figure of 20 log(5.31/1.79) = 9.5 dB. OPA1612: in Rs = 0.34 nV/√Hz, and etotal = √(1.1² + 0.34² + 1.79²) = 2.13 nV/√Hz, a noise figure of 1.5 dB. The OPA1612 adds 8 dB less noise to this source. Over a 20 kHz audio band the totals are 0.75 µV and 0.30 µV rms.
