P: Pelgrom matching constant, working value for a recent process: AVt = 3 mV·µm.
P: Critical field for avalanche, hand values: silicon 3×10⁵ V/cm, 4H-SiC 2.5×10⁶ V/cm, GaN 3.3×10⁶ V/cm.
P: Photon energy and wavelength: λ (nm) ≈ 1240 / E (eV).
P: Human-body ESD model: 100 pF through 1.5 kΩ.

P: Statistics: one-sided Q(3) = 1.35×10⁻³, Q(6) = 9.9×10⁻¹⁰; ±3σ holds 99.73%.
P: Thermal energy kT = 25.9 meV at 300 K. Boltzmann constant k = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K.
P: Silicon lattice constant a = 0.5431 nm; atom density 4.99×10²² cm⁻³. Effective densities of states at 300 K: Nc ≈ 2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³, Nv ≈ 1.04×10¹⁹ cm⁻³.
P: Measured silicon mobilities at 300 K (cm²/V·s), electrons / holes: lightly doped 1350 / 480; 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ 1200 / 440; 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ 800 / 320; 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ 280 / 150. Lattice-limited mobility ∝ T^(−2.4).
P: Dopant ionization energies in silicon: P 45 meV, As 54 meV, B 45 meV.

P: Schottky thermionic emission: JS = A* T² e^(−φB/VT), A* ≈ 112 A/(cm²·K²) for n-type silicon.
P: AlGaN/GaN 2DEG: ns ≈ 1×10¹³ cm⁻², practical current density about 1 A per mm of gate.
P: Poisson die yield Y = e^(−A D0); 300 mm wafer area ≈ 707 cm².
P: Soft errors: 3.6 eV per electron-hole pair in silicon; FIT = failures per 10⁹ device-hours.
P: Representative material constants for Part IX (gap, critical field, electron mobility, relative permittivity, saturation velocity, thermal conductivity): listed in Section 25.1. Orientation values, not design values.

H1: Appendix B. Answers to the Practice Problems
P: Every practice problem is worked in full below: the idea, the numbers, and a check or the usual mistake. Where a problem continues a worked example, the solution uses that example's declared values.
H2: Chapter 1
P: 1. Idea: the dopant sets the majority, mass action sets the minority, and conductivity follows the majority. p = ni²/n = (1.0×10¹⁰)²/1.0×10¹⁵ = 1.0×10⁵ cm⁻³. σ = q n μn = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(1.0×10¹⁵)(1000) = 0.1602 (Ω·cm)⁻¹, so ρ = 1/σ = 6.24 Ω·cm. Check: ten times fewer electrons than Worked Example 1.1 gives ten times its 0.624 Ω·cm. The usual mistake is to keep the hole term; it is ten orders of magnitude smaller.
P: 2. Idea: ni carries the factor e^(−Eg/2kT), so the gap sits in an exponent. A wider gap means fewer bonds broken at any temperature and a much smaller ni. Junction leakage is fed by minority carriers whose concentration scales as ni², so it falls even faster than ni. Check with numbers: going from silicon's 1.12 eV to 4H-SiC's 3.26 eV cut ni by about eighteen orders of magnitude in Worked Example 1.3. The usual mistake is to think a wide gap only raises the forward drop; its main gift is low leakage when hot.
P: 3. Idea: in intrinsic material both carriers are present in equal numbers, so both terms of σ count. σ = q ni (μn + μp) = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(1.0×10¹⁰)(1000 + 480) = 2.37×10⁻⁶ (Ω·cm)⁻¹, a resistivity of about 420 kΩ·cm. The hole share is 480/1480 = 0.32, about a third. Check: not ignorable. Dropping the hole term was fair in Worked Example 1.1 only because doping made p twelve decades smaller than n.
P: 4. Idea: the unit cell holds eight atoms in a cube of edge a. N = 8/a³ = 8/(5.431×10⁻⁸ cm)³ = 8/1.602×10⁻²² = 4.99×10²² cm⁻³. Atoms per dopant: 4.99×10²²/10¹⁶ ≈ 5×10⁶, about five million silicon atoms for each dopant. Check the units: a must be in centimeters before cubing; 0.5431 nm = 5.431×10⁻⁸ cm.
P: 5. Idea: doping moves EF away from midgap by kT times the log of how far the majority exceeds ni. Ei − EF = VT ln(p/ni) = 0.026 × ln(10¹⁷/10¹⁰) = 0.026 × 16.12 = 0.419 eV. EF lies 0.419 eV below Ei, toward the valence band. Check: p-type material must have EF below midgap; an answer above midgap means the sign was dropped.
P: 6. Idea: 25 °C to 70 °C is 45 °C, five doublings at 9 °C each. ni rises about 2⁵ = 32 times, and a leakage proportional to ni² about 32² ≈ 1000 times. The full hand model ni ∝ T^1.5 e^(−Eg/2kT) gives 21.5 and 463, so the doubling rule is generous at this range because the per-kelvin rate (0.077 at 300 K) falls as T rises. The usual mistake is to forget the square for ni² leakage.
P: 7. Multiplying the two Boltzmann expressions adds their exponents, and EF enters one with a plus sign and the other with a minus sign, so it cancels and leaves only Ec − Ev = Eg, a property of the crystal and the temperature alone.
H2: Chapter 2
P: 1. Idea: shallow donors are all ionized at room temperature, so n ≈ ND, and mass action gives the minority. n ≈ 1.0×10¹⁵ cm⁻³. p = ni²/n = 1.0×10²⁰/1.0×10¹⁵ = 1.0×10⁵ cm⁻³. Check: n p = 1.0×10²⁰ = ni². The usual mistake is to write p = 0.
P: 2. Mass action fixes the product n p = ni², so the hole population falls but cannot reach zero. At ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, p = 10²⁰/10¹⁶ = 1.0×10⁴ cm⁻³: ten thousand holes per cubic centimeter. They are rare, and they are exactly the minority carriers a junction later sweeps across, so they matter.
P: 3. Idea: the larger dopant wins, and the net difference sets the majority. Neutrality: n + NA = p + ND. Donors exceed acceptors by 9.0×10¹⁵ cm⁻³, so the material is n-type. The small term is p, the minority; dropping it gives n ≈ ND − NA = 9.0×10¹⁵ cm⁻³. Then p = ni²/n = 10²⁰/9.0×10¹⁵ = 1.1×10⁴ cm⁻³. Check: p is eleven decades below the doping difference, so dropping it changed nothing. The usual mistake is to drop it before naming it, or to add the dopants instead of subtracting them.
P: 4. Both regions have the same free-carrier concentration, but the compensated one holds 2.1×10¹⁶ ionized impurities per cm³ instead of 10¹⁶, and every one of them scatters carriers, so its mobility and therefore its conductivity are lower.
P: 5. Idea: the sign of VH names the carrier, the size gives the concentration without needing the mobility. The negative reading means electrons, so the bar is n-type. In SI units, n = I B/(q |VH| t) = (0.5×10⁻³)(0.40)/[(1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(1.0×10⁻³)(2.5×10⁻⁴)] = 5.0×10²¹ m⁻³ = 5.0×10¹⁵ cm⁻³. Check the units: t = 0.25 mm = 2.5×10⁻⁴ m, and m⁻³ divided by 10⁶ gives cm⁻³. The usual mistake is to mix millimeters and meters, which shifts the answer by a factor of 1000.
P: 6. Idea: a resistor in sheet-resistance units is a count of squares. N = R/Rs = 1000/25 = 40 squares. At 1 µm wide the strip is 40 × 1 µm = 40 µm long. Check: any width works if the length scales with it; 2 µm wide needs 80 µm. Real layouts add a fraction of a square for each contact head.
H2: Chapter 3
P: 1. Idea: the Einstein relation ties diffusion to mobility through the thermal voltage, D = μ VT. Dn = 1000 × 0.026 = 26.0 cm²/s. Dp = 480 × 0.026 = 12.48 cm²/s. Check the units: cm²/V·s times V gives cm²/s. The usual mistake is to use VT in millivolts and get answers a thousand times too large.
P: 2. Idea: diffusion current is charge times diffusion constant times gradient. |Jp| = q Dp |dp/dx| = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(12.48)(1.0×10²⁰) = 200 A/cm². Holes are positive, so conventional current points the same way the holes are spreading, down the gradient. Check against electrons: the same gradient of electrons would give a current pointing opposite to their spread, because their charge is negative.
P: 3. Idea: with no gradient only drift is left, J = q n μn E. J = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(1.0×10¹⁶)(1000)(10) = 16.0 A/cm². Worked Example 3.1 gave 375 A/cm² from a one-micron gradient, about 23 times more. Check: a field of 10 V/cm is tiny; the steep gradients inside a junction are why diffusion matters there and drift dominates in the bulk.
P: 4. Idea: resistance scales as 1/μ, and lattice-limited mobility falls as T^(−2.4). R(350) = R(300) × (350/300)^2.4 = 10.0 kΩ × 1.448 = 14.5 kΩ. Check: that is a 45 % rise over 50 K, about 0.9 % per kelvin on average, consistent with the 0.8 %/K slope at 300 K quoted in Section 3.4. The usual mistake is to apply the exponent with the wrong sign and predict a falling resistance.
P: 5. Idea: τd = εs/σ. σ = 1/ρ = 0.1 (Ω·cm)⁻¹ and εs = 11.7 × 8.854×10⁻¹⁴ = 1.04×10⁻¹² F/cm, so τd = 1.04×10⁻¹²/0.1 = 1.04×10⁻¹¹ s = 10.4 ps. Check: ten times the resistivity of the 1 Ω·cm example gives ten times its 1 ps. Even lightly doped silicon neutralizes majority charge far faster than any circuit event.
P: 6. Idea: at saturation the time is length over the velocity ceiling, t = L/vsat. t = 5×10⁻⁶ cm/1×10⁷ cm/s = 5×10⁻¹³ s = 0.5 ps. Check the conversion: 50 nm = 5×10⁻⁶ cm. The usual mistake is to use μE, which promises a shorter time than silicon can deliver.
H2: Chapter 4
P: 1. Idea: Vbi = VT ln(NA ND/ni²). NA ND/ni² = (1.0×10¹⁸)(1.0×10¹⁶)/1.0×10²⁰ = 1.0×10¹⁴. ln(10¹⁴) = 32.24, so Vbi = 0.026 × 32.24 = 0.838 V. Check: a hundred times more doping on one side than in Worked Example 4.1 adds VT ln(100) = 0.120 V to its 0.718 V. The usual mistake is to forget to square ni.
P: 2. Only somewhat larger. W grows as the square root of Vbi − V. At zero bias the root holds 0.718 V; at −3 V it holds 3.72 V, about 5.2 times more, so W grows by √5.2 ≈ 2.3 times, not 5.2. Check against Worked Example 4.2: going from 0 V to −10 V raised the voltage across the layer thirteenfold and the width only 3.6 times.
P: 3. Vbi = VT ln(NA ND/ni²), and ni grows steeply with temperature, so the argument of the logarithm shrinks faster than VT grows and Vbi falls, by roughly 2 mV per kelvin for typical dopings. Physically, the hotter crystal has more minority carriers on each side, so a smaller barrier already balances diffusion.
P: 4. Idea: a symmetric junction splits the depletion layer equally, so the two-sided formula is needed. Vbi = 0.026 × ln(10³⁴/10²⁰) = 0.838 V. W = √[2 εs Vbi (NA + ND)/(q NA ND)] = √[2 × 1.036×10⁻¹² × 0.838 × 2×10⁻¹⁷/1.602×10⁻¹⁹] = 1.47×10⁻⁵ cm = 0.147 µm, half on each side. Check: ten times the doping of Worked Example 4.1 shrinks its 0.431 µm by about √10 = 3.2, slightly less because Vbi rose. The usual mistake is to use the one-sided formula, which gives 0.104 µm.
P: 5. Idea: set Emax equal to the critical field and solve for the voltage across the layer. Vbi − V = εs Emax²/(2 q ND) = 1.036×10⁻¹² × (3×10⁵)²/(2 × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁶) = 29.1 V, so V ≈ −28.3 V, a reverse bias of about 28 V. Check: Worked Example 4.2 had 1.83×10⁵ V/cm at 10.8 V across the layer, and Emax grows as the square root, so 1.83×10⁵ × √(29.1/10.8) = 3.0×10⁵ V/cm. Measured breakdown of such a junction is nearer 50 to 60 V, because avalanche needs multiplication over a distance; the estimate is conservative.
P: 6. Idea: the capacitance ratio is (1 − V/Vbi)^m. 1 − (−10)/0.7 = 15.29. For m = 0.5 the capacitance falls by 15.29^0.5 = 3.91 times; for m = 1.0 by 15.29 times. Check: the hyperabrupt profile buys a fourfold wider tuning range from the same 10 V swing, which is why tuning varactors use it. The usual mistake is to forget the sign: reverse bias makes V negative, so the bracket grows.
H2: Chapter 5
P: 1. Idea: well above IS the exponential term rules, I ≈ IS e^(V/VT). I = 1.0×10⁻¹⁵ × e^(0.65/0.026) = 1.0×10⁻¹⁵ × e^25 = 1.0×10⁻¹⁵ × 7.20×10¹⁰ = 7.20×10⁻⁵ A = 72.0 µA. Check: Worked Example 5.1 gave 10.5 µA at 0.60 V; 50 mV more is 0.83 decade, a factor of 6.9, and 10.5 × 6.9 = 72.
P: 2. Idea: one decade of current costs VT ln 10. The rule says 59.9 mV. Exact: V = VT ln(I/IS + 1) gives 0.6585 V at 0.1 mA and 0.7184 V at 1 mA, a difference of 59.9 mV. Check: the rule is the exact ideal result once I is far above IS, and 0.1 mA is eleven decades above 1 fA.
P: 3. Idea: in reverse the exponential vanishes and only −IS survives. I = IS(e^(−0.2/0.026) − 1) = 1.0×10⁻¹⁵ × (4.56×10⁻⁴ − 1) = −9.995×10⁻¹⁶ A ≈ −IS. The exponential is already below 0.05 % of the 1, so the current has saturated. A real diode leaks more because generation inside the depletion layer and surface leakage add currents the ideal law leaves out. The usual mistake is to report a positive current or to drop the −1.
P: 4. Idea: at constant current the forward voltage drifts about −1.8 mV per kelvin. ΔT = 60 K, so ΔV ≈ 60 × (−1.8 mV) = −108 mV, and V ≈ 0.650 − 0.108 = 0.54 V at 85 °C. Check: the coefficient (V − Eg/q − 3VT)/T shrinks a little as V falls and T rises, so the estimate is good to a few millivolts. The usual mistake is to add the drift and predict a higher voltage when hot.
P: 5. Idea: one decade costs n VT ln 10, so n = ΔV/(VT ln 10). n = 0.118/(0.026 × 2.3026) = 1.97, about 2. An ideality factor near 2 at nanoamp currents means recombination inside the depletion layer dominates. Check: n = 1 would need only 60 mV for the decade.
P: 6. Idea: rd = n VT/I with n = 1. At 0.1 mA, rd = 0.026/10⁻⁴ = 260 Ω. At 10 mA, rd = 0.026/10⁻² = 2.6 Ω. Check: a hundred times the current gives a hundredth of the resistance. The usual mistake is to use V/I, the large-signal ratio, which gives about 6.6 kΩ at 0.1 mA, far from the slope.
H2: Chapter 6
P: 1. Idea: an abrupt junction's capacitance falls as the square root of 1 − V/Vbi. 1 − (−2)/0.718 = 3.786, whose square root is 1.946, so Cj = 20 pF/1.946 = 10.3 pF. Check: Worked Example 6.1 took 10 pF to 3.54 pF at −5 V; a smaller reverse voltage should cut less, and halving is less than a factor of 2.8. The usual mistake is to forget that V is negative in reverse.
P: 2. Forward current stores minority charge on both sides of the junction, and the diode cannot block until that charge has been pulled back out or has recombined; the recovery time is the wait. A Schottky junction carries its current as majority electrons going over a metal barrier, so almost no minority charge is stored and the recovery tail is largely absent. What remains is the charging of its junction capacitance.
P: 3. The resistor sets the current through the knee, about (12 − 5.1)/R; the junction only sets the voltage, and without the resistor the 12 V rail would try to force 12 V across a 5.1 V knee and destroy the part.
P: 4. Idea: thermionic emission gives JS = A* T² e^(−φB/VT). With VT = kT/q = 0.02585 V at 300 K: JS = 112 × (300)² × e^(−0.6/0.02585) = 1.008×10⁷ × 8.3×10⁻¹¹ = 8.4×10⁻⁴ A/cm². IS = JS A = 8.4×10⁻⁸ A = 84 nA. At 1 mA, V = 0.02585 × ln(10⁻³/8.4×10⁻⁸) = 0.02585 × 9.39 = 0.24 V. Check: Section 6.4 found 0.34 V for φB = 0.7 eV; 0.1 eV less barrier is about 0.1 V less drop. The usual mistake is to use the pn-junction formula with ni².
P: 5. Heavy doping, above about 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, thins the depletion layer under the metal to a few nanometers, so electrons tunnel straight through the barrier in both directions and the contact acts as a small resistor instead of a rectifying Schottky diode.
P: 6. Idea: above breakdown a TVS acts as its breakdown voltage plus a small resistance. The dynamic resistance from the data-sheet points is (9.2 − 6.4)/65.2 = 43 mΩ. At 30 A: Vclamp ≈ 6.4 + 30 × 0.043 = 7.7 V. Check: 30 A is about half the rated 65.2 A, and 7.7 V lies about halfway between 6.4 and 9.2 V. In practice it reads somewhat higher, because the junction heats during the pulse and the breakdown voltage itself rises. The usual mistake is to use the 5.0 V stand-off voltage as the start of the line.
H2: Chapter 7
P: 1. Idea: the optimum load is peak voltage over peak current, and the power is half their product. Ropt = 40/0.50 = 80 Ω. P = (40)(0.50)/2 = 10 W. Check: against the 28 V, 0.20 A case of Worked Example 7.1, the voltage rose 1.43 times and the current 2.5 times, so the power should rise 3.6 times, from 2.8 W to 10 W. The usual mistake is to forget the factor of 2 and report 20 W.
P: 2. Open an InP HEMT first. At 94 GHz with a small input signal the noise figure is the specification, and InP HEMTs give the lowest noise figure from about 30 GHz through W-band. Do not open a GaN power switch or a handset GaAs HBT: neither is a low-noise millimeter-wave part. A GaN limiter in front of the LNA can be useful for survivability, but the 0.4 dB deficit is an InP problem.
P: 3. GaN's wide gap and high critical field let one HEMT structure block hundreds of volts, and GaN grows on large, cheap silicon wafers, so the same material serves a 50 V radar transistor on SiC and a 650 V switch on silicon. InP has a narrow-gap InGaAs channel built for 1 to 5 V and sits on small, fragile wafers that spread heat poorly, so it has no place in a high-voltage switch.
P: 4. Idea: Class A power density is the swing times the current over 8. Vmax ≈ 2 × 28 = 56 V. Power density = (56 − 3) × 0.8/8 = 5.3 W/mm. Periphery for 25 W: 25/5.3 = 4.7 mm of gate. Check: at 1 A/mm Section 7.6 found 6.6 W/mm, and 0.8 of that is 5.3. The usual mistake is to use the supply, 28 V, instead of the peak, 56 V, which halves the answer.
P: 5. The electrons of the 2DEG come from polarization charge at the AlGaN/GaN interface, not from dopant atoms inside the channel, so the sheet can be dense while the well holds almost no ionized impurities to scatter them; Chapter 3's falling mobility comes from those impurities.
P: 6. Idea: the valence-band step adds to the barrier that holes must climb, so the back-injected hole current falls by the Boltzmann factor. Factor = exp(ΔEv/kT) = exp(0.15/0.026) = exp(5.77) = 320. Check: Worked Example 7.3's 0.20 eV gave 2,190, and every 0.06 eV changes the factor by about ten, so 0.15 eV giving a few hundred is consistent. The usual mistake is to use the whole bandgap difference; only the valence-band part of the step blocks holes.
P: 7. Idea: conduction loss is I²R with the resistance that applies while the switch conducts. DC value: 10² × 0.070 = 7.0 W. Dynamic value: R = 1.3 × 70 = 91 mΩ, so 10² × 0.091 = 9.1 W, 2.1 W more. Check: 30 % more resistance gives 30 % more loss at the same current. The usual mistake is to size the heat sink with the DC data sheet value, which a hard-switched GaN stage never sees right after turn-off.
H2: Chapter 8
P: 1. Idea: IS is inversely proportional to the Gummel number NA,B WB, and the transit time grows as WB². Halving WB doubles IS: 2 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁶ = 3.2×10⁻¹⁶ A. The transit time WB²/(2Dn) = (5.0×10⁻⁶)²/(2 × 10) = 1.25×10⁻¹² s = 1.25 ps, a quarter of the 5 ps before. Check: the square law makes thin bases fast, which is why RF transistors chase thin bases; the cost is a lower Early voltage and easier punch-through.
P: 2. Idea: α = β/(β + 1) and IE = IC + IB. α = 150/151 = 0.9934. IB = 2.0 mA/150 = 13.3 µA, so IE = 2.0133 mA, equally IC/α. Check: α is within 1 % of unity for any useful β, which is why IE ≈ IC is a safe hand approximation.
P: 3. Idea: VBE = VT ln(IC/IS + 1). At 0.1 mA: 0.026 × ln(10¹¹) = 0.026 × 25.33 = 0.659 V. At 10 mA: 0.026 × ln(10¹³) = 0.026 × 29.93 = 0.778 V. They differ by 120 mV because two decades of current at 59.9 mV per decade is 119.7 mV. The usual mistake is to treat VBE as a fixed 0.7 V and miss that a hundredfold current change moves it by more than a tenth of a volt.
P: 4. β ∝ NE in the injection ratio, so tripling NE predicts three times the gain. A real device gains less: heavy doping narrows the emitter's bandgap, raising its effective ni² and therefore the backward hole injection, and it lowers the hole diffusion constant and lifetime in the emitter. Above about 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ the gain can even fall, which is one reason the polysilicon emitter and the SiGe base were invented.
P: 5. In saturation the forward-biased collector junction lets excess minority charge pile up in the base and collector, and that charge must be removed or recombine before the collector junction can block; a transistor held in forward-active mode never stored that excess, so it has less charge to remove and turns off sooner.
P: 6. Idea: solve the Ebers-Moll formula for βforced. With βR = 1, αR = 0.5 and 1/αR = 2. Set 0.026 ln[(2 + x)/(1 − x/100)] = 0.1, so (2 + x)/(1 − x/100) = e^(3.846) = 46.8. Then 2 + x = 46.8 − 0.468x, which gives 1.468x = 44.8 and x = 30.5. Check: Worked Example 8.3 found 67 mV at 10 and 121 mV at 50, and 0.1 V falls between them. The usual mistake is to forget the βforced/βR term in the numerator.
P: 7. The Schottky diode turns on at a lower voltage than the collector-base junction, so it diverts the excess base current into the collector before that junction forward-biases deeply. Little minority charge is stored, so there is little to remove at turn-off and the switch recovers quickly.
H2: Chapter 9
P: 1. Idea: gm depends only on the current; rπ adds β. gm = IC/VT = 0.25 mA/26 mV = 9.62 mS. rπ = β/gm = 120/0.00962 = 12.5 kΩ. Check: a quarter of the declared 1 mA gives a quarter of 38.5 mS, and rπ scales as β/IC. The usual mistake is to compute rπ as VT/IB without noticing it is the same thing, then use the wrong β.
P: 2. Idea: ro = VA/IC and gm ro = VA/VT. ro = 60 V/0.5 mA = 120 kΩ. gm = 0.5 mA/26 mV = 19.2 mS, so gm ro = 19.2 mS × 120 kΩ = 2310, which is 20 log(2310) = 67.3 dB. Check: VA/VT = 60/0.026 = 2308, the same number without the current, as Section 9.4 promised.
P: 3. Idea: keep IE at 0.986 mA with a larger VB, so the same VBE change is a smaller fraction of the voltage across RE. RE = (4.0 − 0.718)/0.986 mA = 3.33 kΩ. Check: the fixed-drop term grows linearly and the resistive term quadratically, so at this current they are comparable; a MOSFET would need below 37.5 mΩ to match it.
P: 6. The IGBT conducts by flooding its drift region with injected holes, which must recombine or be swept out after the channel turns off, while a MOSFET's current is carried by majority electrons alone, which stop as soon as the channel closes.
P: 7. Idea: I²R at the hot resistance. I² = 225. Si: 225 × 0.126 = 28.4 W. SiC: 225 × 0.075 = 16.9 W. GaN: 225 × 0.080 = 18.0 W. Check: each is 2.25 times its value at 10 A, and the ranking is unchanged; the SiC part still wins conduction because of its flat temperature curve.
P: 8. Its body diode stores a large minority charge and recovers slowly, so when the opposite switch turns on and forces that diode off while it is conducting, the reverse-recovery current causes large losses and voltage stress in both devices.
H2: Chapter 20
P: 1. Idea: about 44 nm of silicon is consumed for every 100 nm of oxide grown. 20 × 0.44 = 8.8 nm. Check: the oxide grows partly into the wafer and partly above the original surface, 56 % above and 44 % below, because silicon dioxide is less dense in silicon atoms than the crystal.
P: 2. Idea: Arrhenius law D = D0 exp(−Ea/kT). At 1373.15 K, kT = 8.617×10⁻⁵ × 1373.15 = 0.1183 eV, so Ea/kT = 3.5/0.1183 = 29.58, and D = 1.0 × e^(−29.58) = 1.42×10⁻¹³ cm²/s. Check: 100 °C hotter than Worked Example 20.1's 1000 °C raised D about tenfold. The usual mistake is to use Celsius in kT.
P: 3. Idea: the profile spreads by about 2√(Dt). Dt = 1.42×10⁻¹³ × 3600 = 5.11×10⁻¹⁰ cm², √ = 2.26×10⁻⁵ cm, so 2√(Dt) = 4.5×10⁻⁵ cm = 0.45 µm. Check: an hour at 1100 °C moves boron almost half a micrometer, enough to wreck a modern junction; this is the thermal budget Section 20.2 warns about.
P: 4. Idea: average concentration is dose over depth. 5×10¹³ cm⁻²/5×10⁻⁵ cm = 1.0×10¹⁸ cm⁻³. Check the units: 0.5 µm = 5×10⁻⁵ cm, and cm⁻² over cm gives cm⁻³.
P: 5. Idea: R = k1 λ/NA. R = 0.3 × 13.5/0.33 = 12.3 nm. Check: k1 = 0.4 gave 16.4 nm in Worked Example 20.3, so a more aggressive process factor buys a quarter less pitch on the same tool.
P: 6. 193 nm immersion lithography could not print features below about 40 nm half-pitch in one exposure, and EUV tools were years late reaching production, so the industry split each fine layer into two to four aligned exposures, at the cost of more masks, more steps, and tighter overlay.
P: 7. Idea: Poisson yield Y = e^(−A D0). A D0 = 2.0 × 0.2 = 0.4, so Y = e^(−0.4) = 0.67. Check: on average 0.4 defects land on each die, and the fraction of dice with none is 67 %.
P: 8. Yield falls exponentially with area, so each small die has a much higher chance of being defect-free, and the defective small dice are discarded individually before assembly instead of one defect scrapping a whole large die.
H2: Chapter 21
P: 1. Idea: the film is half an acoustic wavelength thick, t = v/(2f). t = 11,350/(2 × 3.5×10⁹) = 1.62×10⁻⁶ m = 1.62 µm. Check: Worked Example 21.1 gave 2.32 µm at 2.45 GHz, and 2.32 × 2.45/3.5 = 1.62. Real films are thinner still, because the electrodes add mass.
P: 2. Idea: the electrode period is one acoustic wavelength, and a finger is a quarter of it. λ = v/f = 4000/2×10⁹ = 2 µm, so the finger width is 0.5 µm. Check: half the 1 µm finger of a 1 GHz device, as Section 21.4 gave; at 5 GHz the fingers would be 0.2 µm, the reason SAW yields to BAW at high frequency.
P: 3. Idea: (fp − fs)/fp = (4/π²) kt². The fraction is 0.10 × 4/π² = 0.0405, so fp = 3.5/(1 − 0.0405) = 3.648 GHz and the spacing is 148 MHz. Check: the shortcut 0.0405 × 3.5 GHz = 142 MHz uses fs in place of fp and is 4 % low. The usual mistake is to take kt² itself, 10 %, as the fractional spacing.
P: 4. Idea: fp = fs √(1 + Cm/C0). fp/fs = √1.05 = 1.0247, so the parallel resonance sits 2.5 % above the series one. Check: Worked Example 21.2 had Cm/C0 = 0.055 for a 2.7 % spacing.
P: 5. Sound in these materials travels tens of thousands of times slower than light, so at the same frequency its wavelength is tens of thousands of times shorter, and a resonator sized in acoustic half-wavelengths is micrometers instead of centimeters.
P: 6. Scandium softens the lattice and raises the piezoelectric response, which raises the coupling kt², and the coupling sets the spacing between the series and parallel resonances that bounds a ladder filter's bandwidth.
P: 7. Idea: the spacing is about (4/π²) kt² of the frequency. 0.0263 × 1900 MHz = 50 MHz. Check: the PCS band near 1.9 GHz needs 60 MHz plus guard band per direction, so a pure AlN ladder is marginal there too; band filters use careful design or higher-coupling films.
P: 8. At 24 % fractional bandwidth it is roughly ten times wider than the resonance spacing a BAW film provides, so a ladder cannot pass it; laterally excited resonators in lithium niobate, lumped LC filters, or splitting the band are used instead.
P: 9. Idea: the electrode period of a SAW transducer is one acoustic wavelength. λ = v/f = 3,158/(433.92×10⁶) = 7.28 µm, so the fingers are about 1.82 µm wide. Check: features this large are easy to print, one reason quartz SAW resonators for 433 MHz remote controls cost so little. The usual mistake is to apply the BAW rule f = v/(2t), which describes a thickness, not an electrode period.
P: 10. Idea: compare the needed fractional bandwidth with each material's resonance spacing, (4/π²) k². Needed: 100/5,000 = 2.0 %. AlN gives 2.6 %, too little once temperature drift and a ladder's narrower usable passband are counted. AlScN at 13 % gives 5.3 %, ample, and a thin-film lithium niobate XBAR offers more. SAW is ruled out at 5 GHz by sub-quarter-micrometer fingers and limited power handling. Choose an AlScN BAW, or an XBAR if the band grows. Check: Worked Example 21.3 reached the same verdict at 3.5 GHz.
H2: Chapter 22
P: 1. Idea: n is the measured voltage per decade divided by the ideal 59.9 mV. n = 0.06/(0.026 × ln 10) = 0.06/0.0599 = 1.00. Check: one decade of current for 60 mV is the ideal diffusion law, so this diode sits in its ideal mid-current window. The usual mistake is to use log base 10 without the ln 10 factor and get n = 2.3.
P: 2. Idea: Pelgrom's law, σ(ΔVt) = AVt/√(WL). WL = 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1 µm², √0.1 = 0.316 µm, so σ = 3/0.316 = 9.5 mV. Check: a tenth of the area of a 1 µm² pair gives √10 = 3.16 times its 3 mV.
P: 3. Idea: solve Pelgrom's law for area. √(WL) = AVt/σ = 3 mV·µm/1 mV = 3 µm, so WL = 9 µm², for example 3 µm × 3 µm. Check: a threefold improvement over a 1 µm² pair needs nine times the area.
P: 4. Idea: a Gaussian keeps 1 − 2Q(3) inside ±3σ. 1 − 2 × 1.35×10⁻³ = 0.9973, so 99.73 % of parts lie inside. Check: 0.27 % outside, about one part in 370.
P: 5. The simulator solves the circuit by Newton iteration, which needs continuous currents and continuous derivatives; a jump or a kink between regions makes the iteration oscillate or fail to converge, and it also gives wrong small-signal values such as gm at the region boundary.
P: 6. A memory has millions or billions of identical cells and all of them must work, so the failure probability of each cell must be extremely small, below about 10⁻⁸ for a 128 Mbit array, which is near 5.7σ; 3σ would leave hundreds of thousands of failing cells.
P: 7. Idea: a two-sided spec at ±3σ fails 2Q(3). 2 × 1.35×10⁻³ = 2.7×10⁻³ = 0.27 %, about 2700 parts per million. Check: Problem 4 found 99.73 % inside, the complement.
P: 8. Idea: σ ∝ 1/√(WL), so halving σ needs √(WL) doubled. Four times the gate area halves σ. Check: Problem 3 found nine times the area for a third of the σ, the same square law.
H2: Chapter 23
P: 1. Idea: invert the diode law. V = N VT ln(I/Is) = 1.8 × 0.026 × ln(10⁻³/3×10⁻⁹) = 0.0468 × 12.72 = 0.595 V. Check: a large N spreads the exponential, and a large Is lowers the curve, so this diode reaches 1 mA below the roughly 0.65 V of a near-ideal small-signal diode. The usual mistake is to use log10 instead of ln.
P: 2. Idea: two points on the straight part of the log curve give the slope and the intercept. N = ΔV/(VT ln(I2/I1)) = 0.07/(0.026 × 2.303) = 1.17. Is = I1 exp(−V1/(N VT)) = 10⁻⁴ × exp(−0.55/(1.17 × 0.026)) = 10⁻⁴ × exp(−18.10) = 1.39×10⁻¹² A. Check: the second point, 1.39×10⁻¹² × exp(0.62/0.0304) = 1.0 mA, is reproduced. The usual mistake is to fit Is first with N = 1, which forces the wrong slope.
P: 3. Idea: the residual at high current is what Rs absorbs in a level 1 card. Matching 0.95 V needs 0.15 V across Rs at 200 mA, so Rs = 0.15/0.2 = 0.75 Ω, which moves the 10 mA point by only 2.5 mV. Raising Rs can still be the wrong fix: the extra drop may come from high injection, which a level 1 card cannot represent and which bends the curve differently at other currents, or from fixture and lead resistance in a measurement made without Kelvin connections, in which case the part itself is better than the bench suggests. Check the fixture first, then decide whether the card needs a high-injection term. The usual mistake is to tune Rs until one point fits and declare the model validated.
P: 4. Idea: the stored charge Tt × IF drains through the reverse current, with the storage time from Chapter 6. ts ≈ Tt ln(1 + IF/IR) = 20 ns × ln(1 + 10/20) = 20 × 0.405 = 8.1 ns. Check: a stronger reverse current shortens it; Worked Example 23.1's 60 mA gave 3.1 ns. The usual mistake is to take Tt itself as the recovery time.
P: 5. Idea: least squares minimizes the residual over the parameters it is allowed to change, so a model with one more free parameter can always do at least as well as before, because setting the new parameter to its old value reproduces the old fit. A smaller in-range residual is therefore guaranteed, not earned. The model is better only if it predicts points the fit did not use, which is why validation data are kept separate. Section 23.4's free Rs of −5.8 kΩ shows the failure: a lower residual and a nonsensical parameter. The usual mistake is to report the in-range residual as the model's accuracy.
P: 6. Idea: evaluate the card outside its fitted range and compare. V = N VT ln(I/Is) = 1.10 × 0.026 × ln(10⁻⁶/0.47×10⁻¹²) = 0.0286 × 14.57 = 0.416 V. The device has 0.387 V, so the card is 29 mV high at 1 µA, or about three times too little current at that voltage. Do not trust it there: 1 µA is three decades below the 1 mA bottom of its fitted range. The usual mistake is to assume an exponential fitted over two decades extends to all currents.
H2: Chapter 24
P: 1. Idea: Tf is the minimum transit delay, so fT cannot exceed 1/(2π Tf). fT ≤ 1/(2π × 400×10⁻¹²) = 398 MHz. Check: the junction capacitances charged through 1/gm add delay and lower the real fT further, most at low current. The usual mistake is to take 1/Tf, forgetting the 2π.
P: 2. Idea: in saturation gm = √(2 K ID), so K = gm²/(2 ID). K = 0.1²/(2 × 0.05) = 0.1 A/V². Check: ID = (K/2) Vov² gives Vov = √(2 × 0.05/0.1) = 1.0 V, and gm = K Vov = 0.1 A/V, as stated. The usual mistake is to use gm = K Vov without first finding Vov.
P: 3. Idea: the data sheet threshold is the gate voltage at which the stated current flows, so Vto lies below it by the overdrive needed for that current. Vov = √(2 × 250×10⁻⁶/0.1) = 0.071 V, so Vto = 1.8 − 0.071 = 1.73 V. Check: (0.1/2) × 0.071² = 250 µA. The usual mistake is to set Vto equal to VGS(th).
P: 4. Idea: on the Miller plateau the gate current goes entirely into Qgd, so the transition time is charge over current. t = 30 nC/1.5 A = 20 ns. Check: a driver of 0.75 A would double it to 40 ns and double the switching loss. The usual mistake is to use Qg, which includes the charge before and after the plateau.
P: 5. Idea: add the Foster terms at t = 10 ms. Zth = 0.4 × (1 − e^(−20)) + 1.2 × (1 − e^(−1)) = 0.400 + 1.2 × 0.632 = 1.159 K/W, so ΔTj = 30 × 1.159 = 34.8 K. Check: the fast term is fully charged and the slow one 63 % charged, so Zth lies between 0.4 and 1.6 K/W. The usual mistake is to use the steady 1.6 K/W, which gives 48 K and an over-designed heat sink.
P: 6. Idea: IDSS fixes Beta through ID = Beta (VGS − Vto)². Beta = 0.010/2.5² = 1.6 mA/V². At VGS = −1 V, ID = 1.6×10⁻³ × (−1 + 2.5)² = 1.6×10⁻³ × 2.25 = 3.6 mA. Check: at VGS = 0 the formula returns 10 mA, the stated IDSS. The usual mistake is to drop the sign of Vto and compute (−1 − 2.5)².
H2: Chapter 25
P: 1. Idea: BFOM ∝ εr μ Ec³, divided by silicon's 442. AlN: 9.8 × 426 × 15.4³ = 9.8 × 426 × 3,652 = 1.52×10⁷, so 1.52×10⁷/442 = 34,500 times silicon. Check: Figure 25.1 shows about 34,500, second only to diamond. The usual mistake is to forget that Ec is cubed.
P: 2. Idea: JFOM ∝ Ec vsat. Diamond: 10 × 2.3 = 23; silicon: 0.3 × 1.0 = 0.3; ratio 77. Check: Figure 25.1 shows about 77. The usual mistake is to use the mobility, which does not appear in the Johnson figure.
P: 3. Idea: scale the critical field with the square of the gap. Ec(GaN) ≈ 0.3 × (3.4/1.12)² = 0.3 × 9.22 = 2.8 MV/cm, about 16 % below the 3.3 MV/cm used in this book. Check: the square law is a trend across materials, good to tens of percent, not a formula for any one crystal. The usual mistake is to use it in place of a measured value.
P: 4. Idea: Ron,sp = 4 BV²/(εs μ Ec³) from Section 19.1. Ron,sp = 4 × 600²/(10.4 × 8.854×10⁻¹⁴ × 1000 × (3.3×10⁶)³) = 4.35×10⁻⁵ Ω·cm² = 0.044 mΩ·cm². Check: a quarter of the 0.17 mΩ·cm² at 1200 V, as the square law requires. The usual mistake is to leave Ec in MV/cm while ε is in F/cm.
P: 5. Idea: a pn junction needs both carrier types. Ga2O3 has no usable p-type doping, so there is no pn junction, no inversion-channel MOSFET body, and no bipolar device; designers use Schottky barriers, depletion-mode and fin transistors, or junctions with other p-type materials. Even a Schottky diode is limited by heat: with 11 to 27 W/m·K, a tenth of SiC's conductivity, the junction temperature rises quickly at high current density, so the die must be thinned, cooled from the top, or bonded to a better conductor. The usual mistake is to read the Baliga figure as a forecast of real device performance.
P: 6. Idea: the off-state stack must hold the peak swing with margin, at 2.5 V per transistor. Required: 15 × 1.2 = 18 V, so 18/2.5 = 7.2, rounded up to 8 transistors. Check: 8 × 2.5 = 20 V, which covers 18 V. The usual mistake is to round 7.2 down, which leaves the stack below the required margin.
H2: Chapter 26
P: 1. Idea: λ (µm) ≈ 1.24/Eg. λ = 1.24/1.9 = 0.653 µm, red light. Check: AlGaInP red LEDs have gaps near 1.9 to 2.0 eV. The usual mistake is to use 1240 with micrometers, which gives 653 µm.
P: 2. Idea: above threshold P = η (I − Ith). I − Ith = 5 mW/0.3 W/A = 16.7 mA, so I = 12 + 16.7 = 28.7 mA. Check: an edge-emitting laser of this kind runs at tens of milliamperes, unlike the sub-milliampere threshold of a VCSEL. The usual mistake is to forget the threshold current.
P: 3. Idea: light goes out and back. t = 2d/c = 2 × 15/3×10⁸ = 100 ns. Check: 6.67 ns per meter × 15 m = 100 ns. The usual mistake is to use the one-way distance.
P: 4. Idea: McIntyre's formula. F = 0.02 × 20 + 0.98 × (2 − 1/20) = 0.40 + 0.98 × 1.95 = 2.31. Check: doubling M from 10 raised F only from 2.06 to 2.31 for silicon. The usual mistake is to forget the (1 − k) factor.
P: 5. Idea: dynamic range = full well/read noise; peak SNR = √(full well). DR = 10,000/1.5 = 6,667, 20 log10(6,667) = 76.5 dB. Peak SNR = √10,000 = 100, or 40 dB. Check: dynamic range and peak SNR are different quantities; the first depends on read noise, the second only on the full well. The usual mistake is to quote dynamic range as the image quality.
P: 6. Idea: conversion gain = q/C. 1.602×10⁻¹⁹/2×10⁻¹⁵ = 80 µV per electron. Check: half of the 160 µV/e for 1 fF. The usual mistake is to forget that a larger capacitance lowers the gain while raising the charge the node can hold.
H2: Chapter 27
P: 1. Idea: x = a/ω0². x = 9.81/(2π × 2000)² = 9.81/1.58×10⁸ = 62 nm per g. Check: a resonance 2.5 times lower than Worked Example 27.1 gives 6.25 times the motion, 6.25 × 9.94 nm = 62 nm. The usual mistake is to use f instead of ω.
P: 2. Idea: the thermomechanical noise scales as 1/√m. Four times the mass halves it: 5.1×10⁻⁴/2 = 2.55×10⁻⁴ m/s² per √Hz, about 26 µg/√Hz. Check: by direct substitution, √(4 × 1.38×10⁻²³ × 300 × 31,416/8×10⁻⁹) = 2.55×10⁻⁴. The usual mistake is to scale linearly with mass.
P: 3. Idea: Coriolis acceleration is linear in Ω. At 100 °/s, Ω = 1.745 rad/s, and a = 2 × 1.745 × 1.257 = 4.39 m/s², about 0.45 g. Check: one hundred times Worked Example 27.2's 0.044 m/s². The usual mistake is to leave Ω in degrees per second.
P: 4. Idea: VH = S × I × B. VH = 100 × 2×10⁻³ × 0.05 = 10 mV. Check: high-sensitivity plates use low-density, high-mobility InSb or GaAs. The usual mistake is to leave the field in millitesla.
P: 5. Idea: the splitting is 2γB. B = 5.6×10⁶/(2 × 28×10⁹) = 1.0×10⁻⁴ T = 100 µT. Check: half the splitting of Worked Example 27.4 gives half its 200 µT. The usual mistake is to forget the factor of 2 between the two lines.
P: 6. Idea: by Beer-Lambert, transmission at three times the concentration is the single transmission cubed. T = 0.9³ = 0.729, or 72.9 %. Check: exp(−3αcL) = (exp(−αcL))³. The usual mistake is to subtract three times 10 %, giving 70 %.
H2: Chapter 28
P: 1. Idea: break-even = ΔNRE/Δ(unit cost). (2.5 − 0.5) million/(12 − 4) = 2,000,000/8 = 250,000 units. Check: at 250,000 units both cost 14 dollars per unit including NRE. The usual mistake is to divide by the sum of the unit costs.
P: 2. Idea: NRE/N + unit cost at N = 100,000. FPGA: 2 + 25 = 27 dollars. Mature ASIC: 30 + 2 = 32 dollars. Advanced ASIC: 400 + 1.50 = 401.50 dollars. Check: 100,000 units lies below the 122,000-unit break-even, so the FPGA should still win, and it does. The usual mistake is to compare unit costs alone.
P: 3. Idea: the respin adds to the ASIC's NRE and moves the break-even up. New NRE = 4.5 million; break-even = (4.5 − 0.2) million/(25 − 2) = 187,000 units. Check: 50 % more NRE moved the break-even about 54 % higher, because the FPGA's own NRE is subtracted. The usual mistake is to forget the respin entirely when planning, which is why the From the Bench note asks for margin.
P: 4. Idea: analog and RF performance do not scale like logic. Analog precision, high-voltage devices, and low leakage are often better or cheaper in mature nodes, which offer thicker oxides and higher supply voltages; the NRE and mask cost are far lower; and the logic content of an analog chip is too small for an advanced node's density to pay. Any two of these answer the question.
P: 5. Idea: the front end of the design flow is shared. The same hardware description and simulation produce an FPGA bitstream in hours, so the design can be tested in real time, with real interfaces and real software, before anyone pays for masks. An error found on the FPGA costs a rebuild; an error found in silicon costs a respin. The usual mistake is to assume FPGA timing and power will carry over to the ASIC unchanged; they do not.
P: 6. Idea: total cost, not area, decides. The FPGA's twenty-fold area raises its unit cost, but it carries almost no NRE, no mask set, and no fab delay, and it can be changed after shipment. At low volume the NRE divided by the number of units dominates the ASIC's cost, so the FPGA is cheaper overall, as Worked Example 28.1 showed below about 122,000 units. The usual mistake is to compare silicon area or unit price alone.
H1: Appendix C. Where This Book Hands Off
P: This book runs from the crystal to the chip: carriers and junctions (Part I), the bipolar transistor (Part II), the MOSFET to the nanosheet (Part III), the amplifier built from them (Part IV), CMOS as a chip (Part V), the junction as a source and detector of light and as a high-voltage switch (Part VI), how devices are made, modeled, and extended to piezoelectric films (Part VII), data sheets read into SPICE models (Part VIII), and the materials, light and sensing devices, and chip economics beyond the silicon logic process (Part IX). Each later book of the series starts from a device this one has explained and stops explaining it.
P: Book 4 takes the transistor's small-signal model, the GaAs, GaN, and InP devices and the load line of Chapter 7, the noise of Chapter 14, the power-device limits of Chapter 19, and the acoustic filters of Chapter 21 into matching networks, power amplifiers, receivers, and synthesizers. Book 5 uses the converters and sample-and-hold circuits of Book 2 and the noise of Chapter 14 to build modems and software-defined radios. Book 6 takes the Schottky and fast-recovery diodes of Chapter 6 and the power MOSFET, superjunction, SiC, GaN, and IGBT of Chapter 19 into converters, magnetics, and the grid. Book 7 picks up the chip of Part V at the package: interposers and chiplets (Chapter 20's yield argument), high-bandwidth memory built from the DRAM cells of Chapter 16, the 875 A of a modern accelerator, MEMS on the films of Chapter 21, and the ESD and latch-up of Chapter 17 as system-level immunity.
P: Two habits travel with the reader. First, every quantity in this book was computed from a declared hand value; when a vendor model or a process design kit gives a different number, the vendor number wins for that part. Second, below about 20 nm the PDK outranks every textbook, including this one.

H1: Appendix E. Datasheet-to-SPICE Parameter Lists
P: Each list names a model parameter and the data sheet line or measurement it comes from, for the devices of Chapters 23 and 24. Fitted values are valid over the range they were fitted in; record that range with the card.
H2: Diode (SPICE level 1)
LI: Is, saturation current: from the straight part of the forward curve on a log axis, fitted together with N.
LI: N, emission coefficient: from the slope of that straight part, N = ΔV/(VT ln(I2/I1)).
LI: Rs, series resistance: from the bend of the forward curve at high current.
LI: Cjo, Vj, M: from the capacitance curve against reverse voltage; Cjo is the zero-bias value, often quoted as CT at 0 V and 1 MHz.
LI: Fc: where the forward-bias capacitance switches to a linear extrapolation; leave at 0.5.
LI: Tt, transit time: from trr in its stated test circuit, through ts ≈ Tt ln(1 + IF/IR).
LI: BV and Ibv: the breakdown voltage and the current that defines it, from the reverse curve or the Zener voltage specification.
LI: Eg and Xti: temperature scaling of Is, from forward curves at two temperatures; 1.11 eV for silicon junctions, the barrier height for Schottky diodes.
LI: Kf and Af: flicker-noise coefficient and exponent; rarely on a data sheet, zero unless measured.
H2: Bipolar transistor (Gummel-Poon)
LI: Is, Bf, Nf: the collector current and the gain ceiling, from the Gummel plot.
LI: Ise, Ne: the non-ideal base current that lowers hFE at small IC.
LI: Ikf: the high-injection knee that lowers hFE at large IC.
LI: Vaf (and Var): the Early voltage, from the slope of the output curves.
LI: Br, Isc, Nc, Ikr: reverse-mode parameters; VCE(sat) checks them.
LI: Rb, Re, Rc: ohmic resistances, from VCE(sat) and VBE(sat) at high current.
LI: Cje, Vje, Mje and Cjc, Vjc, Mjc: junction capacitances, from Cibo and Cobo against voltage.
LI: Tf, Itf, Vtf, Xtf: forward transit time and its growth at high current, from fT against IC.
LI: Tr: reverse transit time, from the storage time in the switching test.
LI: Xtb, Xti, Eg: temperature scaling of β and Is.
H2: MOSFET (level 1 and the power subcircuit)
LI: Vto: the square-law threshold; not the data sheet VGS(th), which is a gate voltage at a stated drain current.
LI: Kp with W and L: from gfs at its test current, K = gfs²/(2 ID).
LI: Lambda: from the slope of the output curves in saturation.
LI: Gamma and Phi: body effect; irrelevant for a discrete part with source tied to body.
LI: Rd and Rs: series resistances that close the gap to the measured RDS(on).
LI: Cgd, Cgs, Cds in a subcircuit: Cgd = Crss, Cgs = Ciss − Crss, Cds = Coss − Crss, each against VDS.
LI: Qgs, Qgd, Qg: checks on the capacitances, from the gate-charge curve.
LI: Body diode: a diode card fitted to the source-drain diode curve, trr, and Qrr.
LI: Thermal behavior: a Foster or Cauer network from Zth(t), and a temperature coefficient for RDS(on) from its curve against TJ.
LI: Safe operating area: not in the model; check against the data sheet curve.
H2: JFET
LI: Vto: from VGS(off), the pinch-off voltage.
LI: Beta: IDSS/Vto².
LI: Lambda: from the slope of the output curves.
LI: Cgs, Cgd, Pb: from the capacitance data.
LI: Is: gate-junction leakage, from IGSS.
H2: Zener, LED, and Schottky diodes
LI: Zener: BV and Ibv from VZ at IZT, the breakdown slope from rz, and a temperature coefficient from the Zener voltage drift.
LI: LED: Is and N from the forward curve, N often 2 or more; light output from a behavioral source proportional to current.
LI: Schottky: a large Is, N near 1.0 to 1.1, Tt near zero, and Eg set to the barrier height so that leakage rises correctly with temperature.
H2: Temperature, corners, and other devices
LI: TNOM: the temperature at which the card was fitted, 27 °C by default.
LI: Foster network: Ri and τi pairs fitted to Zth(t); not extendable with a heat sink.
LI: Cauer network: a ladder whose nodes map to physical layers; extendable with a heat sink.
LI: Corners and Monte Carlo: corner cards built from the data sheet limits, or spreads measured on a batch of parts.
LI: Op-amps and comparators: macromodels, in Book 2, Circuits, Components, and Control, Sections 15.7, 15.8, and 16.8.
