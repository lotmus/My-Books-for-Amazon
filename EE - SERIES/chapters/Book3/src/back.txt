H2: Chapter 1
P: 1. p = ni²/n = 1.0×10⁵ cm⁻³. σ = q n μn = 0.1602 (Ω·cm)⁻¹. ρ = 6.242 Ω·cm. Ten times fewer electrons, ten times the resistivity.
P: 2. A wider gap means fewer bonds broken at a given temperature, so ni is smaller. Reverse current of a junction tracks that minority population, so the leakage falls.
P: 3. σ = q ni (μn + μp) = 2.371×10⁻⁶ (Ω·cm)⁻¹. The hole term is μp/(μn+μp) = 0.32 of the total, about a third. It is not ignorable. It was ignorable in the worked example only because doping had made n huge and p tiny.
P: 4. 4.99×10²² / 10¹⁶ ≈ 5×10⁶, about five million silicon atoms per dopant atom.
P: 5. Ei − EF = 0.026 × ln(10¹⁷/10¹⁰) = 0.026 × 16.12 = 0.419 eV. EF is below midgap, toward the valence band, as it must be for p-type material.
P: 6. 45 °C is five doublings, so ni rises about 2⁵ = 32 times, and an ni² leakage about 1,000 times. The hand model gives 22 and 470; the doubling rule is a slightly generous estimate.
P: 7. Multiplying n = Nc e^(−(Ec − EF)/kT) by p = Nv e^(−(EF − Ev)/kT) adds the exponents, and EF appears once with each sign, leaving only Ec − Ev = Eg.

H1: Appendix C. Where This Book Hands Off
P: This book runs from the crystal to the chip: carriers and junctions (Part I), the bipolar transistor (Part II), the MOSFET to the nanosheet (Part III), the amplifier built from them (Part IV), CMOS as a chip (Part V), the junction as a source and detector of light and as a high-voltage switch (Part VI), and how devices are made, modeled, and extended to piezoelectric films (Part VII). Each later book of the series starts from a device this one has explained and stops explaining it.
P: Book 4 takes the transistor's small-signal model, the GaAs, GaN, and InP devices and the load line of Chapter 7, the noise of Chapter 14, the power-device limits of Chapter 19, and the acoustic filters of Chapter 21 into matching networks, power amplifiers, receivers, and synthesizers. Book 5 uses the converters and sample-and-hold circuits of Book 2 and the noise of Chapter 14 to build modems and software-defined radios. Book 6 takes the Schottky and fast-recovery diodes of Chapter 6 and the power MOSFET, superjunction, SiC, GaN, and IGBT of Chapter 19 into converters, magnetics, and the grid. Book 7 picks up the chip of Part V at the package: interposers and chiplets (Chapter 20's yield argument), high-bandwidth memory built from the DRAM cells of Chapter 16, the 875 A of a modern accelerator, MEMS on the films of Chapter 21, and the ESD and latch-up of Chapter 17 as system-level immunity.
P: Two habits travel with the reader. First, every quantity in this book was computed from a declared hand value; when a vendor model or a process design kit gives a different number, the vendor number wins for that part. Second, below about 20 nm the PDK outranks every textbook, including this one.

P: Sze and Ng, Physics of Semiconductor Devices, 3rd edition; Pierret, Semiconductor Device Fundamentals; Streetman and Banerjee, Solid State Electronic Devices, for Chapters 1 to 6. Schroder, Semiconductor Material and Device Characterization, for the four-point probe, Hall, and C-V measurements. Vendor data sheets for the 1N4148, 1N4007, BAT54, SS34, BZX84C5V1, and SMBJ5.0A.
P: Chapter 7: Cripps, RF Power Amplifiers for Wireless Communications, and Advanced Techniques in RF Power Amplifier Design; Grebennikov, RF and Microwave Transmitter Design, and Grebennikov, Sokal, and Franco, Switchmode RF and Microwave Power Amplifiers; Quay, Gallium Nitride Electronics; Mishra and Rajan, and Morkoç, on the nitride heterostructure; Vendelin, Pavio, and Rohde for GaAs-era microwave circuits; Gonzalez, Microwave Transistor Amplifiers, and Maas, Nonlinear Microwave and RF Circuits; Rodwell's InP HBT and HEMT papers; Wolfspeed and Qorvo application notes, including the CGH40010F data sheet. For millimeter-wave InP, the foundry kits from Teledyne, WIN, Northrop Grumman, and Fraunhofer IAF matter more than any textbook. For power-conversion GaN, Erickson and Maksimović, Fundamentals of Power Electronics.

H1: About the Author
P: Lothar J. Musiol is a graduate of Munich University of Applied Sciences who spent more than forty years in the semiconductor industry, first at Germany's largest company in the field, then at a string of American start-ups. He studied physics alongside all of it, seriously enough to know exactly how much he is simplifying. Dual citizenship, put to its most sensible use, lets him split his time between San Clemente, California, and Passau, Bavaria, where Austria begins at the garden fence. The household includes one married daughter, one grumpy Teacup Pomeranian named Gucci, and eleven chickens, listed in order of seniority rather than volume.
P: He writes to make complex ideas clearer and more engaging than most treatments manage, without ever pretending they are simpler than they actually are.
