H1: Chapter 4. The Junction Sits There with a Voltage Already On It
P: Push a crowded room against an empty hall and leave the door open. People spill through until a jam at the doorway, people pressed shoulder to shoulder, makes further spilling as hard as the crowding that caused it. A pn junction does that with electrons and holes. Nobody has to apply a battery. The voltage is the jam.
H2: 4.1 What crosses, and what is left behind
P: On the n-side, electrons are many and holes are few. On the p-side, the reverse. Electrons diffuse into the p-side. Holes diffuse into the n-side. Each electron that leaves the n-side leaves a positive donor ion behind. Each hole that leaves the p-side leaves a negative acceptor ion behind. Those ions cannot wander off. They are stuck in the crystal. A sheet of positive charge therefore faces a sheet of negative charge, with a region between them that has been emptied of mobile carriers. That region is the depletion layer, or space-charge region.
H2: 4.2 Built-in voltage
P: The field of those two sheets points from the positive donor ions toward the negative acceptor ions, which is from n toward p. It pushes electrons back toward the n-side and holes back toward the p-side, opposing the diffusion that created it. Equilibrium is the balance. The potential difference that belongs to that balance is the built-in voltage,
EQ: Vbi = VT ln(NA ND / ni²)
P: for an abrupt junction with both sides doped well above ni. Notice what raises Vbi: heavier doping on either side, or a smaller ni. Notice what does not appear: the applied voltage. Vbi is a property of the two dopings and the temperature. Applying a battery changes the barrier the carriers actually face, which is Vbi minus the applied forward voltage, but it does not rewrite this formula.
H2: 4.3 How wide the empty region is
P: For a one-sided abrupt junction, almost all of the depletion width sits on the lightly doped side. If that side is n-type with doping ND and the applied voltage is V (positive V means forward),
EQ: W = sqrt( 2 εs (Vbi − V) / (q ND) )
P: εs is the permittivity of silicon, 11.7 times the permittivity of free space. Forward bias shrinks W. Reverse bias grows it. The square root is why doubling the reverse voltage does not double the width.
KEY: Key idea. A pn junction in a drawer already has a built-in voltage. The battery later adds to that story. It does not start it.
H2: Worked Example 4.1
P: Both sides are doped at 1.0×10^16 cm⁻³. Find Vbi. Then treat the junction as one-sided on an n-type side of that same doping, at zero applied volts, and find W. This is a teaching idealization of a symmetric junction, used so the arithmetic stays visible.
P: NA ND / ni² = 1.0×10^32 / 1.0×10^20 = 1.0×10^12. ln(10^12) = 27.631. Vbi = (0.026)(27.631) = 0.718 V. W = 3.048e-05 cm = 0.305 μm. A few tenths of a micron of emptied crystal is enough to hold about seven-tenths of a volt.
H2: 4.4 Both sides, and the field at the middle
P: The one-sided formula of Section 4.3 is an approximation for junctions such as p+n, where one side is doped a hundred times more heavily than the other. The general abrupt junction splits the depletion layer between the two sides. Charge neutrality of the whole layer demands that the uncovered donor charge on the n side equal the uncovered acceptor charge on the p side, so the widths are inversely proportional to the dopings:
EQ: NA xp = ND xn,    W = xn + xp = sqrt( 2 εs (Vbi − V) (NA + ND) / (q NA ND) )
P: For the symmetric junction of Worked Example 4.1, NA = ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, the factor (NA + ND)/(NA ND) is 2/10¹⁶, and the exact total width is 0.431 µm, split equally, 0.215 µm on each side. The one-sided idealization gave 0.305 µm; it is the right formula for a lopsided junction and an underestimate for a symmetric one. When NA is much larger than ND, the factor reduces to 1/ND and the general formula collapses into the one-sided one.
P: The field is zero at both edges of the layer and rises linearly through each side, because the charge density is uniform within each side. Its peak sits at the metallurgical junction:
EQ: Emax = q ND xn / εs = 2 (Vbi − V) / W
P: The second form says the voltage across the layer is the area of a triangle of height Emax and base W. That peak field is what breaks a junction down. Silicon avalanches near 3×10⁵ V/cm, the critical field of Chapter 19, and every breakdown rating in this book is ultimately a statement that Emax stays below it.
H2: 4.5 The capacitance per area, and reading doping from it
P: The depletion layer is a parallel-plate capacitor whose plate spacing is W, so its capacitance per unit area is simply εs/W. Because W grows as the square root of the reverse voltage, the capacitance falls as its inverse square root, which is the formula Chapter 6 uses for Cj. The same relation can be turned around into one of the most useful measurements in semiconductor work. Squaring and inverting the one-sided result gives
EQ: 1 / C² = 2 (Vbi − V) / (q εs ND A²)
P: Plot 1/C² against the applied voltage and the points fall on a straight line. Its slope gives the doping ND of the lightly doped side, and its intercept on the voltage axis gives Vbi. If the doping is not uniform, the slope changes with voltage, and because each voltage corresponds to a particular depletion edge, the measurement maps doping against depth. This capacitance-voltage profile is how process engineers check an implant, and how varactor designers verify the hyperabrupt doping that gives their parts a wide tuning range.
H2: 4.6 Graded junctions
P: A junction made by diffusion is not abrupt. The net doping passes through zero with a slope, and near the junction it is closer to a straight line in position than to a step. For such a linearly graded junction the depletion width grows as the cube root of (Vbi − V), and the capacitance falls as the inverse cube root. Data sheets and SPICE models capture both shapes, and everything between, with one number, the grading coefficient m in Cj = Cj0 / (1 − V/Vbi)^m: m = 1/2 for abrupt, 1/3 for linearly graded, and above 1/2 for the hyperabrupt profiles of tuning varactors.
KEY: Key idea. The depletion layer splits in inverse proportion to the dopings, its field peaks at the junction, and its capacitance εs/W turns a C-V measurement into a doping profile.
H2: Worked Example 4.2
P: A p+n junction has NA = 1.0×10¹⁸ cm⁻³ and ND = 1.0×10¹⁶ cm⁻³, so Vbi = 0.838 V from the Practice of this chapter. Find W, Emax, and the capacitance of a 100 µm × 100 µm junction at zero bias and at a reverse bias of 10 V.
P: εs = 11.7 × 8.854×10⁻¹⁴ = 1.036×10⁻¹² F/cm. At zero bias W = sqrt(2 × 1.036×10⁻¹² × 0.838 / (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁶)) = 0.329 µm, Emax = 2 × 0.838 / 0.329×10⁻⁴ = 5.1×10⁴ V/cm, and C = εs A / W = 1.036×10⁻¹² × 10⁻⁴ / 0.329×10⁻⁴ = 3.15 pF. At −10 V the voltage across the layer is 10.84 V: W = 1.18 µm, Emax = 1.83×10⁵ V/cm, and C = 0.87 pF. The junction is still below the avalanche field, but not by a large factor; this doping cannot block much beyond a few tens of volts, which is why high-voltage devices use far lighter drift regions.
H2: Worked Example 4.3
P: The same 10⁻⁴ cm² junction measures 3.15 pF at 0 V and 1.19 pF at −5 V. Recover the doping from the 1/C² slope.
P: 1/C² is 1.008×10²³ F⁻² at 0 V and 7.06×10²³ F⁻² at −5 V. The slope is (7.06 − 1.01)×10²³ / 5 = 1.21×10²³ F⁻²V⁻¹. ND = 2 / (q εs A² × slope) = 2 / (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.036×10⁻¹² × 10⁻⁸ × 1.21×10²³) = 1.0×10¹⁶ cm⁻³. Extending the line to 1/C² = 0 gives V ≈ +0.83 V, the built-in voltage within rounding. Two capacitance readings told us the doping and Vbi without cutting the wafer open.
P: With its width, field, and capacitance in hand, the junction is ready for a battery. The next chapter applies one and counts the carriers that cross.
