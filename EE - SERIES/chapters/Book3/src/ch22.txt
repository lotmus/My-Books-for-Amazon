H1: Chapter 22. Compact Models and the Process Design Kit
P: This book has derived device behavior from hand equations with declared values. A designer at a foundry's 3 nm node uses none of them directly. The simulator calls a compact model, a set of equations with hundreds of fitted parameters, delivered with the process design kit, the PDK, that also contains the layout rules, the corner and statistical models, and the verified cells. Appendix C's rule that the PDK outranks every textbook below about 20 nm is a statement about these models. This chapter explains what a compact model is, how its parameters are extracted, and how corners and Monte Carlo statistics turn device variation into design margins.
H2: 22.1 What a compact model is
P: A compact model is a set of closed-form equations that give a device's terminal currents and charges as functions of its terminal voltages, smooth and continuous in every region so that the simulator's Newton iterations converge. The diode model is the simplest: the Shockley equation of Chapter 5 with a saturation current Is and an ideality factor n, plus series resistance, junction capacitance, and breakdown. The Gummel-Poon model extends the bipolar transistor of Chapter 8 with high-injection roll-off, the Early effect of Chapter 9, and charge storage. For MOSFETs the Berkeley BSIM family dominates: BSIM4 for planar devices and BSIM-CMG for FinFETs and nanosheets, with hundreds of parameters each for short-channel effects, velocity saturation, quantum confinement, and parasitic resistances.
H2: 22.2 Extracting parameters
P: Parameters are fitted to measurements of test structures across sizes, voltages, and temperatures. For the diode, two points on the exponential region determine n and Is directly: the ideality factor from the slope, n = ΔV/(VT ln(I2/I1)), and Is from either point. For a full MOSFET model, extraction proceeds in stages, long and wide devices first for the basic threshold and mobility, then short and narrow ones for the short-channel terms, then capacitance and noise, each stage holding the earlier parameters fixed. The result is accurate inside the measured range and can be badly wrong outside it, so a model's validity limits are part of the PDK.
EQ: n = ΔV / (VT ln(I2/I1)),   Is = I1 exp(−V1 / (n VT))
H2: Worked Example 22.1
P: A diode carries 1 mA at 0.65 V and 10 mA at 0.71 V at room temperature. Extract n and Is with VT = 26 mV.
P: n = 0.06/(0.026 × ln 10) = 0.06/0.0599 = 1.002, essentially ideal. Is = 10⁻³ × exp(−0.65/(1.002 × 0.026)) = 1.47 × 10⁻¹⁴ A. A third point at higher current that falls below the line would be fitted by the series resistance.
KEY: Key idea. A compact model is a smooth set of equations with fitted parameters, valid inside the range it was fitted over. Corners cover global process shifts; Monte Carlo covers local mismatch; the PDK supplies both.
H2: 22.3 Corners
P: Wafers differ from one another and from lot to lot, shifting every transistor's threshold and mobility together. The PDK captures this global variation in corner models: typical, fast, and slow for the NMOS and PMOS devices, giving the corners TT, FF, SS, FS, and SF, usually set at about three standard deviations. A design is simulated at every corner combined with the extremes of supply and temperature, typically −40 °C to 125 °C for automotive parts. A three-sigma corner on one side excludes only 0.13% of parts, so a design that works at all corners will work in nearly every manufactured chip, as far as global variation goes.
H2: 22.4 Local mismatch and Monte Carlo
P: Neighboring transistors on the same die also differ, randomly, because of the discrete dopant atoms, line-edge roughness, and the grain structure of the gate metal. Chapter 12 described this with Pelgrom's law, σ(ΔVt) = AVt/√(WL). Statistical models in the PDK represent it, and Monte Carlo simulation draws random parameter sets for every device to find the spread of a circuit's performance. For a structure repeated millions of times, like an SRAM cell, the margins must cover far more than three sigma: if a cache of 128 Mbit may contain no failing cell, the per-cell failure probability must be below 1/134,217,728 = 7.5 × 10⁻⁹, which corresponds to 5.7 sigma. Plain Monte Carlo cannot reach such tails in reasonable time, so specialized importance-sampling methods are used.
EQ: σ(ΔVt) = AVt / √(WL);   one-sided tail Q(z): Q(3) = 1.35 × 10⁻³,  Q(6) = 9.9 × 10⁻¹⁰
H2: Worked Example 22.2
P: With AVt = 3 mV·µm, find the threshold mismatch of a minimum-size pair with W = 0.1 µm and L = 0.05 µm, and of a pair with W = L = 1 µm. How many Monte Carlo runs would be needed just to observe about three failures if the failure rate is 10⁻³?
P: For the small pair, σ = 3/√0.005 = 42.4 mV, larger than many circuits' entire offset budget. For the 1 µm² pair, σ = 3 mV. Area buys matching, which is why analog input pairs are large. To see three failures at a rate of 10⁻³ requires about 3000 runs; a 10⁻⁹ rate would need billions, which is why the tails are estimated rather than sampled.
H2: 22.5 From Monte Carlo to a yield number
P: Sections 22.3 and 22.4 gave the designer two tools: corners, which shift every device of a type together, and mismatch, which varies each device independently. A real sign-off uses both. The circuit is simulated at each process corner, typical, slow, and fast for both NMOS and PMOS, across voltage and temperature, and at each corner a Monte Carlo run draws random mismatch for every transistor and reports the distribution of the specification that matters, an offset, a delay, a gain.
P: The result becomes a yield number through the Gaussian tail. If an offset has a standard deviation σ and the specification allows ±k σ, the fraction of parts that fail is 2Q(k): 0.27 % at 3σ, 63 parts per million at 4σ, 0.57 parts per million at 5σ. A circuit used once on a chip can live with 3σ; a circuit repeated a million times, an SRAM sense amplifier or a comparator in a large array, needs 5σ or 6σ so that the whole chip still works. That is why memory designers speak of high-sigma analysis and why their Monte Carlo runs use importance sampling instead of brute force: a 6σ failure would otherwise need billions of samples to observe once.
P: The PDK gives the inputs, and the designer's job is to spend area where the Pelgrom law of Section 12.2 says it buys sigma. Halving an offset's σ takes four times the gate area, so moving from 4σ to 5σ of margin against a fixed specification, a 20 % lower σ, needs about 1.56 times the area. These are the trade-offs that the silicon finally judges.
KEY: Key idea. Corners shift everything together; Monte Carlo mismatch varies each device. A specification at ±kσ fails 2Q(k) of the time, and repeated circuits need 5σ to 6σ.
H2: Worked Example 22.1
P: A comparator's offset has σ = 2.0 mV from Monte Carlo at the worst corner. The specification is ±8 mV. Find the failure rate per comparator, and the chip yield if 1,000 comparators must all pass. What σ would give 99 % chip yield?
P: ±8 mV is ±4σ, so each comparator fails with probability 2Q(4) = 6.3×10⁻⁵. All 1,000 pass with probability (1 − 6.3×10⁻⁵)^1000 ≈ e^(−0.063) = 93.9 %. For 99 % chip yield the per-comparator failure must be about 10⁻⁵, which needs k ≈ 4.4, so σ ≤ 8/4.4 = 1.8 mV, a 10 % reduction that costs about 21 % more input-pair area. A designer who signed off at ±3σ would have shipped a chip that worked only 7 % of the time, since (1 − 0.0027)^1000 ≈ 0.067.
P: This book began with a crystal that could be talked into conducting and ends with a statistical model of billions of transistors that no one will ever measure individually. Every number in between was computed from a declared value. The later books of the series take these devices as given and build radios, modems, power converters, and packages from them; when they need a device's behavior, it is this book's physics, read through the PDK.
