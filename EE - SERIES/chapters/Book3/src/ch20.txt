PART: Part VII. Making, Modeling, and New Materials
P: The earlier parts treated the device as given: a doping profile, a gate oxide, a fin, a junction. This last part asks where those come from and how designers use them. Chapter 20 follows a transistor through the fab, with the numbers that limit each step. Chapter 21 turns to a different kind of device the radio books rely on, the piezoelectric thin film and the acoustic resonator built from it, including the aluminum scandium nitride films of 2026. Chapter 22 explains the compact models and process design kits through which every device in this book reaches a circuit simulator.
H1: Chapter 20. How a Transistor Is Made
P: Every device in this book begins as a polished wafer of nearly perfect silicon, 300 mm across in a modern fab. It becomes a chip through a few hundred steps of a small number of kinds: growing or depositing films, patterning them with light, etching away what is not wanted, adding dopants, and heating to activate and move them. This chapter explains the steps whose physics sets the limits of the devices: diffusion, which blurs doping profiles with every heat cycle; ion implantation, which places dopants precisely; and lithography, whose resolution decides how small anything can be. The process engineer's details are in Plummer, Deal, and Griffin; the designer needs the numbers below.
H2: 20.1 Oxidation and films
P: Silicon's great advantage is its oxide. Heating silicon in oxygen or steam grows a layer of silicon dioxide that is an excellent insulator with a clean interface, and for every 100 nm of oxide grown, about 44 nm of silicon is consumed. Below the 45 nm node, the gate dielectric became a high-κ film, hafnium oxide, deposited atom layer by atom layer, because a silicon dioxide gate thin enough for the required capacitance leaked too much by tunneling. Other films, polysilicon, nitride, and metals, are deposited by chemical vapor deposition, atomic-layer deposition, or sputtering.
H2: 20.2 Diffusion and the thermal budget
P: Dopants move through the crystal by diffusion, the same random walk that Chapter 3 described for carriers, but millions of times slower and strongly dependent on temperature. The diffusivity follows an Arrhenius law, D = D0 exp(−Ea/kT); for boron in silicon a hand value is D0 = 1.0 cm²/s and Ea = 3.5 eV. A profile spreads by a characteristic length of about 2√(Dt). Every high-temperature step after the dopants are placed adds to that spreading, so the total of time and temperature, the thermal budget, must be kept small for shallow junctions. Modern processes use spike and flash anneals that hold the peak temperature for a second or less.
EQ: D = D0 exp(−Ea/kT),   diffusion length ≈ 2√(Dt)
H2: Worked Example 20.1
P: Find boron's diffusivity at 1000 °C and the diffusion length after 30 minutes. Compare it with a 10 s anneal at 1050 °C.
P: At 1273 K, kT = 0.1097 eV and D = 1.0 × exp(−3.5/0.1097) = 1.40 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s. After 1800 s, 2√(Dt) = 2√(2.51 × 10⁻¹¹) cm = 0.10 µm, larger than the whole junction depth of a modern transistor. At 1050 °C, D = 4.66 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s, and in 10 s the diffusion length is 2√(4.66 × 10⁻¹³) cm = 13.7 nm. The short, hot anneal activates the dopants while moving them only a few nanometers.
H2: 20.3 Ion implantation
P: Dopants are placed by ion implantation: ionized boron, phosphorus, or arsenic atoms are accelerated to kilo-electron-volt energies and shot into the wafer, where they stop at a depth set by their energy and spread around it in a roughly Gaussian profile. The dose, in atoms per square centimeter, is counted precisely by measuring the beam current, which is why implantation replaced diffusion from surface sources. The impacts damage the crystal, so an anneal must repair it and move the dopants onto lattice sites where they become active, which is the step Example 20.1 limits.
H2: Worked Example 20.2
P: A source-drain implant delivers 10¹⁵ atoms/cm² into a layer about 0.1 µm deep. Estimate the average concentration.
P: 10¹⁵ cm⁻² / 10⁻⁵ cm = 10²⁰ cm⁻³, about one dopant per 500 silicon atoms, far above the doping of Chapter 2's examples and enough to make the region nearly metallic.
KEY: Key idea. The fab places dopants by implantation and then fights to keep them in place, because every heat step spreads them by about 2√(Dt). Lithography sets how small a feature can be drawn; diffusion sets how sharp it stays.
H2: 20.4 Lithography
P: Each layer's pattern is printed by projecting light through a mask onto a photoresist. The smallest half-pitch that can be printed follows the Rayleigh criterion, R = k1λ/NA, with λ the wavelength, NA the numerical aperture of the lens, and k1 a process factor whose physical limit for a single exposure is 0.25. The depth of focus, about k2λ/NA², shrinks faster than the resolution, which is why wafers must be extremely flat and are polished between layers. Argon-fluoride immersion lithography at 193 nm reached its limit around 40 nm half-pitch, and finer features were made by multiple patterning: printing a layer in two, three, or four exposures. Extreme ultraviolet lithography at 13.5 nm, in production since 2019, and high-NA EUV with NA = 0.55 restore single exposures for the finest layers.
EQ: R = k1 λ / NA,   DOF ≈ k2 λ / NA²
H2: Worked Example 20.3
P: Find the half-pitch resolution of 193 nm immersion at NA = 1.35 and k1 = 0.28, of EUV at NA = 0.33 and k1 = 0.4, and of high-NA EUV at NA = 0.55 and the same k1. Compare the depths of focus with k2 = 0.5.
P: Immersion: 0.28 × 193/1.35 = 40 nm, DOF 0.5 × 193/1.35² = 53 nm. EUV: 0.4 × 13.5/0.33 = 16.4 nm, DOF 62 nm. High-NA EUV: 0.4 × 13.5/0.55 = 9.8 nm, DOF only 22 nm. The finest metal and gate layers of 2026 nodes need high-NA EUV or EUV with double patterning, and the shallow focus of high NA demands thinner resists and flatter wafers.
H2: 20.5 From front end to back end
P: The front end of line builds the transistors: isolation, wells, the fin or nanosheet stack of Chapter 11, the gate, and the source and drain. The back end of line builds the wiring: a dozen or more layers of copper, or of cobalt and ruthenium at the finest pitches, separated by low-κ dielectrics and connected by vias. The wiring's resistance and capacitance, not the transistor, now limit much of a chip's speed and power, and the electromigration of Chapter 17 limits its current. The finished wafer is tested, diced, and packaged, which is Book 7's subject.
H2: 20.6 Yield and the cost of a die
P: Every step of this chapter has a defect rate, and the final test of a process is how many good chips a wafer yields. A 300 mm wafer has an area of about 707 cm². Square dice of area A fit approximately
EQ: dies per wafer ≈ π (d/2)² / A − π d / √(2A)
P: where the second term removes the partial dice lost at the round edge. Random defects, particles and pattern faults, land on the wafer with an average density D0 per cm². If they are scattered independently, the fraction of dice with no defect follows the Poisson model Y = e^(−A D0). Mature processes reach D0 around 0.1 per cm² or better; a new node starts several times worse and improves over its first year or two.
P: The exponential is why large chips are expensive out of all proportion to their area. A 1 cm² die at D0 = 0.1 yields 90 %; a 6 cm² die, the size of a large accelerator, yields only 55 % at the same defect density, and it also loses more area at the wafer edge. That arithmetic drove the industry to chiplets, several smaller dice tested separately and assembled in one package, a move that Book 7 follows into packaging. Redundancy helps too: memories carry spare rows and columns that are switched in by fuses at test, so a single defect costs a spare instead of a chip.
KEY: Key idea. Good dice per wafer is gross dice times e^(−A D0). Yield falls exponentially with die area, which is why big chips cost disproportionately more and why chiplets exist.
H2: Worked Example 20.1
P: Compare a 1.0 cm² die and a 6.0 cm² die on 300 mm wafers with D0 = 0.10 cm⁻². If a processed wafer costs $17,000, find the cost per good die for each.
P: Gross dice: 1.0 cm² gives 707 − π × 30/√2 = 707 − 67 = 640; 6.0 cm² gives 117.8 − π × 30/√12 = 117.8 − 27.2 = 91. Yields: e^(−0.1) = 0.905 and e^(−0.6) = 0.549. Good dice: 579 and 50. Cost per good die: $29 and $340. The large die has six times the area but costs nearly twelve times as much. Split into six 1 cm² chiplets, the same silicon would cost about $176 before the packaging and die-to-die links that chiplets add.
P: Fabrication sets the limits; the next chapter shows how the same tools build devices that are not transistors at all.
