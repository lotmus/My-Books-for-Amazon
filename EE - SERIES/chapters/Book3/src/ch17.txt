H1: Chapter 17. What Breaks a Chip: ESD, Latch-up, and Aging
P: A chip that passes every test on the day it is made can still fail in three ways. It can be destroyed in an instant by a static discharge from a person or a machine. It can lock itself into a short circuit across its own supply through a parasitic device built into every CMOS process. Or it can wear out slowly as atoms in its wires drift and charges accumulate in its gate oxides. Each failure has a physical mechanism from Parts I to III and a design rule that follows from it.
H2: 17.1 Electrostatic discharge
P: A person walking across a carpet can charge to several kilovolts. Touching a pin, they discharge through the chip in a pulse lasting about a hundred nanoseconds. The standard human-body model (HBM) represents the person as a 100 pF capacitor discharging through 1.5 kΩ. The peak current is the test voltage divided by 1.5 kΩ, and it decays with a 150 ns time constant. The charged-device model (CDM) covers the other common case: the chip itself becomes charged during handling and discharges through one pin to a grounded tool, in a pulse lasting about a nanosecond with peak currents of several amperes.
EQ: HBM: Ipeak = VESD / 1.5 kΩ,    τ = 1.5 kΩ × 100 pF = 150 ns
H2: Worked Example 17.1
P: A pin is rated for 2 kV HBM. Find the peak current, the energy stored in the model capacitor, and the pin voltage during the pulse if the on-chip protection clamp triggers at 1.5 V and has 2 Ω of on-resistance.
P: Ipeak = 2000/1500 = 1.33 A. The stored energy is ½ × 100 pF × (2000 V)² = 0.2 mJ, nearly all dissipated in the 1.5 kΩ resistor, which is why the chip survives at all. The clamp holds the pin at about 1.5 + 1.33 × 2 = 4.2 V. A thin gate oxide connected to that pin must withstand 4.2 V for 100 ns, which sets the clamp's required size: a thinner oxide or a higher test level needs a larger, lower-resistance clamp, and that clamp's capacitance loads a high-speed pin, the central trade in I/O design.
P: The usual protection network places a diode from each pin to each supply rail and a large clamp transistor between the rails that turns on during a fast transient. A positive pulse into a pin passes through the upper diode onto the supply rail and through the rail clamp to ground; a negative pulse passes through the lower diode. The diodes are the junctions of Chapter 6 used in forward bias, where they handle amperes in a small area, and the design sets their size and the width of the metal that carries the current.
H2: 17.2 Latch-up
P: A CMOS inverter on a p-type substrate puts the PMOS in an n-type well. The PMOS source (p+), the well (n), and the substrate (p) form a parasitic PNP transistor. The well (n), the substrate (p), and the NMOS source (n+) form a parasitic NPN. The two are connected collector to base in a loop, the four-layer structure of a thyristor, which Chapter 19 treats as a deliberate device. Normally both are off. If a current injected into the substrate or well, from an overshooting I/O pin, a supply transient, or a radiation strike, develops enough voltage across the well or substrate resistance to forward-bias one emitter, that transistor turns on, its collector current turns on the other, and the loop sustains itself. A low-resistance path then connects VDD to ground until the power is removed, often after the metal has melted.
P: The loop can latch only if the product of the two parasitic current gains exceeds unity and the shunt resistances let the emitter junctions forward-bias. Design rules attack both conditions: substrate and well contacts placed frequently, so that injected current is collected before it can build a voltage, and guard rings around I/O transistors, diffusions that collect injected carriers before they reach the core. Silicon-on-insulator and the fully isolated fin and nanosheet devices reduce the risk because the parasitic loop has nowhere to form.
H2: Worked Example 17.2
P: An I/O pin can inject up to 10 mA into the substrate during an overshoot. An emitter junction of the parasitic NPN forward-biases at about 0.6 V. Find the largest substrate resistance between the injection point and the nearest substrate contact that keeps that junction off.
P: R ≤ 0.6 V/10 mA = 60 Ω. A lightly doped substrate has a resistivity of order 10 Ω·cm, so tens of micrometers of distance already exceed 60 Ω; the design rule that places a substrate contact within a short, specified distance of every NMOS, and a guard ring around every I/O device, is this inequality made into geometry. Production qualification tests inject a specified current, typically 100 mA, into every pin to confirm that it cannot latch.
KEY: Key idea. ESD is a failure of energy in nanoseconds, latch-up a failure of a parasitic thyristor, and aging a failure of slow drift. The first two are handled by protection structures and layout rules, the third by designing with margin for the end of life, not the start.
H2: 17.3 Electromigration
P: Electrons flowing through a thin copper or aluminum line transfer momentum to its atoms, and at high current density the atoms migrate downstream. Voids open where atoms leave and hillocks grow where they accumulate, until the line opens or shorts to a neighbor. The median time to failure follows Black's equation, with a current-density exponent n near 2 and an activation energy Ea of about 0.7 to 0.9 eV for copper interconnect.
EQ: MTTF = A J⁻ⁿ exp(Ea / kT)
H2: Worked Example 17.3
P: A copper line with Ea = 0.9 eV and n = 2 is rated for its life at 105 °C. Find the life factor if the line runs at 125 °C instead, and if the current density is doubled at the original temperature.
P: The temperature factor is exp((0.9/8.617 × 10⁻⁵) × (1/378.15 − 1/398.15)) = 4.0, so the line lasts one quarter as long at 125 °C. Doubling J shortens life by 2² = 4. A 20 °C rise costs as much as doubling the current. Design rules therefore set maximum current densities per wire width at a specified temperature, and power-delivery networks, which carry the largest currents, are drawn wide enough to meet them with margin.
H2: 17.4 Transistor aging
P: Transistors drift too. Under a sustained gate bias at high temperature, charges build up at the oxide interface and in the dielectric, and the threshold voltage magnitude rises. In PMOS devices under negative gate bias this is negative-bias temperature instability (NBTI); NMOS devices with high-permittivity dielectrics show the positive-bias version. The shift grows roughly as a power of time, ΔVth ∝ t^n with n near 0.2, so it is fast at first and slows, and it partly recovers when the stress is removed. Hot-carrier injection, in which electrons accelerated near the drain gain enough energy to enter the oxide, degrades devices that switch with high drain voltage. Time-dependent dielectric breakdown, the slow creation of a conducting path through the gate oxide, sets the maximum gate voltage a process allows.
H2: Worked Example 17.4
P: A PMOS device shows a 10 mV threshold shift after one year of stress, following ΔVth ∝ t^0.2. Predict the shift at ten years. If the gate delay changes by 0.12% per millivolt of threshold shift, what timing margin must the design keep for ten years?
P: ΔVth(10 years) = 10 mV × 10^0.2 = 15.8 mV. The delay increase is 15.8 × 0.12% = 1.9%. A clock path designed with zero margin at the start of life would fail timing after a few months; designers sign off with aging-aware models at the end-of-life point, and some chips monitor their own delay and raise the supply voltage slightly as they age.
H2: 17.5 Soft errors: the particle that flips a bit
P: A chip can also fail without any damage at all. An alpha particle from trace radioactive impurities in the package or solder, or a neutron produced when cosmic rays strike the atmosphere, passes through the silicon and leaves a track of electron-hole pairs, one pair for every 3.6 eV it deposits. A 5 MeV alpha particle creates about 1.4 million pairs, 0.22 pC of each sign, along a track some tens of micrometers long. If a sensitive node collects more than its critical charge, the charge that flips it, the stored bit inverts. Nothing is broken; the next write repairs it. That is a soft error.
P: The critical charge shrinks with every generation. An SRAM node of about 0.5 fF at 0.75 V holds only C V ≈ 0.4 fC, about 2,300 electrons, and a particle that deposits a small fraction of the alpha's charge near it can upset it. The rate is quoted in FIT, failures per billion device-hours. Modern SRAM sits in the range of hundreds to a few thousand FIT per megabit at sea level, rising by roughly a factor of ten at airliner altitude, where the neutron flux is higher. Low-alpha solder and mold compounds cut the alpha contribution; the neutrons cannot be shielded with anything practical, so large memories carry error-correcting codes that correct any single flipped bit in a word, and safety-critical logic adds redundancy. FinFETs help: their small collection volume makes them several times less susceptible per bit than planar devices.
KEY: Key idea. A particle strike deposits charge, and if a node collects more than its critical charge of a fraction of a femtocoulomb, a bit flips. Soft errors are managed with error-correcting codes, not prevented.
H2: Worked Example 17.1
P: A processor has 64 Mbit of SRAM cache with a soft-error rate of 1,000 FIT/Mbit. Find the expected time between upsets for one chip, and for a fleet of 100,000 servers.
P: Total rate = 64 × 1,000 = 64,000 FIT = 6.4×10⁻⁵ upsets per hour, so one chip sees an upset about every 15,600 hours, about 1.8 years. A fleet of 100,000 chips sees 6.4 upsets per hour, more than 150 a day. Without error correction, a large data center would corrupt data continuously; with single-error correction, the uncorrectable rate falls by many orders of magnitude. Reliability engineering is a statistics problem as much as a physics one.
P: ESD, latch-up, wear-out, and soft errors complete the list of ways a chip dies or misbehaves. Part VI leaves the logic chip and turns the junction toward light and power.
