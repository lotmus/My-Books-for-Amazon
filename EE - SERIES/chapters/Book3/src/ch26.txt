H1: Chapter 26. Lasers, VCSELs, APDs, SPADs, and Image Pixels
P: A phone's face unlock fires a few milliwatts of infrared from a laser smaller than a grain of sand and times the return to tens of picoseconds. The laser, the detector, and the camera beside them are all junctions from Chapter 18, each given one more trick.
P: Where you meet this: Optical links, LiDAR and time-of-flight sensing, cameras, displays, fiber receivers, and solar power.
H2: 26.1 Choosing a wavelength
P: Chapter 18's rule λ (nm) ≈ 1240/E (eV), or λ (µm) ≈ 1.24/Eg in micrometers, ties every emitter and detector to its bandgap. GaN emits in the near ultraviolet at 1.24/3.4 = 0.365 µm; InGaN, with indium narrowing the gap, gives blue and green; AlGaInP gives red; GaAs and AlGaAs give the 850 nm of short fiber links and VCSELs; InGaAsP on InP gives 1310 and 1550 nm, the fiber windows of lowest dispersion and lowest loss. A detector absorbs only photons with energy above its gap. Silicon's 1.12 eV cuts off near 1.1 µm, so silicon cameras see the 850 and 940 nm illumination of face unlock but not 1550 nm. Germanium, whose direct gap is about 0.8 eV, and InGaAs lattice-matched to InP, at 0.75 eV, cover the fiber bands, and germanium grown on silicon brings 1310 and 1550 nm detection into silicon photonics processes.
H2: Worked Example 26.1
P: Find the cutoff wavelengths of silicon, InGaAs (0.75 eV), and germanium's direct gap (0.80 eV), and the photon energies at 940 nm and 1550 nm. Which detectors see each wavelength?
P: Si: 1.24/1.12 = 1.11 µm. InGaAs: 1.24/0.75 = 1.65 µm. Ge: 1.24/0.80 = 1.55 µm. At 940 nm, E = 1.24/0.94 = 1.32 eV, above all three gaps, so all three detect it; silicon absorbs weakly so close to its indirect edge, so a silicon detector at 940 nm needs a thick absorption region. At 1550 nm, E = 0.80 eV: silicon is transparent, InGaAs detects well, and germanium sits at its edge, which is why Ge-on-Si detectors use tensile strain, which narrows the direct gap, to stretch their response past 1550 nm.
H2: 26.2 From LED to laser
P: An LED emits by spontaneous recombination, in all directions and over a band of wavelengths, with output proportional to current. Its efficiency is the product of the internal quantum efficiency, the fraction of recombinations that emit, and the extraction efficiency, the fraction of photons that escape a high-index crystal. InGaN LEDs also show droop: efficiency falls at high current density, mostly because Auger recombination, a three-carrier process, grows as the cube of the carrier density. A lamp built from many LEDs at moderate current therefore beats a few driven hard.
P: A laser adds two things to the junction: an optical cavity, mirrors that send photons back through the active region, and enough current that stimulated emission, in which a photon triggers a recombination that emits an identical photon, outruns the losses. Below the threshold current Ith the device is a dim LED. Above it, nearly every additional electron yields a coherent photon, and the output grows linearly with a slope efficiency η in watts per ampere. An edge-emitting laser has a cavity hundreds of micrometers long cleaved at the chip's edge, high power, and an elliptical beam. A vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser, VCSEL, has a cavity about a wavelength long between two Bragg mirrors grown above and below a few quantum wells. It emits from the top surface, has a threshold below a milliampere, can be tested on the wafer, and forms arrays, which is why VCSELs sit in data-center links at 850 nm and in phones' face unlock and proximity sensors.
EQ: P = η (I − Ith) for I > Ith;   ηd = η q/(hν) = η λ(µm)/1.24
H2: Worked Example 26.2
P: A VCSEL at 850 nm has Ith = 0.5 mA and a slope efficiency of 0.5 W/A (example values). Find the optical output at 6 mA, the differential quantum efficiency, and the wall-plug efficiency if the forward voltage is 2.0 V.
P: P = 0.5 W/A × (6 − 0.5) mA = 2.75 mW. Each photon carries 1.24/0.85 = 1.46 eV, so ηd = 0.5/1.46 = 0.34: 34 % of the electrons above threshold produce a photon that leaves the device. The electrical input is 2.0 V × 6 mA = 12 mW, so the wall-plug efficiency is 2.75/12 = 23 %. The rest heats a structure a few micrometers across, and because the threshold rises with temperature, a VCSEL driver controls both bias and modulation current over temperature.

H2: 26.3 Detectors with gain: PIN, APD, and SPAD
P: The PIN photodiode of Chapter 18 places an undoped intrinsic layer between p and n, so the depletion region, where absorbed photons become collected current, is wide and set by geometry rather than bias. Its gain is one: at most one electron per photon. An avalanche photodiode, APD, adds a high-field region where carriers gain enough energy to ionize the lattice, so each photoelectron becomes M electrons. The multiplication is random and therefore noisy. The excess noise factor F(M) depends on the ratio k of the hole and electron ionization rates, and a material in which only one carrier type ionizes, k near zero, is quiet. Silicon, with k near 0.02, makes the quietest APDs; InGaAs/InP APDs, with k near 0.4 to 0.5, are noisier but cover the fiber bands; germanium, with k near 0.9, is noisier still.
EQ: F(M) = k M + (1 − k)(2 − 1/M)
H2: Worked Example 26.3
P: Find F at M = 10 for silicon (k = 0.02) and for InGaAs/InP (k = 0.45). Explain why the optimum gain is higher for silicon when amplifier noise dominates without gain.
P: Si: 0.02 × 10 + 0.98 × (2 − 0.1) = 0.2 + 1.86 = 2.06. InGaAs: 0.45 × 10 + 0.55 × 1.9 = 4.5 + 1.05 = 5.55. Gain lifts the signal above the amplifier's noise floor while the multiplied shot noise grows as M² F(M). The optimum M is where the shot-noise term catches up with the amplifier term; F grows far more slowly in silicon, so silicon APDs run at gains of 50 to 100 or more and InGaAs APDs nearer 10.
P: Bias the avalanche region above its breakdown voltage and the diode has no stable gain at all: one carrier triggers a self-sustaining avalanche that a quench circuit must stop. That is the single-photon avalanche diode, SPAD, operated in Geiger mode. Each detected photon produces a large, fast pulse, after which the diode is blind for a dead time of tens of nanoseconds while it recharges. Its figures of merit are the photon detection efficiency; the dark count rate, avalanches triggered by thermal generation or tunneling, which rises steeply when hot; afterpulsing, from trapped carriers released late; and timing jitter, the spread of the delay from photon to pulse, often tens of picoseconds. SPAD arrays built in CMOS image-sensor processes are the receivers of most phone and many automotive LiDAR systems.
P: Timing is the point. Light travels about 30 cm per nanosecond, so a reflection from a target at distance d returns after 2d/c, 6.67 ns per meter of range, and 67 ps corresponds to 1 cm. A SPAD LiDAR fires many laser pulses, builds a histogram of arrival times, and takes its peak, averaging the jitter and rejecting ambient light.
H2: Worked Example 26.4
P: A SPAD time-of-flight sensor sees a return 20 ns after the laser pulse, with a timing jitter of 50 ps rms per detected photon. Find the distance, the single-photon range uncertainty, and the uncertainty after averaging 400 detected photons.
P: d = c t/2 = 3×10⁸ × 20×10⁻⁹/2 = 3.0 m. One photon: σd = c σt/2 = 3×10⁸ × 50×10⁻¹²/2 = 7.5 mm. Averaging 400 photons divides by √400 = 20, giving 0.38 mm. Systematic errors, such as the pulse shape and the drift of the circuit's own delay, usually dominate long before that and are removed by calibration.
H2: 26.4 Image pixels and event pixels
P: A CMOS image sensor is an array of photodiodes, each with a few transistors. In the standard four-transistor pixel, light is collected in a pinned photodiode, a buried p-n junction designed to deplete fully, and a transfer gate moves the whole charge to a floating diffusion, a tiny capacitance whose voltage a source follower reads. The floating diffusion is sampled right after its reset and again after the transfer, and this correlated double sampling removes the reset noise; read noise of one to two electrons is common. The conversion gain is q/C of the floating diffusion: 1 fF gives 160 µV per electron.
P: The pixel's limits are counted in electrons. The full-well capacity is the most charge the photodiode holds. Shot noise is the square root of the signal, so the best signal-to-noise ratio a pixel can reach is the square root of its full well, and its dynamic range is the full well over the read noise. A rolling shutter reads rows one after another, so fast motion skews; a global shutter stores every pixel's charge at the same instant in an in-pixel memory, at the cost of area and leakage.
H2: Worked Example 26.5
P: A pixel has a full well of 6,000 electrons, a read noise of 2 electrons, and a floating-diffusion capacitance of 1.6 fF. Find the conversion gain, the dynamic range, the peak SNR, and the SNR at 100 electrons.
P: q/C = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹/1.6×10⁻¹⁵ = 100 µV per electron. Dynamic range = 6000/2 = 3000, or 69.5 dB. Peak SNR = √6000 = 77.5, or 37.8 dB. At 100 electrons the shot noise is 10 electrons and the read noise 2, together √(100 + 4) = 10.2, so the SNR is 9.8, or 19.8 dB. In dim light the read noise hardly matters; the photon count does. A camera's low-light quality is bought with pixel area and quantum efficiency, not with a quieter amplifier alone.
P: An event pixel, used in event-based vision sensors, takes no frames at all. Each pixel converts its photocurrent logarithmically, remembers the last value it reported, and emits an event, its address, a timestamp, and a sign, whenever the logarithm changes by more than a contrast threshold set by the designer, often in the range of 10 to 30 percent. A still scene produces almost no data; a moving edge produces events with microsecond timing. The logarithm gives a high dynamic range and the asynchronous readout a very low latency, at the cost of a data stream that frame-based vision code cannot read directly; Book 8 handles that stream.
H2: 26.5 Displays and solar: microLED, OLED, and the cell
P: MicroLEDs are the LEDs of Section 26.2 shrunk to a few micrometers and placed one per subpixel. Their brightness and lifetime beat OLED's, but two problems dominate. The sidewalls of a tiny mesa are a large fraction of its volume and recombine carriers without light, so efficiency falls as the LED shrinks; and transferring millions of dice per display with near-perfect yield is a manufacturing problem of its own. OLEDs are not crystals at all but thin organic films in which electrons and holes form excitons, bound pairs that emit when they recombine. Phosphorescent emitters harvest the triplet excitons, three quarters of the total, which fluorescent emitters waste; blue, the shortest-lived color, remains the hard one.
P: Chapter 18's solar cell completes the family. The Shockley-Queisser efficiency limit for a single junction under sunlight is about 33 % near a gap of 1.34 eV; silicon, at 1.12 eV, reaches about 27 % in the best cells. Tandem cells, a perovskite or III-V top cell on silicon, pass the single-junction limit by giving each part of the spectrum its own gap.
P: Key idea. The gap picks the wavelength. A cavity and a threshold make an LED a laser; gain and Geiger-mode bias make a photodiode an APD or a SPAD; a transfer gate and a floating diffusion make it a pixel. Count electrons and photons, and the noise follows.
P: Light and its detectors are one family of sensors. Chapter 27 surveys the others.
BENCH: Treat laser and LED drive currents as thermal problems. Threshold and slope efficiency both move with temperature, so a fixed current that gives the right power on the bench gives too much or too little in a hot enclosure; use the monitor photodiode or a temperature-compensated driver, and stay inside the eye-safety class the product was certified for. For SPAD receivers, measure the dark count rate at the highest operating temperature, not at room temperature, where it can be an order of magnitude lower.
P: On the Job
P: Optoelectronic test engineers characterize lasers, LEDs, and detectors with optical power meters, optical spectrum analyzers, integrating spheres, and picosecond timing systems; Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, covers those instruments in its chapter "Optical, Thermal, and EMC Precompliance Instruments". In a LiDAR or camera company the same engineers own the eye-safety calculation and the temperature behavior of the emitter, and they are the first to see a dark count rate the data sheet did not warn about.
