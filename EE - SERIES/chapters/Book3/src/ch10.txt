PART: Part III. The MOSFET, From Square Law to Below 20 nm
H1: Chapter 10. The MOSFET From the Capacitor Up
P: One volt across a gate oxide 2 nanometers thick is a field of 5 million volts per centimeter, more than a hundred times the field at which air sparks over. A MOSFET's gate lives inside that field all day, and the field is what pulls a conducting layer up to the silicon surface.
P: Where you meet this: Load switches and motor drivers, every CMOS logic gate and analog input, and the gm/ID tables analog designers now size with.
P: A MOSFET is a capacitor with a conducting channel for one plate. Put a positive voltage on a metal gate above a thin oxide over p-type silicon, and the field first pushes holes away from the surface, then, past a threshold, pulls enough electrons to the surface to form a thin n-type layer. Connect that layer at each end to an n-type contact, a source and a drain, and the gate voltage now controls a resistor. Everything else about the device is the details of how much charge that layer holds and how fast it moves.
H2: 10.1 The gate capacitance
P: The oxide is a parallel-plate capacitor. Its capacitance per unit area is the oxide permittivity divided by its thickness:
EQ: Cox = εox / tox,    εox = 3.9 ε0 = 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm
H2: Worked Example 10.1
P: Find Cox for an oxide thickness of 2.0 nm, in F/cm² and in fF/µm².
P: Cox = 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm / 2.0×10⁻⁷ cm ≈ 1.73×10⁻⁶ F/cm². Since 1 µm² is 10⁻⁸ cm², that is 1.73×10⁻¹⁴ F per µm², or about 17.3 fF/µm². A transistor gate one micrometer square on such an oxide is a 17 fF capacitor. Modern processes replace silicon dioxide with high-permittivity hafnium-based dielectrics and quote an equivalent oxide thickness, the SiO₂ thickness that would give the same Cox.
H2: 10.2 Threshold and inversion
P: Below threshold, the gate voltage mostly widens a depletion layer of ionized acceptors under the oxide, just as reverse bias widens a junction in Chapter 4. At threshold Vth, the surface electron concentration reaches the bulk hole concentration, the surface is said to be strongly inverted, and further gate voltage goes almost entirely into adding electrons to the inversion layer. Above threshold, the channel charge per unit area is approximately Cox (VGS − Vth). The quantity VGS − Vth is the overdrive voltage, Vov. Vth itself is set by the doping under the gate, the oxide thickness, and the work-function difference between gate and silicon, and the process designer tunes all three.
H2: 10.3 The long-channel square law
P: With a small drain voltage, the channel is a uniform sheet of charge Cox Vov moving with mobility μn, and the device is a resistor whose conductance is μn Cox (W/L) Vov. As VDS rises, the channel charge near the drain falls, because the gate-to-channel voltage there is smaller. When VDS reaches Vov, the charge at the drain end goes to zero, the channel is pinched off, and the current stops increasing. Integrating along the channel gives the two regions:
EQ: Triode (VDS < Vov):  ID = μn Cox (W/L) [Vov VDS − VDS²/2]
EQ: Saturation (VDS ≥ Vov):  ID = ½ μn Cox (W/L) Vov²,    gm = 2 ID / Vov
FIG: fig10_1.png | Figure 10.1 — Long-channel NMOS output curves for μnCox(W/L) = 2 mA/V². Left of the dashed pinch-off line the device is a resistor (triode); to the right the current stays flat (saturation). Worked Example 10.2 sits at 40 µA.
P: The MOSFET's "saturation" is the bipolar transistor's forward-active region, the amplifying region. The bipolar transistor's "saturation" corresponds to the MOSFET's triode region. The two vocabularies grew up separately, and the collision is permanent.
H2: Worked Example 10.2
P: A long-channel NMOS has μn Cox = 200 µA/V² and W/L = 10. It is biased in saturation at Vov = 0.2 V. Find ID and gm. Compare gm with that of a bipolar transistor at the same current.
P: ID = ½ × 200 µA/V² × 10 × (0.2)² = 40 µA. gm = 2 × 40 µA/0.2 V = 0.40 mS. A bipolar transistor at 40 µA has gm = 40 µA/26 mV ≈ 1.54 mS, almost four times more. The comparison is clearest as transconductance efficiency, gm/ID: 2/Vov = 10 V⁻¹ for this MOSFET, 1/VT ≈ 38.5 V⁻¹ for any bipolar transistor. The MOSFET can approach the bipolar figure only by lowering Vov toward the subthreshold region of Section 10.5.
H2: 10.4 Channel-length modulation
P: Past pinch-off, the pinched region near the drain grows with VDS and the effective channel shortens, so ID rises slightly, the MOSFET's version of the Early effect. A parameter λ, roughly inversely proportional to L, models it: ID is multiplied by (1 + λ VDS), and the output resistance is ro ≈ 1/(λ ID). With λ = 0.1 V⁻¹ at 40 µA, ro = 250 kΩ. Short channels have large λ, which is one reason analog designers draw transistors longer than the minimum the process allows.
H2: 10.5 Below threshold
P: Below Vth the current does not stop. A small population of electrons still reaches the surface, they move by diffusion, and the current is exponential in VGS, like a bipolar transistor with a weaker grip:
EQ: ID ∝ e^(VGS / (n VT)),    subthreshold swing S = n VT ln(10) ≈ n × 60 mV/decade
P: The factor n, typically 1.2 to 1.5 for a planar device, reflects the fact that the gate controls the surface potential only through a capacitive divider between the oxide and the depletion layer. At 300 K no MOSFET of this kind can switch faster than 60 mV per decade of current, because the thermal spread of carrier energies sets that floor. This has a hard consequence for digital design: lowering Vth by 60 to 90 mV to gain speed multiplies the off-state leakage of every transistor on the chip by ten.
H2: 10.6 Velocity saturation
P: The square law assumes carrier velocity is proportional to the lateral field. In silicon, electron velocity saturates near vsat ≈ 1×10⁷ cm/s once the field reaches a few volts per micrometer. In a channel 100 nm long with a few tenths of a volt across it, the field is already there. In the fully velocity-saturated limit, the channel charge moves at vsat regardless of length, and the current becomes linear in overdrive:
EQ: ID ≈ W Cox vsat Vov,    gm ≈ W Cox vsat
H2: Worked Example 10.3
P: A short-channel NMOS has the Cox of Worked Example 10.1 and a width of 1.0 µm, and is fully velocity-saturated. Find ID at Vov = 0.3 V, using vsat = 1.0×10⁷ cm/s.
P: ID ≈ (1.0×10⁻⁴ cm)(1.73×10⁻⁶ F/cm²)(1.0×10⁷ cm/s)(0.3 V) ≈ 5.2×10⁻⁴ A, about 0.52 mA per micrometer of width. Real short-channel devices deliver somewhat less than this limit at the same overdrive, because the carriers are not velocity-saturated over the whole channel. Drive current per micrometer of width, not μn Cox W/L, is how short-channel processes are compared.
P: Key idea. A MOSFET is a field-controlled sheet of charge. Its long-channel current is quadratic in overdrive, its short-channel current approaches linear, and below threshold it is exponential with a swing no better than 60 mV per decade at 300 K.
H2: 10.7 The body effect and the MOS capacitor's three regions
P: Section 10.2 defined the threshold with the source tied to the body. In a real circuit the source of a stacked transistor often sits above the substrate, and a reverse bias VSB between source and body widens the depletion layer under the gate. More depletion charge must be balanced before inversion begins, so the threshold rises:
EQ: Vth = Vth0 + γ ( √(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF) )
P: Here 2φF is twice the Fermi-level offset of Section 1.5, about 0.7 V for a typical well doping, and γ, the body-effect coefficient, is set by the doping and the oxide capacitance, typically 0.3 to 0.5 V^½ for a planar device. The effect matters in every cascode, every source follower, and every NAND gate stack, where the upper transistor has its source raised. It is largely absent from FinFETs and nanosheets, whose thin bodies are fully depleted and weakly coupled to the substrate, one more reason those devices are easier to model.
P: The gate-body structure is a capacitor whose behavior changes with gate voltage in three distinct regions. Drive an n-channel device's gate negative and holes accumulate at the surface: the capacitance is the full oxide capacitance Cox. Raise the gate toward threshold and the holes are pushed away, a depletion layer forms, and its capacitance in series with Cox lowers the total. Above threshold the inversion layer of electrons forms and, at low frequency, the capacitance returns to Cox. This capacitance-voltage curve is the standard test of a gate stack: its flat regions give the oxide thickness, its transition gives the doping and the threshold, and its distortions reveal trapped charge, the same charge that drifts during the aging of Chapter 17.
P: Key idea. A raised source raises the threshold through the body effect, roughly γ times the change in √(2φF + VSB). The gate stack's C-V curve, with its accumulation, depletion, and inversion regions, is how the oxide and threshold are checked.
H2: Worked Example 10.4
P: An NMOS transistor has Vth0 = 0.45 V, γ = 0.40 V^½, and 2φF = 0.70 V. It is the upper device in a cascode with its source at 1.0 V above the body. Find its threshold.
P: Vth = 0.45 + 0.40 × (√1.70 − √0.70) = 0.45 + 0.40 × (1.304 − 0.837) = 0.45 + 0.187 = 0.637 V. The raised source cost almost 0.19 V of threshold, which comes straight out of the voltage headroom of a low-supply analog circuit; designers recover it by placing the transistor in its own well tied to its source, where the process allows.
P: Measure it yourself. The threshold voltage a data sheet quotes is a current criterion, the gate voltage at a stated drain current with the drain tied to the gate, and RDS(on) is a resistance at a stated gate voltage and drain current. Both, and the output curves that give λ, can be measured on a curve tracer; Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, does it in "The Curve Tracer at Work: Diodes, Bipolar Transistors, and MOSFETs". Chapter 24 fits the level 1 model of this chapter to a 2N7000 data sheet and shows where it stops fitting.
P: The planar MOSFET is now fully described: square law, short-channel limits, and body effect. The next chapter shows why the industry abandoned the planar gate below about 20 nm.
BENCH: In a discrete MOSFET circuit, read the threshold voltage as a range, and read the on-resistance at the gate voltage you actually have. A part specified at 10 V of gate drive may barely conduct at 3.3 V; logic-level parts quote RDS(on) at 4.5 V or 2.5 V for that reason. Remember too that the gate is a capacitor: a microcontroller pin driving a large MOSFET with no gate resistor can ring, and one with too large a resistor switches slowly and runs hot.
