H1: Chapter 19. Breakdown and the Power Devices
P: Double the voltage an ideal silicon power switch must block and the on-resistance of its drift region goes up four times. From 600 V to 1200 V, a drift region of 51 mΩ·cm² becomes one of about 206. That square law is why high-voltage switching moved to silicon carbide and gallium nitride.
P: Where you meet this: Choosing MOSFETs, IGBTs, SiC, and GaN switches for chargers, inverters, motor drives, and power supplies.
P: Chapter 6 introduced breakdown as the knee where a reverse-biased diode suddenly conducts. For a power device, that knee is the specification everything else is traded against. A switch that must block 600 V needs a thick, lightly doped region to hold the field, and that same region is the resistance the current must cross when the switch is on. This chapter derives the trade, shows why it favors silicon carbide and gallium nitride at high voltage, and introduces the two silicon devices that work around it by flooding the drift region with carriers: the thyristor and the IGBT.
H2: 19.1 Avalanche and the critical field
P: In a reverse-biased junction, carriers crossing the depletion region are accelerated by the field. Above a certain field, a carrier gains enough energy between collisions to knock an electron out of a bond, creating a new pair, and each of those can do the same. The multiplication runs away at a critical field Ec, about 3 × 10⁵ V/cm in silicon at the doping levels of power devices (it rises slowly with doping). The wide-gap materials need far more energy per ionization and have critical fields about ten times higher: roughly 2.5 × 10⁶ V/cm for 4H-SiC and 3.3 × 10⁶ V/cm for GaN. Avalanche is not destructive in itself, and rated "avalanche-rugged" devices survive it for a specified energy, but it ends the device's ability to block. Narrow, heavily doped junctions break down at a few volts by a different mechanism, Zener tunneling, which is why reference diodes below about 5 V behave differently from those above.
H2: 19.2 Designing a drift region
P: Consider a one-sided junction: a heavily doped p+ region against a lightly doped n-type drift region of doping ND. The field is largest at the junction and falls linearly through the depletion region, with a slope set by the doping. Breakdown occurs when the peak field reaches Ec. Integrating the field over the depletion width gives the breakdown voltage, and solving for the doping and width that just support a required voltage gives the design of the drift region.
EQ: ND = εs Ec² / (2 q BV),    W = 2 BV / Ec,    Ron,sp = W / (q μn ND) = 4 BV² / (εs μn Ec³)
FIG: fig19_1.png | Figure 19.1 — The ideal drift-region limit Ron,sp = 4 BV²/(εs μn Ec³) for silicon and 4H-SiC, with the numbers of Worked Example 19.1. Silicon needs 51 mΩ·cm² at 600 V; SiC needs 0.48 mΩ·cm² at 1200 V.
P: The last expression is the essential result. The specific on-resistance, the resistance of one square centimeter of drift region, rises as the square of the blocking voltage and falls as the cube of the critical field. The product εs μn Ec³ is Baliga's figure of merit, and it is the reason a material with a tenfold higher critical field can have a drift region hundreds of times less resistive for the same voltage.
H2: Worked Example 19.1
P: Design the drift region of an ideal silicon device that blocks 600 V, using εs = 11.7 ε0, Ec = 3 × 10⁵ V/cm, and μn = 1000 cm²/V·s. Find ND, W, and Ron,sp, and the chip area needed for 100 mΩ.
P: ND = (1.036 × 10⁻¹² × 9 × 10¹⁰)/(2 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 600) = 4.8 × 10¹⁴ cm⁻³. W = 2 × 600/3 × 10⁵ = 4.0 × 10⁻³ cm = 40 µm. Ron,sp = 4.0 × 10⁻³/(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1000 × 4.8 × 10¹⁴) = 51 mΩ·cm². For 100 mΩ the area is 0.051/0.1 = 0.51 cm², a die more than 7 mm on a side, for the drift region alone. At 1200 V the square law gives four times the resistance, 206 mΩ·cm². The same calculation for 4H-SiC (εs = 9.7 ε0, Ec = 2.5 × 10⁶ V/cm, μn ≈ 900) at 1200 V gives about 0.48 mΩ·cm², over four hundred times lower; real SiC MOSFETs reach a few mΩ·cm² because their channel and substrate add resistance, which still makes them the standard high-voltage switch.
P: Key idea. A drift region's on-resistance rises as the square of the voltage it blocks and falls as the cube of the material's critical field. High-voltage switching therefore belongs either to wide-gap materials or to bipolar devices that flood the drift region with carriers.
H2: 19.3 The power MOSFET and the superjunction
P: A vertical power MOSFET places the channel at the top surface and the drain at the bottom of the die, with the drift region between them, and parallels hundreds of thousands of cells. Below about 200 V, the channel resistance is a large part of the total, and trench gates that pack the cells densely win. Above it, the drift region dominates. The superjunction MOSFET breaks the square law by building the drift region from alternating, charge-balanced columns of n- and p-type silicon: at high voltage the columns deplete each other sideways, so the field is flat and the n-columns can be doped much more heavily than a plain drift region for the same blocking voltage. Superjunction devices brought 600 V silicon MOSFETs well below the limit of Worked Example 19.1, and they remain the cost-effective choice at 600–900 V where SiC is not required.
H2: 19.4 The thyristor
P: A thyristor is a four-layer pnpn device, the same structure Chapter 17 found as the parasitic latch-up path, now made on purpose. It can be modeled as a PNP and an NPN transistor connected so that each one's collector drives the other's base. A gate current into the NPN base starts the loop. Once the sum of the two transistors' common-base gains reaches one, α₁ + α₂ = 1, the loop sustains itself and the gate loses control: the device stays on until the current falls below a holding current, normally at the next zero crossing of an AC supply. Because both carrier types flood the lightly doped regions, the on-state voltage is only one to two volts even in devices that block several kilovolts, and thyristors still switch the largest currents in power engineering, from light dimmers (as a triac, two thyristors in antiparallel) to high-voltage DC converter stations.
H2: 19.5 The IGBT
P: The insulated-gate bipolar transistor combines a MOSFET's gate with a bipolar output. Structurally, it is a vertical power MOSFET with an extra p+ layer at the bottom. When the MOSFET channel turns on, its electron current flows into the drift region and forward-biases the bottom junction, which injects holes. The drift region fills with both carriers far above its doping, the conductivity modulation that also gives the thyristor its low drop, so its resistance falls. The on-state looks like a diode voltage in series with a small resistance, and the current does not stop instantly at turn-off: the stored holes must recombine, producing a tail current that limits IGBTs to switching frequencies of tens of kilohertz. The structure also contains a parasitic thyristor, which modern designs suppress so thoroughly that IGBT latch-up is now rare.
EQ: MOSFET: Pcond = I² Ron,    IGBT: Pcond ≈ VCE0 I + rCE I²
H2: Worked Example 19.2
P: A 650 V MOSFET has Ron = 50 mΩ at operating temperature. A 650 V IGBT of similar die cost has VCE0 = 1.0 V and rCE = 20 mΩ. Compare their conduction losses at 20 A and 50 A, and find the current at which they are equal.
P: At 20 A: MOSFET 20² × 0.05 = 20 W; IGBT 1.0 × 20 + 0.02 × 400 = 28 W. At 50 A: MOSFET 125 W; IGBT 50 + 50 = 100 W. They are equal when 0.05 I² = 1.0 I + 0.02 I², which gives I = 1.0/0.03 = 33 A. Below that current the MOSFET wins and also switches faster; above it the IGBT's fixed junction drop is cheaper than the MOSFET's rising I²R. That is the usual division: MOSFETs, and increasingly SiC MOSFETs, for high frequency and moderate current; IGBTs for high current at low switching frequency, such as motor drives and traction inverters.
H2: 19.6 Choosing a 650 V switch in 2026
P: The physics of Sections 19.1 to 19.5 reaches the designer as a comparison of three device families at the most contested rating, 650 V, used in power-factor correction stages, server supplies, solar inverters, and on-board chargers. Representative parts make the comparison concrete: a silicon superjunction MOSFET such as one from Infineon's CoolMOS family at about 70 mΩ, a silicon-carbide MOSFET such as Wolfspeed's C3M0060065D at 60 mΩ, and a GaN HEMT such as the GS66508T at 50 mΩ and 30 A.
P: On-resistance at room temperature is only the opening bid. Silicon's resistance roughly doubles between 25 °C and 125 °C, because mobility falls as Section 3.4 described; silicon carbide rises far less, often only 20 to 40 %, because its channel resistance, which falls with temperature, offsets its drift resistance; GaN rises by about 1.5 to 2 times. The switching story is reversed in order. The superjunction's body diode stores charge and recovers slowly, which rules it out of hard-switched bridges where a diode is forced off while conducting. SiC's body diode recovers fast, and GaN has no body diode at all; it conducts in reverse through its channel with no stored charge. GaN's gate charge, about 6 nC for the GS66508T, is roughly a tenth of a comparable silicon part's, so its switching losses are small enough to run at several hundred kilohertz to megahertz, shrinking magnetics.
P: The practical result in 2026: superjunction silicon where it is soft-switched and cost dominates; SiC where voltage, temperature, and ruggedness matter, especially at 1200 V and in automotive traction; GaN where switching frequency and power density matter, in chargers, server supplies, and increasingly in solar microinverters. Book 6 designs the converters in which these choices are made.
P: Key idea. Compare power switches hot, not at 25 °C, and compare their switching charge as well as their resistance. Silicon doubles its resistance by 125 °C; SiC barely moves; GaN switches with almost no stored charge.
H2: Worked Example 19.3
P: Three 650 V switches each carry 10 A rms at a junction temperature of 100 °C. Their 25 °C resistances and approximate hot multipliers are: Si superjunction 70 mΩ × 1.8, SiC 60 mΩ × 1.25, GaN 50 mΩ × 1.6. Find each conduction loss.
P: Si: 10² × 0.126 = 12.6 W. SiC: 10² × 0.075 = 7.5 W. GaN: 10² × 0.080 = 8.0 W. The part with the best room-temperature figure did not win the conduction contest; SiC's flat temperature curve did. At 100 kHz hard switching, however, the GaN part's switching loss would be several watts lower than either competitor, and in a converter run at 500 kHz it would win overall. The choice depends on the converter, not the data-sheet headline.
P: Measure it yourself. RDS(on) against gate voltage and temperature, the body diode's forward drop, and the leakage near breakdown are curve-tracer measurements, made with short pulses so that the die does not heat during the sweep. Gate charge, switching, recovery, and thermal impedance need the setups of Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, in its chapter "Measuring the Datasheet: Capacitance, Gate Charge, Switching, Recovery, Thermal Impedance, and SPICE Models"; the curve-tracer walk-through itself is "The Curve Tracer at Work: Diodes, Bipolar Transistors, and MOSFETs". BENCH: Compare power switches at the temperature they will run at, not at 25 °C. On-resistance rises substantially when hot, by different factors for silicon, SiC, and GaN, so the cheapest part at room temperature may lose at 100 °C. Then add switching loss, which depends on the output charge and on the reverse recovery of the body diode, and check the avalanche and short-circuit ratings against the worst fault your circuit can produce.
