H1: Chapter 7. GaAs, GaN, and InP Are Not One Transistor
P: A handset, a radar brick, and a 94 GHz receiver can all be described as "a microwave transistor on a chip." The crystals are not interchangeable. Gallium arsenide is the mature microwave compound semiconductor. Gallium nitride is the wide-bandgap material for watts, and also, on a silicon wafer, for hundreds of volts. Indium phosphide is the III-V you pick when the gate is tens of nanometers wide and the noise figure is the specification. They meet in millimeter-wave power amplifiers. Almost nowhere else.
P: Where you meet this: Handset power amplifiers, radar and base-station transmitters, satellite low-noise front ends, GaN chargers, and millimeter-wave links.
H2: 7.1 Two figures of merit, not one winner
P: Johnson's figure of merit rewards breakdown voltage times frequency. Transit time and mobility reward a fast, light electron. GaN wins the first contest. InP wins the second. That is the whole split, and it is why a chapter that ranks them on a single "which is more advanced" scale is already wrong.

GaN bandgap about 3.4 eV, breakdown field about 3.3 MV/cm, two-dimensional-electron mobility about 1500 to 2000 cm²/V·s. InP itself is about 1.34 eV. The channel in an InP HEMT is usually InGaAs, about 0.5 to 0.75 eV depending on its indium content, with a very high mobility and the highest saturation velocity of the usual RF III-Vs. RF supply voltage is about 3 to 12 V for GaAs, about 1 to 3 V for an InP HBT and about 1 to 5 V for an InP HEMT, and about 20 to 50 V for GaN. Power density is about 0.5 to 2 W per millimeter of gate for GaAs, about 0.3 to 1.5 W/mm for InP, and about 5 to 12 W/mm for GaN. Production GaN reaches roughly 80 to 200 GHz ft. The best published InP HEMTs are past 600 GHz ft, with fmax in the terahertz class.
H2: 7.2 Say HEMT or HBT before you say the crystal
P: GaAs comes as a MESFET, which is old, a pHEMT, and an HBT. The pHEMT is the low-noise amplifier, the switch, and some power amplifiers. The HBT is the handset power amplifier and the gain block. GaN, in any part you can buy for RF or for power conversion, is a HEMT. There is no serious GaN HBT in production. InP comes as a HEMT, or a metamorphic HEMT, with an InGaAs channel, and as an HBT. The HEMT is the low-noise amplifier, the mixer, the switch, and the low-power millimeter-wave amplifier. The HBT is high-speed analog, data converters, and optical modulator drivers, plus some power amplifiers.
P: Most RF HEMTs are depletion-mode. The gate wants a negative supply, and the drain must not come up before that gate is in a safe place. That is a sequencer, not the enhancement-mode MOSFET of a later chapter. Enhancement-mode GaN shows up in power conversion. Do not import that bias story into a radar PA without reading the data sheet.
P: Key idea. Name the device. "InP versus GaN" is not a comparison until you have said HEMT or HBT.
H2: 7.3 Why voltage buys watts
P: For a sine-wave load line, the optimum resistance is the voltage swing divided by the current swing, and the RF power is half the product of those peaks. A higher legal voltage, at the same current, is both more power and an easier match.
H2: Worked Example 7.1
P: A cell is allowed 0.20 A peak. Compare a 5 V peak swing with a 28 V peak swing. This is a GaAs-like rail against a GaN-like rail, not a promise about a foundry.
P: At 5 V, Ropt = 5 / 0.20 = 25 Ω, and P = (5)(0.20)/2 = 0.50 W. At 28 V, Ropt = 28 / 0.20 = 140 Ω, and P = (28)(0.20)/2 = 2.8 W. Same current, 5.6 times the voltage, 5.6 times the power, and the load the matcher has to present moved from 25 Ω toward 140 Ω, closer to a 50 Ω system and kinder to a wideband network. GaN's extra power density, several watts per millimeter rather than about one, is a second gift on top of this arithmetic. Heat still collects the bill. SiC is the reason a GaN PA can pay it. InP's substrate cannot.
H2: 7.4 Where each one is the right part
P: Use a GaAs pHEMT when the noise figure of a microwave receiver is the spec. Use a GaAs HBT for a phone or WLAN power amplifier at 3 to 5 V. Use GaAs when you want a dense MMIC whose passives sit on semi-insulating GaAs, and for many sockets above about 40 GHz where that foundry still has more gain and a better noise figure than production GaN.
P: Use GaN when the radio needs watts: radar, electronic warfare, a satcom uplink, a macro base station, anything past about 5 to 10 W. The rail is 28 to 50 V. The device is rugged into a bad mismatch when it is GaN on SiC. Use GaN-on-silicon for a 100 to 900 V switch that replaces a silicon MOSFET. That market contains no GaAs and no InP.
P: Use an InP HEMT for the lowest noise figure from about 30 GHz through W-band and into G-band, and whenever ft in the hundreds of gigahertz is the reason the gate exists. Use an InP HBT for an optical driver and for mixed-signal above about 100 Gbaud. InP is also a photonics crystal. GaN is not.
P: The only fair fight is millimeter-wave power. A 28 to 40 GHz base-station PA is GaN. From about 77 GHz up, at milliwatts to a few watts, InP often still has more gain and needs less combining. GaN shows up where watts and bandwidth are both required. A module may use an InP low-noise amplifier in front of a GaN power amplifier. Neither crystal is the phone PA. Below about 3 GHz at hundreds of watts, look at LDMOS or GaN, not InP. Cheap WLAN is SiGe, CMOS, or GaAs.
P: Key idea. A 94 GHz low-noise amplifier starts with InP. A 20 W X-band brick starts with GaN. A 650 V buck starts with GaN-on-silicon and never with InP.
H2: 7.5 What the circuit books still are
P: Cripps's load line still draws both the GaAs-era example and the GaN PA. GaN adds trapping, knee walkout, and memory, so the linearity fix is backoff or digital predistortion, not a polynomial copied from a handset HBT. InP circuit design looks like high-speed analog plus a foundry HBT or HEMT kit. A GaN PA looks like that load line, a thermal path through SiC, and a 50 V supply whose hot loop was already a subject in the regulator discussion of a later book. There is no popular textbook chapter that replaces the foundry model handbook.
H2: 7.6 The two-dimensional electron gas in numbers
P: The HEMT's advantage can be put in a single multiplication. At an AlGaN/GaN interface, the difference in spontaneous and piezoelectric polarization between the two crystals pins a sheet of electrons in a quantum well a few nanometers thick, with no dopant atoms in it to scatter them. Its sheet density is about ns = 1×10¹³ electrons per cm² without any intentional doping, and its mobility is around 1500 to 2000 cm²/V·s, high for so dense a sheet. If every electron in that sheet moved at a saturated velocity near 1.5×10⁷ cm/s, the current per unit gate width would be q ns vsat = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹³ × 1.5×10⁷ = 24 A/cm, or 2.4 A per millimeter of gate.
P: Real devices deliver about 1 A/mm, because the electrons are not saturated over the whole channel and the gate cannot open the full sheet without leakage. Combined with a breakdown field ten times silicon's, which lets the drain swing to 100 V or more, that current density produces the power densities that make GaN the default for base stations and radar. A Class A load line, the subject of Book 4, delivers (Vmax − Vknee) × Imax / 8. With a 28 V supply, so Vmax ≈ 56 V, a knee of 3 V, and Imax = 1 A/mm, that is 53 × 1/8 = 6.6 W per millimeter of gate width. GaAs pHEMTs, limited to roughly 10 to 15 V of swing, deliver around 1 W/mm.
P: Gate periphery is therefore the designer's unit of power. A packaged 10 W GaN transistor such as Wolfspeed's CGH40010F, a 28 V part usable to about 6 GHz, contains a die with a gate periphery of a couple of millimeters, laid out as many parallel fingers; a 100 W part is ten times wider. Periphery also sets capacitance and therefore the input and output impedances that Book 4 must match, which is why higher power always means lower impedance and harder matching.
P: Key idea. A GaN HEMT carries about 1 A per millimeter of gate and swings tens of volts, so its power density is several watts per millimeter. Power is bought with gate periphery.
H2: Worked Example 7.2
P: Estimate the gate periphery needed for a 40 W GaN amplifier at 48 V supply, with a 4 V knee, assuming 1 A/mm and the Class A load-line estimate. Then compare with a GaAs pHEMT at 1 W/mm.
P: At 48 V, Vmax ≈ 96 V, and the power density is (96 − 4) × 1/8 = 11.5 W/mm. Forty watts needs 40/11.5 = 3.5 mm of gate. A GaAs pHEMT at about 1 W/mm would need 40 mm, ten times the width, ten times the capacitance, and an output impedance so low that matching it to 50 Ω over any useful bandwidth would be nearly impossible. Real GaN designs at 48 V run at lower current density to manage heat, and the channel temperature, not the load line, usually sets the final periphery.
H2: 7.7 Heterojunctions, the HBT, and trapped charge in GaN
P: A heterojunction joins two crystals with different bandgaps whose lattices match closely enough to grow one on the other: AlGaAs on GaAs, InGaP on GaAs, InAlAs and InGaAs on InP, AlGaN on GaN, and SiGe on silicon. The gap difference splits into a step in the conduction band, ΔEc, and a step in the valence band, ΔEv. Chapter 1's bands now have corners, and devices are built by placing those corners. In a HEMT the conduction-band step holds electrons in the narrow-gap channel next to the interface, away from the dopants or polarization charge that supply them: the two-dimensional electron gas of Section 7.6.
P: In a heterojunction bipolar transistor the emitter is the wide-gap side. Electrons flowing from emitter into base see the same barrier as in a silicon transistor, but holes flowing back from base into emitter see an extra step ΔEv, so the back-injected hole current, the base current of Chapter 8, falls by about exp(ΔEv/kT). The designer spends that factor less on β itself than on a base doped far more heavily than a homojunction would allow, which lowers the base resistance and permits a thin base with a short transit time. That trade is why the GaAs HBT is the handset power amplifier and why the InP HBT reaches hundreds of gigahertz. The SiGe HBT does the same inside a silicon process: germanium narrows the gap of the base, and grading the germanium across the base adds a built-in field that speeds electrons through it. Chapter 25 places SiGe among the other RF technologies.
EQ: hole back-injection reduced by ≈ exp(ΔEv / kT)
H2: Worked Example 7.3
P: A wide-gap emitter adds a valence-band step of 0.20 eV (an example value) against hole back-injection. By what factor does the hole current fall at room temperature? If the designer keeps β at 100, roughly how much more heavily may the base be doped than in a homojunction transistor?
P: With kT = 26 meV the factor is exp(0.20/0.026) = exp(7.69) = 2,190. In a homojunction transistor β scales with the ratio of emitter to base doping; with the hole current suppressed 2,190 times, the base doping can rise by roughly that factor for the same β. Real HBTs dope the base one to two orders of magnitude above the emitter and still reach β near 100, with the rest of the margin spent on recombination and on grading the junction. The base resistance falls in proportion to the doping, which raises fmax and lowers noise.
P: GaN has a problem of its own: trapped charge. Surface states between gate and drain, and deep levels in the buffer layer under the channel, capture electrons when the drain swings to high voltage and release them slowly, over microseconds to seconds. While they are full they deplete part of the two-dimensional gas. The result is current collapse, also called dynamic on-resistance: right after a high-voltage off state the device conducts less than its DC curves promise, and the knee of the RF load line walks out. Silicon nitride passivation, field plates that spread the gate-drain field, and carefully chosen carbon or iron doping in the buffer reduce the effect. Pulsed I-V measurements, which apply the drain voltage briefly from a stated quiescent point, expose it; a DC curve tracer does not.
H2: Worked Example 7.4
P: A GaN HEMT on a 28 V supply has a 3 V knee and 1 A/mm in DC measurement. After a high-voltage pulse, trapped charge removes part of the sheet charge in the access region; assume this raises the knee to 6 V and lowers the maximum current to 0.8 A/mm. Compare the Class A power density before and after, using the estimate of Section 7.6.
P: Before: (56 − 3) × 1/8 = 6.6 W/mm. After: (56 − 6) × 0.8/8 = 5.0 W/mm, 24 % less. Dynamic on-resistance in a power converter is the same mechanism read at a different terminal: if 20 % of the access-region charge is trapped, the access resistance rises by about 1/0.8 = 1.25, so a switch specified at 50 mΩ conducts like 62 mΩ for a while after each turn-off. Data sheets for GaN power transistors now state dynamic RDS(on), and RF foundries publish pulsed curves; use those, not the DC curves, in both designs.
P: Key idea. A heterojunction is a designed band step. In a HEMT it confines the channel; in an HBT it blocks back-injection so that the base can be doped heavily. GaN's traps make its behavior depend on its recent history, which only pulsed measurements reveal.
P: That is the compound-semiconductor landscape: name the device, then let the material's field, velocity, and heat flow choose the application. Part II returns to silicon and builds the transistor that started it all.
BENCH: Treat the bias sequence of a depletion-mode GaAs or GaN HEMT as part of the circuit. The gate must be negative before the drain comes up and must stay there until the drain is down again; a sequencer, or at least a carefully planned power-up order, is what keeps an expensive transistor alive. Thermal design comes next. The power density that makes GaN attractive also concentrates the heat, so check the channel temperature, not the case temperature, against the data sheet.
