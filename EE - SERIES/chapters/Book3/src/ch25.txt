PART: Part IX. Materials, Light, Sensing, and the Chip
H1: Chapter 25. Materials for Speed, Power, and Heat
P: Why does a 5G base station use gallium nitride, a phone's antenna switch silicon on insulator, a satellite receiver indium phosphide, while laboratories chase gallium oxide and diamond? Each crystal wins a different contest, and a handful of material constants say which.
P: Where you meet this: Choosing an RF or power technology, reading foundry offerings, evaluating wide-bandgap power devices, and judging research news about ultrawide-bandgap materials.
H2: 25.1 The constants that decide
P: Five material properties carry most of the decision. The bandgap Eg sets the intrinsic carrier density and so the highest operating temperature, and through the critical field it sets the voltage a given thickness can block. The critical field Ec, which rises roughly as the square of the gap, sets breakdown. The electron mobility μ sets on-resistance and low-field speed, and the saturation velocity vsat sets how fast carriers cross a short channel at high field. The thermal conductivity k decides whether the heat that a small, powerful device makes can leave it.
P: Representative room-temperature values follow, rounded, from Sze and Ng for the classic semiconductors and from the reviews of Tsao and colleagues (2018) and Woo and colleagues (2024) for the wide-gap and ultrawide-gap ones. These are orientation values, not design inputs; use the current datasheet or foundry process design kit for a specific device. Each line gives the gap, critical field, electron mobility, relative permittivity, saturation velocity, and thermal conductivity:
LI: Silicon: 1.12 eV; 0.3 MV/cm; 1,400 cm²/V·s; 11.7; 1.0×10⁷ cm/s; about 150 W/m·K.
LI: Gallium arsenide: 1.42 eV; 0.4 MV/cm; 8,500 cm²/V·s; 12.9; about 1×10⁷ cm/s; about 55 W/m·K.
LI: Indium phosphide: 1.34 eV; 0.5 MV/cm; 5,400 cm²/V·s; 12.5; about 1×10⁷ cm/s; about 68 W/m·K.
LI: 4H silicon carbide: 3.26 eV; 2.5 MV/cm; 900 cm²/V·s; 9.7; 2×10⁷ cm/s; about 370 W/m·K.
LI: Gallium nitride: 3.4 eV; 3.3 MV/cm; about 1,000 cm²/V·s in bulk and 1,500 to 2,000 in the two-dimensional gas; 10.4; 1.5 to 2×10⁷ cm/s; about 253 W/m·K.
LI: Beta gallium oxide: 4.8 to 4.9 eV; about 8 MV/cm; about 180 cm²/V·s; about 10; about 1.1×10⁷ cm/s; only 11 to 27 W/m·K, depending on direction.
LI: Diamond: 5.5 eV; about 10 MV/cm; up to about 4,500 cm²/V·s in the purest crystals; 5.7; about 2.3×10⁷ cm/s; 2,290 to 3,450 W/m·K, the highest of any material.
LI: Aluminum nitride: about 6.0 eV; about 15 MV/cm; about 430 cm²/V·s; about 9.8; about 1.3×10⁷ cm/s; about 285 to 319 W/m·K.
P: The critical field depends on doping and on how breakdown is defined; mobility depends on doping, temperature, and crystal quality. The ultrawide-gap values, in particular, come from crystals and devices still under development. Use these values consistently in this book's comparisons, and replace them with the vendor's values for any real part. The silicon mobility here is the undoped value; the problems in Parts I to VII use the declared 1000 cm^2/V/s.
H2: 25.2 Two figures of merit
P: Section 19.1 derived the ideal unipolar drift region and its specific on-resistance, Ron,sp = 4 BV²/(εs μn Ec³). Baliga's figure of merit is the denominator, εs μ Ec³: at a given blocking voltage, a material with a higher BFOM has a less resistive drift region. Johnson's figure of merit for RF power transistors, met in Section 7.1, is Ec vsat/2π: the highest frequency at which a transistor can still swing a given voltage, or equally, the voltage it can swing at a given frequency. Because Ec grows roughly as Eg², the Baliga figure grows roughly as the sixth power of the gap.
EQ: BFOM = εs μ Ec³,   JFOM = Ec vsat / 2π
H2: Worked Example 25.1
P: Compute the BFOM and JFOM of 4H-SiC, GaN, and β-Ga2O3 relative to silicon with the values of Section 25.1, and the ideal drift-region Ron,sp of each at 1200 V.
P: With Ec in MV/cm, silicon's εr μ Ec³ is 11.7 × 1400 × 0.3³ = 442. SiC: 9.7 × 900 × 2.5³ = 136,400, 308 times silicon. GaN: 10.4 × 1000 × 3.3³ = 373,700, 845 times. Ga2O3: 10 × 180 × 8³ = 921,600, about 2,080 times. The JFOM ratio is the ratio of Ec vsat: SiC 2.5 × 2/0.3 = 16.7, GaN 3.3 × 2/0.3 = 22, and Ga2O3 8 × 1.1/0.3 = 29 (Figure 25.1). The ideal drift region at 1200 V comes to 0.48 mΩ·cm² for SiC, as in Worked Example 19.1, 0.17 for GaN, and 0.071 for Ga2O3, against about 150 for silicon at this section's mobility (Figure 25.2). Ga2O3 wins both ratings on paper; its thermal conductivity, less than a tenth of SiC's, is why it is not yet in your power supply.

P: Key idea. Baliga's figure εμEc³ ranks materials for high-voltage switches, Johnson's Ec vsat for RF power. Both rise steeply with the gap; neither says anything about heat, cost, or whether the crystal can be doped and grown.
H2: 25.3 Silicon variants: SOI and SiGe
P: Two variants of silicon deserve their own place. Silicon on insulator, SOI, puts the active layer on a buried oxide. For RF switches the point is the substrate: a high-resistivity handle wafer with a trap-rich layer under the oxide keeps the substrate from becoming a lossy, nonlinear conductor, and stacking transistors in series lets a switch built from 2.5 V devices hold tens of volts of RF. Nearly every phone antenna switch, and many of its low-noise amplifiers, are RF SOI. Fully depleted SOI, FD-SOI, uses a silicon film so thin that the channel has no neutral body, which removes the floating-body effects and most junction capacitance, and it adds a back gate through the thin buried oxide that shifts the threshold by tens of millivolts per volt, a knob that low-power and millimeter-wave designs use. SOI's price is heat: the buried oxide conducts heat about a hundred times worse than silicon, so self-heating is larger.
P: Silicon-germanium puts the heterojunction of Section 7.7 into the base of a bipolar transistor inside a CMOS-compatible process. Production SiGe BiCMOS offers transistors with fT and fmax in the hundreds of gigahertz next to CMOS logic, and research devices have passed 500 GHz fmax, which is why SiGe carries automotive radar at 77 GHz, optical-link drivers, and many millimeter-wave front ends.
H2: Worked Example 25.2
P: An RF SOI antenna switch must hold a 20 V peak RF swing in its off state. Each transistor in the stack withstands 2.5 V. How many must be stacked for a 25 % margin, and what happens to the on-resistance?
P: The stack must hold 20 × 1.25 = 25 V, so it needs 25/2.5 = 10 transistors in series. In the on state their resistances add, so each must be ten times wider than a single device would need for the same total on-resistance, and the off-state capacitance, ten small capacitors in series, falls accordingly. The product of on-resistance and off-capacitance, the switch figure of merit, is a property of the process, which is why RF SOI foundries compete on it.
H2: 25.4 InP, GaAs, and GaN as materials
P: Chapter 7 named the devices; here are the material reasons. GaAs and InP have light electrons with several times silicon's mobility and a high peak velocity, so their HEMTs and HBTs are fast at low voltage; their thermal conductivities, about a third and a half of silicon's, limit their power density. InP lattice-matches the InGaAs and InAlAs layers that give the highest-mobility channels and the narrow-gap photodetectors of Chapter 26, which is why one crystal serves both the 100 GHz amplifier and the 1550 nm receiver. GaN's polarization gives the two-dimensional gas without doping, its critical field gives the voltage, and its usual RF substrate, SiC at about 370 W/m·K, removes the heat. GaN on silicon trades some of that heat path and crystal quality for large, inexpensive wafers, which suits power conversion; GaN on SiC remains the choice for RF power.
H2: 25.5 The ultrawide-gap crystals: Ga2O3, diamond, and AlN
P: Gallium oxide has the attraction of melt growth: large single-crystal wafers can be pulled from a melt, as for silicon and sapphire, which SiC and GaN cannot do. Its n-type doping is controllable, and Schottky diodes and transistors blocking more than a kilovolt have been reported. It has two deep weaknesses. No usable p-type doping exists, so there is no pn junction, no conventional MOSFET body, and no bipolar device. And its thermal conductivity is the lowest in the list, so heat must leave through the top surface, through thinned wafers, or through a bonded layer of a better conductor.
P: Diamond has the opposite profile: the best thermal conductivity of any material, a high critical field, and high mobilities for both carriers, but its dopants sit deep in the gap, boron about 0.37 eV above the valence band and phosphorus about 0.6 eV below the conduction band, so only a small fraction of them is ionized at room temperature. Devices use hydrogen-terminated surfaces, which form a hole channel without dopants, or very thin, heavily doped layers. Wafers are small and expensive. Diamond's most practical electronic role today is as a heat spreader under GaN, and its most exciting one is the quantum sensor of Chapter 27.
P: Aluminum nitride, with a gap near 6 eV, has the highest critical field of the three and good thermal conductivity, and it is already a production material in another role, the piezoelectric film of Chapter 21. As a transistor material it appears mostly in AlGaN alloys and as a barrier or buffer layer in GaN devices, because making pure AlN conductive is hard. Ultraviolet LEDs for disinfection near 265 nm, a photon energy of 1240/265 = 4.7 eV, use AlGaN with a high aluminum content.
P: Beyond these crystals, the research frontier includes two-dimensional semiconductors such as molybdenum disulfide, studied as channels for transistors beyond the nanosheet, and carbon nanotubes and graphene, whose remarkable mobilities have not yet become a manufacturable logic switch; graphene has no bandgap at all. Ferroelectric hafnium oxide, a relative of the high-κ gate dielectric of Chapter 20, has been demonstrated in embedded memories. Whether any of these reaches volume production is a question this book leaves open; the constants of Section 25.1 are the tool for judging the claims.
H2: 25.6 A map of RF and power technologies
P: Together, the materials divide the frequency-and-power plane into rough regions with wide overlaps. CMOS and FD-SOI integrate whole radios up to millimeter waves at milliwatts to about a watt. RF SOI supplies the switches and many low-noise amplifiers of every phone front end. SiGe BiCMOS covers radar and links from about 10 GHz to beyond 100 GHz at milliwatts to hundreds of milliwatts. GaAs HBTs and pHEMTs serve handset and Wi-Fi power amplifiers near a watt and low-noise functions to about 100 GHz. InP HEMTs and HBTs give the lowest noise and the highest speed, above about 100 GHz and in optical links. LDMOS covers base stations and industrial heating below about 4 GHz at tens to hundreds of watts, and GaN on SiC covers watts to kilowatts from about 1 to 40 GHz in radar, base stations, and satellites. For power conversion, Chapter 19 placed silicon MOSFETs and IGBTs, SiC up to 3.3 kV and beyond, and GaN on silicon from 100 to 900 V. Book 4 designs the circuits that live in each region.
P: The materials decide what is possible. Chapter 26 shows what some of them do with light.
BENCH: When a vendor's slide compares materials, check which figure of merit it uses and at what temperature. A bar chart of Baliga's figure says nothing about heat removal, switching loss, gate reliability, or the price of a wafer, and a device that wins on paper at 25 °C can lose at 150 °C. Ask for the measured device, at your temperature, in your circuit.
P: On the Job
P: Compound-semiconductor fabs and RF foundries employ device engineers who sit between materials and circuits. They grow and qualify epitaxial layers, tune the HEMT or HBT structure, run the reliability stresses of Chapter 17 on GaN and GaAs parts, and support their customers' PA and LNA designers with models and application notes. Much of the day is this chapter's kind of reasoning, a material constant turned into a device limit, together with the pulsed I-V and load-pull measurements that show whether a device meets it.
