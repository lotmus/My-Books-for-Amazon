H1: Chapter 6. The Diode You Can Buy
P: The part in the drawer has a glass or plastic body, two leads, a junction area someone chose, and a data sheet with a curve that only roughly matches Chapter 5. The law is still in there. Three extras decide whether a circuit works: the junction behaves as a capacitor, a large reverse voltage eventually breaks the crystal down, and a forward current does not stop the instant you reverse the voltage.
H2: 6.1 Junction capacitance
P: The depletion layer is an insulator between two conducting regions, which is a capacitor. Its value falls as reverse bias widens the layer. For an abrupt junction a serviceable model is
EQ: Cj = Cj0 / sqrt(1 − V/Vbi)
P: with V negative in reverse, and Cj0 the zero-bias value set by area and doping. Varactor diodes are ordinary junctions used on purpose as voltage-controlled capacitors. Rectifier diodes carry the same capacitance whether you asked for it or not. At radio frequency that capacitance is part of the matching network. At audio it is often ignorable. The frequency, not the part's name, decides.
H2: 6.2 Breakdown
P: Push reverse bias high enough and the field in the depletion layer rips bonds apart or gives carriers enough energy between collisions to create more carriers. Current then rises steeply at nearly constant voltage. A Zener diode is a junction designed so that this knee sits at a useful, specified voltage, and so that the part can survive the current you intend to run through that knee. A signal diode's breakdown voltage is a limit, not a feature. Exceed it without a current limit and the junction overheats.
P: Two microscopic stories share that knee. Zener tunneling dominates at low breakdown voltages. Avalanche, the carrier-multiplication story, dominates at higher ones. Data sheets still say "Zener" for both. The circuit fact is the steep reverse knee. The name on the bag is older than the distinction.
H2: 6.3 Reverse recovery
P: Forward current stores minority charge on both sides of the junction. When the circuit reverses the voltage, that stored charge has to come back out, or recombine, before the diode can block. For a interval on the order of the storage delay the diode still conducts backward. Fast-recovery and Schottky diodes exist because that interval is expensive in a switching supply. A Schottky diode is a metal-semiconductor junction. It has a lower forward drop and almost no stored minority charge, and it generally leaks more and breaks down sooner than a pn diode of similar size. Choose it when the switching edge is the problem. Choose a pn diode when the reverse voltage and the leakage are the problem.
KEY: Key idea. The exponential law is the junction. Capacitance, breakdown, and stored charge are why the data sheet is thicker than the equation.
H2: Worked Example 6.1
P: An abrupt-junction diode has Cj0 = 10 pF and the Vbi of Worked Example 4.1, 0.718 V. Find Cj at a reverse bias of 5 V.
P: V/Vbi = −5 / 0.718 = -6.960. 1 − V/Vbi = 7.960. The square root is 2.821. Cj = 10 pF / 2.821 = 3.54 pF. Five volts of reverse bias cut the capacitance to about a third. A tuner that expects a wide capacitance range is using this same square root, over whatever bias its varactor is allowed.
H2: 6.4 The Schottky junction in numbers
P: Section 6.3 described the Schottky diode in words. Its physics is simpler than the pn junction's. Where a metal meets lightly doped n-type silicon, electrons face a barrier of height φB, set by the metal and the interface, typically 0.5 to 0.8 eV. Forward current is electrons from the semiconductor flowing over that barrier into the metal, a majority-carrier current with no minority injection to speak of. Its saturation current follows from thermionic emission:
EQ: JS = A* T² e^(−φB / VT),    A* ≈ 112 A/(cm²·K²) for n-type silicon
P: The exponential in φB/VT replaces the ni² of the pn junction, and the difference is dramatic. For φB = 0.7 eV at 300 K, JS = 112 × 9×10⁴ × e^(−27.1) = 1.75×10⁻⁵ A/cm². A 10⁻⁴ cm² Schottky junction then has IS = 1.75 nA, about three million times the 0.57 fA of the same-area pn diode of Worked Example 5.2, and carries 1 mA at 0.34 V instead of 0.73 V. The price is the same factor in reverse leakage, which also rises steeply with temperature, and a lower practical breakdown voltage, because the lightly doped layer that blocks voltage adds series resistance quickly.
P: The same physics explains the ohmic contact. Every device needs metal connections that do not behave as diodes. The trick is to dope the silicon under the contact above about 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. The depletion layer under the metal then becomes only a few nanometers thick, electrons tunnel straight through the barrier instead of climbing it, and the contact behaves as a small resistor in both directions. The n+ and p+ regions under every contact in Chapter 20's process, topped with a metal silicide, exist for this reason.
H2: 6.5 Reading four data sheets
P: The parameters of this chapter are the columns of a diode data sheet. Four widely used parts show the trade space. The 1N4148 small-signal diode blocks 100 V, carries up to a few hundred milliamps, specifies at most 1.0 V at 10 mA, a reverse recovery time of at most 4 ns, and at most 4 pF of capacitance at zero bias: a small junction built for speed. The 1N4007 rectifier blocks 1000 V and carries 1 A with at most 1.1 V forward at 1 A; it has a large junction, a lightly doped thick base for voltage, and a recovery time that its data sheet does not even advertise, because it was designed for 50 and 60 Hz. The BAT54 small Schottky blocks only 30 V, but drops at most 0.32 V at 1 mA. The SS34 power Schottky blocks 40 V and carries 3 A at a maximum of about 0.5 V.
P: Two columns are easy to overlook. The first is the test condition: a forward voltage means nothing without its current and temperature, and a recovery time means nothing without the forward and reverse currents used to measure it. The second is the thermal resistance from junction to ambient or to the lead. A diode dissipating 0.5 W with 100 K/W to ambient runs 50 K above it, and from Section 5.5 its forward voltage is already 90 mV lower and its leakage hundreds of times higher than the room-temperature figures on page one.
H2: 6.6 Zener references and transient suppressors
P: A Zener used as a reference is specified at a test current, because its voltage still moves with current through its dynamic resistance rz. The BZX84C5V1 is a 5.1 V part with a tolerance of about ±5 % (4.8 to 5.4 V) at 5 mA and a dynamic resistance of at most 60 Ω there. Its temperature coefficient is close to zero: below about 5 V the tunneling mechanism of Section 6.2 gives a negative coefficient, above about 6 V avalanche gives a positive one, and parts near 5 to 6 V sit at the crossover. For precision, a bandgap reference from Book 2 beats any Zener, but a Zener remains the cheapest way to clamp a rail.
P: A transient-voltage suppressor is a Zener built for energy instead of precision, with a large junction and a package designed to absorb a short pulse. The SMBJ5.0A, a common 5 V-rail protector, stands off 5.0 V with microamp leakage, begins to break down at 6.4 V minimum, and clamps at 9.2 V while conducting 65.2 A during a standard 10/1000 µs surge, a peak power of 600 W. The ratio of the clamp-voltage rise to the current, (9.2 − 6.4)/65.2 = 43 mΩ, is its effective dynamic resistance, and it is why a TVS protects a 5 V circuit only if that circuit survives 9 V for a millisecond.
KEY: Key idea. A Schottky trades a low forward drop and no stored charge for leakage and limited voltage. A data sheet's numbers come with test conditions and a thermal resistance, and they must be read together.
H2: Worked Example 6.2
P: A 12 V to 5 V buck converter delivers 3 A at 500 kHz. Its freewheeling diode conducts during the off-time, a fraction 1 − 5/12 = 0.583 of each period. Compare the diode losses of an SS34 Schottky (0.5 V at 3 A, negligible recovery) with a fast-recovery pn diode (1.0 V at 3 A, recovered charge Qrr = 50 nC).
P: Schottky conduction loss: 0.5 × 3 × 0.583 = 0.88 W. The pn diode: 1.0 × 3 × 0.583 = 1.75 W of conduction loss, plus a recovery loss of roughly Qrr × Vin × f = 50×10⁻⁹ × 12 × 500×10³ = 0.30 W that lands mostly in the switching transistor. The Schottky saves more than a watt, about 7 % of the 15 W output. At 3 A and 12 V the Schottky's higher leakage is a few milliamps at most, harmless. This is why low-voltage converters use Schottkys, and why the highest-efficiency ones replace the diode with a MOSFET, the synchronous rectifier of Book 6.
H2: Worked Example 6.3
P: A BZX84C5V1 is fed from 12 V through a resistor to run at its 5 mA test current. Find the resistor, and how much the output moves when the load draws 2 mA away from the Zener.
P: R = (12 − 5.1)/5×10⁻³ = 1.38 kΩ; the nearest standard value is 1.3 kΩ or 1.5 kΩ. With 2 mA drawn by the load the Zener current falls to about 3 mA, and the voltage falls by roughly rz × 2 mA = 60 × 0.002 = 0.12 V at worst. A 2 % shift from a small load change is why a Zener alone is a clamp, not a precision reference.
P: Part I has now followed the junction from the crystal to the parts drawer. The last chapter of the part looks beyond silicon, at the compound semiconductors that own radio frequencies.
