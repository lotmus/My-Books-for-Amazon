PART: Part VIII. From Datasheet to Model
H1: Chapter 23. A Model Is a Fit: Reading a Diode Datasheet into SPICE
P: A simulation can agree with a measured curve to a millivolt and still be wrong by a hundred millivolts a few decades away. Nothing in the simulator warns you. The model was fitted where the data were, and it says nothing reliable anywhere else.
P: Where you meet this: Board-level SPICE simulation, vendor model libraries, characterization labs, and every design review that asks whether a simulated margin is real.
P: Chapter 22 described the compact models a foundry ships in its PDK. A board designer rarely has a PDK. The diode on the schematic is a 1N4148 or an SS34, and its model is a single line of text, a .model card with a dozen parameters, written by its vendor, copied from a library, or fitted by an engineer to the curves of the data sheet. This chapter reads that line, fits one, and then uses a single diode to make the most important point about every model in this book.
H2: 23.1 A model is a fit, not the device
P: The statistician George Box wrote in 1976 that "all models are wrong," and in a 1979 paper he gave a section the heading that is now quoted everywhere: "All models are wrong but some are useful." A SPICE model is useful in exactly that sense. Its equations are the physics of Chapters 4 to 6, simplified until they can be evaluated millions of times per simulation. Its parameters are numbers chosen so that those simplified equations reproduce a set of measurements over a stated range of current, voltage, and temperature. Inside that range a good model is excellent. Outside it, the same equations keep producing smooth, confident curves that nobody has checked.
P: Three consequences follow, and this part of the book returns to them every time. First, a model has a validity range, and an honest model file states it; when it does not, find out which data sheet curves the vendor fitted. Second, fitted parameters are not physical constants. The Is in a diode card is whatever saturation current makes the curve fit, together with the N and the Rs found at the same time; a different range of data gives a different trio, and none of them is the junction's true saturation current. Third, more parameters do not make a model truer. A model with enough free parameters fits any set of points, noise included, and then extrapolates worse. That is overfitting, and it is as real in device modeling as in statistics.
P: Models form a hierarchy, and each step up buys range with parameters. The SPICE level 1 diode and the level 1 MOSFET of Chapter 10 have a handful of parameters and capture the main exponential or square law. The Gummel-Poon bipolar model adds high injection and base-width modulation; VBIC and HICUM extend it with the self-heating, substrate, and quasi-saturation effects of RF and SiGe transistors. BSIM, the industry-standard MOSFET model of Chapter 22, has hundreds of parameters because a nanometer transistor needs them. At the other end, a behavioral model describes only what a circuit needs, a limit, a gain, a delay, and makes no claim about the physics at all. The right model is the simplest one that is valid over the range the circuit will use.
P: Key idea. A model is a fit: physics equations with parameters chosen to match measurements over a range. Inside that range it can be excellent; outside it, it is a guess that looks exactly like a prediction.
H2: 23.2 The diode card, parameter by parameter
P: SPICE's level 1 diode is Chapter 5's ideal law with three corrections. The current through the junction is Is(exp(VD/(N VT)) − 1), where VD is the internal junction voltage and the emission coefficient N, between about 1 and 2, absorbs recombination and high injection into one number. A series resistance Rs separates VD from the terminal voltage. A charge model adds the depletion capacitance of Chapter 4 and the stored charge of Chapter 6. Temperature enters through the bandgap parameter Eg and the exponent Xti, and breakdown is a separate exponential near −BV.
EQ: V = N VT ln(I/Is + 1) + I Rs;   Cj = Cjo / (1 − VD/Vj)^M;   Qstored = Tt × I
P: The data sheet supplies the data for each group, if you know where to look. The forward curve, VF against IF on a log axis, gives Is and N from its straight part and Rs from the bend at high current. The capacitance curve against reverse voltage gives Cjo, Vj, and M; Fc, usually left at 0.5, only sets where the simulator switches to a linear extrapolation in forward bias. The reverse-recovery time trr, measured in a stated test circuit, gives Tt. The reverse curve near breakdown gives BV and Ibv, and the forward curve at two temperatures gives Eg and Xti. Kf and Af set flicker noise and are almost never on a data sheet; leave them at zero unless you measure them. Appendix E lists every parameter of this chapter and the next with the data sheet line it comes from.
H2: Worked Example 23.1
P: A widely distributed library model of the 1N4148 reads Is = 2.52 nA, N = 1.752, Rs = 0.568 Ω, Cjo = 4 pF, M = 0.4, and Tt = 20 ns. The onsemi data sheet promises VF ≤ 1.0 V at 10 mA, and for the closely related 1N4448, 0.62 to 0.72 V at 5 mA and ≤ 1.0 V at 100 mA. Check the model at those three currents with VT = 26 mV. Then check Tt against the data sheet's trr ≤ 4 ns, measured from 10 mA forward into a 6 V, 100 Ω reverse circuit.
P: N VT = 1.752 × 0.026 = 45.55 mV. At 5 mA: 45.55 mV × ln(5×10⁻³/2.52×10⁻⁹) = 45.55 × 14.50 = 0.661 V, plus 2.8 mV across Rs, 0.663 V, inside the 1N4448 window. At 10 mA: 45.55 × 15.19 + 5.7 mV = 0.698 V, well below the 1.0 V limit. At 100 mA: 45.55 × 17.50 + 56.8 mV = 0.854 V. ngspice, which uses VT = 25.85 mV at its default 27 °C, gives 0.660, 0.694, and 0.850 V (Figure 23.1). For recovery, the reverse current is about 6 V/100 Ω = 60 mA, and the storage time of Chapter 6 is about Tt ln(1 + IF/IR) = 20 ns × ln(1 + 10/60) = 3.1 ns, inside the 4 ns limit; a Tt of 20 ns only looks inconsistent with trr ≤ 4 ns. The card describes a typical part comfortably inside every limit, which is what a library model should do. It does not describe the worst-case part that the limits protect, and a design that relies on the 1.0 V limit must be checked at that limit, not with the typical card.

H2: 23.3 Fitting a diode from measured points
P: When no model exists, or the one that exists does not match your parts, fit your own. The cleanest data come from a pulsed forward sweep on a source-measure unit, a few points per decade from nanoamperes to the rated current, taken fast enough that the junction does not heat. Three points already give the level 1 forward parameters: two in the exponential region for N and Is, as in Worked Example 22.1, and one at high current for Rs.
H2: Worked Example 23.2
P: A bench measurement of one 1N4148-class diode (values stated for this example) gives 0.560 V at 0.1 mA, 0.640 V at 1 mA, and 0.830 V at 50 mA. Fit N, Is, and Rs, then predict the voltage at 10 mA.
P: From the two low points, N = 0.080/(0.026 × ln 10) = 0.080/0.0599 = 1.336, and Is = 10⁻⁴ × exp(−0.560/(1.336 × 0.026)) = 10⁻⁴ × exp(−16.12) = 1.00×10⁻¹¹ A. At 50 mA the ideal law gives N VT ln(I/Is) = 0.03474 × ln(5.0×10⁹) = 0.03474 × 22.33 = 0.776 V; the measured 0.830 V leaves 0.054 V across Rs, so Rs = 1.08 Ω. The card reproduces all three points exactly because three parameters were fitted to three points. That is arithmetic, not evidence. The evidence comes from a point the fit did not use. At 10 mA the card predicts 0.03474 × ln(10⁻²/1.00×10⁻¹¹) + 0.0108 = 0.720 + 0.011 = 0.731 V. If the bench says 0.73 V, the card has earned some trust between 0.1 and 50 mA. It has earned none at 1 µA.
P: Three points show the mechanics. In practice a least-squares fit uses every measured point, weights the decades evenly, and reports the residual, the model minus the measurement, at each point. A short script does it in a few lines; Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, writes such scripts in its chapter "Python on the Bench", and Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, sets up the measurement in "Measuring the Datasheet: Capacitance, Gate Charge, Switching, Recovery, Thermal Impedance, and SPICE Models".
H2: 23.4 One diode, two good fits
P: Here is an experiment everyone who uses SPICE models should run once. Take one diode and measure it from 1 nA to 100 mA. Fit a level 1 model to the low-current decades only, and a second level 1 model to the high-current decades only. For teaching, the "measured" device here is generated from a fuller model with the physics of Chapter 5: an ideal diffusion current with N = 1 and Is = 50 fA, a recombination current with N = 2 and 0.5 nA, high injection setting in near 20 mA, and 0.5 Ω of series resistance. Figure 23.2 labels these data as teaching data; a real diode behaves the same way.
P: Fit A uses 10 nA to 1 µA and gives N = 1.93, Is = 0.42 nA, and Rs = 0, since series resistance is invisible at those currents. Fit B uses 1 mA to 100 mA and gives N = 1.10, Is = 0.47 pA, and Rs = 0.86 Ω. Both are good fits: each stays within about 2.5 mV of the device over its own range. They share not one parameter, and both fail outside their ranges. Fit A predicts 0.85 V at 10 mA, where the device has 0.69 V, an error of 164 mV. Fit B predicts a voltage 76 mV too high at 100 nA, which as a current error at a fixed voltage is a factor of about 14. Neither fit found the true Is or N of either mechanism, and fit B's Rs is about 70 % above the true 0.5 Ω because it also absorbs the high-injection bend.
P: A third fit shows overfitting. Let the fitter adjust Rs over the low range as well, and it returns a series resistance of about −5.8 kΩ: a negative resistance with no physical meaning, chosen because a slightly smaller residual is all the arithmetic asks for. The residual inside the range fell a little, and the model got much worse everywhere else.

H2: Worked Example 23.3
P: Fit B of Section 23.4 (N = 1.10, Is = 0.47 pA) is used to estimate the leakage of a sample-and-hold whose diode sees 0.30 V of forward bias. What current does the model predict, and what does the device of Figure 23.2 conduct?
P: Model: 0.47×10⁻¹² × exp(0.30/(1.10 × 0.026)) = 0.47×10⁻¹² × exp(10.5) = 17 nA; Rs is negligible at this current. Device: the diffusion term is 50×10⁻¹⁵ × exp(0.30/0.026) = 5.1 nA and the recombination term 0.5×10⁻⁹ × exp(0.30/0.052) = 160 nA, about 165 nA in all, ten times the model's value. The card fitted at power-supply currents underestimates the hold-capacitor leakage tenfold, and nothing in the simulation output says so. The fix is a model fitted, or at least checked, at the currents the circuit uses.
H2: 23.5 The extraction workflow
P: Device modeling follows four steps whatever the device. Measure: sweeps that cover the currents, voltages, temperatures, and frequencies the circuits will use, pulsed where self-heating would bend the curves, with the fixture's resistance and capacitance removed. Fit: parameter groups extracted in an order that keeps them independent, DC before capacitance, low current before high, room temperature before hot. Validate: compare the model with measurements the fit never used, including a few at the edges of the range and one circuit, such as a ring oscillator or a rectifier, that exercises the device the way a design will. Document: the parameter values, the range over which they were fitted, the residuals, the temperature, the number of samples, and the date. A model without that last step can be trusted by nobody but its author, and after a year not even by its author.
P: Corners and statistics follow the logic of Chapter 22. A single fitted card describes one sample, or the median of several. A designer who needs worst case builds corner cards from the data sheet limits, for example an Is and Rs that reproduce the maximum VF, or runs Monte Carlo over parameter spreads measured on a batch of parts. Both are still fits, now to a distribution.
P: Key idea. Measure over the range the circuit uses, fit in an order that keeps the parameters independent, validate on data the fit never saw, and write down the range. A model's documentation is part of the model.
P: A diode needs a dozen parameters and already shows every trap. Chapter 24 applies the same method to the transistors, where the parameter count and the traps both grow.
BENCH: The perfect simulation is the dangerous one. A circuit simulates beautifully, matching the bench prototype at its operating point within a few millivolts, and then the first production lot fails a low-current specification. The model had been fitted at the prototype's operating currents; the failure happened three decades lower, in standby, where neither the model nor anyone else had looked. Before trusting a simulated margin, ask over what range each critical model was fitted, and put one measured point inside the range the circuit actually uses.
P: On the Job
P: Device modeling engineers do this for a living. In a semiconductor company they measure test structures on a wafer prober, fit the compact models that go into the PDK or the product's SPICE library, and answer the designers' question "can I trust the model here?" with residual plots instead of opinions. In a systems company the same role builds models for purchased parts, often because the vendor's model ends where the application begins. The work sits between the characterization lab and the design teams, and it rewards a feel for the device physics of this book, a scripting language for fitting, and the discipline to write down the validity range every time.
