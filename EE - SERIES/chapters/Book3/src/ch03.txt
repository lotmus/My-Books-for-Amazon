H1: Chapter 3. Two Ways Charge Moves
P: Put 1 volt across a 10-micrometer strip of lightly doped silicon and its electrons drift at about 10 kilometers per second. Shrink the strip to a tenth of a micrometer and the same formula promises 1,000 kilometers per second; the crystal refuses, and the electrons stall near 100. That is drift, the first way charge moves. The second, diffusion, needs no field at all, only a crowd that is denser in one place than another. A diode uses diffusion at the junction and drift in the bulk, so circuits need both pictures.
P: Where you meet this: The speed limit of every short transistor channel, the temperature coefficient of diffused and implanted resistors, and the diffusion current inside every diode.
H2: 3.1 Drift
P: An electric field pulls electrons one way and holes the other. The drift speeds are μn E and μp E, in opposite directions, but both movements carry positive current in the direction of the field. Holes moving with the field are a positive current. Electrons moving against the field are also a positive current, because negative charge moving backward is the same as positive charge moving forward. That is why the conductivity formula adds the two terms instead of subtracting them.
H2: 3.2 Diffusion and the Einstein relation
P: Where the concentration changes with position, carriers spread from crowded toward empty. The diffusion constants Dn and Dp have units of cm²/s. In equilibrium they are not a second independent property of the crystal. Einstein's relation ties them to the mobilities and to VT:
EQ: Dn = μn VT,    Dp = μp VT
P: with VT in volts. This book's μn and VT give Dn = 26.0 cm²/s. Dp = μp VT = 12.48 cm²/s. The electron diffusion current density along x is q Dn dn/dx, and the sign is fixed by the fact that electrons diffusing toward +x carry negative charge that way, which is a negative current in the +x direction. Holes diffusing toward +x are a positive current. The formulas used below take magnitudes when the question is "how large," and they keep the sign when the question is "which way."
H2: 3.3 Drift and diffusion together
P: In the bulk of a uniformly doped resistor, the gradient is about zero and drift does the work. At a pn junction in equilibrium, a large diffusion current wants to pour carriers across the boundary, and a drift current from the built-in field cancels it. Net current is zero. The cancellation is exact in equilibrium and is the reason the next chapter can compute a voltage without ever connecting a battery.
P: Key idea. Field moves carriers as drift. A crowd moving into an empty region is diffusion. Equilibrium at a junction is those two currents canceling.
H2: Worked Example 3.1
P: Electron concentration falls from 1.0×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ to 1.0×10¹⁵ cm⁻³ across 1 μm (1×10⁻⁴ cm). Using this book's Dn, find the magnitude of the diffusion current density.
P: |dn/dx| = 9.0×10¹⁵ / 1×10⁻⁴ = 9.0×10¹⁹ cm⁻⁴. |Jn| = q Dn |dn/dx| = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(26.0)(9.0×10¹⁹) = 374.909 A/cm². That is a serious current density for a gradient only a micron long, which is why junctions, whose gradients are even steeper, care so much about diffusion.
H2: 3.4 Mobility is a working value, not a constant
P: This book's μn = 1000 and μp = 480 cm²/V·s are declared hand values, and Section 3.1 used them as if mobility were a property of silicon alone. It is not. A carrier accelerates in the field until it collides with something, and mobility measures how far it gets between collisions. Two kinds of collision dominate. The lattice itself vibrates, and those vibrations, phonons, scatter carriers more as the temperature rises. Ionized dopants scatter them too, more as the doping rises.
P: Measured values for silicon at 300 K show both effects. Electrons in lightly doped material reach about 1350 cm²/V·s; at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ about 1200; at 10¹⁷ about 800; at 10¹⁸ about 280. Holes start near 480 and fall to roughly 440, 320, and 150 over the same doping steps. The working values of this book sit in the middle of the range a designer meets. When a problem names a doping above 10¹⁷ cm⁻³, the low numbers apply, and a resistivity computed with the declared μn will be optimistic by a factor of three or more.
P: Temperature works the other way round from ni. In lightly doped silicon, where lattice scattering dominates, mobility falls roughly as T^(−2.4). Warming from 300 K to 350 K cuts it by a factor of (350/300)^2.4 = 1.45. The resistance of a lightly doped resistor therefore rises with temperature, by up to about 0.8 % per kelvin; heavily doped layers, where impurity scattering dominates, change much less, which is one reason PDKs offer several resistor types with different temperature coefficients. The same falling mobility is why a hot MOSFET carries less current at a given gate drive, a self-protecting property that lets power MOSFETs be paralleled more easily than bipolar transistors.
H2: 3.5 When the field gets strong, speed stops growing
P: Drift velocity equals μE only while the field is modest. Above a few thousand volts per centimeter, carriers gain energy from the field faster than they can shed it to the lattice, they begin emitting optical phonons on every flight, and their velocity levels off at the saturation velocity, about 1×10⁷ cm/s for electrons in silicon and somewhat less for holes. The crossover field is roughly vsat/μ, which for μn = 1000 cm²/V·s is 10⁴ V/cm, or 1 V across a micrometer.
FIG: fig3_1.png | Figure 3.1 — Drift velocity stops following μE above about 10⁴ V/cm and levels off near 10⁷ cm/s. A 100 nm channel with 1 V across it works far up the flat part.
P: That number explains a large part of modern device physics. A transistor channel 100 nm long with 1 V across it sees an average field of 10⁵ V/cm, ten times the crossover. Its carriers travel at vsat over most of the channel, and the square-law MOSFET of Chapter 10 gives way to the velocity-saturated device of Section 10.6. The transit time across a length L at vsat is L/vsat: 10 ps for a micrometer, 1 ps for 100 nm. Velocity saturation sets the speed of every short device, and the materials of Chapter 7 are chosen partly for higher peak velocities.
H2: 3.6 Why the bulk stays neutral
P: Section 2.3 asserted that a lump of semiconductor carries no net charge in its bulk. The reason is speed. If extra charge appears inside a conductor, it pushes like charges apart, and they flow outward until the field is gone. The time constant of that process is the dielectric relaxation time,
EQ: τd = εs / σ
P: For silicon at 1 Ω·cm, εs = 11.7 × 8.854×10⁻¹⁴ = 1.04×10⁻¹² F/cm, so τd ≈ 1 ps. Any imbalance of majority charge disappears in about a picosecond, far faster than any circuit event. Minority carriers are a different matter: an excess of them is neutralized almost instantly by a matching pile of majority carriers, and then the pair lives until it recombines, typically for nanoseconds to milliseconds. That long life of injected minority carriers, protected by instant majority neutralization, is the effect on which the diode of Chapter 5 and the transistor of Chapter 8 are both built. Chapter 18 measures it as a lifetime and a diffusion length.
P: Key idea. Mobility falls with doping and with temperature, and drift velocity saturates near 10⁷ cm/s above about 10⁴ V/cm. Majority charge imbalances vanish in picoseconds; minority excesses live long enough to make devices.
H2: Worked Example 3.2
P: Find the resistivity of n-type silicon doped at 1.0×10¹⁷ cm⁻³, first with this book's working μn = 1000 cm²/V·s, then with the measured value of about 800 cm²/V·s at that doping.
P: With 1000: ρ = 1/(q n μn) = 1/[(1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(10¹⁷)(1000)] = 0.0624 Ω·cm. With 800: ρ = 0.0780 Ω·cm, 25 % higher. At 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, where the real mobility is near 280, the error of the working value would be a factor of 3.6. The lesson is the one in Appendix A: hand values are for teaching arithmetic; a real design takes mobility from the process's tables.
H2: Worked Example 3.3
P: Electrons cross a 2 µm drift region in which the field is 5×10⁴ V/cm. Estimate the transit time, and compare it with what μE would predict.
P: The field is five times the crossover, so the electrons are near vsat: t = L/vsat = 2×10⁻⁴ / 10⁷ = 20 ps. The low-field formula would give v = μE = 1000 × 5×10⁴ = 5×10⁷ cm/s, five times faster than silicon allows, and a transit time of only 4 ps. Using μE beyond its range overestimates speed by exactly the factor by which the field exceeds the crossover.
P: Drift, diffusion, and their limits are now on the table. The next chapter puts an n region against a p region and lets the two currents fight to a standstill.
BENCH: Use the declared mobility for arithmetic and the process tables for design. A diffused or implanted resistor can move by several tenths of a percent per kelvin, while the ratio of two resistors of the same type holds far better than either value. That is why good IC designs set gains by ratios, and why a discrete designer who needs precision reaches for a thin-film part.
