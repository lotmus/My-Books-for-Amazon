H1: Chapter 28. ASICs, FPGAs, and the Economics of a Chip
P: A product needs a digital function that no catalog part offers. Program a microcontroller, configure an FPGA, or design a chip? The physics of this book says what each can do. The economics says which one you can afford, and the answer changes with every factor of ten in volume.
P: Where you meet this: Product planning, architecture reviews, startup business plans, and the career choice among board, FPGA, and chip design.
H2: 28.1 Three ways to build a function
P: A microcontroller is a Swiss Army knife: a processor, memory, and a drawer of peripherals, all fixed, all inexpensive, made by the hundred million. The function lives in firmware, which the user changes at will, and the limit is speed, because everything happens one instruction at a time. An FPGA is a box of Lego: a sea of lookup tables, flip-flops, memory blocks, and multipliers joined by programmable routing and configured by a bitstream into whatever circuit the designer describes. It runs truly in parallel at clock rates of hundreds of megahertz and can be rebuilt overnight, but each function costs much more silicon, power, and money than it would as a dedicated circuit, because the programmability itself occupies most of the die. An ASIC, an application-specific integrated circuit, is a custom machine: transistors laid out for one function only, the most efficient in power, speed, and unit cost, and the only one of the three that can put the analog, RF, sensor, and high-voltage devices of this book on the same die. It is also the only one that cannot be changed once made, and the only one with a large up-front cost.
H2: 28.2 From idea to silicon
P: An ASIC follows the path of this book in reverse. A specification becomes an architecture, the architecture becomes register-transfer-level code in a hardware description language, and simulation and formal checks verify it, often for longer than the design itself took. Synthesis maps the code onto the standard cells of the PDK of Chapter 11; placement and routing lay them out; timing, power, and signal-integrity analysis check the layout at the corners of Chapter 22; design-rule and layout-versus-schematic checks confirm the geometry. Analog and RF blocks are designed by hand with the device models of this book. The finished layout goes to the foundry as mask data, the tape-out, and wafers come back weeks to months later. Test, using the scan chains and built-in self-test inserted during design, separates good dice from bad; the yield of Chapter 20 decides how many good dice a wafer gives; and packaging, the subject of Book 7, finishes the part.
P: The FPGA path shares the first half: the same hardware description languages and the same simulation, then synthesis onto the FPGA's fabric and a bitstream instead of masks. That shared front end is why many ASICs are prototyped on FPGAs first. Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, teaches the languages in its chapter "HDL: Software That Becomes Hardware", with an inexpensive hobby FPGA board as the target.
H2: 28.3 NRE against unit cost
P: Non-recurring engineering, NRE, is everything paid once: engineering salaries, design-tool licenses, purchased IP blocks, the mask set, prototypes, and qualification. Unit cost is paid per part: silicon, package, test, and margin. The total cost per unit at a volume N is NRE/N plus the unit cost, and the cheapest option changes as N grows. The break-even volume between two options is the difference in NRE divided by the difference in unit cost.
EQ: cost per unit = NRE / N + unit cost;   N(break-even) = (NRE2 − NRE1) / (unit1 − unit2)
H2: Worked Example 28.1
P: Compare three ways to build a function, using example figures chosen for the arithmetic, not quoted prices: an FPGA design with an NRE of 0.2 million dollars and a unit cost of 25 dollars; an ASIC in a mature node with an NRE of 3 million and a unit cost of 2 dollars; and an ASIC in an advanced node with an NRE of 40 million and a unit cost of 1.50 dollars. Find the break-even volumes, and the cost per unit of each option at 10,000 and at 1 million units.
P: FPGA against mature ASIC: (3 − 0.2) million/(25 − 2) = 121,700 units. Mature against advanced ASIC: (40 − 3) million/(2 − 1.5) = 74 million units. At 10,000 units the FPGA costs 0.2 million/10⁴ + 25 = 45 dollars per unit, the mature ASIC 300 + 2 = 302, and the advanced one 4,000 + 1.50 = 4,001.50. At 1 million units: FPGA 25.20, mature ASIC 5.00, advanced ASIC 41.50. The FPGA wins small volumes, the mature-node ASIC wins from about 122,000 units, and the advanced node pays off on cost alone only beyond tens of millions (Figure 28.1); it is usually chosen for performance or power the mature node cannot deliver. If an off-the-shelf microcontroller can do the job at all, with an NRE that is mostly firmware time and a unit cost of a few dollars, it beats all three at almost any volume. The custom options earn their place only where it cannot.

P: Real figures vary by orders of magnitude with the node, the design size, and how much IP is bought rather than written; the mask set alone for an advanced node costs more than a whole mature-node project. Two refinements change the picture. Multi-project wafers share one mask set among many designs, so a prototype run costs a small fraction of a full mask set. Structured approaches, an existing chip with a configurable layer or a chiplet added to a standard package, sit between the FPGA and full custom.
P: For students and hobbyists, the shared shuttle has gone further. Programs such as Tiny Tapeout collect many small designs onto one chip, built on open process design kits such as those for the SkyWater 130 nm, IHP 130 nm SiGe BiCMOS, and GlobalFoundries 180 nm processes, and charge according to the area a design occupies. Prices and schedules change from run to run, so check the program's own pages. The point is that a real ASIC, with your own logic or a small analog block on it, is now within reach of a classroom.
H2: 28.4 Analog, mixed-signal, and RF ASICs
P: Most of the devices in this book cannot be configured into FPGA fabric. A sensor interface, a battery charger, a power-management unit, an RF transceiver, or a motor driver needs the matched pairs of Chapter 12, the op-amps of Chapter 13, the noise budget of Chapter 14, the high-voltage devices of Chapter 19, or the MEMS of Chapter 27 on the same die as its logic, and that is the analog and RF ASIC niche. Such chips are often built in mature nodes, from 180 nm down to 40 nm and below, because analog precision, high voltage, and low leakage do not improve as fast as logic density, and because the NRE is far lower. Their design is still largely manual, which is why experienced analog and RF designers remain scarce.
H2: 28.5 Who does what
P: A chip project is a team effort. Architects define the function and partition it. RTL designers write the hardware description, and verification engineers, often more numerous, build the testbenches and coverage that try to break it. Physical-design engineers synthesize, place, route, and close timing. Analog and RF designers build the blocks no synthesis tool can, and layout engineers draw them. Design-for-test engineers insert the scan and self-test structures, and test engineers write the production programs. Device and modeling engineers, met in Chapter 23, supply the models; product and yield engineers take the chip into production and keep it there; applications engineers help customers use it. A board designer, an FPGA engineer, and a chip designer start from the same physics and the same digital logic, and many careers move among the three.
P: Key idea. Microcontroller, FPGA, and ASIC trade flexibility for efficiency. Cost per unit is NRE/N plus unit cost, so the right choice depends on volume, and an ASIC is also the only way to put this book's analog, RF, power, and sensor devices on the same die as the logic.
P: That closes the path from a crystal that can be talked into conducting to a chip someone decides to pay for. The later books of the series take these devices and chips as given and build radios, modems, power converters, and packages from them; when they need a device's behavior, it is this book's physics, read through a PDK or a fitted model.
BENCH: Before committing to an ASIC, prototype the digital part on an FPGA and the analog parts on a test chip or an evaluation board, and write down the volume forecast that justifies the NRE. Forecasts are usually optimistic, so run the break-even arithmetic at half and at a quarter of the forecast. If the ASIC still wins, it is probably the right decision; if it wins only at the forecast, the FPGA or a catalog part is the safer bet.
P: On the Job
P: Chip companies hire for every role of Section 28.5, and the entry points differ. Verification and physical design take many graduates with strong digital and scripting skills. Analog and RF design favors those who enjoyed Parts II to IV of this book and are patient with both the simulator and the bench. Test and product engineering suits people who like data, statistics, and the factory floor, and applications engineering those who like customers and circuits equally. FPGA design is the most accessible start, since a board costs little and the vendors' tools have free editions, and its skills carry straight into ASIC front-end work.
