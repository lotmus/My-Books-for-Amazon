H1: Chapter 25. The Reader Lab on the Air: A LoRa Mesh, Indoor Positioning, and a Network of Nodes
P: Two 22 dBm LoRa radios, the size of a postage stamp each, can in principle close a link over more than a thousand kilometers of free space. On the ground they reach a few kilometers, and from a hilltop a hundred. The difference is the subject of this chapter: the curvature of the earth, the Fresnel zone, the airtime that every node shares, and the battery behind each radio. The free-space figure is correct; the hill is where the argument begins.
P: Where you meet this: Off-grid messaging with Meshtastic, environmental sensor networks, farms and ranches, indoor asset tracking with BLE, home automation, model aircraft and drone control links, and the vehicle buses of robots and cars.
P: This chapter builds a small wireless system from the parts of the reader lab in Book 1, Foundations of Electronics: ESP32-S3 and ESP32-C6 boards, an nRF52840 board, SX1262 LoRa modules, a GNSS module, and a few sensors. Each project is sized with the arithmetic of the earlier chapters, link budget, airtime, multiple access, positioning, and energy, before any firmware is written. The firmware itself, with its libraries and sleep modes, is Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, chapters "BLE, LoRa, and GPS Firmware", "Wi-Fi and MQTT", and "Low Power and Sleep"; the antennas and the propagation over hills are Book 4, RF, Microwave, and Transceivers, chapters "Propagation Beyond Free Space" and "Antennas and the Sky: LoRa, GPS, Satellites, and the Hydrogen Line".
H2: 25.1 Analog first: the 433 MHz remote
P: Analog first. Before LoRa, garage doors and wireless doorbells used on-off keying at 433.92 MHz, and the parts are still sold. A Holtek HT12E encoder turns eight address bits and four data bits into a pulse-width-coded word; a SAW-stabilized transmitter keys its carrier on and off with it; a superregenerative or superheterodyne receiver module recovers the pulses; and an HT12D decoder checks the address and latches the data. No processor is needed. The link carries a few hundred bits per second over a few tens of meters, and receiving one with an RTL-SDR and an AM envelope detector of the kind in Section 1.4 is a good first exercise before building the LoRa network.
H2: 25.2 The LoRa mesh: Meshtastic
P: Meshtastic is open firmware that turns an ESP32 or nRF52840 board with an SX1262 radio into a node of a text-messaging and position-sharing mesh. Each node rebroadcasts packets it hears, up to a hop limit, so messages travel beyond any one link. Its default preset, LongFast, uses spreading factor 11 in 250 kHz with coding rate 4/5. The chirp spread spectrum of Section 12.4 sets the airtime: each symbol lasts 2^SF/B, and a packet's symbol count follows Semtech's formula for preamble, header, payload, and CRC. The sensitivity is the noise floor of the bandwidth plus the noise figure plus the demodulation SNR, which for SF11 is −17.5 dB.
EQ: Tsym = 2^SF/B;   sensitivity = −174 + 10 log₁₀B + NF + SNRreq dBm
H2: Worked Example 25.1
P: Problem. A LongFast packet (SF11, 250 kHz, coding rate 4/5, 8-symbol preamble, explicit header, CRC) carries 50 bytes. Find the symbol time, the airtime, the sensitivity with a 6 dB noise figure, the link budget at 22 dBm with 0 dBi antennas, and the free-space distance at 915 MHz that the budget allows.
P: Solution. Tsym = 2048/250 kHz = 8.192 ms. Semtech's formula gives 8 + ⌈(8 × 50 − 4 × 11 + 28 + 16)/(4 × 11)⌉ × 5 = 58 payload symbols, plus 12.25 preamble symbols, so the airtime is 70.25 × 8.192 ms = 575.5 ms. Meshtastic's own preamble and header settings add a little; its airtime calculator gives the exact figure. The sensitivity is −174 + 54.0 + 6 − 17.5 = −131.5 dBm, and the budget 22 + 131.5 = 153.5 dB. Free-space loss reaches 153.5 dB at 1236 km. Real links stop far shorter, because terrain, vegetation, and the earth's curvature add loss long before that.

H2: 25.3 The hilltop link
P: Two things limit a terrestrial link before its budget does. The earth's curvature hides one antenna from the other beyond the radio horizon, about 4.12(√h1 + √h2) km for antenna heights in meters with the standard refraction allowance. And the path must clear the first Fresnel zone, the ellipsoid around the direct line whose radius at the middle of a link is 17.32 √(d1 d2/(f d)) meters, with distances in kilometers and frequency in gigahertz; an obstacle reaching into it adds loss even when the far antenna is visible. Meshtastic users who put a node on a hill or a tall building routinely see links of tens of kilometers, and record links of a few hundred kilometers have been reported between mountain peaks.
EQ: d_horizon ≈ 4.12 (√h1 + √h2) km;   r1 = 17.32 √(d1 d2/(f d)) m
H2: Worked Example 25.2
P: Problem. Two nodes sit on hilltops 500 m above a flat plain. Find their radio horizon distance, and the first Fresnel radius at the middle of a 50 km link at 915 MHz between them.
P: Solution. d = 4.12 × (√500 + √500) = 184 km. At the middle of 50 km, d1 = d2 = 25 km, and r1 = 17.32 × √(625/(0.915 × 50)) = 64 m. The ground under the path must stay at least about 0.6 × 64 = 38 m below the line between the antennas for near-free-space loss; trees and a ridge closer than that cost decibels, which the margin of Worked Example 25.1 can afford, a large one.
H2: 25.4 Sharing the air
P: Every packet occupies the channel for its airtime, and a mesh multiplies packets by rebroadcasting them. With no coordination, the access is the pure ALOHA of Section 14.2, whose success probability for offered load G, in packets per packet time, is e^(−2G). LoRa receivers can capture the stronger of two overlapping packets, and Meshtastic listens before transmitting, which both help; but nodes on two hills that cannot hear each other collide at a receiver in the valley, the hidden-node problem of Section 14.3. European duty-cycle limits are sub-band-specific: under stated power and bandwidth conditions the rules include 1 %, 0.1 %, and 10 % limits, as well as listen-before-talk alternatives. Check the current national implementation and device class. If software ignores required off-time, queued telemetry arrives late; if it transmits anyway, it may violate the regional access rule. More nodes turn the missing airtime budget into missed field reports. An unbounded firmware retry loop can keep sending a measurement after it is useful; cap retries and mark stale reports for replacement, as Book 8, Chapter 18 explains.
H2: Worked Example 25.3
P: Problem. Thirty nodes each send a 50-byte LongFast position every 15 minutes, and each packet is transmitted on average four times, once by its source and three times by relays. Find the channel utilization and the pure-ALOHA success probability.
P: Solution. G = 30 × 4 × 0.5755 s/900 s = 0.077, a utilization of 7.7 %. The pure-ALOHA success probability is e^(−0.154) = 0.858, so about one packet in seven collides on a busy channel even at this light load. Halving the position interval doubles G and drops the success to 0.74; switching to a faster preset, shorter airtime at less range, is the usual remedy in dense meshes. This is a baseline model: traffic that arrives in bursts can produce worse loss than the average utilization suggests.
H2: 25.5 Indoor positioning with BLE
P: Indoors, GNSS fails and the cheapest position comes from Bluetooth signal strength. A beacon advertising at a known power is heard with an RSSI that falls with distance as A − 10 n log₁₀d, where A is the RSSI at 1 m and n is a path-loss exponent near 2 in open space and higher with walls and bodies. Inverting the model gives distance; three or more distances give position by the trilateration of Section 16.3. The weakness is fading. Multipath and the human body make the RSSI wander by several decibels, and a 4 dB error multiplies the distance estimate by 10^(4/(10n)), 1.45 for n = 2.5. Averaging over time and over the three advertising channels helps; the angle-of-arrival arrays of Chapter 23 and the phase-based channel sounding of Section 16.6 do much better.
EQ: RSSI = A − 10 n log₁₀d;   d = 10^((A − RSSI)/(10 n))
H2: Worked Example 25.4
P: Problem. A beacon reads −59 dBm at 1 m, and the path-loss exponent is 2.2. A receiver measures −70 dBm. Find the distance, and the range of distances for a ±4 dB RSSI error.
P: Solution. d = 10^(11/22) = 3.16 m. With the RSSI 4 dB higher or lower, d = 10^(7/22) = 2.08 m or 10^(15/22) = 4.81 m. A single reading therefore places the receiver anywhere in a 2.7 m wide ring, which is why RSSI positioning is used to find the room, not the shelf.

H2: 25.6 The node network: Wi-Fi, BLE, LoRa, and GNSS
P: A home or farm network uses each radio where it fits. Wi-Fi, on an ESP32-S3 or ESP32-C6, carries high rates to the house's router and the internet but costs about 100 to 300 mA while awake and a second or more to associate and send. BLE, on an nRF52840 or the ESP32's own radio, costs microamps on average for a beacon or a sensor that a gateway reads. LoRa covers the barn and the far field. The ESP32-C6 adds an 802.15.4 radio for Thread and Zigbee, the meshes of the Matter smart-home standard of Chapter 21. A GNSS module, a u-blox or a Quectel part with a patch antenna, needs open sky, and its first fix after a cold start takes tens of seconds, which a battery budget must count. The radios share one board: the coexistence and desensitization of Section 8.9 apply between a LoRa transmitter at 22 dBm and a GNSS receiver a few centimeters away, which is why such boards filter the GNSS input and route the antennas apart.
H2: Worked Example 25.5
P: Problem. A Wi-Fi temperature node on an ESP32-C6 wakes every 10 minutes, connects, and posts a reading by MQTT, drawing an average of 180 mA for 1 s. Ignoring sleep current, find its average current and the life of a 3000 mAh battery. What does a BLE advertiser drawing 15 µA on average achieve?
P: Solution. 180 mA × 1 s/600 s = 0.30 mA average, and 3000 mAh/0.30 mA = 10 000 h, 1.14 years, before the deep-sleep current, typically 10 to 20 µA, and the battery's self-discharge are counted. The BLE advertiser lasts 3000/0.015 = 200 000 h on paper, 23 years, so in practice the battery's shelf life limits it. The lesson of the chapter "Low Power and Sleep" in Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics is that radio-on time, not radio current, sets battery life.
H2: 25.7 Cameras, control links, and the vehicle bus
P: Three more projects round out the network. An ESP32-S3 camera board streams JPEG frames from its OV2640 over Wi-Fi to a browser; at 640 × 480 with 50 kB frames at 30 frames per second it needs 12 Mbit/s, near the practical limit of the chip's Wi-Fi in a busy home, and the raw stream of Worked Example 22.6 would be impossible. A model aircraft or drone control link needs low latency more than range: the open ExpressLRS system uses LoRa and FLRC modulation at packet rates of hundreds per second, so that a stick movement reaches the aircraft within a few milliseconds, and Espressif's ESP-NOW sends short connectionless Wi-Fi frames between ESP32 boards without an access point. On wheels and in robots, the wired CAN bus of ISO 11898 links controllers: the ESP32's TWAI controller with a 3.3 V SN65HVD230 transceiver joins a bus at up to 1 Mbit/s, and a standard frame with 8 data bytes is 111 bits, 111 µs, before stuff bits.
P: A capstone joins them: a smart thermostat on an ESP32-C6 with a temperature and humidity sensor and a relay, reporting by Wi-Fi and MQTT to a home server; BLE beacons in each room telling it which rooms are occupied; a LoRa node in the garden with a soil-moisture sensor; and a GNSS-timed node that keeps the network's clocks right. Each link is sized by a budget from this book before it is built, and each failure is found with the SDR of Chapter 8 and the instruments of Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, chapter "The Budget Bench: NanoVNA, tinySA, SDRs, and Low-Cost Scopes and Supplies".
H2: Worked Example 25.6
P: Problem. A drone link must deliver a control packet within 5 ms. Compare the airtime of a 20-byte LoRa packet at SF7 in 250 kHz with that requirement, and find the time of an 8-byte CAN frame at 1 Mbit/s.
P: Solution. At SF7 and 250 kHz, Tsym = 128/250 kHz = 0.512 ms; the packet has 8 + ⌈(160 − 28 + 44)/28⌉ × 5 = 43 payload symbols plus 12.25 preamble symbols, 55.25 × 0.512 = 28.3 ms, far too slow. Control links therefore shorten the preamble, drop the header, use small payloads, and move to faster modulations such as FLRC, trading range for latency. The CAN frame takes 111 bits/1 Mbit/s = 111 µs, plus up to about 24 stuff bits.
BENCH: Before blaming firmware for a lost LoRa or BLE packet, look at the air. An RTL-SDR in a waterfall display shows whether the node transmitted at all, at what frequency, and whether another signal overlapped it; a LoRa packet appears as a band of diagonal chirps. Measure the node's current with a shunt and a scope, or a dedicated power profiler, through one complete wake cycle: the area under the curve, not the data sheet's sleep current, decides the battery life. The channel does not reserve airtime for nodes that missed the calculation.
P: On the Job
P: Who uses this: IoT and embedded engineers at product companies, smart-agriculture and utility-metering firms, and robotics and drone makers, and field engineers who deploy and maintain wireless sensor networks. Typical parts are ESP32-S3 and ESP32-C6, Nordic nRF52 and nRF54, Semtech SX1262 and LR1121, u-blox GNSS modules, and CAN transceivers. The tools are an SDR, a power profiler, a NanoVNA for the antennas, a LoRa airtime calculator, and the gateway's logs. A realistic task: a farm's soil sensors drop one reading in five after the system grew from 20 to 60 nodes; compute the channel load, find the collisions in the gateway's logs, and move the farthest nodes to a lower spreading factor through a relay on the barn roof.
