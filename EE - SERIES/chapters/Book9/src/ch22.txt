H1: Chapter 22. FFT, Real-Time Analyzers, and EMI Receivers
P: The Bluetooth interference appears for 400 µs every few seconds, and the swept analyzer never sees it: when it hops into the band, the analyzer is sweeping somewhere else. A real-time analyzer catches every occurrence, color-codes how often each pixel was hit, and triggers on it. On the EMC side, the problem is the opposite: the emission is steady, but the limit is defined for a quasi-peak detector with a one-second dwell, and the full scan takes hours unless you know how to prescan.
MEET: Interference hunting, frequency-hopping and bursty signals, radar and pulse analysis, transient emissions from switching converters, EMC precompliance and compliance testing, and audio and vibration analysis at low frequencies.
H2: 22.1 FFT analysis
P: An FFT analyzer digitizes a block of N samples at a rate fs and transforms it, giving N/2 frequency bins spaced fs/N apart. A window function (Hann, flat-top, Blackman-Harris, Kaiser) tapers the block to reduce leakage, and sets the trade between resolution and amplitude accuracy: the effective resolution bandwidth is the window's equivalent noise bandwidth (ENBW, in bins) times fs/N, and the scalloping loss is the amplitude error for a tone between bins. Hann has an ENBW of 1.5 bins and a scalloping loss of up to 1.42 dB; flat-top windows have about 3.8 bins and under 0.01 dB, the choice for amplitude accuracy. Modern spectrum analyzers use FFTs for narrow RBWs and for the vector signal analysis of Part VIII.
WE: Worked Example 22.1: Bins, windows, and RBW
PROB: An analyzer captures 4096 samples at 102.4 MS/s with a Hann window. What are the bin spacing, the RBW, and the worst-case scalloping error? What changes with a flat-top window?
SOL: Bin spacing fs/N = 102.4 MHz/4096 = 25 kHz. RBW = 1.5 × 25 kHz = 37.5 kHz. A tone halfway between bins reads up to 1.42 dB low. A flat-top window gives RBW ≈ 3.8 × 25 kHz = 95 kHz, but the tone's amplitude is right within about 0.01 dB; use it when the level matters and Hann when the resolution does.
H2: 22.2 Real-time analysis
P: A real-time analyzer computes overlapping FFTs on a continuous stream with no gaps, for a real-time bandwidth (often 40 MHz to 1 GHz in current instruments), and accumulates the results as a persistence or density display: each pixel's color shows how often the signal occupied it. Its key specification is the minimum event duration for 100 % probability of intercept (POI): an event at least as long as one FFT frame plus one hop between frames is always captured at full amplitude. A frequency-mask trigger fires when any part of the spectrum crosses a drawn mask, and the analyzer stores the time record around it for later demodulation.
WE: Worked Example 22.2: Probability of intercept
PROB: A real-time analyzer samples at 200 MS/s with 1024-point FFTs overlapped by 50 %. What is the minimum event duration for 100 % POI at full amplitude? Will a 400 µs Bluetooth burst be caught? A 5 µs radar pulse?
SOL: One frame is 1024/200 MS/s = 5.12 µs; the hop is half of that, 2.56 µs; the minimum duration is 5.12 + 2.56 = 7.68 µs. The 400 µs burst is caught every time, in many frames. The 5 µs pulse is shorter than one frame: it is always seen (no gaps), but at reduced amplitude, so use a shorter FFT (wider RBW) to catch it at full level.
H2: 22.3 The EMI receiver
P: Emission limits (CISPR 32 for multimedia equipment, CISPR 11 for industrial and medical, CISPR 25 for vehicles, FCC Part 15 in the US) are defined for a measuring receiver specified by CISPR 16-1-1: fixed bandwidths (200 Hz from 9 to 150 kHz, 9 kHz from 150 kHz to 30 MHz, 120 kHz from 30 MHz to 1 GHz, 1 MHz above 1 GHz), and detectors: peak, quasi-peak (QP, weighting by repetition rate with defined charge and discharge time constants), average, and RMS-average. Conducted emissions are measured through a line impedance stabilization network (LISN) on the mains or DC supply; radiated emissions with calibrated antennas at 3 or 10 m in a semi-anechoic chamber or an open-area test site. A spectrum analyzer with CISPR bandwidths and detectors does precompliance; a compliant receiver adds preselection, overload protection, and specified accuracy.
WE: Worked Example 22.3: Why we prescan
PROB: A radiated scan from 30 to 1000 MHz uses the 120 kHz bandwidth with steps of half the bandwidth. How many frequencies? How long does it take with a QP detector and a 1 s dwell, and with a peak detector and a 1 ms dwell? A peak reading of 42 dBµV/m at 150 MHz faces a limit of 40 dBµV/m (QP, 3 m). What next?
SOL: (1000 − 30) MHz/60 kHz = 16 167 frequencies. QP at 1 s: 16 167 s = 4.5 hours per antenna height and polarization. Peak at 1 ms: 16 s. So scan with peak, then measure QP only at the frequencies within a margin (often 6 dB) of the limit, after maximizing each by turntable and antenna height. Peak is always at least as high as QP, so a peak result below the limit passes; 42 dBµV/m peak needs a QP measurement at 150 MHz, which for a broadband switching emission is often several dB lower and may pass.

H2: 22.4 Units of EMC
P: EMC levels are in dBµV (voltage) and dBµV/m (field strength). On 50 Ω, dBµV = dBm + 107. The field strength is the receiver reading plus the antenna factor (AF, in dB/m, from the antenna's calibration) plus the cable loss: E = V + AF + L. Precompliance with a spectrum analyzer, a LISN, near-field probes, and a calibrated antenna in an ordinary room finds most problems before the expensive chamber (Chapter 33).
JOB: An EMC test engineer at an accredited laboratory runs compliance tests for customers' products: conducted and radiated emissions to CISPR 32, CISPR 11, or CISPR 25, immunity tests (ESD, radiated and conducted immunity, burst, and surge), and the reports that go to certification bodies. A typical day: a product fails radiated emissions at 150 MHz by 3 dB; the engineer finds with a near-field probe that a cable from the switching supply radiates, a ferrite fixes it, and the customer goes home with a list of three design changes. Tools: R&S, Keysight, or Gauss Instruments EMI receivers, semi-anechoic chambers, LISNs, antennas, immunity generators, and the accreditation's measurement-uncertainty budgets (Chapter 2).
MIS: A peak reading above the QP limit is a failure. Peak is always at least as high as QP, so it is a reason to measure QP, not a verdict; a peak reading below the limit is a pass, a peak above it is a question.
BENCH: Before a chamber appointment, scan the product on your bench with a near-field probe and a spectrum analyzer in peak mode. Record the strongest frequencies and the cables and parts that radiate. You will not get absolute field strengths, but you will walk into the chamber knowing where to look and what to fix.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) What is the bin spacing of a 2048-point FFT at 51.2 MS/s?
PP: 2. (Core) What is −60 dBm in dBµV on 50 Ω?
PP: 3. (Core) Why does an EMC test use a quasi-peak detector?
PP: 4. (Extension) A receiver reads 25 dBµV, the antenna factor is 14 dB/m, and the cable loss 2 dB. What is the field strength?
PP: 5. (Extension) A real-time analyzer at 500 MS/s with 2048-point FFTs and 75 % overlap: what is the minimum event duration for 100 % POI?
PP: 6. (Challenge) A DC-DC converter at 2 MHz fails conducted emissions between 2 and 20 MHz. Plan the precompliance measurement setup and the diagnostic steps to tell common-mode from differential-mode noise.
ANS: 1. 51.2 MHz/2048 = 25 kHz. The usual mistake is to divide by N/2.
ANS: 2. −60 + 107 = 47 dBµV. The usual mistake is to use 120 (that converts dBV to dBµV).
ANS: 3. Limits were set to protect broadcast reception, and the annoyance of interference grows with its repetition rate; the QP detector weights impulsive noise by repetition rate, so rare pulses count less than continuous signals of the same peak. The usual mistake is to think QP is "more accurate" than peak; it measures a different quantity.
ANS: 4. 25 + 14 + 2 = 41 dBµV/m. The usual mistake is to subtract the antenna factor.
ANS: 5. Frame 2048/500 MS/s = 4.096 µs; hop at 75 % overlap is 25 % of the frame, 1.024 µs; minimum 5.12 µs. The usual mistake is to take the frame length alone.
ANS: 6. A LISN (CISPR 16 type, 50 µH/50 Ω) in the supply line, the spectrum analyzer with the 9 kHz bandwidth and a transient limiter at its input, a ground plane under the setup, and the cable lengths as in the standard. Diagnose with a current probe around both supply wires together (common mode) and around one (differential mode), or with a CM/DM separator on the LISN outputs: differential-mode noise is fixed with more input capacitance and a differential filter, common-mode noise with a common-mode choke and by reducing the switch node's capacitance to the chassis (Book 7). The usual mistake is to add filtering at random before knowing which mode fails.
