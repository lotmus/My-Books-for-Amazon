PART: Part IV. The Time Domain
H1: Chapter 16. Inside the Oscilloscope
P: A 2.0 ns clock edge appears as 4.0 ns on a 100 MHz scope. The scope itself has a 3.5 ns rise time, and the displayed edge combines the two. A 500 MHz scope shows the same edge as 2.12 ns. The screen records the measurement chain as well as the signal. The scope has measured the signal correctly; it has not measured the signal alone.
P: Where you meet this: Every board bring-up, power-supply ripple and transients, motor drives, digital timing, serial buses, RF envelopes, and the troubleshooting of anything that changes in time.
H2: 16.1 The signal path
P: A digital storage oscilloscope has, per channel, an input attenuator and amplifier with selectable 1 MΩ or 50 Ω input, an anti-alias response, an analog-to-digital converter (8 bits in most scopes, 10 to 12 bits in high-resolution models), and acquisition memory. A trigger system decides which part of the stream to keep, and a processor draws the waveform, measures it, and decodes buses. The headline specifications are bandwidth, sample rate, memory depth, and vertical resolution, and each one limits a different measurement. Bench examples run from entry scopes by Rigol, Siglent, and Owon (70 to 500 MHz) through mid-range models from Keysight, Tektronix, R&S, and Teledyne LeCroy, to real-time scopes beyond 100 GHz for optical and SerDes work.
H2: 16.2 Bandwidth and rise time
P: The bandwidth is the frequency at which a sine is displayed 3 dB low, that is 29 % smaller. A scope's amplitude response falls gradually below that, so for a 3 % amplitude error the signal must stay below about a third of the bandwidth for a Gaussian-like response, and below a fifth for 1.4 %. For edges, the scope's rise time is about 0.35/bandwidth (10 % to 90 %), and the displayed rise time is roughly the root sum of squares of the signal's and the scope's (and the probe's) rise times. The rule of thumb: choose a scope whose rise time is three to five times faster than the edge you measure.
H2: Worked Example 16.1: Two nanosecond edges on 100 and 500 MHz
P: Problem. A 2.0 ns edge is measured with a 100 MHz scope and with a 500 MHz scope. What do they display?
P: Solution. The 100 MHz scope's rise time is 0.35/100 MHz = 3.5 ns; it displays √(2.0² + 3.5²) = 4.03 ns, twice the truth. The 500 MHz scope's rise time is 0.7 ns; it displays √(2.0² + 0.7²) = 2.12 ns, 6 % high. Subtracting the scope's rise time in quadrature is a reasonable correction only when the scope is several times faster than the edge; at 100 MHz the answer depends on the exact shape of the response and should not be trusted.

H2: Worked Example 16.2: Amplitude at the bandwidth
P: Problem. A 100 MHz sine of 1.00 V peak is measured on a 100 MHz scope, a 300 MHz scope, and a 500 MHz scope, each with a Gaussian-like response. What do they show?
P: Solution. At the bandwidth the response is −3 dB: 0.707 V, 29 % low. At a third of the bandwidth, with |H| = exp(−(f/fbw)² ln √2), the 300 MHz scope shows 0.962 V (3.8 % low); at a fifth, the 500 MHz scope shows 0.986 V (1.4 % low). Ripple and harmonic measurements need that margin; edge timing needs the rise-time margin of Worked Example 16.1.
H2: 16.3 Sample rate, memory, and aliasing
P: The sample rate must be well above twice the highest frequency in the signal; real-time scopes sample at 2.5 to 5 times their bandwidth and interpolate (sin(x)/x) between samples. Memory depth sets how long a capture can be at full rate: capture time = memory/sample rate. At slow time bases a scope with short memory must lower its sample rate, and then a fast glitch or a high-frequency component aliases into a false, slow waveform. Peak-detect acquisition keeps the minimum and maximum of each interval to catch glitches; high-resolution mode averages adjacent samples to gain bits at lower bandwidth.
H2: Worked Example 16.3: Memory at a slow time base
P: Problem. A scope samples at 5 GS/s with 10 Mpoints of memory. How long a capture keeps the full rate? To capture 100 ms of a motor start-up, what sample rate remains, and what bandwidth can that represent?
P: Solution. 10 Mpoints/5 GS/s = 2 ms at full rate. For 100 ms, the scope must drop to 10 Mpoints/100 ms = 100 MS/s, which represents signals up to about 40 MHz without aliasing; a 200 MHz switching edge in the drive will alias or be smoothed away. Use segmented memory (Chapter 18), a deeper-memory scope, or two captures: one slow for the envelope and one triggered on the edges.
H2: 16.4 Vertical resolution, ENOB, and noise
P: An 8-bit converter splits the full screen (usually eight vertical divisions) into 256 levels, so at 10 mV/div a level is 80 mV/256 = 0.31 mV; a 12-bit scope gives 0.02 mV. Bits are not the whole story: noise and distortion reduce the effective number of bits (ENOB), defined by SINAD = 6.02 ENOB + 1.76 dB, and an 8-bit scope typically achieves 6 to 7.5 ENOB depending on bandwidth. Use the most sensitive vertical scale that keeps the signal on screen, because a small signal on a large scale uses only a few levels. For power-rail ripple, use 50 Ω input or AC coupling with a 1:1 path (Chapter 17), the bandwidth limit (often 20 MHz), and a high-resolution scope or mode.
H2: Worked Example 16.4: Bits, levels, and ENOB
P: Problem. A scope with 8 bits and a measured ENOB of 7.2 views a 20 mV ripple at 10 mV/div. How many levels does the ripple span, and what is the scope's SINAD? What would a 12-bit scope with 9.5 ENOB give?
P: Solution. One level is 0.3125 mV, so 20 mV spans 64 levels. SINAD = 6.02 × 7.2 + 1.76 = 45.1 dB. The 12-bit scope's levels are 0.0195 mV (1024 levels across the ripple) and its SINAD = 6.02 × 9.5 + 1.76 = 59.0 dB, 14 dB better. For ripple and small signals on top of large offsets, the bits matter; for logic edges they rarely do.

P: Common misconception. A scope's bandwidth is the highest frequency it can measure. At the bandwidth the reading is already 29 % low; for accurate amplitudes stay below a third of it, and for edges the scope's rise time must be several times faster than the edge.
BENCH: Before measuring a fast edge, measure the scope's own rise time with the fastest edge you have (a calibrator output or a fast pulse generator), through the probe you will use. That number, not the datasheet's, is the floor of your bench. If the scope changes a compliant edge into a violation—or hides a slow edge—the board will be redesigned for the instrument instead of the requirement.
