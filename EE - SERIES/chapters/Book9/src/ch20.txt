H2: 20.1 The analog signal generator
P: An RF signal generator has a synthesizer (a PLL with a VCO or YIG oscillator, locked to an internal or external 10 MHz reference), frequency dividers and multipliers to cover its range, a modulator for AM, FM, phase, and pulse modulation, an automatic level control (ALC) loop that holds the output power with a detector and feedback, and a step attenuator for low levels. Its key specifications: frequency range, level range and accuracy (typically ±0.5 to ±1 dB, worse at the extremes), phase noise (dBc/Hz at offsets such as 10 kHz from 1 GHz), harmonics and spurious outputs, and switching speed. Examples range from R&S's SMA100B and Keysight's MXG and PSG families to compact generators by Anritsu, Signal Hound, Windfreak, and others.
WE: Worked Example 20.1: Mismatch uncertainty
PROB: A generator with output VSWR 1.5 drives a receiver input with VSWR 1.5. What is the mismatch uncertainty of the delivered power?
SOL: VSWR 1.5 is |Γ| = (1.5 − 1)/(1.5 + 1) = 0.2 for each. The delivered power varies with the relative phase of the two reflections between 20 log(1 − |Γs||Γl|) and 20 log(1 + |Γs||Γl|): with |Γs||Γl| = 0.04, from −0.35 dB to +0.34 dB. A 6 dB attenuator pad at the receiver reduces the receiver's apparent |Γ| to about 0.05 (the reflection passes the pad twice) and the uncertainty to about ±0.09 dB, at the cost of 6 dB of level.
H2: 20.2 Phase noise and spectral purity
P: A synthesizer's output is not a single line but a carrier with phase-noise skirts, described by L(f), the single-sideband noise in a 1 Hz bandwidth at an offset f, in dBc/Hz. Integrated over an offset range, phase noise gives an RMS phase error (which limits EVM, Chapter 29) and an RMS jitter (which limits ADC and clock performance). Reciprocal mixing turns LO phase noise into noise on a receiver when a strong neighboring signal is present, which is why blocking tests specify the generator's phase noise. Chapter 24 measures phase noise.
WE: Worked Example 20.2: From phase noise to jitter
PROB: A 1 GHz signal has a flat phase noise of −100 dBc/Hz from 1 kHz to 1 MHz offset and much less elsewhere. Find the integrated phase noise, the RMS phase error, and the RMS jitter.
SOL: Integrated single-sideband noise: −100 dBc/Hz + 10 log(999 kHz) = −40.0 dBc. Both sidebands count, so φrms² = 2 × 10⁻⁴ rad², φrms = 0.0141 rad = 0.81°. Jitter = φrms/(2π f) = 0.0141/(2π × 1 GHz) = 2.25 ps rms.
H2: 20.3 The vector signal generator
P: A vector signal generator (VSG) adds an I/Q modulator: two baseband signals, I and Q, multiply a cosine and a sine of the carrier, so any amplitude and phase, and thus any modulation, can be generated. A baseband generator plays I/Q samples from an arbitrary waveform memory or generates standard-compliant signals in real time (with signal-creation software that builds Wi-Fi, LTE, 5G NR, or Bluetooth frames from parameters), and options add fading simulation and multiple coherent channels for MIMO. The modulator's imperfections appear in every signal it makes: I/Q gain and phase imbalance create an image at the mirror frequency, a DC offset leaks the carrier (LO leakage), and the modulator's noise floor limits the ACLR and EVM a generator can show. A VSG's own EVM must be well below the device's limit for a test to mean anything.
WE: Worked Example 20.3: I/Q imbalance and the image
PROB: A VSG plays a single tone at +1 MHz offset with an I/Q gain imbalance of 0.1 dB and a phase error of 1°. How far below the tone is the image at −1 MHz?
SOL: The image rejection ratio is IRR = (1 + 2g cos φ + g²)/(1 − 2g cos φ + g²) with g = 10^(0.1/20) = 1.0116 and φ = 1°: IRR = 4.0461/0.000442 = 9150, 39.6 dB. The phase error dominates: 1° alone gives about 41 dB, 0.1 dB alone about 45 dB. Calibrated VSGs reach 50 to 60 dB; a single-tone test like this is how you check, and a misaligned external I/Q modulator is often far worse.

JOB: An RF test engineer at a company that makes wireless modules spends the day with vector signal generators and analyzers in a shielded room: receiver sensitivity and blocking tests against the standard's limits, calibration of the path losses of every cable, switch, and fixture in the rack, and automated sequences that run each module through a hundred frequency and power points. A typical problem: sensitivity results drift by 1 dB from morning to afternoon, traced to an unlocked reference (the generator ran on its internal TCXO) and a cable whose loss changes with temperature. Tools: Keysight, R&S, or Anritsu signal generators and analyzers, power meters for path calibration (Chapter 23), and test software in Python or C# (Chapter 34).
MIS: The generator's level setting is the power at the device. It is the power at the generator's connector into a perfect load; cables, adapters, and the mismatch between the two ends change it, and at low levels only a path calibration with a power meter gives the delivered power.
BENCH: Calibrate the path, not the generator: set the generator to the level of interest, connect a power sensor at the end of the cable where the device will be, and record the difference as the path loss at each frequency. Repeat after any cable change. A sensor used at −20 dBm and a step-attenuator reference avoid the sensor's own noise floor.
