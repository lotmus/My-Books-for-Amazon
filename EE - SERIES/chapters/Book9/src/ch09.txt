H1: Chapter 9. Electronic Loads and Battery Simulators
P: The new 5 V converter starts perfectly into a 2 Ω power resistor. Set the electronic load to draw a constant 10 W instead, the same power at 5 V, and the converter never comes up: its output creeps to a volt, stalls, and its current limit glows on the scope. The converter is fine. Constant power at 1 V means 10 A, and the load mode, not the converter, created the failure.
P: Where you meet this: Power-supply and charger development, battery and fuel-cell testing, LED driver and solar tests, and every production test of a converter.
H2: 9.1 Inside an electronic load
P: An electronic load is a set of power MOSFETs operated in their linear region, controlled so that the current they draw follows a programmed rule, with heatsinks and fans to carry the heat away. It sinks power in one quadrant only: positive voltage, current into the load. It has three ratings that form its operating envelope: maximum current, maximum voltage, and maximum power, so a 150 W, 30 A, 120 V load can draw 30 A only up to 5 V, and only 1.25 A at 120 V. At the low end it has a minimum operating voltage, typically below 1 V at full current, under which its MOSFETs cannot conduct the full current; testing a 0.8 V core rail at high current needs a load specified for it.
H2: 9.2 The four modes
P: Constant current (CC) draws a set current whatever the voltage: the standard test of a voltage source, such as a power supply, a converter, or a battery being discharged. Constant voltage (CV) holds its terminal voltage by drawing whatever current that requires, like a battery being charged or a Zener: it tests current sources and chargers. Constant resistance (CR) draws I = V/R, like a resistor, and is the gentlest for start-up tests. Constant power (CP) draws I = P/V, like the input of a switching converter downstream, and it is the mode that reproduces the stress of a real converter load. Its current rises as the voltage falls, which is a negative incremental resistance.
H2: Worked Example 9.1: The same 10 W, three ways
P: Problem. A 5 V converter is loaded with CR 2.5 Ω, CC 2 A, and CP 10 W, all 10 W at 5 V. What happens when the output sags to 4.5 V, and what happens at start-up when the output passes through 1 V?
P: Solution. At 4.5 V, CR draws 1.8 A, CC 2 A, and CP 2.22 A: only CP draws more as the voltage falls. At 1 V during start-up, CR draws 0.4 A, CC 2 A, and CP 10 A. If the converter's current limit is 3 A, CR and CC let it start, but CP demands more than 3 A at every voltage below 10 W/3 A = 3.3 V: the load turns its MOSFETs fully on trying to reach its set power, the converter sits in current limit, and the output stays near zero. The real system behind the converter (a downstream converter with undervoltage lockout) would not draw power until its input was up; many loads offer a CP start voltage or a delayed enable for the same reason. Start in CC or CR, or enable the load after the rail is up.

H2: 9.3 Transient tests
P: A load can switch between two levels with a set slew rate, frequency, and duty cycle, which is the standard test of a regulator's load-step response. The slew rate decides what you test: the fast part of the response (the output capacitors' ESR and ESL) or the loop (Book 6, Chapter 10). The load's own wiring inductance limits how fast it can really slew, so keep the leads short and twisted. Measure the response on the scope at the regulator's output capacitor, with a short ground spring (Chapter 17), not at the load's terminals.
H2: Worked Example 9.2: A load step
P: Problem. A load steps from 0.5 A to 2.5 A at 0.1 A/µs. The converter's output capacitors have 10 mΩ of ESR. How long does the step take, and how large is the immediate voltage step from the ESR?
P: Solution. 2 A at 0.1 A/µs takes 20 µs. The ESR step is 2 A × 10 mΩ = 20 mV, and with a slow 20 µs ramp the loop may partly respond during the ramp, so the measured dip is smaller than the same step at 1 A/µs would give. State the slew rate with every transient result, because a dip measured at 0.1 A/µs is not comparable with one measured at 2.5 A/µs.
H2: 9.4 Battery simulators
P: A battery is not a stiff voltage source: its voltage depends on its state of charge, its internal resistance rises in the cold and with age, and it can sink charging current. A battery simulator is a two-quadrant supply (source and sink) that follows a battery model: an open-circuit voltage table against state of charge, a programmable output resistance, and a capacity that it counts down. It tests what happens to a device at the end of discharge, in the cold, or with an aged cell, repeatably and without waiting hours. A plain bench supply is too stiff and too slow to do this well: its output resistance is milliohms and its loop has its own overshoot. Battery chemistry and modeling are Book 6's (Chapter 12).
H2: Worked Example 9.3: The ESP32-S3 node in the cold
P: Problem. In this teaching scenario, the reader-lab node draws 0.5 A from an 18650 cell during a 5 ms Wi-Fi transmit burst once per second. Its configured reset threshold is 3.05 V; that threshold is a scenario input, not a universal ESP32 value. Model a new cell as 3.6 V with 50 mΩ internal resistance and an aged, cold, end-of-discharge cell as 3.3 V with 150 mΩ. The board uses a 3.3 V LDO with 0.2 V dropout. Does the aged-cell burst cross the reset threshold?
P: Solution. The new cell drops 0.5 A × 50 mΩ = 25 mV, leaving 3.575 V at the LDO input and ample headroom. The aged, cold cell drops 75 mV to 3.225 V; the LDO can then supply at most 3.225 − 0.2 = 3.025 V. That is 25 mV below the scenario’s 3.05 V reset threshold, so the node can reset on each burst near end of discharge. A stiff 3.6 V bench supply hides the fault. This is a dropout bound, not a predicted waveform: the regulator’s transient response, cell impedance versus frequency, wiring, and board capacitance still have to be measured.
P: Common misconception. Constant power is only another way to set the same load. At one operating point CP, CR and CC are the same load; away from it they behave in opposite directions, and CP's negative incremental resistance can stall a supply at start-up or interact with its control loop.
BENCH: Before a long discharge test, set the load's undervoltage cut-off to the cell's minimum voltage, and check that the load turns off at it with a test that takes a minute (a bench supply ramped down slowly). An overnight discharge with no cut-off takes a lithium cell below its safe minimum.
