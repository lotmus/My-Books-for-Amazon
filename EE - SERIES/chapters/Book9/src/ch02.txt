H1: Chapter 2. Uncertainty Budgets, Calibration, and Traceability
P: The calibration certificate says the resistor is 10 000.3 Ω. The customer's limit is 10 000 Ω ± 0.02 %, so 2 Ω of margin either way, and the reading is 0.3 Ω inside. Does it pass? Not until you know how far the reading itself could be off, and that number is not on the display. It comes from an uncertainty budget: a short list of everything that could move the reading, each converted to the same currency and added up the right way.
MEET: Calibration certificates, test limits on a production line, specifications you write for a supplier, and any dispute about a number.
H2: 2.1 Two kinds of uncertainty, one currency
P: The international guide to uncertainty, the GUM, sorts the contributions by how they are evaluated, not by where they come from. Type A contributions come from statistics on your own repeated readings: the standard error s/√n of Chapter 1. Type B contributions come from everything else: the datasheet, the calibration certificate, the resolution of the display, the temperature of the room. To add them, convert each to a standard uncertainty, the equivalent of one standard deviation. A datasheet limit ±a with no other information is treated as a rectangular distribution: every value in the band equally likely, standard uncertainty a/√3. A certificate that states an expanded uncertainty U with a coverage factor k gives U/k. A display resolution of one count has a half-width of half a count, again divided by √3.
H2: 2.2 The budget
P: List the contributions, convert each to a standard uncertainty in the unit of the result, and multiply each by its sensitivity coefficient when the result depends on the quantity through a formula. For independent contributions the combined standard uncertainty is the root of the sum of squares. Multiply by a coverage factor, k = 2 for about 95 % confidence when the result is roughly normal, to get the expanded uncertainty you report. Root-sum-square has a consequence worth knowing by heart: the largest contribution dominates. Halving a contribution that is a third of the largest changes the total by a few percent; halving the largest changes it by nearly half.
EQ: u_c = √(u₁² + u₂² + … + u_n²),   U = k u_c (k = 2 for about 95 %)
WE: Worked Example 2.1: Budget for a 10 kΩ resistor
PROB: A 6.5-digit meter measures a 10 kΩ resistor four-wire on its 100 kΩ range: five readings average 10 000.3 Ω with a standard deviation of 0.08 Ω; the display resolves 0.1 Ω. The meter's 1-year specification is ±(0.010 % of reading + 0.001 % of range). The resistor has a temperature coefficient of 5 ppm/K, and the room is known only to within ±3 K of the 23 °C reference. Build the budget.
SOL: Meter specification: 0.010 % × 10 000.3 Ω + 0.001 % × 100 kΩ = 1.000 + 1.000 = 2.00 Ω, rectangular, so 2.00/√3 = 1.155 Ω. Temperature: 5 ppm/K × 3 K × 10 000.3 Ω = 0.150 Ω, rectangular, 0.087 Ω. Repeatability (Type A): 0.08 Ω/√5 = 0.036 Ω. Resolution: half of 0.1 Ω is 0.05 Ω, so 0.05/√3 = 0.029 Ω. Combined: √(1.155² + 0.087² + 0.036² + 0.029²) = 1.159 Ω. Expanded (k = 2): 2.32 Ω. The result is 10 000.3 Ω ± 2.3 Ω, or ±0.023 %. The meter's specification is 99 % of the variance; averaging more readings or controlling the room would change nothing. Against the customer's ±0.02 % (±2 Ω) limit, this meter cannot decide the question at all: its uncertainty is wider than the tolerance. A better-specified meter, or the same meter's tighter 24-hour specification after a fresh comparison with a standard resistor, is needed.

MIS: Averaging more readings makes any measurement more certain. Averaging reduces only the Type A part. In Worked Example 2.1 an infinite number of readings would lower the expanded uncertainty from 2.32 Ω to 2.32 Ω: the third significant figure does not move.
H2: 2.3 Calibration
P: Calibration is a comparison of your instrument with a better one, the standard, under stated conditions, with the result and its uncertainty written down. It is not the same as adjustment. A calibration lab first records the as-found readings, which tell you whether the instrument was in specification during the past year and therefore whether last year's measurements are still good. Then, if needed, it adjusts and records the as-left readings. An accredited lab (to ISO/IEC 17025) has had its procedures and uncertainties audited; its certificate lists the points measured, the uncertainty of each, and the standards used. A certificate that says only "passed" is a statement of compliance, useful for a production instrument but not enough for a budget like Worked Example 2.1, which needs the numbers.
H2: 2.4 Traceability
P: Traceability is an unbroken chain of calibrations, each with a stated uncertainty, from your reading to the SI units. Your meter is calibrated against a lab's 8.5-digit meter and standard resistors, those against a national metrology institute's standards (NIST in the United States, PTB in Germany), and those against quantum standards: the Josephson effect for the volt, the quantum Hall effect for the ohm, and atomic clocks for the second. Each link adds uncertainty, so the chain gets wider toward your bench. The word on a certificate means nothing unless the uncertainties along the chain are stated.
H2: 2.5 Decisions: TUR and guard bands
P: A pass-or-fail test compares a reading with a limit, and the uncertainty of the reading turns some passes into gambles. Two tools manage it. The test uncertainty ratio (TUR) compares the tolerance with the measurement uncertainty; 4:1 is a common minimum. A guard band shrinks the acceptance limits by the measurement uncertainty, so that a unit that passes is in specification with high confidence, at the cost of rejecting some good units near the edges. The decision rule must be agreed with the customer before the test; Book 8 (Chapter 22) builds guard-banded limits into a C# test bench.
WE: Worked Example 2.2: Ratio and guard band
PROB: A resistor tolerance is ±1 %. The test setup measures with an expanded uncertainty of ±0.25 %. What is the TUR, and what are the guard-banded acceptance limits?
SOL: TUR = 1 %/0.25 % = 4:1, the usual minimum. Guard-banded limits: ±(1 % − 0.25 %) = ±0.75 %. A resistor that reads +0.8 % is rejected even though it may be good, because the reading could be as much as 0.25 % below the true value, which could then be 1.05 %. The cost of this rule is yield; the benefit is that a shipped part is in tolerance with about 95 % confidence or better.
JOB: Calibration and metrology labs, in-house at semiconductor and aerospace companies or as service providers, keep the chain of Section 2.4 intact for thousands of instruments. A metrology technician's day is as-found readings against an 8.5-digit meter, a multifunction calibrator, and standard resistors; adjustment where the procedure allows it; certificates; and the out-of-tolerance reports that make engineers recheck last year's data. The typical task: a DMM used in production fails its as-found check on the 100 mA range by 0.03 %, and the lab must tell quality which product lots were measured with it and whether the error could have passed a bad unit. Tools: calibration management software, Fluke or Keysight calibrators, temperature-controlled rooms, and uncertainty budgets like Worked Example 2.1 for every point.
BENCH: Put a calibration due date where you can see it on every instrument, and keep the last certificate (with as-found data) in a folder. When a certificate reports an out-of-tolerance as-found reading, go back to the measurements made with that instrument since its previous calibration. That habit is what the word traceability means in practice.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) A datasheet gives ±0.02 % for a reading of 2.5000 V, with no other information. What is the standard uncertainty?
PP: 2. (Core) Three independent standard uncertainties are 0.3 µV, 0.4 µV, and 1.2 µV. Find the combined and the expanded (k = 2) uncertainty.
PP: 3. (Core) Explain in one sentence what makes a measurement traceable.
PP: 4. (Extension) A tolerance is ±0.5 % and the measurement uncertainty is ±0.2 %. Find the TUR and the guard-banded acceptance limits.
PP: 5. (Extension) Power is computed as P = V²/R from V = 5.000 V with u(V) = 1 mV and R = 10.000 Ω with u(R) = 2 mΩ. Find P and its combined standard uncertainty.
PP: 6. (Challenge) A resistor specified 1000 Ω ± 1 Ω reads 1000.8 Ω with an expanded uncertainty of ±0.6 Ω (k = 2, normal). Does it pass with simple acceptance? With guard-banded acceptance? Estimate the probability that it is actually out of tolerance.
ANS: 1. The half-width is 0.02 % × 2.5000 V = 0.5 mV. With no other information treat it as rectangular: u = 0.5 mV/√3 = 0.289 mV. The usual mistake is to use the half-width itself as the standard uncertainty, which overstates it by √3.
ANS: 2. u_c = √(0.3² + 0.4² + 1.2²) = √1.69 = 1.3 µV; U = 2 × 1.3 = 2.6 µV. The usual mistake is to add the three linearly (1.9 µV), which overstates the combined uncertainty of independent contributions.
ANS: 3. A measurement is traceable when its result is linked to the SI units by an unbroken, documented chain of calibrations, each with a stated uncertainty. The usual mistake is to think a calibration sticker or the word "traceable" alone is enough.
ANS: 4. TUR = 0.5/0.2 = 2.5:1, below the common 4:1. Guard-banded limits: ±(0.5 − 0.2) = ±0.3 %. The usual mistake is to accept the low TUR without guard banding, which ships parts that may be out of tolerance.
ANS: 5. P = 25/10 = 2.500 W. Relative sensitivities: P depends on V squared, so u_rel from V is 2 × (1 mV/5 V) = 0.04 %; from R it is 2 mΩ/10 Ω = 0.02 %. Combined relative: √(0.04² + 0.02²) = 0.0447 %, so u(P) = 2.5 W × 0.000447 = 1.12 mW. The usual mistake is to forget the factor 2 from the square, which gives 0.028 % and 0.71 mW.
ANS: 6. Simple acceptance: 1000.8 Ω is inside 999 to 1001 Ω, so it passes. Guard-banded: the limits shrink to 1000 ± 0.4 Ω, and 1000.8 Ω is outside, so it fails. The standard uncertainty is 0.3 Ω; the upper limit is (1001 − 1000.8)/0.3 = 0.67 standard deviations above the reading, and the probability that a normal variable exceeds +0.67 σ is about 25 %. So there is roughly a one-in-four chance the part is out of tolerance. The usual mistake is to believe that "inside the limits" means "in tolerance" when the uncertainty is comparable to the margin.
