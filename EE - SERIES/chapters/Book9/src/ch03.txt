H1: Chapter 3. Grounds, Loading, and the Instrument in the Circuit
P: The divider is two 1 MΩ resistors from 10 V, so the middle reads 5 V on paper. The bench meter says 4.762 V. Swap it for an old analog multimeter, 20 kΩ per volt on its 10 V range, and the needle says about 1.4 V. Touch the scope probe to the same node and a 5 mV, 60 Hz hum appears that was not there before. Nothing is broken. Each instrument has become part of the circuit, through its input resistance or through its ground, and each changed what it measured.
MEET: High-impedance nodes (dividers, references, sensor outputs), anything measured with two mains-powered instruments at once, and every measurement across a resistor that does not sit at ground.
H2: 3.1 Every instrument is a load
P: A voltmeter connected to a node forms a divider with the node's Thevenin resistance. The reading is V_th × R_in/(R_in + R_th), low by the fraction R_th/(R_in + R_th). Most DMMs have 10 MΩ input resistance on their DC voltage ranges; many bench DMMs offer a high-impedance mode (above 10 GΩ) on their lower ranges, and electrometers go far higher (Chapter 14). An ammeter does the opposite: it adds its shunt and leads in series, and the voltage across them is called the burden. At higher frequencies the input capacitance takes over, which is the subject of probes (Chapter 17). The circuit side of loading, followers and buffers, is Book 2's (Chapter 12); here it is a measurement error to estimate before you trust the number.
EQ: V_read = V_th × R_in/(R_in + R_th)
WE: Worked Example 3.1: The divider and three meters
PROB: Two 1 MΩ resistors divide 10 V. Find the reading with a 10 MΩ meter and with a meter in 10 GΩ mode.
SOL: The node's Thevenin equivalent is 5 V behind 1 MΩ ∥ 1 MΩ = 500 kΩ. With 10 MΩ: 5 V × 10/(10 + 0.5) = 4.762 V, 4.8 % low. With 10 GΩ: 5 V × 10¹⁰/(10¹⁰ + 5 × 10⁵) = 4.99975 V, 50 ppm low. The analog meter of the hook, 200 kΩ on its 10 V range, reads 5 V × 200/(200 + 500) = 1.43 V. The 6.5-digit display of the 10 MΩ meter would show 4.761905 V with full confidence, and five of those digits would be wrong. The usual fix is the high-impedance mode; when the meter has none, correct the reading with the formula, as in Problem 6.

H2: 3.2 Where is ground?
P: Three different things are called ground. Protective earth is the green-yellow wire in the mains cord; it is there to blow a fuse if a live wire touches the case. Chassis is the metal case, bonded to protective earth in most bench instruments. Signal common is the reference of the circuit you measure. An oscilloscope's probe ground clip is connected to its chassis and therefore to protective earth, so every scope channel measures against earth. A bench supply's negative terminal is usually floating, isolated from earth, with a separate earth terminal you can strap to it. A battery-powered handheld meter floats completely. Knowing which of your instruments tie their inputs to earth is the first step of every multi-instrument setup.
H2: 3.3 Ground loops
P: Connect two earthed instruments to one circuit and you have built a loop: from the first instrument's earth through the mains wiring to the second instrument's earth, and back through the shields of the cables that connect them to the circuit. Stray magnetic fields and leakage currents drive a current around that loop, usually at the mains frequency and its harmonics, and the current flows through the cable shield, which is also the signal return. Its voltage drop appears in series with the signal.
WE: Worked Example 3.2: Hum from a shield
PROB: A loop current of 50 mA at 60 Hz flows through a coaxial cable shield of 0.1 Ω between a function generator and a scope. What appears on the scope, and how can the loop be broken?
SOL: The shield drop is 50 mA × 0.1 Ω = 5 mV at 60 Hz, added to the signal. On a 1 V waveform it is invisible; on a 10 mV ripple measurement it is half the reading. Cures, in order of preference: power the circuit and one instrument from a floating source (battery, isolated supply) so the loop has no second earth path; use one instrument with differential inputs; slide a ferrite or common-mode choke over the cable to raise the loop impedance at higher frequencies (it does little at 60 Hz); or use an isolated USB or LAN link between the computer and the instrument when the loop runs through the PC. Never remove the protective earth of an instrument to break the loop: that makes the case live in a fault (Chapter 4).
H2: 3.4 Common mode and differential measurements
P: A voltage across a current shunt in the high side of a supply sits at 12 V above ground, while the signal of interest is the 100 mV across the shunt. The 12 V is common mode, present on both terminals; the 100 mV is differential. A single-ended scope channel cannot measure it at all without connecting its earthed ground clip to the 12 V node, which shorts the supply. A differential probe or amplifier measures the difference and rejects most of the common mode; its common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) says how much of the common mode leaks through. CMRR falls with frequency, so a probe that rejects 80 dB at 60 Hz may reject only 30 dB at 10 MHz. Common mode on cables is Book 7's subject (Chapter 22); here it is a number on a probe datasheet.
WE: Worked Example 3.3: CMRR as an error
PROB: The 100 mV signal across a high-side shunt rides on 12 V of common mode. How large is the error with a CMRR of 60 dB, and with 80 dB?
SOL: 60 dB is a factor of 1000, so 12 V/1000 = 12 mV leaks into the measurement, a 12 % error on 100 mV. At 80 dB (a factor of 10 000) the leak is 1.2 mV, 1.2 %. If the common mode changes (a switching node), the leak looks like a signal. A dedicated current-sense amplifier or a current probe (Chapter 17) is often the better tool.
MIS: A floating meter cannot have ground problems. A battery handheld meter floats, but its input still has capacitance to the surroundings, typically tens to hundreds of picofarads, and on a fast switching node that capacitance carries current. Floating removes the low-frequency loop, not every coupling path.
H2: 3.5 Shields and guards
P: A shield is a conductor around the signal path, tied to a fixed potential, that intercepts electric fields. A guard is a conductor driven to the same potential as the signal so that no current flows from the signal across the insulation. Shields matter for low-level and high-impedance work and for every RF measurement; guards matter for picoampere and teraohm measurements. Chapter 14 uses both in detail.
BENCH: Before the first measurement of a new setup, draw it: every instrument, every cable, and every connection to earth. Count the earth connections in the signal path. If there are two or more, you have a loop; decide where it breaks before you chase the hum it causes.

ANS: 1. Error = −R_th/(R_in + R_th) = −100 k/10.1 M = −0.99 %. The usual mistake is to think a 10 MΩ input is "infinite" compared with 100 kΩ; it is only 100 times larger, which costs 1 %.
ANS: 2. A ground loop: the two instruments' protective earths and the cable shields form a loop in which mains-frequency current flows, and its drop in the shield adds to the signal. Cures: power the circuit or one instrument from a floating source, use a differential input, or isolate the PC link if the loop goes through the computer. The usual mistake, and a dangerous one, is to remove the earth pin from one instrument.
ANS: 3. 80 dB = 10^(80/20) = 10 000:1. The usual mistake is to divide by 10 instead of 20 and get 10⁸, which treats a voltage ratio as a power ratio.
ANS: 4. The drop is 100 mA × 1.1 Ω = 0.11 V, so the board sees 3.19 V, and during Wi-Fi transmit bursts of several hundred milliamperes the drop is several times larger and may trip the brownout detector. Measure current with a lower-burden method: a small shunt and a differential measurement, a current probe, or a dedicated low-burden current meter. The usual mistake is to blame the board's firmware for resets the meter caused.
ANS: 5. The Thevenin source is 5 V behind 5 MΩ, so the meter reads 5 × 10/(10 + 5) = 3.333 V, a third low. Use the meter's high-impedance mode (above 10 GΩ) on a range that has it, an electrometer, or a unity-gain buffer with a low-bias-current op-amp. The usual mistake is to blame the resistors' tolerance for a 33 % error.
ANS: 6. V_th = V_read × (1 + R_th/R_in) = 4.762 × 1.05 = 5.000 V. The correction is 5 % of the reading; a 1 % uncertainty in R_th changes the correction by 1 % of 5 %, that is 0.05 % of the reading (about 2.5 mV), plus a similar share from the tolerance of the meter's 10 MΩ, typically also about 1 %. The usual mistake is to apply the correction with full confidence and forget that it inherits the tolerances of the two resistances.
