H1: Chapter 26. VNA Calibration, De-embedding, and Time-Domain Gating
P: Uncalibrated, the analyzer says the 20 dB return-loss connector has 15 dB; calibrated with an old kit whose load is worn, it says 23 dB; calibrated with a fresh electronic module, 20.2 dB. The device did not change; the error model did. A VNA's accuracy is not in its hardware but in the calibration, and the calibration is only as good as the standards and the care behind it.
P: Where you meet this: Every VNA measurement: connectors and cables, PCB test fixtures and probe stations, on-wafer measurements, filters in fixtures, and the extraction of device models from fixtured data (Chapter 8 for low frequencies, Book 4 for RF models).
H2: 26.1 The error model and its terms
P: A VNA's hardware is imperfect: couplers leak (directivity), the port is not a perfect 50 Ω (source match), the receivers' frequency responses differ (tracking), and the ports leak into each other (crosstalk). The 12-term error model (two ports, both directions) describes these, and calibration measures known standards to solve for the terms, which the analyzer then removes from every measurement. One-port calibrations (open, short, load) solve directivity, source match, and reflection tracking; two-port calibrations add load match and transmission tracking.
H2: Worked Example 26.1: What directivity does to return loss
P: Problem. A device has a return loss of 20 dB (|Γ| = 0.1). How far can the reading be off with an effective directivity of −40 dB after calibration, and with −20 dB uncalibrated?
P: Solution. The directivity error adds a vector of magnitude 0.01 (−40 dB) to Γ with an unknown phase, so |Γ| reads between 0.09 and 0.11: return loss between 19.2 and 20.9 dB. Uncalibrated, with 0.1 (−20 dB), the reading lies between 0 and 0.2: from 14.0 dB to an "infinite" return loss. A device can be measured reliably only when it reflects much more than the directivity error, a rule worth applying to every good-match claim.
H2: 26.2 Calibration methods
P: SOLT (short, open, load, through) uses defined standards whose models (offset delays, fringing capacitance, inductance) come from the calibration kit's definition file, which must match the kit used. TRL (through, reflect, line) and its variants need only a transmission line of known impedance and a reflect of unknown value but equal at both ports; they are the most accurate in fixtures and on wafers, but each line covers only about 8:1 in frequency (electrical lengths of about 20° to 160°). Electronic calibration modules (ECal and similar) switch characterized impedance states inside one box, are fast, and reduce operator error. Unknown-through (SOLR) handles non-insertable devices. Whatever the method, the calibration plane is where the standards were connected.
H2: Worked Example 26.2: A TRL line
P: Problem. A TRL line on a microstrip fixture (effective permittivity 3.4) should be a quarter wave at 5 GHz. How long is it, and what frequency range does it cover?
P: Solution. λ/4 = c/(4 f √εeff) = 3.0 × 10⁸/(4 × 5 GHz × 1.844) = 8.13 mm longer than the through. At 5 GHz it is 90°; the usable range of 20° to 160° is 5 × 20/90 = 1.11 GHz to 5 × 160/90 = 8.89 GHz. Wider bands need several lines, and below about 1 GHz a match (load) standard replaces the line (TRM).
H2: 26.3 De-embedding and port extension
P: When the standards cannot be placed where the device is (a part soldered on a test board, a connector launch, a probe pad), measure or model the fixture and remove it mathematically. Port extension subtracts a delay and optional loss: simple, adequate for short, well-matched launches. Full de-embedding cascades the inverse of the fixture's S-parameters (as transfer matrices) on each side; the fixture's S-parameters come from a measurement of a 2x-thru (two fixture halves back to back, split by IEEE P370-style algorithms) or from an electromagnetic simulation. The quality check is the de-embedded through: it should be 0 dB, 0° to within the expected error.

H2: 26.4 Time-domain transformation and gating
P: A VNA can transform its frequency-domain data into a time-domain response by an inverse Fourier transform, showing reflections against time (and distance) like a TDR (Chapter 18). The frequency span sets the time resolution (roughly 1/span, wider with windowing), and the frequency step sets the alias-free range (1/Δf). A gate selects one region in time, for example the device, and removes the others, such as a connector reflection or a chamber wall's echo; transforming back gives the device's frequency response without them. Gating has edge effects at the band ends and inside the gate, so it is a tool for insight and for well-separated reflections, not a replacement for calibration.
H2: Worked Example 26.3: Range and resolution
P: Problem. A VNA sweeps 10 MHz to 10 GHz in 1601 points. What are the time resolution and the alias-free range? For a cable with velocity factor 0.7, what distance does that range cover?
P: Solution. The step is 10 GHz/1600 = 6.25 MHz, so the alias-free range is 1/6.25 MHz = 160 ns of round trip, which is 0.7 × 3.0 × 10⁸ × 160 ns/2 = 16.8 m of cable. The resolution is about 1/(10 GHz) = 100 ps before windowing (about 7 mm of round-trip distance in this cable). For a longer cable, add points or reduce the span; for finer resolution, widen the span.

P: Common misconception. Calibration makes the analyzer perfect. It removes the systematic errors to the accuracy of the standards and their definitions; drift, cable movement, connector repeatability, and noise remain, and a worn or wrong standard puts its error into every later measurement.
BENCH: Torque every connector with the right wrench (0.9 N·m for SMA, 0.45 N·m for 3.5 mm on most kits; check the kit), clean them with isopropyl alcohol and lint-free swabs, gauge precision connectors regularly, and never rotate the connector body against its mate. A damaged center pin on a cable will damage every port it touches. For a release measurement, record the kit identity, calibration date, cable, torque practice, IF bandwidth and verification result; a calibration that cannot be reproduced is weak evidence.
