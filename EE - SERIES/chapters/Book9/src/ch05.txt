H1: Chapter 5. The Bench Power Supply

Nothing is wrong with the supply: 0.38 V is lying in the two thin leads and the clip contacts, and the supply cannot know, because it regulates the voltage at its own terminals. Unless you give it a second pair of wires.

H2: 5.1 Constant voltage, constant current, and the crossover
P: A bench supply has two settings: a voltage and a current limit. It regulates whichever one the load makes active. With a light load it holds the set voltage (constant voltage, CV) and delivers whatever current the load draws. When the load tries to draw more than the limit, the supply switches to holding the current (constant current, CC) and lets the voltage fall to whatever the load resistance makes it. The boundary is the crossover resistance R = V_set/I_set. The front panel shows which mode is active, and that indicator is the first thing to look at when a board misbehaves: a supply in CC is not delivering the voltage you set.
WE: Worked Example 5.1: Where the supply operates
PROB: A supply is set to 12 V with a 1 A limit. Where does it operate with an 8 Ω load and with a 20 Ω load?
SOL: The crossover resistance is 12 V/1 A = 12 Ω. The 8 Ω load would draw 1.5 A at 12 V, more than the limit, so the supply goes to CC: 1 A, and the voltage falls to 1 A × 8 Ω = 8 V. The 20 Ω load draws 0.6 A at 12 V, inside the limit, so the supply stays in CV at 12 V. Loads below 12 Ω run in CC, loads above in CV.

H2: 5.2 Linear and switching supplies
P: A linear supply rectifies the mains through a transformer and drops the excess voltage across a pass transistor. Its output is quiet, with ripple and noise typically in the hundreds of microvolts rms, and it recovers from a load step in tens of microseconds, but it is heavy and dissipates the difference between its internal rail and the output as heat. Supplies with several transformer taps switch to a lower tap at low output voltage to limit that heat. A switching supply converts at tens to hundreds of kilohertz, is light and efficient, and has higher noise, often a few millivolts peak to peak with spikes at the switching edges. Many modern bench supplies combine a switching preregulator with a linear post-regulator. For noise-sensitive work (low-noise amplifiers, ADC references, RF oscillators) a linear output still wins; for high power, switching wins. Regulator design is Book 6's subject (Chapters 2 and 3).
WE: Worked Example 5.2: Heat in a linear supply
PROB: A linear supply with a 35 V internal rail delivers 5 V at 3 A. How much does its pass stage dissipate, and how does that change with a 12 V tap? Compare with a switching supply of 85 % efficiency.
SOL: On the 35 V rail the pass stage drops 30 V at 3 A: 90 W, for 15 W delivered, an efficiency of 14 %. On a 12 V tap it drops 7 V: 21 W. The switching supply loses 15 W/0.85 − 15 W = 2.6 W. This is why a linear 30 V, 3 A supply has a large heatsink and a fan, and why it limits its current at low voltages on some models.
H2: 5.3 Remote sense
P: The supply regulates the voltage where its sense lines connect. Normally they are strapped to the output terminals inside or on the back panel. Connect them with a separate pair of thin wires directly to the load, and the supply raises its terminal voltage until the load sees the set value: the drop in the load leads is inside the loop. Sense lines carry almost no current, so their own resistance does not matter. The supply can compensate only a limited drop per lead, given in the manual, and the leads add inductance to the loop, so twist each force-sense pair and keep them short.
WE: Worked Example 5.3: The hook's missing 0.38 V
PROB: In the hook, 1.2 A flows and the board sees 4.62 V for 5.00 V set. What is the lead resistance, and what does remote sense do?
SOL: The two leads and contacts drop 0.38 V at 1.2 A, so together they are 0.38/1.2 = 0.32 Ω, about 0.16 Ω each, which is mostly thin clip leads and contact resistance. With remote sense connected at the board's input connector, the supply raises its terminals to 5.38 V and the board sees 5.00 V. The current limit and the overvoltage protection still act on the terminal values, so set the OVP above 5.38 V or the supply will trip on the compensation itself.
H2: 5.4 Protection, sequencing, and transients
P: The current limit protects the circuit from the supply; overvoltage protection (OVP) protects it from a fault in the supply or a wrong setting, by shutting the output down in microseconds when the terminal voltage crosses a threshold. Set both on every bring-up: current a little above the expected draw, OVP a little above the rail. Boards with several rails often need them in order (core before I/O, for example), and multi-channel supplies can sequence their outputs with programmed delays; Listing 5.1 does it from Python. Watch the turn-on: some supplies overshoot when the output is enabled, and a board that is sensitive to that (or a chip with an absolute maximum close to its rail) should be connected after the output is on and settled, or through a load switch.
CODE: l05_1_psu_sequence.py | Programming three rails with outputs off, then turning them on in order, measuring each, and shutting all down in reverse order on any fault (Python with PyVISA; runs in simulation with --sim).
P: In simulation the listing prints rail 1 at 3.300 V and 0.300 A, rail 2 at 1.800 V and 0.200 A, and rail 3 at 5.000 V and 0.500 A, all inside their limits. The design choices are the point: everything is programmed while the outputs are off, every setting is followed by an error-queue check, and any failure turns all rails off in reverse order before the exception reaches you.
MIS: The current limit protects the board. A current limit stops the supply delivering more than the set current, but the capacitors on its output can discharge into a short far faster than the limit acts, and a board that draws its limit through a single shorted part may already have burned that part. Set the limit low for first power-up, and watch for CC mode.
BENCH: For first power-up of a new board, set the rail voltage and a current limit of about twice the expected idle current, turn the output on with the board connected, and watch the current and the CV/CC indicator for five seconds before you do anything else. A board in CC at first power-up has a short or a part in backwards, and five seconds of attention saves the board.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) A supply is set to 5 V and 0.5 A. A 4 Ω load is connected. What voltage and current result, and in which mode?
PP: 2. (Core) What is the crossover resistance of a supply set to 24 V and 2 A?
PP: 3. (Core) What is the difference between the current limit and overvoltage protection, and which protects against a failure inside the supply?
PP: 4. (Extension) A 1.2 V rail draws 3 A through two leads of 25 mΩ each. What does the load see without remote sense, and by how much in percent is it low?
PP: 5. (Extension) A linear supply with a 20 V internal rail delivers 3.3 V at 2 A. Find the pass-stage dissipation and the efficiency.
PP: 6. (Challenge) During a remote-sense measurement one sense lead comes loose. What happens to the load voltage, what protects the board, and how should OVP be set to cover this?
ANS: 1. At 5 V the 4 Ω load would draw 1.25 A, above the 0.5 A limit, so the supply runs in CC: 0.5 A and 0.5 × 4 = 2 V. The usual mistake is to assume the set voltage appears and calculate 1.25 A.
ANS: 2. 24 V/2 A = 12 Ω. Loads below 12 Ω run the supply in CC. The usual mistake is to invert the ratio.
ANS: 3. The current limit caps the output current; it protects the circuit from drawing too much. OVP shuts the output down when the terminal voltage exceeds a threshold; it protects against a wrong setting, a failure of the supply's regulator, or an external source back-driving the output. Only OVP covers a failure inside the supply. The usual mistake is to rely on the current limit alone.
ANS: 4. The drop is 3 A × 0.05 Ω = 0.15 V, so the load sees 1.05 V, 12.5 % low, far outside the usual ±5 % rail tolerance. The usual mistake is to ignore lead drop because it is "only milliohms".
ANS: 5. Dissipation (20 − 3.3) × 2 = 33.4 W; delivered 6.6 W; efficiency 6.6/40 = 16.5 %. The usual mistake is to compute efficiency from the voltages alone without noticing that the input current equals the output current in a linear regulator, which is the same answer here, and then to apply the same shortcut to a switching supply, where it is wrong.
ANS: 6. With a sense lead open, the regulation loop loses its feedback from that side. Most supplies have internal resistors from each sense terminal to its output terminal, so the output falls back to local regulation and the load sees the set value minus the lead drop; without them, or with a sense lead shorted to the wrong point, the output can rise toward its maximum. OVP is the protection: set it a little above the highest terminal voltage you expect with compensation (set value plus both lead drops), not at the rail value, which would trip during normal compensation. The usual mistake is to set OVP before thinking about the compensation, then disable it after it trips.
