H1: Chapter 24. Noise Figure and Phase Noise Analyzers
P: The low-noise amplifier's datasheet promises 0.8 dB of noise figure. The lab measures 1.9 dB, and the designer has spent a week looking for the problem in the circuit. The problem is in the measurement: a 1 m cable between the noise source and the amplifier adds 0.5 dB, the analyzer's own noise figure was not corrected, and the noise source's excess-noise ratio table was for a different serial number. Noise measurements are differences of small numbers, and they forgive nothing.
P: Where you meet this: LNA and receiver design, satellite and radio-astronomy front ends, radar receivers, oscillator and synthesizer design, clock jitter for ADCs and SerDes, and any system whose sensitivity matters.
H2: 24.1 Noise figure and the Y-factor method
P: The noise factor F is the ratio of the signal-to-noise ratio at the input to that at the output, with the input noise at 290 K; the noise figure is NF = 10 log F (Book 4). The Y-factor method uses a calibrated noise source, a diode that switches between a "cold" state (290 K) and a "hot" state whose excess noise is the source's ENR (excess noise ratio, in dB, from its calibration table against frequency). The analyzer measures the output noise power with the source on and off; their ratio Y gives F = ENR/(Y − 1), in linear terms. The measured F includes the analyzer's own noise, removed with the Friis formula: F_DUT = F_sys − (F_analyzer − 1)/G_DUT, which needs a calibration step that measures the analyzer alone. Noise-figure analyzers and spectrum analyzers with noise-figure options and smart noise sources (Keysight, R&S) automate this; the cold-source method with a VNA is the alternative for well-matched devices.
H2: Worked Example 24.1: A Y-factor measurement
P: Problem. A noise source with ENR 15.0 dB drives an amplifier with 20 dB of gain, followed by an analyzer with a noise figure of 10 dB. The measured Y is 9.50 dB. Find the system noise figure and the amplifier's noise figure.
P: Solution. ENR = 10^1.5 = 31.62; Y = 10^0.95 = 8.913. F_sys = 31.62/(8.913 − 1) = 3.997, NF_sys = 6.02 dB. Second-stage correction: F_DUT = 3.997 − (10 − 1)/100 = 3.907, NF = 5.92 dB. With 20 dB of gain the correction is 0.1 dB; with 10 dB of gain it would be about 1 dB, so low-gain devices need a low-noise analyzer or a preamplifier, and a calibrated correction.
H2: 24.2 What makes noise-figure measurements wrong
P: Losses in front of the device add directly to its noise figure (a 0.5 dB cable adds about 0.5 dB); the ENR table must belong to the noise source used, at the measured frequency; the noise source's match changes between hot and cold, which matters for devices with poor input match; interference from mobile phones and Wi-Fi in an unshielded room appears as noise; and the measurement must be in the device's linear range, with the hot noise power far from compression. Temperature matters too: the cold state is assumed to be 290 K, and a source at 300 K reads a little differently (meters correct for a measured temperature).
H2: 24.3 Phase noise measurement
P: Three methods cover most needs. The direct spectrum method reads the noise beside a carrier on a spectrum analyzer: L(f) = noise power in the RBW − carrier power − 10 log(RBW), with corrections for the detector and the filter's noise bandwidth, limited by the analyzer's own LO phase noise. The phase-detector method mixes the device with a quiet reference in quadrature, so amplitude noise cancels and phase noise appears as a baseband voltage. The cross-correlation method uses two independent references and averages the cross-spectrum, which cancels the uncorrelated noise of the references by 5 log10(N) dB for N averages, so a phase-noise analyzer can measure oscillators quieter than its own references. Keysight's E5052B and R&S's FSWP are examples of signal-source analyzers.
H2: Worked Example 24.2: Direct spectrum method and its floor
P: Problem. A 0 dBm carrier is viewed with a 1 kHz RBW and an RMS detector, already corrected for the filter's noise bandwidth. At 10 kHz offset the noise reads −85 dBm. What is L(10 kHz)? The analyzer's own phase noise at that offset is −120 dBc/Hz; correct for it. How many cross-correlations would a 10 dB improvement in the floor need?
P: Solution. L = −85 − 0 − 10 log(1000) = −115 dBc/Hz. The analyzer adds its own −120 dBc/Hz in power: the device's value is 10 log(10^−11.5 − 10^−12.0) = −116.7 dBc/Hz, 1.7 dB better than displayed. With the analyzer only 5 dB below the reading, the correction is already uncertain; a 10 dB margin is the rule. Cross-correlation improves the floor by 5 log10(N): 10 dB needs N = 100.

P: Common misconception. A spectrum analyzer's noise marker reads the phase noise directly. It reads the sum of the device's phase noise, its amplitude noise, and the analyzer's own phase noise and noise floor; when the reading is within 10 dB of the analyzer's own, the result is the analyzer's.
BENCH: Put the noise source directly on the device's input connector, with no cable or adapter that is not part of the device. If one is unavoidable, measure its loss and correct for it at its temperature. And measure noise figure in a shielded enclosure or at least with phones and Wi-Fi away; one stray carrier in the band ruins the Y-factor.
