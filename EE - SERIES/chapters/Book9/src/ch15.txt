H1: Chapter 15. Counters, Timers, and Frequency References
P: The counter reads 10.000 012 3 MHz with nine digits, and the oscillator's specification is ±1 ppm. Is it 1.23 ppm high, out of specification? The counter's own timebase is a TCXO with ±2 ppm accuracy. The nine digits are resolution; the counter does not know the right answer any better than the oscillator under test. A frequency measurement is a comparison with a reference, and the reference must be better than what you measure.
P: Where you meet this: Crystal oscillator and PLL test, clock jitter and stability, timing of pulses and delays, GPS-disciplined references in labs, RF calibration (Chapters 20 and 21 all lock to 10 MHz), and the 10 MHz cable that ties a bench together.
H2: 15.1 How counters count
P: A conventional counter opens a gate for a set time, often 1 s, and counts input cycles; its resolution is ±1 count of the input, so 1 Hz in 1 s, regardless of how good the timebase is. A reciprocal counter instead counts a fast internal clock between input edges and computes the frequency as cycles/time; its resolution is ±1 count of the clock, so the relative resolution is the same for any input frequency. Modern counters add interpolators that measure the fractions of a clock period down to about 10 to 20 ps, and continuous timestamping that improves resolution further by regression over many edges; Keysight's 53230A (20 ps single shot, 12 digits per second) is an example of the class, along with counters from Tektronix, Pendulum, and others. Time interval, pulse width, phase, and totalize modes use the same core.
H2: Worked Example 15.1: Conventional versus reciprocal
P: Problem. A 1 kHz signal is measured with a 1 s gate. What resolution does a conventional counter give, and a reciprocal counter with a 100 MHz clock? What does a 20 ps interpolator give?
P: Solution. Conventional: ±1 count of the input, 1 Hz in 1 kHz, 1 × 10⁻³. Reciprocal: ±1 clock period in 1 s, 10 ns/1 s = 1 × 10⁻⁸, 0.01 mHz. With a 20 ps interpolator, 20 ps/1 s = 2 × 10⁻¹¹. Reciprocal counting is why a modern counter measures audio frequencies to many digits in a second.

H2: 15.2 Trigger error and averaging
P: The counter decides that an edge has arrived when the input crosses its trigger level, and noise on a slow edge moves that moment: the timing error is the noise voltage divided by the slew rate. Fast edges and a trigger level at the steepest point help; so does averaging over many periods, because the error appears only at the first and last edge of the gate.
H2: Worked Example 15.2: Noise on a slow sine
P: Problem. A 1 kHz, 1 V-peak sine carries 1 mV rms of noise. What timing jitter does the trigger see at the zero crossing, and what is the single-period error? How does a 1 s gate change it?
P: Solution. The slew rate at zero crossing is 2π × 1000 × 1 V = 6283 V/s, so the jitter per edge is 1 mV/6283 V/s = 159 ns rms. A period uses two edges: √2 × 159 = 225 ns on 1 ms, 2.3 × 10⁻⁴. Over a 1 s gate, the same two edges' error is spread over 1000 periods: 225 ns/1 s = 2.3 × 10⁻⁷. Trigger noise, not the clock, limits this measurement; squaring the signal with a fast comparator first would help more than a better counter.
H2: 15.3 References: TCXO, OCXO, rubidium, GPSDO
P: The counter's accuracy is its timebase's accuracy. A TCXO is good to about ±1 to 2 ppm; an oven-controlled OCXO to parts in 10⁸ to 10⁹ after aging and calibration; a rubidium standard to about 10⁻¹¹; a GPS-disciplined oscillator (GPSDO) steers an OCXO to GPS time and averages to 10⁻¹² or better over a day, while being no better than its OCXO over seconds. Stability over a time interval τ is described by the Allan deviation σy(τ), which tells you how much two frequency readings τ apart differ, and which reference is best for which averaging time. A lab distributes one 10 MHz reference to every counter, generator, and analyzer (the rear-panel REF IN), so that all instruments agree with each other, and calibrates or disciplines that one reference.
H2: Worked Example 15.3: Is the oscillator in specification?
P: Problem. The hook's counter reads 10.000 012 3 MHz with a ±2 ppm TCXO timebase. The oscillator's specification is 10 MHz ±1 ppm. What can you say? What if the counter runs from a GPSDO with ±1 × 10⁻¹⁰?
P: Solution. The reading is 1.23 ppm high, but the timebase allows ±2 ppm, so the true value is somewhere between −0.77 and +3.23 ppm: the counter cannot decide. The test uncertainty ratio is 1:2, the wrong way round (Chapter 2). With the GPSDO the uncertainty is 0.0001 ppm and the oscillator is 1.23 ppm high, out of specification. Locking the counter to a good reference costs one cable.
P: Listing 15.1 — Frequency against gate time with a counter (or simulation): reads a series of frequencies, computes the mean, standard deviation, and overlapping Allan deviation for τ from 0.1 to 100 s (Python with PyVISA and NumPy).
P: """Frequency readings from a counter and their statistics: mean, standard deviation, and theoverlapping Allan deviation for averaging times from 0.1 s to 100 s.SCPI in the style of the Keysight 53230A (CONF:FREQ, SENS:FREQ:GATE:TIME, READ?); check your manual.Run:  python l15_1_counter_avg.py --sim"""import argparseimport numpy as npclass SimCounter:    """A 10 MHz OCXO: white frequency noise 3e-11 at 0.1 s, random walk 2e-12 per sqrt(s), offset +4.2e-9."""    def __init__(self):        self.rng, self.n = np.random.default_rng(15), 1    def write(self, cmd):        if cmd.startswith('SAMP:COUN '):            self.n = int(cmd.split()[1])    def query(self, cmd):        if cmd == '*IDN?':            return 'SIM,COUNTER,0,1.0'        white = self.rng.normal(0, 3e-11, self.n)        walk = np.cumsum(self.rng.normal(0, 2e-12 * np.sqrt(0.1), self.n))        return ','.join('%.6f' % x for x in 10e6 * (1 + 4.2e-9 + white + walk))def adev(y, m):    """Overlapping Allan deviation of fractional-frequency samples y at averaging factor m."""    c = np.cumsum(np.insert(y, 0, 0.0))    avg = (c[m:] - c[:-m]) / m    d = avg[m:] - avg[:-m]    return np.sqrt(0.5 * np.mean(d ** 2))def main():    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser()    ap.add_argument('resource', nargs='?')    ap.add_argument('--sim', action='store_true')    ap.add_argument('--nominal', type=float, default=10e6)    a = ap.parse_args()    ctr = SimCounter() if a.sim else __import__('pyvisa').ResourceManager().open_resource(a.resource)    if not a.sim:        ctr.timeout = 600000    print(ctr.query('*IDN?'))    for cmd in ('*RST', 'CONF:FREQ %g' % a.nominal, 'SENS:FREQ:GATE:TIME 0.1', 'ROSC:SOUR EXT', 'SAMP:COUN 6000'):        ctr.write(cmd)                                  # 6000 x 0.1 s = 10 minutes; external 10 MHz reference    f = np.array([float(x) for x in ctr.query('READ?').split(',')])    y = f / a.nominal - 1    print('mean offset %+.3e, standard deviation %.2e (fractional)' % (y.mean(), y.std(ddof=1)))    for m in (1, 10, 100, 1000):        print('tau = %6.1f s   ADEV = %.2e' % (0.1 * m, adev(y, m)))if __name__ == '__main__':    main()
P: Common misconception. More digits on the counter mean a more accurate frequency. Digits show resolution; the timebase sets accuracy, and a 12-digit counter on an uncalibrated TCXO is accurate to 6 digits.
BENCH: Put a 10 MHz distribution amplifier on the bench and lock every instrument's REF IN to the best reference you own, even a used OCXO or a GPSDO module. Then every frequency, every spectrum, and every modulation measurement agrees across instruments, and the analyzer's carrier offsets stop wandering.
