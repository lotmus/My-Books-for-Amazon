PART: Part XI. The Budget Bench and the Home Lab
PINTRO: A working RF and electronics bench used to cost as much as a car. Today a NanoVNA, a tinySA, an SDR dongle, a 12-bit scope, and a programmable supply cost less than a good phone, and they are good enough for most hobby work and much professional debugging, provided you know where their limits are. Chapter 37 measures those limits; Chapter 38 builds the home lab around them, with simple instruments you make yourself, including the mini curve tracer of Chapter 7.
H1: Chapter 37. The Budget Bench: NanoVNA, tinySA, SDRs, and Low-Cost Scopes and Supplies
P: A student tunes a 435 MHz antenna with a NanoVNA and reports 42 dB return loss, better than the lab's calibrated antenna. It is not. The NanoVNA's calibrated S11 floor at that frequency is about −50 dB, and a true 35 dB match can read anywhere from 33.6 to 36.7 dB on a good day; with a worn SMA adapter between calibration and antenna, 42 dB means "somewhere above 30." Cheap instruments are honest about everything except their limits; finding those is your job.
MEET: Ham radio and maker labs, university teaching labs, startups before their first real budget, field work where a $3000 instrument is not going up the tower, and the professional engineer's second bench at home.
H2: 37.1 The vector network analyzer for the price of a dinner
P: The NanoVNA family (an open-source design by edy555, developed further as the NanoVNA-H and -H4 by Hugen and as the LiteVNA and others) is a two-port, one-path VNA: port 1 measures S11, port 2 receives S21, and the DUT must be turned around for S22 and S12. The NanoVNA-H4 covers 10 kHz to 1.5 GHz; above 300 MHz it uses harmonics of its synthesizer, so its specified S21 dynamic range falls from 70 dB (below 300 MHz) to 60 dB (to 900 MHz) and 40 dB (to 1.5 GHz), and its S11 range from 60 to 50 to 30 dB. The LiteVNA 64 covers 50 kHz to 6.3 GHz with more than 70 dB below 3 GHz and more than 50 dB above. Calibration (SOLT, Chapter 26) with the supplied SMA standards is essential, at the plane where the DUT will connect; the standards are not characterized like a lab kit, so treat results above a few GHz as approximate. PC software (NanoVNA-Saver and others) adds TDR, Smith charts, and Touchstone export.

WE: Worked Example 37.1: How good a match can you prove?
PROB: With a −50 dB S11 floor, what range of readings can a true 20 dB and a true 35 dB return loss produce? And with a −40 dB floor?
SOL: The residual error vector of magnitude 10^(−50/20) = 0.00316 adds to the device's reflection at an unknown phase. 20 dB: |Γ| = 0.100, readings from −20 log(0.1032) = 19.73 dB to −20 log(0.0968) = 20.28 dB. 35 dB: |Γ| = 0.0178, from −20 log(0.0209) = 33.6 dB to −20 log(0.0146) = 36.7 dB. With a −40 dB floor (0.010), the 35 dB match reads from 31.1 to 42.2 dB. Rule: a VNA proves a match only to about 10 dB short of its floor.

H2: 37.2 The pocket spectrum analyzer
P: The tinySA (Erik Kaashoek's design) is a swept superheterodyne analyzer in a handheld case. The tinySA Ultra (ZS405) covers 100 kHz to 800 MHz in its normal mode and up to 5.3 GHz in "Ultra" mode, with RBW from 200 Hz to 850 kHz, a 0 to 31 dB step attenuator, a switchable 20 dB LNA (noise figure about 5 dB), and level accuracy of about ±2 dB after calibration. Its maker's specification is unusually frank: absolute maximum input +6 dBm without attenuation, best measurements below −25 dBm, input IP3 about +15 dBm, and 1 dB compression at −1 dBm. Above 800 MHz the Ultra mode uses mixer harmonics and image responses; the firmware's spur-removal option and a second measurement with a small frequency offset separate real signals from internal ones (the attenuation test of Chapter 21 works too). It also serves as a simple signal generator.
WE: Worked Example 37.2: What the tinySA can see
PROB: With the LNA on (noise figure 5 dB) and RBW 200 Hz, estimate the displayed average noise level. Without the LNA at RBW 30 kHz the specification gives −102 dBm at 30 MHz; what noise figure does that imply? Which input level keeps the analyzer's own third-order products 80 dB down?
SOL: With LNA: −174 + 5 + 10 log(200) = −146 dBm, against the specified −145 dBm. Without: −102 = −174 + NF + 10 log(30 000) = −174 + NF + 44.8, so NF = 27 dB, the usual figure for a mixer-first analyzer. Two tones at input level P produce third-order products 2(IIP3 − P) below the tones (Chapter 21): 80 dB needs IIP3 − P = 40 dB, P = 15 − 40 = −25 dBm per tone, which is the maker's own recommendation.
H2: 37.3 Software-defined radios as receivers and sources
P: An SDR dongle such as the RTL-SDR Blog V4 (500 kHz to 1.766 GHz, an 8-bit ADC, 2.4 MHz of stable bandwidth, a 1 ppm TCXO, receive only) is a wideband receiver whose I/Q goes to the PC: with GNU Radio, SDR++, or Python, it becomes a spectrum monitor, a demodulator, a VSA for simple signals (Chapter 28), and a spectrogram for chirps (Chapter 32). The HackRF One (1 MHz to 6 GHz, 8-bit, up to 20 MS/s, half duplex) also transmits, a few milliwatts up to about +15 dBm depending on frequency, which makes it a flexible but uncalibrated signal source. Neither is a measuring instrument: their gain varies with frequency, temperature, and settings, their 8-bit converters limit the dynamic range to about 50 dB in the full bandwidth (more in a narrow FFT bin), and their images and spurs are not specified. Calibrate the level against a known source at the frequency of use, and transmit only into a dummy load or a shielded box unless you are licensed for the band.
H2: 37.4 Low-cost scopes, supplies, and meters
P: The 12-bit scope has reached the hobby price: in October 2026, four-channel 70 MHz models with 12-bit ADCs, deep memory, serial decoding, and LAN with SCPI cost about US $400 to $500 (for example the Rigol DHO804 and Siglent SDS804X HD). Their bandwidth is real; their probes are the usual weak point (Chapter 17), and their SCPI interfaces make them as programmable as lab scopes (Chapter 34). Programmable linear supplies with LAN or USB, handheld DMMs with true rms and CAT III ratings, and USB oscilloscopes and logic analyzers complete the bench. What you give up is mostly specified accuracy, calibration traceability, and service; for a hobby bench, a few references (Chapter 38) restore much of the first.
MIS: A cheap instrument's display resolution shows its accuracy. A tinySA shows 0.1 dB steps with ±2 dB accuracy; a NanoVNA shows return loss to 0.01 dB with a floor that makes the second digit meaningless above 30 dB.
BENCH: Calibrate the NanoVNA at the end of the cable you will use, with the adapters in place, and recheck by measuring the load standard afterward: if it reads worse than 40 dB return loss, the calibration has drifted or a connector is worn. Torque SMA connectors gently (about 0.5 to 1 N·m); cheap SMA adapters are consumables.
