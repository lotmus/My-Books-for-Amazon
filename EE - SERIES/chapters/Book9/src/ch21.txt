H1: Chapter 21. The Swept Spectrum Analyzer
P: The spur the customer complained about is not there. The engineer's analyzer, set to a 3 MHz span with automatic settings, shows a clean noise floor at −100 dBm. The customer's analyzer, at a 100 kHz span, shows the spur at −115 dBm, 5 dB above its floor. Same spur, same frequency, different resolution bandwidth: the engineer's floor was 15 dB higher and buried it. A spectrum analyzer shows you what its settings allow, and the noise floor is a setting.

H2: 21.1 Inside the swept analyzer
P: The swept spectrum analyzer is a superheterodyne receiver whose local oscillator sweeps. The input passes an attenuator and a low-pass or preselector filter, a mixer converts each input frequency to a fixed intermediate frequency (IF) as the LO sweeps, the IF filter sets the resolution bandwidth (RBW), a logarithmic detector produces the level, the video filter (VBW) smooths it, and the display draws level against the LO's frequency. Modern analyzers digitize the IF and implement the RBW filters, detectors, and averaging in DSP, and switch to FFT analysis for narrow RBWs (Chapter 22). Examples: Keysight's X-Series (EXA to UXA), R&S's FSV and FSW, Tektronix and Anritsu analyzers, and the entry analyzers of Rigol and Siglent.
H2: 21.2 RBW, noise floor, and sweep time
P: The analyzer's displayed average noise level (DANL) is its own noise in the RBW: DANL ≈ −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10 log(RBW/1 Hz), where NF is the analyzer's noise figure (typically 20 to 25 dB without a preamplifier, 8 to 12 dB with one). Narrowing the RBW by 10 lowers the floor by 10 dB, for a continuous-wave signal whose level does not change. The cost is time: a swept analyzer needs a sweep time of about k × span/RBW², with k between 2 and 3, because the narrow filter needs time to respond. The VBW smooths the noise trace (making it easier to read) but does not lower the average noise; with log averaging the noise displays about 2.5 dB below its true power, so noise measurements use an RMS (power) detector. A signal equal to the noise floor reads 3 dB high, because the analyzer adds their powers.
WE: Worked Example 21.1: RBW against the noise floor
PROB: An analyzer has a noise figure of 24 dB. What is its DANL at RBW 100 kHz, 10 kHz, and 1 kHz? A −115 dBm tone sits in the span. What does each setting display at the tone's frequency, and how long does a 100 MHz span take at 10 kHz and at 1 kHz?
SOL: DANL = −174 + 24 + 10 log(RBW): −100 dBm at 100 kHz, −110 dBm at 10 kHz, −120 dBm at 1 kHz. The tone adds to the noise in power: at 100 kHz, −99.9 dBm (invisible, 0.1 dB of bump); at 10 kHz, −108.8 dBm, 1.2 dB above the floor (barely visible); at 1 kHz, −113.8 dBm, 6.2 dB above the floor, and the reading is still 1.2 dB high. The sweep takes 2 × 100 MHz/(10 kHz)² = 2 s and 2 × 100 MHz/(1 kHz)² = 200 s, which is why analyzers switch to FFT mode for narrow RBWs and why you narrow the span around a known spur.

H2: 21.3 Attenuation, distortion, and dynamic range
P: The input mixer distorts when driven hard: its second- and third-order intermodulation products grow by 2 and 3 dB per dB of input. More input attenuation reduces the mixer level and the distortion, but raises the displayed noise by the same amount. The best dynamic range for third-order products is reached at the mixer level where distortion equals noise: Lopt = (2 TOI + DANL)/3, and the distortion-free range is 2/3 (TOI − DANL), both in the chosen RBW. Analyzers show this trade as an "optimize for dynamic range" setting; the manual way is to change the attenuation by 10 dB: if a displayed spur changes, the analyzer made it.
WE: Worked Example 21.2: Optimum mixer level
PROB: An analyzer has a third-order intercept of +15 dBm at the mixer and a DANL of −120 dBm in a 1 kHz RBW. What is the optimum mixer level and the third-order dynamic range? A two-tone test has tones at −10 dBm. How much attenuation should you add?
SOL: Lopt = (2 × 15 + (−120))/3 = −30 dBm, and the range is 2/3 × (15 + 120) = 90 dB. With −10 dBm tones the mixer is 20 dB above the optimum, so add 20 dB of attenuation (beyond the analyzer's default), then check by changing it by 10 dB: products from the device stay put, products from the analyzer move by 20 dB. At −10 dBm into the mixer, the analyzer's own third-order products would be 2 × (15 − (−10)) = 50 dB below the tones, masking any device better than that.
H2: 21.4 Detectors and markers
P: Each display point covers a frequency interval and a time interval, and the detector decides what to show: peak (the maximum, for CW signals and spurs), sample (one sample, for noise statistics), negative peak, average or RMS (the power, for noise and modulated signals), and quasi-peak (the weighted detector of EMC limits, Chapter 22). Marker functions read frequency and level, noise in dBm/Hz (normalized to 1 Hz with detector corrections), band power, and the frequency of a signal with a counter more accurate than the display grid.

MIS: Narrowing the VBW lowers the noise floor. It smooths the trace but does not change the average noise power; only the RBW (or a preamplifier, or less attenuation) lowers the floor for a CW signal.
BENCH: When a spur appears on the screen, ask the analyzer whether it made it: change the input attenuation by 10 dB. A real signal stays at the same displayed level; a mixer product changes by 20 or 30 dB. Then check the reference: an unlocked analyzer shows spurs that move with temperature.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) An analyzer has NF = 22 dB. What is the DANL at RBW 300 Hz?
PP: 2. (Core) A tone equal to the noise floor reads how many dB above it?
PP: 3. (Core) How long does a 10 MHz span take at RBW 3 kHz with k = 2.5?
PP: 4. (Extension) TOI +20 dBm, DANL −150 dBm/Hz. What is the third-order dynamic range in a 100 Hz RBW?
PP: 5. (Extension) A displayed spur changes by 20 dB when the attenuator changes by 10 dB. What is it?
PP: 6. (Challenge) You must verify that a transmitter's harmonics are 60 dB below a +20 dBm carrier at 2.4 GHz, and the spurious limit of −47 dBm per 100 kHz up to 12.75 GHz. Set up the analyzer: attenuation, filters, RBW, and the checks.
ANS: 1. −174 + 22 + 10 log 300 = −174 + 22 + 24.8 = −127.2 dBm. The usual mistake is to use 10 log of the RBW in kHz.
ANS: 2. 3 dB (power addition of equal powers). The usual mistake is to read the displayed level as the tone's level.
ANS: 3. 2.5 × 10 MHz/(3 kHz)² = 2.78 s. The usual mistake is to scale with 1/RBW instead of 1/RBW².
ANS: 4. DANL in 100 Hz = −150 + 20 = −130 dBm; range = 2/3 × (20 + 130) = 100 dB. The usual mistake is to use the 1 Hz DANL with a 100 Hz measurement.
ANS: 5. A third-order product generated in the analyzer's mixer: a 10 dB lower mixer level lowers it by 30 dB, while the attenuator also raises the display by 10 dB, a net change of 20 dB. The usual mistake is to report it as the device's spur.
ANS: 6. Carrier +20 dBm: use at least 30 dB of attenuation, or better a high-pass or notch filter at 2.4 GHz to remove the carrier for the harmonic search (calibrating the filter's loss at each harmonic), so the analyzer can run with low attenuation and a low floor. RBW 100 kHz for the spurious limit (as the standard specifies), peak detector for the search and RMS where the standard requires it, and a span split into ranges up to 12.75 GHz with a preselector. Checks: the attenuator test for each finding, the reference locked, the cable losses calibrated (Chapter 20). The usual mistake is to look for −40 dBm harmonics with the full +20 dBm carrier into the mixer, measuring the analyzer's own harmonics.
