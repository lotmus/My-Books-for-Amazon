H1: Appendix A. Quick Reference: Formulas, Spec Cheat Sheets, Checklists, and SCPI Basics
H2: A.1 Formulas used in this book
P: Accuracy (Chapter 1): error = ±(% of reading × reading + % of range × range), or ±(% of reading + counts × resolution).
P: Uncertainty (Chapter 2): combine independent standard uncertainties as the root sum of squares; a rectangular limit ±a has u = a/√3; expanded U = k u with k = 2 for about 95 %. Test uncertainty ratio = tolerance/U, 4:1 or better.
P: Loading (Chapter 3): a source Rs read by a meter Rin reads Rin/(Rs + Rin) of the true value; a probe's capacitance has a reactance 1/(2πfC).
P: Remote sense (Chapter 5): the load voltage error without sense is I × (R_lead+ + R_lead−).
P: Diode from the log plot (Chapter 7): n = slope per decade/(Vt ln 10), Vt ln 10 = 59.5 mV at 300 K; Rs = excess voltage/I at high current; Zener rz = ΔV/ΔI.
P: Early voltage (Chapter 7): VA = Ic/slope − VCE at the point used; ro = 1/slope.
P: MOSFET (Chapter 7): subthreshold slope S = ΔVgs per decade, slope factor n = S/(Vt ln 10); gm = ΔId/ΔVgs; Rds(on) = Vds/Id at small Vds, four-wire.
P: Capacitances (Chapter 8): Cgd = Crss, Cgs = Ciss − Crss, Cds = Coss − Crss. Junction: Cj = Cjo/(1 + Vr/Vj)^M.
P: Gate charge and double pulse (Chapter 8): Q = Ig t at constant gate current; first pulse t₁ = L I/V; E = ∫ v i dt; Qrr ≈ ½ Irrm trr for a triangle; Zth = ΔT/P with ΔT = ΔV/K.
P: Integrating converters (Chapter 12): rejection |sin(πfT)/(πfT)| for aperture T; noise ∝ 1/√T; true RMS of a square wave equals its peak, of a triangle peak/√3; average-responding meters read 1.111 × the rectified average.
P: Impedance (Chapter 13): D = ESR/X = 1/Q; Cs = Cp(1 + D²); SRF = 1/(2π√(LC)).
P: Low level (Chapter 14): Johnson noise √(4kTRB), 4.07 nV for 10 kΩ in 0.1 Hz at 300 K; delta method V = (V₊ − V₋)/2; settling to 0.1 % takes 6.9 τ.
P: Counters (Chapter 15): reciprocal resolution = clock period (or interpolator resolution)/gate time; trigger jitter = noise/slew rate.
P: Oscilloscopes (Chapter 16): rise time ≈ 0.35/bandwidth; displayed rise time = √(signal² + scope² + probe²); capture time = memory/sample rate; SINAD = 6.02 ENOB + 1.76 dB.
P: Probes (Chapter 17): ground-lead inductance about 1 nH/mm, ringing at 1/(2π√(LC)); a 10× passive probe loads with 10 MΩ and 10 to 15 pF.
P: TDR (Chapter 18): ρ = (Z − Z0)/(Z + Z0); distance d = v t/2; resolution about v tr/2.
P: Generators (Chapters 19 and 20): DDS f_out = M f_clk/2^N; 0 dBm on 50 Ω = 0.224 V rms; AWG sinc(f/fs) roll-off and images at fs ± f; mismatch uncertainty 20 log(1 ± |Γs||Γl|); φrms = √(2 × 10^(dBc/10)), jitter = φrms/(2πf).
P: Spectrum analyzers (Chapter 21): DANL = −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10 log(RBW); sweep time ≈ 2 to 3 × span/RBW²; optimum mixer level (2 TOI + DANL)/3; dynamic range 2/3 (TOI − DANL).
P: FFT and EMI (Chapter 22): bin = fs/N; RBW = ENBW × fs/N; dBµV = dBm + 107; field E = V + AF + cable loss.
P: Power (Chapter 23): pulse power = average/duty cycle; mismatch 20 log(1 ± |Γs||Γl|).
P: Noise (Chapter 24): F = ENR/(Y − 1) in linear terms; Friis F = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1; L(f) = noise in RBW − carrier − 10 log(RBW); cross-correlation gains 5 log10(N) dB.
P: Network analysis (Chapters 25 to 27): Γ = (Z − Z0)/(Z + Z0); RL = −20 log|Γ|; VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|); group delay = −dφ/dω; time-domain range = 1/Δf, resolution ≈ 1/span; OIP3 = Pout + ΔIM3/2; PAE = (Pout − Pin)/Pdc.
P: Analog modulation (Chapter 28): AM sidebands 20 log(m/2) dBc; FM β = Δf/fm, Carson 2(Δf + fm); carrier nulls at β = 2.405, 5.520, 8.654.
P: Digital modulation (Chapter 29): EVM from noise 1/√SNR, from phase noise φrms in radians, from gain imbalance half the split, combined as a root sum of squares; MER = −20 log(EVM) with average normalization; device EVM = √(measured² − residual²); BER = ½ erfc(Q/√2); occupied bandwidth Rs(1 + α).
P: Transmitter spectrum (Chapter 30): ACLR = P_adjacent/P_main; noise in a channel = N(dBm/Hz) + 10 log(BW); uncorrelated contributions add in power, correlated ones in voltage; mask integration + 10 log(limit BW/RBW); backoff ≥ CCDF value at 0.01 %.
P: OFDM (Chapter 31): subcarrier spacing 15 × 2^µ kHz; symbol and CP scale by 1/2^µ; NR occupied bandwidth = RBs × 12 × spacing; path covered by the CP = c × CP.
P: Chirps and pulses (Chapter 32): LoRa symbol 2^SF/B, bit rate SF × B/2^SF × CR; FMCW beat fb = 2RS/c, resolution c/(2B); duty = τ × PRF; average = peak × duty; lines 20 log(duty) below the peak.
P: Optical, thermal, and EMC (Chapter 33): Δf = cΔλ/λ²; OTDR resolution ≈ cτ/(2n); spot = IFOV × distance, three pixels per target; common-mode radiation E ≈ 1.257e−6 × I f L/d.
P: Automation (Chapters 34 and 36): block header #<n><length>; time per reading = integration + round trip unless buffered; Cpk = min(USL − μ, μ − LSL)/(3σ); GR&R = 6σ_ms/tolerance; UPH = 3600 × sites/(test + handling).
P: Threshold voltage (Chapter 35): by linear extrapolation, Vth = Vgs(gm,max) − Id/gm,max − Vds/2; by constant current, the Vgs at which Id reaches the stated current (often 250 µA or 1 mA) with the drain tied to the gate. Name the method with the number.
P: Temperature soak (Chapter 35): after a step ΔT with time constant τ, the error falls to a band b after t = τ ln(ΔT/b); 300 s × ln(50/0.2) = 1656 s, plus the stability window.
P: Budget bench (Chapters 37 and 38): a VNA proves a match only to about 10 dB short of its S11 floor (|Γ| ± floor); 8-bit SNR 49.9 dB plus 10 log(Nyquist bandwidth/bin); mini tracer current resolution = ADC LSB/R_sense; test uncertainty ratio = tolerance/reference uncertainty.
H2: A.2 Safe limits before every curve-tracer sweep
LI: Voltage below the part's absolute maximum (or the breakdown you intend to find, with the current limited).
LI: Current compliance or series resistance below the maximum continuous current; a low limit for breakdown sweeps (100 µA to 1 mA).
LI: Peak power below what the package survives for the pulse width; pulsed sweeps for anything above about 0.5 W.
LI: Gate drive within the gate's ±V rating; a gate resistor (10 to 100 Ω) at the socket against oscillation.
LI: High-voltage interlock closed; never touch the fixture with the sweep enabled.
H2: A.3 Reading a VI you did not write
LI: Open the front panel first and run the VI with its default values on a simulated or disconnected setup, if the VI allows it.
LI: On the block diagram, follow the error wire from left to right; it gives the real order of the instrument calls.
LI: Use Highlight Execution (the light bulb) to watch the data move, and probes on wires to see values.
LI: Open each subVI's help and connector pane; read its icon text, then its diagram, one level at a time.
LI: Before you change anything, save a copy and commit it to version control with a note of what the VI does today.
H2: A.4 Specification cheat sheets
P: Power supply: ranges and power envelope, programming and readback accuracy, load and line regulation, ripple and noise (rms and peak-to-peak, with bandwidth), transient recovery, remote sense, output impedance; ask for the current-limit behavior.
P: SMU: quadrants and power envelope, lowest current range and its noise, force and measure accuracy per range, settling time, pulse width, guard and triax; ask for the speed at the needed accuracy.
P: DMM: digits and counts, accuracy ±(% of reading + % of range) for each function and period, NMRR at NPLC ≥ 1, input resistance (10 MΩ or above 10 GΩ), true-rms bandwidth and crest factor, CAT rating.
P: LCR meter: frequency range and resolution, test signal level, basic accuracy and its dependence on impedance, frequency, and level, DC bias, fixture compensation.
P: Counter: single-shot resolution (ps), digits per second, timebase aging and temperature stability, input sensitivity and bandwidth, burst and pulse modes.
P: Oscilloscope: bandwidth and rise time, sample rate per channel when all are on, memory, ADC bits and noise floor (µV rms at a stated bandwidth), ENOB, trigger jitter, waveform rate, probe bandwidth and loading.
P: Generators: frequency range and resolution, amplitude accuracy and flatness, harmonics and spurs (dBc), phase noise at the offsets that matter, modulation bandwidth, AWG sample rate and memory, residual EVM for a VSG.
P: Spectrum analyzer: frequency range, DANL (with and without preamplifier), TOI, phase noise, RBW range, amplitude accuracy, analysis bandwidth and real-time POI, EMI detectors.
P: Power meter and sensors: frequency and power range, linearity, zero and drift, calibration factor uncertainty, sensor match (SWR), video bandwidth for peak sensors.
P: VNA: frequency range, dynamic range at a stated IFBW, trace noise, source power range, receiver compression, calibrated directivity and source match for the kit used, number of ports, time-domain option.
P: Signal and modulation analysis: analysis bandwidth, residual EVM for the standard, supported standards and their licenses, capture memory, ACLR dynamic range.
H2: A.5 SCPI basics
P: Syntax: keywords separated by colons, short forms in capitals (MEASure:VOLTage:DC? as MEAS:VOLT:DC?), parameters after a space, queries ending in ?, several commands separated by semicolons; numbers in SI units with an optional suffix (10E-3 or 10 mV where supported).
P: IEEE 488.2 common commands: *IDN? identify; *RST reset; *CLS clear status and errors; *OPC? wait and reply 1; *OPC set the operation-complete bit; *WAI wait before the next command; *ESE and *ESR? event status enable and register; *SRE and *STB? service request enable and status byte; *TST? self-test; *TRG software trigger.
P: Errors: SYST:ERR? returns the oldest error (0,"No error" when empty); −100 to −199 command errors (−113 undefined header), −200 to −299 execution errors (−222 data out of range), −300 to −399 device errors, −400 to −499 query errors (−410 query interrupted).
P: Data: FORM ASC or FORM REAL,32; FORM:BORD NORM or SWAP; binary blocks #<n><length><bytes>; a terminating newline after the block.
P: Triggers and buffers: TRIG:SOUR, TRIG:COUN, SAMP:COUN, INIT, then FETC? for buffered readings; READ? = INIT + FETC?; MEAS? = CONF + READ? with default settings.
H1: Appendix B. Worked Solutions to the Practice Problems
P: Each solution shows the working and, where one is common, the usual mistake. Problems are numbered as in their chapter.
H2: Chapter 1
P: 1. A 3.5-digit meter shows 0 to 1999, so it has 2000 counts. On the 2 V range one count is 2 V/2000 = 1 mV. The usual mistake is to read "3.5 digits" as 3500 counts.
P: 2. Reading term: 0.0035 % × 1 V = 35 µV. Range term: 0.0005 % × 10 V = 50 µV. Error band ±85 µV, or ±0.0085 % of the reading, almost twice the percentage of Worked Example 1.1, because the fixed range term now dominates. The usual mistake is to quote only 0.0035 %.
P: 3. Precise but not accurate: it repeats within 0.1 kg but has a systematic offset of +2.0 kg. Fix it by calibration: zero it with nothing on it, or subtract 2.0 kg from every reading. The usual mistake is to call a repeatable instrument accurate.
P: 4. The standard error is s/√n, so n = (s/2 µV)² = (21.7/2)² = 117.7, which rounds up to 118 readings. Ten times as many readings improve the result by only √10 = 3.2 times. The usual mistake is to expect a ten-fold improvement from ten-fold averaging.
P: 5. On the 2 V range: 0.05 % × 0.0123 V = 6.2 µV plus 3 counts of 100 µV = 300 µV, so ±0.31 mV, which is 2.5 % of the reading. On the 200 mV range, 3 counts are 30 µV, so the band is 6.2 µV + 30 µV = ±36 µV, or 0.3 % of the reading. A small reading on a large range is dominated by the counts term; use the lowest range that holds the signal. The usual mistake is to leave the meter on a high range because autorange "knows best", when a manual or slow autorange may sit on the wrong range.
P: 6. 10 V range: 3.5 µV + 50 µV = 53.5 µV. 1 V range: 4 µV + 7 µV = 11 µV. 100 mV range (0.1 V is the full-scale value, inside the 120 % overrange): 5 µV + 3.5 µV = 8.5 µV. The 100 mV range is best, about six times better than the 10 V range. The usual mistake is to compare only the percent-of-reading terms, which favor the 10 V range, and ignore the range terms, which decide the result.
H2: Chapter 2
P: 1. The half-width is 0.02 % × 2.5000 V = 0.5 mV. With no other information treat it as rectangular: u = 0.5 mV/√3 = 0.289 mV. The usual mistake is to use the half-width itself as the standard uncertainty, which overstates it by √3.
P: 2. u_c = √(0.3² + 0.4² + 1.2²) = √1.69 = 1.3 µV; U = 2 × 1.3 = 2.6 µV. The usual mistake is to add the three linearly (1.9 µV), which overstates the combined uncertainty of independent contributions.
P: 3. A measurement is traceable when its result is linked to the SI units by an unbroken, documented chain of calibrations, each with a stated uncertainty. The usual mistake is to think a calibration sticker or the word "traceable" alone is enough.
P: 4. TUR = 0.5/0.2 = 2.5:1, below the common 4:1. Guard-banded limits: ±(0.5 − 0.2) = ±0.3 %. The usual mistake is to accept the low TUR without guard banding, which ships parts that may be out of tolerance.
P: 5. P = 25/10 = 2.500 W. Relative sensitivities: P depends on V squared, so u_rel from V is 2 × (1 mV/5 V) = 0.04 %; from R it is 2 mΩ/10 Ω = 0.02 %. Combined relative: √(0.04² + 0.02²) = 0.0447 %, so u(P) = 2.5 W × 0.000447 = 1.12 mW. The usual mistake is to forget the factor 2 from the square, which gives 0.028 % and 0.71 mW.
P: 6. Simple acceptance: 1000.8 Ω is inside 999 to 1001 Ω, so it passes. Guard-banded: the limits shrink to 1000 ± 0.4 Ω, and 1000.8 Ω is outside, so it fails. The standard uncertainty is 0.3 Ω; the upper limit is (1001 − 1000.8)/0.3 = 0.67 standard deviations above the reading, and the probability that a normal variable exceeds +0.67 σ is about 25 %. So there is roughly a one-in-four chance the part is out of tolerance. The usual mistake is to believe that "inside the limits" means "in tolerance" when the uncertainty is comparable to the margin.
H2: Chapter 3
P: 1. Error = −R_th/(R_in + R_th) = −100 k/10.1 M = −0.99 %. The usual mistake is to think a 10 MΩ input is "infinite" compared with 100 kΩ; it is only 100 times larger, which costs 1 %.
P: 2. A ground loop: the two instruments' protective earths and the cable shields form a loop in which mains-frequency current flows, and its drop in the shield adds to the signal. Cures: power the circuit or one instrument from a floating source, use a differential input, or isolate the PC link if the loop goes through the computer. The usual mistake, and a dangerous one, is to remove the earth pin from one instrument.
P: 3. 80 dB = 10^(80/20) = 10 000:1. The usual mistake is to divide by 10 instead of 20 and get 10⁸, which treats a voltage ratio as a power ratio.
P: 4. The drop is 100 mA × 1.1 Ω = 0.11 V, so the board sees 3.19 V, and during Wi-Fi transmit bursts of several hundred milliamperes the drop is several times larger and may trip the brownout detector. Measure current with a lower-burden method: a small shunt and a differential measurement, a current probe, or a dedicated low-burden current meter. The usual mistake is to blame the board's firmware for resets the meter caused.
P: 5. The Thevenin source is 5 V behind 5 MΩ, so the meter reads 5 × 10/(10 + 5) = 3.333 V, a third low. Use the meter's high-impedance mode (above 10 GΩ) on a range that has it, an electrometer, or a unity-gain buffer with a low-bias-current op-amp. The usual mistake is to blame the resistors' tolerance for a 33 % error.
P: 6. V_th = V_read × (1 + R_th/R_in) = 4.762 × 1.05 = 5.000 V. The correction is 5 % of the reading; a 1 % uncertainty in R_th changes the correction by 1 % of 5 %, that is 0.05 % of the reading (about 2.5 mV), plus a similar share from the tolerance of the meter's 10 MΩ, typically also about 1 %. The usual mistake is to apply the correction with full confidence and forget that it inherits the tolerances of the two resistances.
H2: Chapter 4
P: 1. Appliance at an outlet: CAT II. Breaker panel: CAT III. Utility meter and service entrance: CAT IV. ESP32-S3 on an 18650 cell: not mains, outside the categories (CAT I in older editions, O in the current one). The usual mistake is to rate a measurement by its voltage alone instead of its location.
P: 2. E = ½ × 100 µF × 400² = 8 J. The usual mistake is to forget the ½, giving 16 J, or to use V instead of V², giving 0.02 J and a false sense of safety.
P: 3. CAT IV 600 V. The service entrance is a CAT IV location, and a CAT III rating, whatever its voltage, does not cover it; 240 V is well within 600 V. The usual mistake is to pick the higher voltage number.
P: 4. τ = 10 kΩ × 470 µF = 4.7 s; t = 4.7 s × ln(400/50) = 4.7 × 2.079 = 9.77 s. The usual mistake is to wait one or two time constants, which leaves 147 V or 54 V on the capacitor.
P: 5. I = 3.7 V/(40 mΩ + 10 mΩ) = 74 A; power in the wiring 74² × 0.01 Ω = 55 W, and 219 W inside the cell, enough to melt insulation and start a thermal runaway. A protected cell or module disconnects within milliseconds. The usual mistake is to think a 3.7 V source is harmless because it cannot shock you.
P: 6. 40 dB is a factor of 100, so 400 V of common-mode step leaks through as up to 4 V of apparent signal, more than a quarter of the 15 V gate drive, exactly during the switching edge you want to see. Use an optically isolated probe or an isolated-channel scope with high CMRR at high frequency, and keep its capacitance to earth low (Worked Example 4.2). The usual mistake is to trust the probe's low-frequency CMRR from the first line of its datasheet.
H2: Chapter 5
P: 1. At 5 V the 4 Ω load would draw 1.25 A, above the 0.5 A limit, so the supply runs in CC: 0.5 A and 0.5 × 4 = 2 V. The usual mistake is to assume the set voltage appears and calculate 1.25 A.
P: 2. 24 V/2 A = 12 Ω. Loads below 12 Ω run the supply in CC. The usual mistake is to invert the ratio.
P: 3. The current limit caps the output current; it protects the circuit from drawing too much. OVP shuts the output down when the terminal voltage exceeds a threshold; it protects against a wrong setting, a failure of the supply's regulator, or an external source back-driving the output. Only OVP covers a failure inside the supply. The usual mistake is to rely on the current limit alone.
P: 4. The drop is 3 A × 0.05 Ω = 0.15 V, so the load sees 1.05 V, 12.5 % low, far outside the usual ±5 % rail tolerance. The usual mistake is to ignore lead drop because it is "only milliohms".
P: 5. Dissipation (20 − 3.3) × 2 = 33.4 W; delivered 6.6 W; efficiency 6.6/40 = 16.5 %. The usual mistake is to compute efficiency from the voltages alone without noticing that the input current equals the output current in a linear regulator, which is the same answer here, and then to apply the same shortcut to a switching supply, where it is wrong.
P: 6. With a sense lead open, the regulation loop loses its feedback from that side. Most supplies have internal resistors from each sense terminal to its output terminal, so the output falls back to local regulation and the load sees the set value minus the lead drop; without them, or with a sense lead shorted to the wrong point, the output can rise toward its maximum. OVP is the protection: set it a little above the highest terminal voltage you expect with compensation (set value plus both lead drops), not at the rail value, which would trip during normal compensation. The usual mistake is to set OVP before thinking about the compensation, then disable it after it trips.
H2: Chapter 6
P: 1. Positive voltage, current flowing into the SMU: quadrant 4, a sink quadrant. The usual mistake is to think an instrument can only deliver power.
P: 2. At 5 V the resistor would draw 50 mA, above the 10 mA compliance, so the SMU limits at 10 mA, and the voltage is 10 mA × 100 Ω = 1 V; it reports compliance. The usual mistake is to report 5 V, the programmed value.
P: 3. The guard is the inner shield, driven at the same potential as the center conductor, so no current flows across the cable insulation from the signal to the guard; leakage goes from guard to outer shield instead and is not measured. The cable capacitance also does not need to charge through the measurement. The usual mistake is to use ordinary coax for picoamperes and measure the cable.
P: 4. 0.060 % × 50 nA = 30 pA plus 100 pA = 130 pA, ±0.26 % of the reading. The usual mistake is to use the 1 µA range because autorange chose it, which gives 300 pA, more than twice the error.
P: 5. Seven decades × 10 + 1 = 71 points. The usual mistake is to forget the endpoint (70) or to count decades wrong (1 nA to 10 mA is 10⁷).
P: 6. End of pulse: 10 W × 1 K/W = 10 K. Average power 10 W × 200 µs/100 ms = 20 mW, × 40 K/W = 0.8 K. DC: 10 W × 40 K/W = 400 K, which no silicon device survives. The usual mistake is to multiply the average power by the transient impedance or the peak power by the steady-state resistance.
H2: Chapter 7
P: 1. n = 70 mV/(Vt ln 10) = 70/59.5 = 1.18. The usual mistake is to divide by 26 mV, forgetting the ln 10 of a per-decade slope, which gives 2.7.
P: 2. rz = (6.29 − 6.20) V/(8 − 2) mA = 0.09/0.006 = 15 Ω. The usual mistake is to divide the voltage by one of the currents (V/I), which gives the static resistance of about 3 kΩ.
P: 3. At breakdown the current rises steeply with voltage, and the voltage across the junction is high, so even a few milliamperes dissipate a lot of power in a small area; an unlimited sweep destroys the junction in microseconds. A 100 µA to 1 mA limit finds the breakdown voltage without damage. The usual mistake is to sweep breakdown with the forward-test compliance.
P: 4. Slope (11.4 − 10.6) mA/10 V = 80 µS, ro = 12.5 kΩ. Extrapolating from 2 V: 10.6 mA/80 µS = 132.5 V, so the line meets zero at 2 − 132.5 = −130.5 V, VA ≈ 130 V. The usual mistake is to compute VA as ro × Ic at one point without the voltage offset, which gives 132.5 V; close here, but not in general.
P: 5. 8 + 2 × 15 = 38 mΩ, a 375 % error, almost five times the true value. The usual mistake is to believe a short lead is "zero ohms"; at milliohms only Kelvin connections work.
P: 6. Not necessarily. The datasheet's limit applies to its own method, the constant-current method at 250 µA, which for this kind of device typically reads lower than the √Id extrapolation (Worked Example 7.5 shows 0.14 V). Measure at 250 µA with drain tied to gate before you reject it. The usual mistake is to compare thresholds from different methods.
P: 7. Self-heating (mobility falls with temperature at high gate drive) or an oscillation or measurement artifact (a gate oscillating at high current, or a sense lead drop that grows with current). Repeat the sweep pulsed with short pulses at low duty: if the pulsed curve stays flat, it was heating; if it still falls, check the gate with a resistor at the socket and the Kelvin connections. The usual mistake is to report the drooping curve as the device's output characteristic.
P: 8. Sweep VCE from 0 to about 20 V (half the rating, enough to see the active region). Base steps of 100 µA give Ic steps of about 20 mA at hFE 200, so five steps reach about 100 mA. Compliance or series resistance limiting Ic to about 150 mA, below the 200 mA rating. Power: 20 V × 100 mA = 2 W, three times the 625 mW rating, so pulse: 300 µs pulses at 1 % duty keep the average at 20 mW, or limit the DC sweep to VCE × Ic below about 0.3 W (for example only to 3 V at 100 mA). The usual mistake is to set the voltage and current limits from the ratings and forget that their product exceeds the power rating.
H2: Chapter 8
P: 1. Cgd = Crss = 60 pF; Cgs = 2400 − 60 = 2340 pF; Cds = 380 − 60 = 320 pF. The usual mistake is to take Coss as Cds and forget that it includes Cgd.
P: 2. Qg = 2 mA × 25 µs = 50 nC. The usual mistake is to integrate the voltage instead of using Q = I t at constant current.
P: 3. t₁ = L I/V = 200 µH × 30 A/600 V = 10 µs. The usual mistake is to use V/L × I, which has the wrong units.
P: 4. Qrr ≈ ½ × 12 A × 80 ns = 480 nC; Tt ≈ 480 nC/30 A = 16 ns, as a starting value only, valid near this di/dt. The usual mistake is to forget the ½ for the triangle (960 nC).
P: 5. ΔT = 330 mV/2.2 mV/K = 150 K; Zth = 150 K/25 W = 6 K/W at that pulse length. The usual mistake is to drop the sign of the K-factor and get a temperature fall.
P: 6. C = 10 pF/(1 + 6.3/0.7)^0.5 = 10/√10 = 3.16 pF. The usual mistake is to forget the 1 + and compute (9)^0.5 = 3, giving 3.33 pF.
P: 7. 2 ns caused 13.3 %, so 1 % allows about 2 ns × 1/13.3 = 150 ps. The usual mistake is to think the scope's sample interval sets the limit; deskew can be set much finer than one sample with interpolation, and it must be.
P: 8. Over which range was the fit made, and what are the residuals in the subthreshold part (a fit dominated by the many points above threshold can bend the subthreshold slope to reduce the error there)? Was the model validated against measured data at the currents the sensor bias will use? At what temperature and drain voltage was it measured, and does the circuit operate there? The usual mistake is to read a fitted parameter as a physical property of the device, outside the range where it was fitted.
H2: Chapter 9
P: 1. 12 V × 3 A = 36 W. The usual mistake is to forget that all of it becomes heat in the load, which must be within its power rating.
P: 2. I = 12 V/4 Ω = 3 A. The usual mistake is to confuse CR with CC and set 4 A.
P: 3. CP draws I = P/V, so at the low voltage during start-up it demands a current far above the converter's limit, and the converter stalls in current limit. The usual mistake is to blame the converter's soft-start.
P: 4. (3 − 1) A/(0.25 A/µs) = 8 µs. The usual mistake is to divide the final current by the slew rate (12 µs).
P: 5. At 12 V the power limit sets 150/12 = 12.5 A. At 3.3 V the current limit sets 30 A (99 W). The usual mistake is to take 30 A at any voltage, which at 12 V would be 360 W.
P: 6. Dip 2 A × 80 mΩ = 0.16 V, to 3.54 V. A bench supply has milliohms of output resistance, so it would show almost no dip and hide a brownout; its control loop also undershoots and overshoots on the step in a way no battery does. The simulator's programmed resistance reproduces the cell. The usual mistake is to validate battery operation on a bench supply.
H2: Chapter 10
P: 1. 8 × 16 = 128 crosspoints. The usual mistake is to add rows and columns (24).
P: 2. A moment in which the old and the new path are both closed, which would connect two instruments (or an instrument and a source) together, possibly damaging an input or disturbing the device. The usual mistake is to assume the switch driver always does it in a custom sequence.
P: 3. Switching under current draws an arc that erodes the contacts and shortens their life, and discharging a capacitor through reed contacts can weld them. Turning the source off first costs milliseconds and saves relays. The usual mistake is to optimize test time by hot switching.

The usual mistake is to report the analyzer's own residual.
