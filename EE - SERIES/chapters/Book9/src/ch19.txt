PART: Part V. Making Signals
PINTRO: To measure a circuit you often have to feed it first: a clock, a test tone, a pulse, a modulated carrier. Chapter 19 covers function, arbitrary waveform, and pulse generators, from the direct digital synthesis inside them to the 50 Ω output that confuses everyone once. Chapter 20 climbs to RF: analog signal generators with their synthesizers, level control, and phase noise, and vector signal generators that make any modulation from I and Q. Modulation theory is Book 5's; here the question is what the generator really puts out.
H1: Chapter 19. Function, Arbitrary Waveform, and Pulse Generators
P: The generator says 1.00 Vpp. The scope says 2.00 Vpp. Every new lab member reports this as a fault, and every generator manual explains it on page 30: the generator has a 50 Ω output and assumes a 50 Ω load, which halves the open-circuit voltage. Into the scope's 1 MΩ input there is no halving. One menu setting, "load: high Z," makes the display honest.
MEET: Clock and stimulus signals for boards, filter and amplifier response tests, sensor simulation, power-supply load steps, digital patterns, laser and LED pulsing, and the test tones of audio and ultrasound work.
H2: 19.1 Direct digital synthesis
P: A modern function generator creates its waveforms digitally. In direct digital synthesis (DDS), a phase accumulator of N bits adds a tuning word M every clock cycle; the top bits address a sine (or other) table, and a DAC with a reconstruction filter produces the output, at f_out = M f_clk/2^N. The frequency resolution is f_clk/2^N, microhertz for 48-bit accumulators. The cost of the elegance is spurs: truncating the phase and the DAC's own nonlinearity create spurious tones, typically 50 to 70 dB below the carrier in bench generators. Examples run from entry generators by Rigol, Siglent, and Owon to Keysight's 33500B and Tektronix's AFG31000 series.
WE: Worked Example 19.1: A DDS tuning word
PROB: A DDS has a 32-bit accumulator clocked at 125 MHz. What is its frequency resolution, what tuning word gives 1 kHz, and what frequency does it really produce?
SOL: Resolution = 125 MHz/2³² = 0.0291 Hz. M = 1000 × 2³²/125 MHz = 34 359.74, rounded to 34 360, which gives 34 360 × 0.0291 Hz = 1000.0076 Hz, 7.6 ppm high. The generator's timebase adds its own error (often ±1 to ±20 ppm); for exact frequencies lock it to the lab's 10 MHz reference (Chapter 15).
H2: 19.2 The 50 Ω output
P: Bench generators have a 50 Ω source impedance so that they can drive coaxial lines without reflections. The displayed amplitude assumes a matched 50 Ω load, which forms a divider: the open-circuit voltage is twice the display. Into a high-impedance input (a scope at 1 MΩ, a CMOS input), the voltage doubles. Either set the generator's load setting to high Z, which makes the display show the open-circuit voltage, or terminate the cable with 50 Ω at the far end, which also stops reflections on long cables with fast edges.
WE: Worked Example 19.2: Volts, dBm, and the load
PROB: A generator is set to 0 dBm into 50 Ω. What is the RMS voltage at a 50 Ω load, the peak-to-peak value, and what does a 1 MΩ scope show? What setting gives 1 Vpp at a CMOS input with the load set to 50 Ω?
SOL: 0 dBm = 1 mW: V = √(P R) = √(10⁻³ × 50) = 0.224 V rms, 0.632 Vpp at a 50 Ω load. Into 1 MΩ the scope shows 1.265 Vpp. With the load set to 50 Ω, a display of 0.5 Vpp gives 1 Vpp at the CMOS input; with the load set to high Z, a display of 1 Vpp does.
H2: 19.3 Arbitrary waveforms and their limits
P: An arbitrary waveform generator (AWG) plays a list of samples from memory at a sample clock rate. Its limits: the sample rate (the output can contain frequencies up to about 40 % of it before the images get close), the memory depth (a long, detailed waveform needs many points), vertical resolution (14 to 16 bits in bench AWGs), and the DAC's zero-order hold, which shapes the spectrum by sinc(f/fs) and creates images at fs ± f. True arbitrary (point-by-point) generators play every sample at the clock; DDS-based arbitrary modes play a table with a phase accumulator and may skip or repeat points, which smears narrow pulses. Sequencing chains segments with loops and conditional jumps, so a long test pattern needs little memory.
WE: Worked Example 19.3: Sinc roll-off and images
PROB: An AWG samples at 1 GS/s and plays a 400 MHz tone. How much lower is it than a 100 MHz tone of the same digital amplitude, and where is the first image?
SOL: The zero-order hold's response is sinc(f/fs) = sin(πf/fs)/(πf/fs). At 100 MHz: 0.984, −0.14 dB. At 400 MHz: 0.757, −2.42 dB, so 2.28 dB less than the 100 MHz tone unless the generator pre-compensates. The first image is at fs − f = 600 MHz, attenuated by the same sinc envelope (sinc(0.6) = 0.505, −5.9 dB) and by the reconstruction filter; at 400 MHz the filter's transition band is only 200 MHz wide, which is why 40 % of fs is the practical limit.

H2: 19.4 Pulse generators
P: Pulse generators make fast, clean, precisely timed pulses: rise times from about 1 ns to below 100 ps, independently variable edges, delays with picosecond resolution, low jitter, and double pulses (the double-pulse test of Chapter 8 can use one for the gate). Function generators make pulses too, with edges of a few nanoseconds and adequate timing for most digital work. For laser diodes and avalanche testing, high-current pulsers deliver amperes in nanoseconds into low impedances.

MIS: The generator's display is the voltage at the circuit. It is the voltage into the load the generator was told about, 50 Ω by default; into a high impedance the voltage doubles.
BENCH: When a generator's square wave rings on the scope, check the cable before the generator: a 1 m unterminated coaxial cable with 5 ns edges reflects. A 50 Ω feed-through terminator at the scope input (with the generator's load set to 50 Ω) fixes both the ringing and the amplitude.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) A generator's load setting is 50 Ω and the display reads 2 Vpp. What appears at a 1 MΩ input?
PP: 2. (Core) What is +10 dBm in volts RMS and peak-to-peak into 50 Ω?
PP: 3. (Core) A 48-bit DDS runs at 250 MHz. What is its frequency resolution?
PP: 4. (Extension) An AWG at 2.5 GS/s: what is the sinc loss at 1 GHz?
PP: 5. (Extension) A 10 ms waveform needs 1 ns timing detail. How many points does it need without sequencing?
PP: 6. (Challenge) You need to simulate an automotive crank-pulse supply profile (12 V dropping to 4.5 V for 15 ms, 6.5 V for 1 s, recovery over 100 ms) into a 2 A ECU load. Which instruments do you need, and how do you program the profile?
ANS: 1. The open-circuit voltage is twice the display: 4 Vpp. The usual mistake is to report a fault in the generator.
ANS: 2. P = 10 mW: V = √(0.01 × 50) = 0.707 V rms, 2.0 Vpp. The usual mistake is to compute with 1 W.
ANS: 3. 250 MHz/2⁴⁸ = 0.89 µHz. The usual mistake is to think the resolution equals the frequency accuracy, which the timebase sets.
ANS: 4. f/fs = 0.4: sinc(0.4) = 0.757, −2.42 dB. The usual mistake is to forget that the roll-off depends on f/fs, not on f alone.
ANS: 5. 10 ms/1 ns = 10 million points. The usual mistake is to forget that sequencing (a short segment repeated) can reduce this when the waveform is mostly flat.
ANS: 6. A generator alone cannot deliver 2 A; use a programmable power supply with a list or arbitrary mode fast enough for the edges, or a generator driving a power amplifier (a bipolar power supply or a four-quadrant amplifier). Program the profile as a list: 12 V, a ramp down to 4.5 V in about 1 ms, 4.5 V for 15 ms, 6.5 V for 1 s, a 100 ms ramp back to 12 V; verify with a scope and a current probe at the ECU terminals, using remote sense (Chapter 5). The usual mistake is to drive the ECU from a function generator's 50 Ω output.
