H1: Chapter 32. Bursts, Chirps, and Pulses: BLE, LoRa, FMCW Radar, and Pulsed RF
P: The radar module sees one target as three. The antenna is fine, the FFT is fine, the target is a single corner reflector at 10 m. A VSA captures one 40 µs ramp and plots its frequency against time minus the ideal straight line: a slow bow and a ripple of a few megahertz on a 4 GHz sweep, 0.06 % rms. Small, but it smears the beat frequency over eight FFT bins, and the range resolution of 3.75 cm becomes 30 cm with sidelobes. The radar is only as linear as its chirp.
MEET: Bluetooth Low Energy product test, LoRa and other IoT radios, automotive and industrial FMCW radar, pulsed radar and avionics transponders, medical and industrial pulsed RF, and anything that transmits in bursts.
H2: 32.1 Bursts and time-gated analysis
P: Most modern radios transmit in bursts. A swept analyzer that ignores the timing measures a mixture of on and off; the right tools are a trigger on the RF envelope (IF power trigger) or the device's own signal, a time gate that measures only during the burst's stable part (Chapter 21), and a VSA capture long enough to contain whole bursts. Power against time shows ramp-up, overshoot, droop, and ramp-down, each with limits in most standards.
H2: 32.2 Bluetooth Low Energy
P: BLE's 1 Msym/s and 2 Msym/s modes use GFSK with a modulation index of 0.45 to 0.55, a frequency deviation near ±250 kHz. The RF-PHY test specification defines the transmitter tests: output power, modulation characteristics with two test patterns (Δf1avg from the 00001111 pattern must lie between 225 and 275 kHz; Δf2max from the 10101010 pattern must exceed 185 kHz for 99.9 % of the symbols; Δf2avg/Δf1avg at least 0.8), carrier frequency offset (±150 kHz), and drift within a packet. Most VSAs and dedicated Bluetooth testers run these with one button; a VSA in FSK mode shows the frequency against time and the eye, which explain a failure. Check the numbers against the RF-PHY test specification version your product is qualified to.
H2: 32.3 LoRa and chirp spread spectrum
P: LoRa modulates the starting frequency of linear chirps that sweep the channel bandwidth B (125, 250, or 500 kHz). The spreading factor SF sets the symbol time 2^SF/B and the bit rate SF × B/2^SF × CR, where CR is the coding rate (4/5 to 4/8). A spectrogram (short-time FFT, which every VSA and real-time analyzer offers) shows the chirps as diagonal lines: preamble upchirps, two downchirps for synchronization, then data chirps with jumps. Its time and frequency resolution trade against each other: a window of length T resolves about 1/T in frequency.
H2: 32.4 FMCW radar chirps
P: An FMCW radar transmits a ramp of bandwidth B in time T (slope S = B/T) and mixes the echo with the transmitted signal; a target at range R produces a beat at fb = 2RS/c, and the FFT of the beat over one ramp resolves range to about c/(2B). Any departure of the ramp from a straight line turns the beat into a chirp itself and smears the peak. The measurement is a wideband I/Q capture (a VSA with enough analysis bandwidth, or a downconverter and a fast scope), instantaneous frequency from the phase derivative, a straight-line fit over a stated window that excludes the turnaround, and the residual: rms and peak frequency error, and the local slope variation.

WE: Worked Example 32.1: Chirp linearity and range
PROB: The ramp of Figure 32.1 sweeps 4 GHz in 40 µs. Find the slope, the beat frequency of a target at 10 m, and the ideal range resolution. The listing finds that the local slope varies by 2.9 % peak to peak over the fit window. How far does the beat spread, compared with the FFT bin?
SOL: S = 4 GHz/40 µs = 100 MHz/µs = 1e14 Hz/s. fb = 2 × 10 m × 1e14/3e8 = 6.67 MHz. Resolution c/(2B) = 3e8/8e9 = 3.75 cm. The beat follows the local slope, so it varies by 2.9 % of 6.67 MHz = 194 kHz, against an FFT bin of 1/40 µs = 25 kHz: the peak smears over about eight bins and the resolution becomes about eight times worse. The fit was made over 2 to 38 µs only; it says nothing about the turnaround, and it describes this module at this temperature: repeat at the temperature extremes before the linearization tables are frozen.

H2: 32.5 Pulsed RF
P: Pulsed signals are described by pulse width τ, pulse repetition interval (PRI, the inverse of the PRF), duty cycle τ × PRF, rise and fall times, droop, overshoot, and intrapulse phase or frequency modulation. A peak power sensor or a VSA's pulse analysis measures them directly (Chapter 23). A spectrum analyzer sees a sinc envelope with nulls at multiples of 1/τ around the carrier; with RBW below the PRF it resolves the spectral lines, each 20 log(duty cycle) below the peak power (line spectrum); with RBW well above the PRF it shows the envelope, at a level that depends on the RBW (pulse desensitization). Without knowing which mode applies, the displayed level is not the pulse power.
WE: Worked Example 32.2: Pulse power and the spectrum
PROB: A radar transmits 1 µs pulses at 1 kHz PRF with 1 kW peak power. Find the duty cycle, the average power, and the level of the central spectral line on an analyzer with RBW 100 Hz. Where are the first nulls of the envelope?
SOL: Duty cycle 1 µs × 1 kHz = 0.001; average 1 kW × 0.001 = 1 W (30 dB below the peak). With RBW 100 Hz < PRF, the analyzer resolves lines 1 kHz apart; the central line is 20 log(0.001) = −60 dB below the peak power: 0 dBm at the line, against 60 dBm peak. Envelope nulls at ±1/τ = ±1 MHz. A thermal power sensor reads the 1 W average; only a peak sensor or the duty-cycle correction gives the 1 kW.
MIS: The spectrum analyzer's marker on a pulsed signal reads the pulse power. It reads a line (20 log duty below the peak) or an RBW-dependent envelope level, depending on the RBW; use a peak power sensor or the VSA's pulse measurement, or apply the correct desensitization.
BENCH: For any bursty or pulsed device, put a 30 to 40 dB attenuator rated for the peak power, not the average, between it and the instrument. A 1 kW pulse at 0.1 % duty is 1 W average and still destroys a 1 W-rated attenuator's input element if its peak rating is lower.

PP: 6. (Challenge) Plan the characterization of a pulsed 9 GHz radar transmitter: which instruments, what you measure with each, and the protections.
ANS: 1. 2^12/125 kHz = 32.8 ms; 12 × 125e3/4096 × 0.8 = 293 bit/s. The usual mistake is to forget the coding rate.
ANS: 2. S = 1 GHz/50 µs = 2e13 Hz/s; fb = 2 × 30 × 2e13/3e8 = 4.0 MHz; resolution 3e8/(2 × 1e9) = 15 cm. The usual mistake is to drop the factor 2 for the round trip.
ANS: 3. Duty 2 µs × 10 kHz = 0.02; average 2 W; line 20 log(0.02) = −34 dB below peak. The usual mistake is to use 10 log(duty) (−17 dB) for the line, which is the average power, not a line.
ANS: 4. 214/262 = 0.817, at least 0.8: pass (and Δf1avg is inside 225 to 275 kHz). The usual mistake is to invert the ratio.
ANS: 5. About 1/10 kHz = 100 µs window; the time resolution is then about 100 µs too. The usual mistake is to expect both fine time and fine frequency from one window.
ANS: 6. A peak power sensor and meter for peak and average power, pulse width, rise and fall time, droop, and PRI; a signal analyzer for the spectrum (envelope nulls, spurious, and harmonics, with a waveguide or 18 GHz front end and preselection); a VSA with enough analysis bandwidth for intrapulse frequency and phase, pulse-to-pulse stability; a frequency counter with burst mode for the carrier frequency. Protections: a high-power attenuator or a directional coupler rated for the peak power, followed by further attenuation; compute the peak level at each instrument; radiation safety for the open waveguide. The usual mistake is to size the attenuator for the average power.
