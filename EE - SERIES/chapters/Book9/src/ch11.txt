H1: Chapter 11. Instrument Architectures: GPIB, USB, LXI, and PXI
P: The test takes 41 seconds per board and the line needs 20. The measurements themselves take 9 seconds. The rest is the computer asking each instrument a question, waiting for the answer, and asking the next, two thousand times per board, and moving one long waveform record over a bus that was designed in 1975. The test is not slow because the instruments are slow; it is slow because of how they are connected and how they are asked.
P: Where you meet this: Automated test racks, production lines, characterization benches with several instruments, and modular test systems in semiconductor and RF test.
H2: 11.1 GPIB
P: GPIB (IEEE 488, first HP-IB) is a parallel bus with 8 data lines and handshake lines, up to 15 devices on one bus (the controller included), and cables up to about 20 m in total. Its classic data rate is about 1 MB/s; a later high-speed extension raised it to about 8 MB/s. It is old, robust, deterministic, and still everywhere, because instruments last decades and test programs longer. It also gave the world IEEE 488.2, the common commands (*IDN?, *RST, *OPC?) and the status-reporting model that every modern instrument still uses over every other bus (Chapter 34). Its continued presence is less a triumph of fashion than evidence that working test programs are difficult to retire.
H2: 11.2 USB, LAN, and LXI
P: USB instruments use the USB Test and Measurement Class (USBTMC), which carries the same SCPI messages. USB is plug-and-play and fast enough for most benches, but limited in cable length and sensitive to ground loops through the PC. LAN instruments speak SCPI over VXI-11, over HiSLIP (a newer protocol that behaves like GPIB on a network, with service requests and locking), or over a raw TCP socket, conventionally port 5025. LXI is the standard for LAN instruments: a web interface, discovery, a defined configuration, and, in its extended functions, IEEE 1588 time synchronization and LAN trigger messages. A VISA library hides these transports behind one interface, so a script addresses an instrument by a resource string such as TCPIP0::192.168.1.20::hislip0::INSTR or USB0::0x2A8D::0x1301::MY12345678::INSTR.
H2: 11.3 PXI and modular instruments
P: PXI (PCI eXtensions for Instrumentation) puts instruments on cards in a chassis with a PCI Express backplane and an embedded controller or a link to a PC. Its additions to PCI are what instruments need: a shared 10 MHz reference clock, trigger lines across the slots, and a star trigger with matched delays to each slot. Modules have no front panel; software is their only interface. The benefits are bandwidth (a PCIe link, gigabytes per second), tight synchronization, and density; the costs are software dependency and a chassis to buy. AXIe is a larger modular format for high-end instruments. Production test in RF and semiconductor companies uses PXI heavily (NI, Keysight, and others make modules), and benches use box instruments.
H2: Worked Example 11.1: Moving a 10 MB waveform
P: Problem. A scope record of 10 million 8-bit samples is to be transferred to the PC. Estimate the time over GPIB at 1 MB/s, USB 2.0 at a practical 30 MB/s, Gigabit Ethernet at a practical 100 MB/s, and a PCIe Gen 3 ×8 link (about 7.9 GB/s).
P: Solution. 10 MB/1 MB/s = 10 s over GPIB; 10/30 = 0.33 s over USB; 10/100 = 0.1 s over LAN; 10 MB/7.9 GB/s = 1.3 ms over PCIe. The practical USB and LAN rates depend on the instrument's own processing and are often lower; the factor of 7500 between GPIB and PCIe is the reason long waveforms are analyzed inside the instrument, or transferred from a modular one. When you need only a measurement, query the measurement and not the waveform.

H2: 11.4 Latency, not bandwidth, sets most test times
P: Most test steps exchange short messages: set, trigger, query. Each round trip costs the bus latency plus the instrument's parsing and settling, often around a millisecond, and a test with thousands of round trips spends seconds in them. The cures are structural: program the instrument once and let it run a list or sweep from its own memory (list mode, sequence mode, sweep tables), trigger it in hardware rather than by command, read back results in one block at the end, and run independent instruments in parallel. The 41-second test of the hook usually falls by half from these changes, without new hardware.
H2: Worked Example 11.2: Round trips versus list mode
P: Problem. A board test makes 200 measurements. Done one by one, each needs 5 command round trips of 1 ms plus 10 ms of measurement. In list mode the instrument is programmed once (5 ms) and measures all 200 points with 10 ms each, then returns them in one read (5 ms). Compare the times.
P: Solution. One by one: 200 × (5 × 1 ms + 10 ms) = 3.0 s. List mode: 5 ms + 200 × 10 ms + 5 ms = 2.01 s. The 1 s saved is the 1000 round trips. If the measurement itself could be shortened (Chapter 12), list mode would gain proportionally more.
P: On the Job
P: A test engineer at an electronics manufacturer builds and maintains the functional test rack at the end of the line: a PXI or LXI chassis with a DMM, a power supply, an electronic load, a switch matrix to the fixture's bed of nails, and a programmable supply for the device, run by a TestStand or Python sequence (Chapters 34 to 36). The typical task: the line is moving from 600 to 1000 boards a day and the test must drop from 41 s to under 25 s. The engineer profiles the sequence, finds that most of the time is spent in round trips and in a 1 s settling delay copied from an old program, moves the DMM to list mode, triggers the load from the DMM in hardware, and verifies with a gauge study (Chapter 36) that the faster test still measures the same.
P: Common misconception. A faster bus makes every test faster. A faster bus helps transfers of large records. A test dominated by many short exchanges is limited by latency and by the instrument's own response time, and moving from USB to PCIe may change it very little.
BENCH: Before you buy faster hardware, time the test. Put a timestamp before and after every instrument call in the sequence, sort the steps by total time, and look at the top five. It is usually a fixed delay, a waveform transfer of which only one number is used, or a query loop that could be a trigger.
