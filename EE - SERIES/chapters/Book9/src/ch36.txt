H1: Chapter 36. Test Sequencing, Production Test, and Measurement Data
P: The line reports 99.4 % yield on the power board, and the field return rate is ten times the target. The test data tells the story in five minutes, once somebody looks at it: the efficiency step failed 3 % of boards on the first try, and the operators had learned that a second, third, or fourth run usually passed. The sequence allowed unlimited retests. The boards that passed on the fourth try were the ones coming back. A test station is a measurement system with a policy, and both need engineering.
P: Where you meet this: Electronics manufacturing (EMS and OEM lines), semiconductor final test, automotive and medical production with traceability requirements, characterization labs that run thousands of devices, and any team that has to answer "how many passed, and why did the others fail?"
H2: 36.1 What a test executive does
P: Whether it is TestStand (Chapter 35), an open-source Python framework such as OpenHTF or pytest-based sequencers, or a home-grown program, a test executive does the same jobs: identify the unit (serial number, barcode), run steps in order with setup and cleanup, compare each result with limits, decide on retests and on stopping at the first failure, log every result with its limits and context, and produce a report and a pass/fail decision for the handler or operator. Separate the sequence (what to test, in which order) from the step code (how to talk to the instruments) and from the limits (a versioned file or database table, not constants in the code), so each can change under its own review.
H2: 36.2 Limits, guard bands, and retest policy
P: Test limits are the specification limits moved inward by the guard band from the measurement uncertainty (Chapter 2), so that a pass is a pass with the required confidence; the price is that some good units near the limits fail (false rejects). Retests are legitimate for problems of the test, not of the unit: a bad contact, a missed trigger, a settling timeout. Allow one retest, record both results, and count the first-pass yield (FPY) as well as the final yield. A large gap between them is a fixture or test problem to fix, not a yield to celebrate. Units that fail and then pass deserve a look: they are often marginal.
H2: 36.3 Production statistics: Cpk, Pareto, and gauge capability
P: The process capability index Cpk = min(USL − mean, mean − LSL)/(3σ) says how well the distribution fits inside the specification: 1.33 is a common minimum, 1.67 for critical parameters; below 1, a visible fraction fails. Compute it from the readings that represent the unit, not from contact failures, which would wreck the statistics of a good process. A Pareto chart of failures by step shows where to work first. The measurement system itself is qualified with a gauge repeatability and reproducibility (GR&R) study: several operators measure the same units several times, and the measurement spread 6σ_ms is compared with the tolerance; under 10 % is good, 10 to 30 % may be acceptable depending on the application (AIAG MSA).
H2: Worked Example 36.1: Reading the run
P: Problem. Listing 36.1 runs 500 boards. The output voltage (specification 3.201 to 3.399 V) has a mean of 3.3015 V and a standard deviation of 0.0207 V; the efficiency (specification at least 88.0 %, test limit 88.3 % with a 0.3 % guard band) has a mean of 90.21 % and a standard deviation of 0.902 %. Find both Cpk values and the expected numbers of efficiency failures and false rejects.
P: Solution. Vout: min(3.399 − 3.3015, 3.3015 − 3.201)/(3 × 0.0207) = 0.0975/0.0621 = 1.57, capable. Efficiency: (90.21 − 88.0)/(3 × 0.902) = 0.82, not capable. Below the test limit: z = (88.3 − 90.21)/0.902 = −2.12, 1.7 %, about 8.6 boards of 500 (the run rejects 11). Below the specification: z = −2.45, 0.7 %, about 3.6 boards: so roughly 5 of the rejected boards are good boards failed by the guard band. The fix is in the design (efficiency) or in a better measurement that allows a smaller guard band, not in the limit.

H2: 36.4 Throughput and the production station
P: Station throughput is units per hour (UPH) = 3600 × sites/(test time + handling time). Shorter tests come from buffered measurements (Chapter 34), from removing waits that were added "to be safe," from testing in parallel on multiple sites (instruments with several channels, or several instruments behind a switch matrix, Chapter 10), and from moving tests to where they are cheapest: in-circuit test and boundary scan (JTAG) for assembly faults, functional test for performance. Fixtures (bed-of-nails, pogo pins, RF shield boxes) are wear parts: count insertions and replace pins before contact failures start.
H2: Worked Example 36.2: Sites, time, and the gauge
P: Problem. A board takes 42 s to test and 6 s to handle on a single-site station. A quad-site fixture tests four boards in 52 s with the same handling time. Compare the UPH. The Vout measurement has a repeatability of σ = 4 mV against a tolerance of 198 mV. Is the gauge good enough?
P: Solution. Single site: 3600/48 = 75 UPH. Quad site: 4 × 3600/58 = 248 UPH, 3.3 times more for one fixture and a switch matrix. GR&R: 6 × 4 mV/198 mV = 12.1 %, in the 10 to 30 % band: acceptable for this parameter, but the guard band of 10 mV is sized by this measurement; a DMM at a higher NPLC (Chapter 12) would cut both.
H2: 36.5 Measurement data that lasts
P: Store results with their context: unit serial, step, value, unit, limits, pass/fail, attempt, timestamps in UTC, station, operator, sequence and limit-file versions, instrument identities and calibration dates (*IDN? and the asset numbers), and environmental conditions where they matter. A relational database (SQLite on a station, PostgreSQL or SQL Server for a factory) answers questions that files cannot; columnar files (Parquet, HDF5, TDMS) suit waveforms and large characterization data. Write raw data once and never edit it; corrections go into new records. Plot only from stored data, so that every figure can be regenerated, and keep the scripts that made it with it. For regulated products, traceability and electronic-record rules (for example FDA 21 CFR Part 11 for medical devices) add signatures and audit trails.
P: On the Job
P: A production test engineer at an electronics manufacturer owns the test stations for a product family: writing and maintaining sequences, qualifying fixtures and instruments with GR&R studies, setting guard bands with the quality team, watching the first-pass yield and Cpk dashboards every morning, and leading the failure analysis when a Pareto bar grows. A typical issue: the first-pass yield on one of six identical stations drops from 98 % to 91 % over two weeks; the data shows the failures cluster on site 3 of its quad fixture, and the pogo-pin insertion counter is at 210 000 for 100 000-rated pins. Tools: TestStand or Python sequencers, SQL databases and dashboards, switch matrices, DMMs, SMUs, scopes, and boundary-scan controllers.
P: Common misconception. Final yield is the number to watch. First-pass yield and the retest pattern show the problems of the test and the marginal units that final yield hides; watch all three.
BENCH: Before a new station goes live, run the same 10 units through it 3 times each and compare with the reference station. The spread tells you the gauge capability; the offset tells you whether the two stations would sort units differently.
