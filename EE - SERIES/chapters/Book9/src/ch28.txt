PART: Part VIII. Modulation and Signal Analysis
PINTRO: A spectrum analyzer shows where the power sits; a vector signal analyzer shows what the signal says. This part measures modulated signals the way receivers and standards see them. Chapter 28 introduces the vector signal analyzer (VSA) and analog modulation, Chapter 29 digital modulation quality (constellations, EVM, MER, eyes), Chapter 30 the transmitter's spectrum (channel power, ACLR, emission masks, CCDF), Chapter 31 OFDM systems (Wi-Fi 6 and 7, LTE, 5G NR), and Chapter 32 the special signals: Bluetooth Low Energy, LoRa chirps, FMCW radar ramps, and pulses. Modulation theory lives in Book 6; here we measure it.
H1: Chapter 28. Vector Signal Analysis and Analog Modulation
P: The FM transmitter passes its power test and fails in the field: listeners hear distortion on loud passages. The spectrum looks normal, a hump of sidebands around the carrier. A vector signal analyzer demodulates it and plots frequency against time: the deviation flattens at 68 kHz on every loud peak, well short of the 75 kHz the station may use: the audio limiter was set for the wrong pre-emphasis and clips. A spectrum shows power; demodulation shows the message.
MEET: Radio and transmitter service, broadcast compliance, two-way radio alignment, signal-generator verification, avionics and navigation (VOR, ILS), audio links, and the first step of any digital-modulation measurement.
H2: 28.1 From spectrum analyzer to vector signal analyzer
P: A modern signal analyzer downconverts a band to an intermediate frequency, digitizes it, and turns the samples into complex baseband I/Q: I(t) + jQ(t), the signal's amplitude and phase relative to a reference at the center frequency (Book 6 has the theory). Everything afterward is mathematics: an FFT gives the spectrum, the magnitude gives the envelope (AM), the phase gives PM, and its derivative gives the instantaneous frequency (FM). A VSA is that software, running inside the analyzer or on a PC that reads the I/Q (Keysight 89600 VSA, R&S VSE, Tektronix SignalVu, and the vendors' built-in "I/Q analyzer" and "analog demod" modes).
FIG: b9_vsa_block.png | Figure 28.? — The vector signal analyzer: the RF front end of a spectrum analyzer feeds an ADC; a digital downconverter makes I/Q at a sample rate set by the span; software computes the spectrum, demodulation, and modulation quality from the same record. | A block diagram from left to right: RF input, attenuator, mixer with local oscillator, IF filter, ADC, digital downconverter with numerically controlled oscillator producing I and Q, a capture memory, and three outputs labeled spectrum (FFT), analog demodulation (AM, FM, PM), and digital demodulation (constellation, EVM).
P: Three settings govern a capture. The span sets the sample rate (typically about 1.25 times the span, complex samples) and must contain the whole modulated signal. The record length T sets the frequency resolution (about 1/T) and the time the analysis covers. The trigger (free run, IF power, external, or periodic) decides which part of a bursty signal is captured. Gap-free capture into memory, followed by analysis, is what lets a VSA show the frequency hop or the AGC transient that a swept analyzer misses.
H2: 28.2 AM: depth from the envelope or the sidebands
P: Amplitude modulation by a tone at depth m produces a carrier and two sidebands, each at 20 log(m/2) dBc. A VSA reads m directly from the envelope, (max − min)/(max + min); a spectrum analyzer reads it from the sidebands. The envelope method also shows distortion (a flattened trough is overmodulation) and incidental phase modulation that the sideband method hides.
WE: Worked Example 28.1: AM depth from a spectrum
PROB: A spectrum analyzer shows the carrier of an AM signal with sidebands at −12.0 dBc at ±1 kHz. What is the modulation depth, and by how much does the modulation raise the total power?
SOL: m = 2 × 10^(−12.0/20) = 0.502, essentially 50 %. Each sideband carries (m/2)² = 0.0625 of the carrier power; together they add m²/2 = 0.125: total power 1.125 times the carrier, +0.51 dB. A thermal power sensor reads the 1.125; a peak sensor reads the envelope peaks at (1 + m)² = 2.25 times the carrier power, +3.5 dB.
H2: 28.3 FM and PM: deviation, Carson, and the Bessel null
P: Frequency modulation with peak deviation Δf by a tone fm has a modulation index β = Δf/fm and a spectrum of lines at multiples of fm whose amplitudes follow the Bessel functions Jn(β). About 98 % of the power lies within Carson's bandwidth, 2(Δf + fm). Phase modulation is the same spectrum with β set directly in radians. A VSA demodulates FM by differentiating the unwrapped phase; the post-detection filter (for example 300 Hz to 3 kHz for voice, 50 Hz to 15 kHz for broadcast) and de-emphasis must match the standard or the reading means nothing.
P: The Bessel null is the classic absolute check: the carrier line vanishes at β = 2.405 (then 5.520, 8.654). Set the audio frequency so that the null falls at the deviation you want, adjust until the carrier disappears on a narrow-RBW display, and the deviation is known to the accuracy of the audio frequency. It is how deviation meters were calibrated and is still the fastest check of a VSA's FM reading.

WE: Worked Example 28.2: Calibrating deviation with the null
PROB: You need to verify a two-way radio's 5.0 kHz deviation setting. What audio tone makes the carrier vanish at exactly 5.0 kHz deviation? Then, a broadcast FM transmitter runs 75 kHz deviation with 15 kHz audio: what span should the VSA use?
SOL: First null at β = 2.405: fm = 5.0/2.405 = 2.079 kHz. Feed 2.079 kHz, raise the audio level from zero, and stop at the first carrier null; the deviation is then 5.0 kHz. Carson's bandwidth for broadcast is 2(75 + 15) = 180 kHz; with the stereo pilot and RDS subcarriers the real occupied bandwidth is wider, so a 400 kHz span leaves margin and keeps the sample rate modest.

H2: 28.4 Residual modulation and what limits it
P: A generator's residual FM (in a stated post-detection bandwidth) and residual AM are its phase noise and amplitude noise seen through a demodulator; Chapter 20 relates the numbers. When you measure a quiet source, the analyzer's own residual FM sets the floor: check it with a better source, or use a phase-noise analyzer (Chapter 24).
MIS: The analyzer's span only needs to cover the carrier. An FM signal with sidebands cut off by a narrow span demodulates with distortion and reads low deviation; the span must cover Carson's bandwidth or more.
BENCH: Before believing a deviation reading, put the generator you trust into the analyzer with a Bessel-null setting. Two minutes with a tone at 2.079 kHz has caught more than one wrongly configured de-emphasis and post-detection filter.

PP: 6. (Challenge) Plan the measurement of a signal generator's residual FM in a 0.3 to 3 kHz bandwidth, and decide how you will know the analyzer is not the limit.
ANS: 1. m = 2 × 10^(−6/20) = 1.00, 100 %. The usual mistake is to forget the factor 2 (each sideband carries m/2).
ANS: 2. 2(5 + 3) = 16 kHz. The usual mistake is to use 2Δf only.
ANS: 3. Δf = 2.405 × 2.000 = 4.81 kHz. The usual mistake is to stop at the second null (5.520), which gives 11.04 kHz.
ANS: 4. PM with β = 0.5 rad at 10 kHz has instantaneous frequency deviation β × fm = 5 kHz peak. The usual mistake is to report 0.5 as a frequency deviation.
ANS: 5. T = 1/10 Hz = 100 ms; 1.25 × 200 kHz = 250 kS/s, so 25 000 complex samples. The usual mistake is to forget that the resolution comes from the record length, not the span.
ANS: 6. Set the generator to a CW carrier at the frequency of interest, put the analyzer in FM demodulation with a 0.3 to 3 kHz post-detection filter and rms detection, average over at least 1 s, and read the residual FM in Hz rms. To prove the analyzer is not the limit, measure a source known to be about 10 dB quieter (an OCXO-referenced low-noise generator) or switch to a phase-noise analyzer and integrate L(f) over 0.3 to 3 kHz (residual FM rms = √(2 ∫ f² 10^(L(f)/10) df)). The usual mistake is to report the analyzer's own residual.
