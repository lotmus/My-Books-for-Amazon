PART: Part III. Basic Measurements
PINTRO: Volts, ohms, farads, and hertz: the four quantities every bench measures first and trusts most, which is why their errors hide so well. H1: Chapter 12. The Digital Multimeter
P: Two technicians measure the same 50 Hz-powered sensor output in a lab in Germany with a benchtop meter imported from the US. One sets the meter to 1 PLC and sees the last two digits jump by 300 counts; the other sets it to 10 PLC and the reading is rock steady. Nothing is wrong with the sensor. The meter's integration time was set for 60 Hz power lines, and on 50 Hz it rejects hum by only 14 dB instead of by a null. One setting in a menu decides whether a 6.5-digit meter shows 6.5 digits or 3.
MEET: Every bench, every service call, calibration of other instruments, production test of power rails, temperature sensors (RTDs and thermocouples), and data logging with scanner cards.
H2: 12.1 Inside the meter: the integrating converter
P: A DC voltmeter has an input divider or amplifier, an analog-to-digital converter, and a reference. Bench meters from 5.5 to 8.5 digits use integrating converters: dual slope, multislope, or charge balance. The common property is that the converter integrates the input over a set time, the aperture, and reports the average. Averaging over exactly one period of the power line, one power-line cycle (PLC, 16.67 ms at 60 Hz and 20 ms at 50 Hz), removes hum at the line frequency and all its harmonics, because a whole number of sine periods averages to zero. That rejection is the normal-mode rejection ratio (NMRR), and it is why every serious meter lets you set the integration time in PLC and asks for the line frequency. Longer apertures average more noise: the noise falls roughly as the square root of the aperture, at the cost of speed.
WE: Worked Example 12.1: The wrong line frequency
PROB: A meter integrates for 1 PLC at 60 Hz (16.67 ms) on a 50 Hz grid. How much 50 Hz hum does it reject, and how does 10 PLC change that?
SOL: An integrator of length T has the response |sin(π f T)/(π f T)|. At f = 50 Hz and T = 1/60 s, π f T = 2.618 rad, sin(2.618) = 0.500, and the response is 0.500/2.618 = 0.191, a rejection of only 14.4 dB. 100 mV of hum at the input leaves 19 mV on the reading, plenty to scramble the last digits. Setting the meter to 50 Hz makes T = 20 ms, which nulls 50 Hz and its harmonics. At 10 PLC of the wrong frequency (166.7 ms), the rejection improves to 29.6 dB, because the residual from the last, partial period is spread over ten periods; better, but still no null. Set the line frequency first.

H2: 12.2 Digits, counts, noise, and speed
P: Digits are display; noise and accuracy are performance (Chapter 1). A 6.5-digit meter displays 1 200 000 counts on its 1 V range, but at 0.02 PLC its noise may fill the last two digits, and the 24-hour accuracy is a separate specification. Datasheets give a table of noise in ppm of range against the aperture, and of reading rate against the aperture. As a rough rule, reading rate falls as 1/aperture and noise as 1/√aperture: 100 times faster is 10 times noisier. An 8.5-digit reference meter such as Keysight's 3458A, Keithley's DMM7510-class 7.5-digit meters, and Fluke's 8588A sit at the top; the 6.5-digit bench meter (Keysight 34465A class, and similar meters from Keithley, Rigol, and Siglent) is the workhorse; handheld meters with 4.5 digits do service work.
WE: Worked Example 12.2: Choosing the aperture
PROB: A meter's noise is 0.3 ppm of range rms at 10 PLC and the reading rate is set by the aperture alone. What noise and rate do you get at 1 PLC and at 0.1 PLC on 60 Hz power?
SOL: Noise scales as 1/√aperture: 0.3 × √10 = 0.95 ppm at 1 PLC and 0.3 × √100 = 3.0 ppm at 0.1 PLC. Rates: 10 PLC = 166.7 ms, 6 readings per second; 1 PLC, 60 per second; 0.1 PLC, 600 per second (less in practice, with autozero and transfer overhead). Below 1 PLC hum rejection is lost as well. For a slow DC quantity, 10 PLC is the right choice; for a scan of 40 channels, 1 PLC; for capturing a transient, the digitizing mode at the lowest aperture, accepting the noise.
H2: 12.3 Resistance: two wires, four wires, and offset compensation
P: The meter measures resistance by forcing a known current and measuring the voltage. Two-wire, the voltage includes the leads; four-wire (Kelvin), the current flows in one pair of wires and the voltage is sensed with the other, which carries no current, so the leads drop out. Below about 100 Ω, four wires are mandatory for anything better than a percent. Thermocouple voltages in the circuit add a DC offset; offset-compensated ohms measures with the current on and off and subtracts, which removes thermal EMFs (Chapter 14) at the cost of half the speed. RTDs, strain gauges, shunts, and contact resistance are the daily uses.
WE: Worked Example 12.3: A Pt100 sensor two-wire and four-wire
PROB: A Pt100 (100 Ω at 0 °C, 0.385 Ω/K) is wired with two leads of 0.5 Ω each and read at 1 mA. A 5 µV thermal EMF sits in the circuit. Compare two-wire, four-wire, and four-wire offset-compensated readings as temperatures.
SOL: Two-wire, the leads add 1.0 Ω, which the meter reports as 1.0/0.385 = 2.6 K too warm. Four-wire removes the leads but not the EMF: 5 µV/1 mA = 5 mΩ, 0.013 K, negligible for most work but not for a calibration bath. Offset compensation removes the 5 µV entirely. The sensor's self-heating at 1 mA (0.1 mW) is the next error, and that one depends on how it is mounted, not on the meter.
H2: 12.4 AC: true RMS, crest factor, and bandwidth
P: An average-responding meter rectifies the signal, averages it, and multiplies by 1.111, the ratio of RMS to average for a sine; for any other waveform it is wrong. A true-RMS meter computes the root mean square, by an analog RMS converter or, in modern meters, digitally from samples. Its limits are the crest factor (peak over RMS) it can handle at full accuracy, its AC bandwidth (often 100 kHz to 300 kHz, with accuracy specified for less), and a minimum signal level (often 1 % to 5 % of range). DC coupling matters too: AC+DC RMS includes the offset, AC RMS removes it.
WE: Worked Example 12.4: Square and triangle on an average-responding meter
PROB: An average-responding meter reads a 1 V-peak square wave and a 1 V-peak triangle wave, both symmetric. What does it show, and what is the true RMS?
SOL: The square wave's average of the rectified signal is 1 V and its RMS is 1 V; the meter shows 1.111 × 1 = 1.111 V, 11.1 % high. The triangle's rectified average is 0.5 V and its RMS 1/√3 = 0.577 V; the meter shows 0.555 V, 3.8 % low. A true-RMS meter shows 1.000 V and 0.577 V. On motor drives, dimmers, and switching supplies, only true RMS gives a meaningful number, and its crest factor and bandwidth must cover the waveform.
H2: 12.5 Using the meter well
P: Autozero on (it subtracts the converter's offset every reading, at the cost of speed). High input impedance (10 GΩ instead of 10 MΩ) on the low DC ranges when the source is high impedance, as Chapter 3 showed. Current measurements put a shunt in the circuit with its burden voltage (Chapter 3); a fuse protects the current input, and a meter left on the current jacks and touched to a mains outlet is a short circuit: use the CAT-rated meter of Chapter 4. Scanner cards turn the meter into a data logger for tens of channels (Chapter 10), and SCPI commands such as CONF:VOLT:DC 10, VOLT:NPLC 10, and READ? drive it from a script (Chapter 34).

MIS: More digits mean a more accurate reading. Digits set the resolution; the accuracy is a separate line in the datasheet, and noise at a short aperture can make the last digits meaningless (Chapter 1).
BENCH: Short the input with a low-thermal shorting plug, set the meter to the range and aperture you will use, and watch the reading for a minute. That is your noise floor and offset today, in your lab; a datasheet does not know your fluorescent lights.

PP: 1. (Core) What is 1 PLC in milliseconds on a 50 Hz and on a 60 Hz grid?
PP: 2. (Core) A two-wire resistance measurement of a 0.5 Ω shunt uses leads of 0.1 Ω each. What does the meter read, and what is the error?
PP: 3. (Core) An average-responding meter reads 2.22 V on a symmetric square wave. What are its peak and its RMS?
PP: 4. (Extension) A meter's noise is 2 ppm rms at 1 PLC. What aperture gives 0.5 ppm, and how many readings per second at 50 Hz?
PP: 5. (Extension) An integrating meter is set to 20 ms on a 60 Hz grid. What is its rejection of 60 Hz hum?
PP: 6. (Challenge) Design a 40-channel temperature scan of Pt100 sensors that must finish every 10 s with 0.05 K resolution, using a 6.5-digit meter and a scanner card. Choose the wiring, aperture, and current, and check the time budget.
ANS: 1. 20 ms at 50 Hz and 16.67 ms at 60 Hz. The usual mistake is to assume the meter knows the grid; most must be told.
ANS: 2. 0.5 + 2 × 0.1 = 0.7 Ω, 40 % high. The usual mistake is to subtract a lead resistance measured once; it changes with temperature and contact pressure, so four wires are the cure.
ANS: 3. The meter shows 1.111 × average; for a square wave the rectified average equals the peak, so the average is 2.22/1.111 = 2.0 V, the peak is 2.0 V, and the RMS is 2.0 V. The usual mistake is to take 2.22 V as the RMS.
ANS: 4. Noise scales as 1/√aperture, so 2/0.5 = 4 needs 16 times the aperture: 16 PLC = 320 ms at 50 Hz, about 3 readings per second. The usual mistake is to scale the aperture by 4.
ANS: 5. π × 60 × 0.020 = 3.770 rad, |sin(3.770)/3.770| = 0.588/3.770 = 0.156, a rejection of 16.1 dB. The usual mistake is to expect a null because 20 ms is "a whole cycle"; it is a whole cycle only of 50 Hz.
ANS: 6. Four-wire (0.05 K is 19 mΩ, far below any lead resistance), 1 mA current (0.1 mW self-heating). Resolution: 19 mΩ in 100 Ω is 190 ppm, easily met at 1 PLC (about 1 ppm noise). Time: 40 channels × (one 20 ms reading at 1 PLC on 50 Hz, doubled by offset compensation, plus about 5 ms relay settling and switching) ≈ 40 × 45 ms = 1.8 s, well inside 10 s; 2 PLC with offset compensation (85 ms per channel, 3.4 s) is affordable and halves the hum sensitivity. The usual mistake is to forget relay settling and the doubling by offset compensation in the time budget.
