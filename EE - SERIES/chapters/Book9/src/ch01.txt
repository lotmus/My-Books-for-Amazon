PART: Part I. Measuring Well
PINTRO: Before any instrument, the rules of the game. Chapter 1 separates accuracy, resolution, and precision, three words that are used as if they were one. Chapter 2 turns a datasheet into an uncertainty budget and explains calibration and traceability. Chapter 3 shows how the instrument becomes part of the circuit it measures, through its input resistance and its ground. Chapter 4 keeps you alive: measurement categories, isolation, and stored energy. The statistics behind all of this, significant figures and the standard deviation, are Book 1's (Chapter 15); this part applies them on the bench.
H1: Chapter 1. Accuracy, Resolution, and Precision
P: The new 6.5-digit meter shows 5.000213 V, the old handheld shows 5.00 V, and the reference says 5.000 000 V. Which one is right? The expensive one has more digits, but digits are resolution, not truth. Whether those last three digits mean anything depends on a line in the datasheet most people never read, and on whether the meter gives the same answer twice. This chapter teaches you to read that line.
MEET: Every datasheet, every calibration certificate, every test limit, and every argument about whether a board passes or fails.
H2: 1.1 Three words that are not the same
P: Accuracy is how close a reading is to the true value. Precision is how close repeated readings are to each other. Resolution is the smallest change the instrument can display. A meter can be precise and wrong: it repeats 5.0021 V every time while the true value is 5.0000 V, which is a systematic error, an offset that calibration can remove. A meter can be accurate on average and imprecise: its readings scatter around the true value, which is random error, reduced by averaging. And resolution says nothing about either. A 6.5-digit display resolves 1 µV on its 10 V range, but if the instrument has drifted by 200 µV since its last calibration, the last two digits show noise and drift, not the circuit.

KEY: Resolution is what the display can show, precision is what the instrument repeats, accuracy is what the calibration guarantees. Only the last one tells you how far the reading can be from the truth.
H2: 1.2 Reading an accuracy specification
P: A multimeter specification looks like this: DC voltage, 10 V range, 1 year, 23 °C ± 5 °C: ±(0.0035 % of reading + 0.0005 % of range). The first term scales with the reading and describes gain error; the second is fixed for the range and describes offset and noise. Many handheld meters write the second term as counts: ±(0.05 % + 3 counts) means three steps of the last digit. The conditions matter as much as the numbers. "1 year" is the time since calibration; many meters also list 24-hour and 90-day specifications, which are tighter. "23 °C ± 5 °C" is the temperature band; outside it, a temperature coefficient per degree is added. And "typical" values are not guaranteed: only the specified ones are.
WE: Worked Example 1.1: The error band of a 6.5-digit reading
PROB: A 6.5-digit meter with the specification above reads 5.000213 V on its 10 V range. How large is the error band, and which digits can you trust?
SOL: The reading term is 0.0035 % × 5 V = 175 µV; the range term is 0.0005 % × 10 V = 50 µV. The error band is ±225 µV, or ±0.0045 % of the reading. The true voltage lies between 4.999988 V and 5.000438 V, so the reference value of 5.000000 V is inside the band and the meter agrees with it. The display resolves 1 µV, but the guarantee is ±225 µV, so the last two digits are useful for seeing small changes (precision) and not for the absolute value (accuracy). The usual mistake is to read the error as 0.0035 % alone and forget the range term, which here is more than a fifth of the total.
WE: Worked Example 1.2: A handheld meter in counts
PROB: A 4.5-digit handheld meter (19 999 counts) on its 2 V range is specified ±(0.05 % + 3 counts). It reads 1.2345 V. What is the error band?
SOL: On the 2 V range one count is 0.1 mV. The reading term is 0.05 % × 1.2345 V = 0.617 mV, and three counts add 0.3 mV, so the band is ±0.92 mV. Report 1.2345 V ± 0.0009 V. The fourth decimal place is displayed, but the uncertainty is nine of those steps.
H2: 1.3 Digits and counts
P: The digit count describes the display. A 3.5-digit meter shows up to 1999: three full digits and a leading "half" digit that can only be 0 or 1, so it has 2000 counts. A 4.5-digit meter has 20 000 counts, a 6.5-digit meter 2 000 000 (often 1 200 000, as many meters overrange only to 120 %). Counts set the resolution on each range: 1 count on the 10 V range of a 6.5-digit meter is 10 V/1 000 000 = 10 µV in the plain reading, or 1 µV with the extended display. More counts help only if the noise and the drift are below one count, which is why a high-digit meter also integrates longer (Chapter 12).
MIS: More digits mean a more accurate meter. Digits set the resolution. Two meters with the same 6.5-digit display can differ by a factor of ten in accuracy, and a 4.5-digit bench meter with a fresh calibration can be more accurate than an uncalibrated 6.5-digit one.
H2: 1.4 Precision, averaging, and the standard error
P: Repeat a reading and it changes in its last digits. The spread of those readings is the precision, described by the sample standard deviation s. Averaging n readings shrinks the random part by √n: the standard error of the mean is s/√n. It does nothing for a systematic error, which stays at its full size however long you average. Book 1, Chapter 15, has the statistics; on the bench the rule is short: average to fight noise, calibrate to fight bias.
WE: Worked Example 1.3: Ten readings of a 1 V reference
PROB: Ten readings of a 1 V reference on a 6.5-digit meter are 1.00012, 1.00009, 1.00015, 1.00011, 1.00008, 1.00013, 1.00010, 1.00014, 1.00011, and 1.00012 V. Find the mean, the standard deviation, and the standard error of the mean.
SOL: The mean is 1.000115 V. The sample standard deviation is 21.7 µV (sum the squared deviations, divide by n − 1 = 9, take the root). The standard error of the mean is 21.7 µV/√10 = 6.9 µV. So the meter repeats to about 22 µV per reading, and the average is known to about 7 µV of random error. The offset of 115 µV from the nominal 1 V is a separate question: it is either the reference, the meter's calibration, or both, and averaging cannot tell which. Only a comparison with a better standard can (Chapter 2).
H2: 1.5 The kinds of error
P: Systematic errors repeat: offset (a constant added to every reading), gain error (a constant factor), nonlinearity (an error that depends on where in the range you are), and the loading of the circuit by the instrument (Chapter 3). Random errors change from reading to reading: thermal noise, quantization, interference that is not synchronized with the measurement. Drift is slow: the reference voltage inside the meter ages, the temperature of the room changes. The cure differs for each. Calibration removes offset and gain at the calibration points; averaging reduces random error; a warm-up period and a stable room reduce drift; and a better measurement setup removes loading and interference. Knowing which kind of error you are fighting saves hours of averaging a bias.
BENCH: Before you trust a new setup, short the inputs of the meter with a short copper link and read it on the range you intend to use. The reading is the offset of the meter plus the thermal voltages of the connections. Then measure a known reference, even a fresh voltage-reference module from a hobby shop, and compare. Two minutes of this tell you more about the setup than the datasheet does.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) How many counts does a 3.5-digit meter have, and what is its resolution on the 2 V range?
PP: 2. (Core) The 6.5-digit meter of Worked Example 1.1 reads 1.000000 V on its 10 V range. What is the error band in microvolts and in percent of the reading?
PP: 3. (Core) A bathroom scale reads 2.0 kg too high, every time, to within 0.1 kg. Is it accurate, precise, both, or neither? How would you fix it?
PP: 4. (Extension) The meter of Worked Example 1.3 shows a standard deviation of 21.7 µV per reading. How many readings must you average to reach a standard error of 2 µV?
PP: 5. (Extension) The handheld meter of Worked Example 1.2 reads 0.0123 V on its 2 V range. What is the error band, and why is the reading poorly suited to this range if the meter also has a 200 mV range with ±(0.05 % + 3 counts) and 10 µV counts?
PP: 6. (Challenge) A meter measures 0.1 V. Its specifications are: 10 V range ±(0.0035 % of reading + 0.0005 % of range); 1 V range ±(0.0040 % + 0.0007 %); 100 mV range ±(0.0050 % + 0.0035 %), with 120 % overrange. Which range gives the smallest error band, and by how much?
ANS: 1. A 3.5-digit meter shows 0 to 1999, so it has 2000 counts. On the 2 V range one count is 2 V/2000 = 1 mV. The usual mistake is to read "3.5 digits" as 3500 counts.
ANS: 2. Reading term: 0.0035 % × 1 V = 35 µV. Range term: 0.0005 % × 10 V = 50 µV. Error band ±85 µV, or ±0.0085 % of the reading, almost twice the percentage of Worked Example 1.1, because the fixed range term now dominates. The usual mistake is to quote only 0.0035 %.
ANS: 3. Precise but not accurate: it repeats within 0.1 kg but has a systematic offset of +2.0 kg. Fix it by calibration: zero it with nothing on it, or subtract 2.0 kg from every reading. The usual mistake is to call a repeatable instrument accurate.
ANS: 4. The standard error is s/√n, so n = (s/2 µV)² = (21.7/2)² = 117.7, which rounds up to 118 readings. Ten times as many readings improve the result by only √10 = 3.2 times. The usual mistake is to expect a ten-fold improvement from ten-fold averaging.
ANS: 5. On the 2 V range: 0.05 % × 0.0123 V = 6.2 µV plus 3 counts of 100 µV = 300 µV, so ±0.31 mV, which is 2.5 % of the reading. On the 200 mV range, 3 counts are 30 µV, so the band is 6.2 µV + 30 µV = ±36 µV, or 0.3 % of the reading. A small reading on a large range is dominated by the counts term; use the lowest range that holds the signal. The usual mistake is to leave the meter on a high range because autorange "knows best", when a manual or slow autorange may sit on the wrong range.
ANS: 6. 10 V range: 3.5 µV + 50 µV = 53.5 µV. 1 V range: 4 µV + 7 µV = 11 µV. 100 mV range (0.1 V is the full-scale value, inside the 120 % overrange): 5 µV + 3.5 µV = 8.5 µV. The 100 mV range is best, about six times better than the 10 V range. The usual mistake is to compare only the percent-of-reading terms, which favor the 10 V range, and ignore the range terms, which decide the result.
