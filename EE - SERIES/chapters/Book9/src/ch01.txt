PART: Part I. Measuring Well
H1: Chapter 1. Accuracy, Resolution, and Precision
P: The new 6.5-digit meter shows 5.000213 V, the old handheld shows 5.00 V, and the reference says 5.000 000 V. Which one is right? The expensive one has more digits, but digits are resolution, not truth. Whether those last three digits mean anything depends on a line in the datasheet most people never read, and on whether the meter gives the same answer twice. This chapter teaches you to read that line.
P: Where you meet this: Every datasheet, every calibration certificate, every test limit, and every argument about whether a board passes or fails.
H2: 1.1 Three words that are not the same
P: Accuracy describes closeness to a reference value; it is a qualitative concept, not a numerical property by itself. Precision describes how closely repeated readings agree. Resolution is the smallest change the instrument can display. A meter can repeat 5.0021 V while a suitable reference indicates 5.0000 V: the readings are precise, but show a bias of about 2.1 mV under those conditions. Calibration compares an instrument with a reference and documents the result; adjustment may reduce a known bias, but calibration alone does not guarantee that every later reading is correct. A meter can also show scatter around a reference value. Averaging may reduce the random component when observations are independent and conditions remain stable. Resolution says nothing by itself about accuracy or uncertainty. A 6.5-digit display can show 1 µV steps on a range, yet specifications, calibration data, environment, and setup determine what those digits support.

P: Key idea. Resolution describes the display step; precision describes repeatability; accuracy describes closeness to a reference. A calibration certificate reports results and associated uncertainty under stated conditions. To judge how far a reading may be from a reference, combine applicable specifications, calibration information, environment, and setup in an uncertainty evaluation.
H2: 1.2 Reading an accuracy specification
P: A multimeter specification looks like this: DC voltage, 10 V range, 1 year, 23 °C ± 5 °C: ±(0.0035 % of reading + 0.0005 % of range). The first term scales with the reading and describes gain error; the second is fixed for the range and describes offset and noise. Many handheld meters write the second term as counts: ±(0.05 % + 3 counts) means three steps of the last digit. The conditions matter as much as the numbers. "1 year" is the time since calibration; many meters also list 24-hour and 90-day specifications, which are tighter. "23 °C ± 5 °C" is the temperature band; outside it, a temperature coefficient per degree is added. And "typical" values are not guaranteed: only the specified ones are.
H2: Worked Example 1.1: The error band of a 6.5-digit reading
P: Problem. A 6.5-digit meter with the specification above reads 5.000213 V on its 10 V range. How large is the error band, and which digits can you trust?
P: Solution. The reading term is 0.0035 % × 5 V = 175 µV; the range term is 0.0005 % × 10 V = 50 µV. The error band is ±225 µV, or ±0.0045 % of the reading. The true voltage lies between 4.999988 V and 5.000438 V, so the reference value of 5.000000 V is inside the band and the meter agrees with it. The display resolves 1 µV, but the guarantee is ±225 µV, so the last two digits are useful for seeing small changes (precision) and not for the absolute value (accuracy). The usual mistake is to read the error as 0.0035 % alone and forget the range term, which here is more than a fifth of the total.
H2: Worked Example 1.2: A handheld meter in counts
P: Problem. A 4.5-digit handheld meter (19 999 counts) on its 2 V range is specified ±(0.05 % + 3 counts). It reads 1.2345 V. What is the error band?
P: Solution. On the 2 V range one count is 0.1 mV. The reading term is 0.05 % × 1.2345 V = 0.617 mV, and three counts add 0.3 mV, so the band is ±0.92 mV. Report 1.2345 V ± 0.0009 V. The fourth decimal place is displayed, but the uncertainty is nine of those steps.
H2: 1.3 Digits and counts
P: The digit count describes the display. A 3.5-digit meter shows up to 1999: three full digits and a leading "half" digit that can only be 0 or 1, so it has 2000 counts. A 4.5-digit meter has 20 000 counts, a 6.5-digit meter 2 000 000 (often 1 200 000, as many meters overrange only to 120 %). Counts set the resolution on each range: 1 count on the 10 V range of a 6.5-digit meter is 10 V/1 000 000 = 10 µV in the plain reading, or 1 µV with the extended display. More counts help only if the noise and the drift are below one count, which is why a high-digit meter also integrates longer (Chapter 12).
P: Common misconception. More digits mean a more accurate meter. Digits set the resolution. Two meters with the same 6.5-digit display can differ by a factor of ten in accuracy, and a 4.5-digit bench meter with a fresh calibration can be more accurate than an uncalibrated 6.5-digit one.
H2: 1.4 Precision, averaging, and the standard error
P: Repeat a reading and it changes in its last digits. The spread of those readings is the precision, described by the sample standard deviation s. Averaging n readings shrinks the random part by √n: the standard error of the mean is s/√n. It does nothing for a systematic error, which stays at its full size however long you average. Book 1, Chapter 15, has the statistics; on the bench the rule is short: average to fight noise, calibrate to fight bias.
H2: Worked Example 1.3: Ten readings of a 1 V reference
P: Problem. Ten readings of a 1 V reference on a 6.5-digit meter are 1.00012, 1.00009, 1.00015, 1.00011, 1.00008, 1.00013, 1.00010, 1.00014, 1.00011, and 1.00012 V. Find the mean, the standard deviation, and the standard error of the mean.
P: Solution. The mean is 1.000115 V. The sample standard deviation is 21.7 µV (sum the squared deviations, divide by n − 1 = 9, take the root). The standard error of the mean is 21.7 µV/√10 = 6.9 µV. So the meter repeats to about 22 µV per reading, and the average is known to about 7 µV of random error. The offset of 115 µV from the nominal 1 V is a separate question: it is either the reference, the meter's calibration, or both, and averaging cannot tell which. Only a comparison with a better standard can (Chapter 2).
H2: 1.5 The kinds of error
P: Systematic errors repeat: offset (a constant added to every reading), gain error (a constant factor), nonlinearity (an error that depends on where in the range you are), and the loading of the circuit by the instrument (Chapter 3). Random errors change from reading to reading: thermal noise, quantization, interference that is not synchronized with the measurement. Drift is slow: the reference voltage inside the meter ages, the temperature of the room changes. The cure differs for each. Calibration removes offset and gain at the calibration points; averaging reduces random error; a warm-up period and a stable room reduce drift; and a better measurement setup removes loading and interference. Knowing which kind of error you are fighting saves hours of averaging a bias.
BENCH: Before you trust a new setup, short the inputs of the meter with a short copper link and read it on the range you intend to use. The reading is the offset of the meter plus the thermal voltages of the connections. Then measure a known reference, even a fresh voltage-reference module from a hobby shop, and compare. Two minutes of this tell you more about the setup than the datasheet does.

P: 3. (Core) A bathroom scale reads 2.0 kg too high, every time, to within 0.1 kg. Is it accurate, precise, both, or neither? How would you fix it?

P: 5. (Extension) The handheld meter of Worked Example 1.2 reads 0.0123 V on its 2 V range. What is the error band, and why is the reading poorly suited to this range if the meter also has a 200 mV range with ±(0.05 % + 3 counts) and 10 µV counts?
P: 6. (Challenge) A meter measures 0.1 V. Its specifications are: 10 V range ±(0.0035 % of reading + 0.0005 % of range); 1 V range ±(0.0040 % + 0.0007 %); 100 mV range ±(0.0050 % + 0.0035 %), with 120 % overrange. Which range gives the smallest error band, and by how much?
