H1: Chapter 14. Low-Level Measurements: Nanovolts, Picoamps, and Teraohms
P: The engineer touches the cable to straighten it, and the picoammeter jumps by 200 pA and takes a minute to come back. The insulation resistance of the new connector is "infinite" on the megohmmeter and 40 GΩ on the electrometer, unless someone breathes on it. At these levels the cable, the connector, the air, and the operator's hand are part of the circuit, and the instrument's specification is the easy part.
MEET: Semiconductor leakage and gate current (Chapters 6 and 7), insulation resistance of PCBs, cables, and connectors, photodiodes and radiation detectors, thermocouples and strain bridges, superconducting and low-resistance contacts, and metrology.
H2: 14.1 The instruments
P: A nanovoltmeter measures low voltages from low-impedance sources, with a resolution in the nanovolts and very low noise and drift: thermocouples, contact resistance, Hall effect, and resistance with currents small enough not to heat the sample. Keithley's 2182A is the classic example. A picoammeter measures currents down to femtoamperes with a feedback ammeter that has a very low burden voltage. An electrometer adds a very high input impedance (above 200 TΩ) and a built-in voltage source, so it measures voltage from high-impedance sources, current, charge, and, with the source, resistance up to petaohms; Keithley's 6517B is an example. A low-current SMU (Chapter 6) does much of the same in a test system.
H2: 14.2 Noise and thermal EMF: the nanovolt problems
P: Every resistor generates Johnson noise, vn = √(4kTRB), which sets the floor no instrument can beat. Junctions of different metals generate thermocouple voltages wherever there is a temperature gradient: copper against copper is about 0.2 µV/K, copper against tin-lead solder 1 to 3 µV/K, and copper against oxidized copper up to 1 mV/K, which is why low-level connections are clean, crimped or cold-welded copper. The cures: low-thermal cables and connectors, everything at one temperature (wait after touching), and current reversal: the delta method measures with +I and −I and takes half the difference, which cancels any constant thermal EMF and, with three-point alternation, a linear drift too.
WE: Worked Example 14.1: The Johnson-noise floor
PROB: A 10 kΩ source is measured with a 0.1 Hz noise bandwidth at 300 K. What is the noise floor? What does a 1 kΩ source give in 1 Hz?
SOL: vn = √(4 × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 300 × 10⁴ × 0.1) = 4.07 nV rms. For 1 kΩ in 1 Hz the result is the same, 4.07 nV rms, since the product R × B is the same. A nanovoltmeter's own noise must be well below that, and its integration time sets B: longer integration, smaller B.
WE: Worked Example 14.2: The delta method on a milliohm
PROB: A 1 mΩ sample is measured with 10 mA, so the signal is 10 µV. A thermal EMF of 2 µV sits in the circuit. Compare a single-polarity reading with the delta method.
SOL: Single polarity: 10 + 2 = 12 µV, read as 1.2 mΩ, 20 % high. Delta: (+10 + 2) and (−10 + 2) give 12 and −8 µV; half the difference is 10 µV, exactly 1.000 mΩ. A drift of the EMF between the two readings remains; alternating faster, or with three readings (+, −, +), removes a linear drift.
H2: 14.3 Picoamps and teraohms: leakage, guarding, settling
P: At picoamperes, every insulator leaks. A cable whose insulation is 10¹² Ω, with 10 V across it, leaks 10 pA. Guarding fixes this: the guard is a conductor driven to the same potential as the signal, so the insulator between signal and guard has no voltage across it and carries no current, and the leakage flows from guard to ground, where the meter does not measure it (Chapter 3). Triaxial cables carry signal, guard, and shield. Cable motion generates triboelectric currents, piezoelectric insulators generate charge under stress, and dielectric absorption in capacitors and insulators releases charge for minutes after a voltage change. Settling is slow: a source resistance of 1 TΩ and a cable capacitance of 100 pF make a 100 s time constant, unless the guard bootstraps the cable capacitance away.
WE: Worked Example 14.3: Waiting for a teraohm
PROB: A 1 TΩ resistance is measured with 100 pF of unguarded cable capacitance. How long until the reading is within 0.1 % of its final value? What if guarding reduces the effective capacitance to 1 pF?
SOL: τ = RC = 10¹² × 100 × 10⁻¹² = 100 s. Within 0.1 % after ln(1000) τ = 6.91 τ = 691 s, 11.5 minutes per reading. With 1 pF effective, τ = 1 s and 6.9 s. Guarding is not only a leakage fix; it is the only way to measure high resistances at a usable speed.

JOB: In a wafer-level reliability and leakage lab, an engineer measures gate leakage of thin-oxide transistors at femtoamperes across 300 mm wafers on a probe station inside a dark, shielded enclosure, with triaxial cabling, guarded probes, and low-current SMUs. A typical day: correlating a creeping leakage map with the humidity log of the cleanroom, proving that the "leakage" was moisture on the probe card, and adding a dry-nitrogen purge to the prober. Tools: Keysight B1500A or Keithley 4200A-SCS parameter analyzers with preamplifiers, FormFactor or MPI probe stations, electrometers, and a habit of waiting before trusting a reading.
MIS: A higher input resistance meter always solves high-impedance measurements. Leakage of the cable and fixture in parallel with the source, and the RC settling time, usually dominate long before the meter's input resistance does.
BENCH: Before measuring anything at picoamperes, measure the setup with the device removed: open fixture, same voltage, same cables. That reading is your floor; subtract nothing you have not measured, and if the floor drifts, wait, because the setup is still settling.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) What is the Johnson noise of 1 MΩ in 1 Hz at 300 K?
PP: 2. (Core) A circuit with copper-to-tin-lead joints at 3 µV/K has a 0.5 K gradient. What thermal EMF can appear?
PP: 3. (Core) Why does a guard remove leakage current from the measurement?
PP: 4. (Extension) A 100 GΩ source with 200 pF of cable: how long until within 1 %?
PP: 5. (Extension) A delta-method measurement reads +25.4 µV and −24.6 µV at ±1 mA. What is the resistance and the thermal EMF?
PP: 6. (Challenge) Plan the insulation-resistance test of a connector (expected about 10 TΩ at 500 V): instrument, connections, test time, and environmental controls.
ANS: 1. √(4 × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 300 × 10⁶ × 1) = 129 nV rms. The usual mistake is to forget the square root and get a number that is far too small.
ANS: 2. 3 µV/K × 0.5 K = 1.5 µV, per pair of unbalanced joints. The usual mistake is to assume the two joints of a loop cancel; they cancel only if they are at the same temperature.
ANS: 3. The guard is held at the signal's potential, so there is no voltage across the insulation between signal and guard and no current flows in it; the leakage flows from the guard (driven by a low-impedance source) to ground, outside the measured path. The usual mistake is to connect the guard to ground, which turns it into a shield and the leakage back on.
ANS: 4. τ = 10¹¹ × 200 × 10⁻¹² = 20 s; ln(100) τ = 4.6 × 20 = 92 s. The usual mistake is to wait one time constant.
ANS: 5. R = (25.4 − (−24.6))/2/1 mA = 25.0 µV/1 mA = 25.0 mΩ; the EMF is (25.4 + (−24.6))/2 = 0.4 µV. The usual mistake is to average the magnitudes without the signs, which hides the EMF.
ANS: 6. An electrometer with a 500 V source (or a dedicated high-resistance meter), the connector in a shielded, guarded fixture with triaxial cables, 500 V applied and the current read after a fixed electrification time (often 60 s, following the connector standard), so the result is about 50 pA at 10 TΩ. Control humidity (below about 50 % RH, logged) and temperature, clean the part with the specified solvent and handle it with gloves, measure the open fixture first, and discharge the part through the instrument after the test (Chapter 4). The usual mistake is to report the reading at an arbitrary time, while dielectric absorption is still decaying.
