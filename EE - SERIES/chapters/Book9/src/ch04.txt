H1: Chapter 4. Safety: Measurement Categories, Isolation, and Stored Energy
P: Two meters lie on the bench. Both say 600 V on the front. One is rated CAT II 600 V, the other CAT III 600 V, and the second costs twice as much. In a wall outlet they behave the same. In the breaker panel, the cheaper one is the wrong tool: the lightning-induced transient that the panel can carry is larger than the cheaper meter was tested to survive, and when a meter fails on mains, it fails as an arc in your hand. The number that matters is not the 600 V but the category in front of it.
P: Where you meet this: Mains wiring, solar and battery installations, motor drives, power-supply development, and any oscilloscope measurement on a circuit that is not at earth potential.
H2: 4.1 What hurts, and why categories exist
P: Current through the body is what injures: a few milliamperes are felt, a few tens of milliamperes across the chest can stop the heart, and arc flash burns with energy, not current. Mains measurements add a hazard most bench work lacks: transients. A lightning strike nearby or a large motor switching off sends kilovolt spikes along the wiring, attenuated by each stage of the installation. A meter connected at that moment must not flash over internally, because the arc it starts is fed by the mains itself, which near the service entrance can deliver thousands of amperes.
H2: 4.2 Measurement categories
P: The safety standard for measuring equipment, IEC 61010 (with part 2-030 for measuring circuits), groups mains measurements by where in the installation they are made. CAT IV is the origin of the installation: the utility connection, the meter, the service entrance, overhead lines. CAT III is the building distribution: breaker panels, fixed wiring, busbars, permanently installed motors. CAT II is equipment plugged into an outlet: appliances, tools, the outlet itself. Circuits not connected to mains, such as battery-powered boards and the secondary side of a properly isolated supply, are outside these categories (CAT I in older editions, "O" in the current one). Each category and working voltage comes with a transient the instrument must withstand: CAT II 600 V, 4 kV; CAT III 600 V, 6 kV; CAT III 1000 V, 8 kV; CAT IV 600 V, 8 kV; CAT IV 1000 V, 12 kV, and the CAT III and IV tests use a lower source impedance, so more current flows.

H2: Worked Example 4.1: Choosing the meter for the panel
P: Problem. You must measure the voltage on a breaker in a 120/240 V split-phase panel. Which of the two meters of the hook may you use, and what else must be rated?
P: Solution. The panel is CAT III. The line-to-earth voltage is 120 V and line-to-line 240 V, both under 600 V, so a CAT III 600 V meter is suitable: it was tested with 6 kV transients. The CAT II 600 V meter was tested only to 4 kV and is not rated for this location, however high its voltage number. The leads, probes, and clips must carry a CAT III rating of at least 600 V as well, because the system is only as safe as its lowest-rated part; tip shrouds that expose no more than about 4 mm of metal are standard for CAT III and IV leads. And the meter's current jacks must have high-rupture-capacity fuses, because a lead left in the current jack and touched across a live breaker is a dead short through the meter.
H2: 4.3 Never float the oscilloscope
P: The scope's ground clip is earthed (Chapter 3). Clip it to a node that is not at earth, such as the switch node of a half-bridge or the neutral-referenced primary of a power supply, and you short that node to earth through the probe. The old fix was to remove the scope's earth connection with a cheater plug so that its whole chassis floated at the node's potential. Do not do this: the chassis, the knobs, the USB port, and every other probe's ground are then at the node voltage, and nothing will trip. The correct tools are a high-voltage differential probe or an oscilloscope with isolated inputs, rated for the common-mode voltage and measurement category. An isolation transformer on the device under test can be appropriate in a controlled setup, but it does not make the circuit safe to touch; keep the oscilloscope's protective earth connected.
H2: Worked Example 4.2: Common-mode current on a fast node
P: Problem. A half-bridge switches 400 V in 20 ns. A measurement setup has 10 pF of capacitance from the floating node to earth. How much current flows during the edge?
P: Solution. The slew rate is 400 V/20 ns = 20 V/ns, or 2 × 10¹⁰ V/s. The current is i = C dv/dt = 10 pF × 2 × 10¹⁰ V/s = 0.2 A, for 20 ns at every edge. It flows through whatever path the setup provides, and on its way it can create volts of error in a measurement of a 15 V gate signal. This is why isolated probes advertise low capacitance to earth and very high CMRR at high frequency, and why a differential probe whose CMRR has fallen to 40 dB at the edge frequency is the wrong tool for gate-drive work (Problem 6).
H2: 4.4 Stored energy
P: Switch the power off and the danger may stay. Bulk capacitors in supplies and inverters hold charge for minutes if their bleeder resistor has failed or was never fitted. Battery packs have no off state at all: an 18650 cell can drive tens of amperes into a short, and a 48 V pack can weld a tool. Measure before you touch: verify that the voltage is gone with a meter you have first checked on a known live source.
H2: Worked Example 4.3: Discharging a capacitor bank
P: Problem. A 2200 µF capacitor is charged to 450 V. How much energy does it hold? A 1 kΩ discharge resistor is connected. How long until the voltage is below 50 V, and what is the peak power in the resistor?
P: Solution. E = ½CV² = 0.5 × 2200 µF × 450² = 222.75 J, enough to injure and to vaporize a screwdriver tip. The time constant is RC = 1 kΩ × 2200 µF = 2.2 s, and the voltage falls below 50 V after 2.2 s × ln(450/50) = 4.83 s. The peak power is 450²/1 kΩ = 202.5 W at the first instant, so the resistor needs a pulse-energy rating for 223 J, not a 202 W continuous rating; a wirewound or ceramic resistor sized from its datasheet's pulse curve does the job. Use a tool with insulated handles, and measure afterward.
P: Bench safety checklist
LI: Meter, leads, and probes rated for the measurement category and voltage of the location, all of them.
LI: Test the meter on a known live source before and after checking a circuit is dead.
LI: One hand away from the circuit on mains; no jewelry; insulated tools.
LI: Differential or isolated probes for any node not at earth; never defeat a protective earth.
LI: Discharge and measure capacitors before touching; protected cells and fused leads for batteries.
LI: Respect the instrument too: check the maximum input of every RF instrument before connecting a transmitter, and put an attenuator in front of it.
P: Common misconception. A CAT II 1000 V meter is safer than a CAT III 600 V meter because its voltage rating is higher. The category sets the transient the meter survives and the energy available in the fault; a CAT II 1000 V rating does not qualify the meter for a CAT III location, and the 600 V CAT III meter is the right one for a 240 V panel.
BENCH: Instruments need protection as well as people. Every RF analyzer and power sensor has a maximum input, often +20 to +30 dBm, and many hobby analyzers only a few dBm; a 1 W LoRa or ham transmitter is +30 dBm. Keep a 30 dB and a 20 dB attenuator, rated for the power, on the bench, and put one in front of the input before the transmitter is keyed.
H2: Practice
P: 1. (Core) Name the measurement category for: an appliance plugged into an outlet; a breaker panel; the utility meter at the service entrance; an ESP32-S3 board running from an 18650 cell.
P: 2. (Core) How much energy does a 100 µF capacitor hold at 400 V?
P: 3. (Core) CAT III 1000 V and CAT IV 600 V share the same 8 kV transient. Which do you choose for work at the service entrance of a 240 V system, and why?
P: 4. (Extension) A 470 µF capacitor at 400 V discharges through 10 kΩ. How long until it is below 50 V?
P: 5. (Extension) An 18650 cell at 3.7 V with 40 mΩ internal resistance is shorted through 10 mΩ of wiring. Estimate the current and the power in the wiring. Why do the books in this series insist on protected cells?
P: 6. (Challenge) A differential probe has a CMRR of 40 dB at the frequencies that make up a 400 V switching edge. You measure a 15 V gate signal on the high side. How large can the error be, and what tool would you choose instead?
