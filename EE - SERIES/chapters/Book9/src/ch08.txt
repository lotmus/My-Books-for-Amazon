H1: Chapter 8. Measuring the Datasheet: Capacitance, Gate Charge, Switching, Recovery, Thermal Impedance, and SPICE Models
P: A power MOSFET's datasheet has about forty numbers, and the curve tracer of Chapter 7 measures perhaps fifteen of them. The rest, the capacitances, the gate charge, the switching energies, the reverse recovery of the body diode, and the thermal impedance curve, come from other test setups, each with its own fixture and its own traps. And the SPICE model at the end of the datasheet is not a measurement at all: it is a best fit of an approximate equation to some of those measurements, valid where it was fitted. This chapter measures the rest and then builds a model the honest way.
MEET: Power-electronics design (choosing a MOSFET or diode by Qg, Coss, and Qrr), device characterization labs, incoming inspection of power parts, and anyone who simulates a circuit and wonders why the simulation and the bench disagree.
H2: 8.1 What the datasheet promises, and where each number comes from
P: Every datasheet line has a test condition, and the condition is part of the number: Vgs(th) at a stated drain current, Rds(on) at a stated gate voltage, current, and temperature, capacitances at a stated drain voltage and frequency, Qg at a stated drain voltage and current, switching times with a stated gate resistor and load. Minimum and maximum values are tested or guaranteed by design; typical values are neither. Graphs show typical behavior. Book 3 explains what each parameter means physically and how it maps to the SPICE model, and Book 6 how a designer uses them; this chapter is about the measurement. The instruments: SMUs and the curve tracer (Chapters 6 and 7), a C-V meter or LCR meter with a DC bias (Chapter 13), a gate-charge tester, a double-pulse test bench with an oscilloscope and wide-band probes (Chapters 16 and 17), and a thermal transient tester.
H2: 8.2 Capacitance against voltage
P: A junction's capacitance falls with reverse voltage, Cj = Cjo/(1 + Vr/Vj)^M, and the three SPICE parameters Cjo, Vj, and M are fitted to a C-V sweep: an LCR meter or C-V unit applies a small AC signal (often 1 MHz, tens of millivolts) on top of a DC bias and measures the capacitance at each bias point, after open and short compensation of the fixture (Chapter 13). A MOSFET has three terminals and three capacitances, and the datasheet gives the combinations a three-terminal measurement can reach: Ciss = Cgs + Cgd (input, drain shorted to source for AC), Coss = Cds + Cgd (output, gate shorted to source for AC), and Crss = Cgd (reverse transfer, measured with the source as the guard). A capacitance analyzer for power devices applies up to hundreds of volts of drain bias through bias tees while it guards the third terminal.
WE: Worked Example 8.1: From Ciss, Coss, and Crss to the three capacitances
PROB: At Vds = 25 V and 1 MHz a power MOSFET reads Ciss = 1000 pF, Coss = 200 pF, and Crss = 50 pF. Find Cgs, Cgd, and Cds.
SOL: Cgd = Crss = 50 pF. Cgs = Ciss − Cgd = 950 pF. Cds = Coss − Cgd = 150 pF. Cgd is the smallest and the most important for switching, because the Miller effect multiplies it by the voltage gain during a transition (Section 8.3). All three depend strongly on Vds, so a single-point value is only a starting point; the datasheet's curves against Vds are what a nonlinear model must fit.
H2: 8.3 Gate charge
P: The gate-charge test drives the gate with a constant current while the drain switches a load at a stated voltage and current, and records Vgs against time. Because the gate current is constant, time is charge: Q = Ig × t. The curve rises to the Miller plateau (charging Cgs: that charge is Qgs), stays flat while the drain voltage swings and Cgd is charged through the Miller effect (Qgd), and then rises again to the final gate voltage (total Qg). The plateau voltage tells you the gate voltage at which the device carries the test current. Gate charge, not capacitance, is what sizes a gate driver, because it includes the nonlinear Cgd over the whole voltage swing.
WE: Worked Example 8.2: Reading a gate-charge curve
PROB: With a constant gate current of 1 mA, the plateau begins at 2.2 µs and ends at 6.0 µs, and Vgs reaches 10 V at 14 µs. Find Qgs, Qgd, and Qg, and estimate the drain-voltage transition time with a 1 A gate driver.
SOL: Qgs = 1 mA × 2.2 µs = 2.2 nC; Qgd = 1 mA × (6.0 − 2.2) µs = 3.8 nC; Qg = 1 mA × 14 µs = 14 nC. The drain voltage swings during the plateau, so with 1 A of gate current the transition takes about Qgd/Ig = 3.8 nC/1 A = 3.8 ns; with a 100 mA driver, 38 ns. Check the test conditions (drain voltage and current) before comparing with a datasheet: Qgd grows with the drain voltage swing.
H2: 8.4 The double-pulse test
P: Switching energies are measured in the circuit they describe: a half-bridge with an inductive load. The double-pulse test turns the device under test on for a first pulse long enough to ramp the inductor current to the test value (I = V t₁/L), turns it off (the turn-off edge is measured at that current), keeps it off briefly while the current freewheels through the other device's diode, and turns it on again for a short second pulse (the turn-on edge is measured, including the diode's reverse recovery). Two pulses keep the energy small, so the device stays at the test temperature. The measurement is all about the probes: a high-bandwidth, low-inductance current measurement (a coaxial shunt, or a Rogowski coil for isolated measurement), a voltage probe with enough bandwidth and the right rating, an isolated probe for the high-side gate (Chapter 4), and deskew: the delay between the voltage and current channels must be calibrated to a fraction of a nanosecond, because energy is the integral of their product.
WE: Worked Example 8.3: Setting the first pulse, and what skew costs
PROB: A double-pulse bench runs at 400 V with a 100 µH inductor, and the test current is 20 A. How long is the first pulse? At turn-off the drain voltage rises from 0 to 400 V in 15 ns and the current then falls from 20 A to 0 in 15 ns. What is Eoff, and what does a 2 ns uncorrected delay of the current channel do to it?
SOL: t₁ = L I/V = 100 µH × 20 A/400 V = 5 µs. Eoff = ½ × 400 V × 20 A × 15 ns + ½ × 400 V × 20 A × 15 ns = 60 + 60 = 120 µJ. If the current trace arrives 2 ns late, the scope multiplies the rising voltage with current that has not started to fall yet, and the integral becomes 136 µJ, 13 % high. The same skew makes Eon read low. Deskew the probes with a deskew fixture or a known resistive edge before every measurement series, and quote the bandwidth and deskew with the result.

H2: 8.5 Reverse recovery
P: A conducting PN diode stores charge, and when it is switched off it first conducts in reverse until that charge is removed. The reverse-recovery current peaks at Irrm and decays; trr is the duration and Qrr the charge, the area under the reverse current. Both depend strongly on the forward current, on di/dt, and on temperature, so they are measured in the double-pulse setup (the diode of the low side recovering during the second turn-on) with the datasheet's di/dt. In the simplest charge-control model the stored charge is the forward current times the transit time Tt, which links Qrr to the SPICE parameter Tt, as a first estimate only.
WE: Worked Example 8.4: Qrr and a first Tt
PROB: At IF = 20 A and a controlled di/dt, a body diode's recovery current peaks at 8 A, and the reverse current lasts 50 ns, roughly a triangle. Estimate Qrr and a first value of Tt.
SOL: Qrr ≈ ½ × 8 A × 50 ns = 200 nC. Tt ≈ Qrr/IF = 200 nC/20 A = 10 ns. That Tt is a starting value for a fit, not a measured physical constant: real diodes recombine part of the charge during the fall of the current, so the measured Qrr depends on di/dt, and a model with a single Tt fits only near the conditions it was fitted at.
H2: 8.6 Thermal impedance
P: The thermal impedance curve Zth(t) tells how far the junction heats in a power pulse of length t. It is measured with the device itself as the thermometer: a temperature-sensitive parameter, usually the forward voltage of the body diode at a small sense current, is calibrated against temperature in an oven (the K-factor, typically about −2 mV/K). The device is then heated with a known power until it is in equilibrium, the power is switched off, and the sense voltage is recorded as the device cools; the cooling curve, converted to temperature and divided by the power, is Zth(t). Thermal transient testers automate this and fit Foster or Cauer RC networks to the curve, which become the thermal model in a simulator (Book 6 and Book 7 use them).
WE: Worked Example 8.5: A point on the Zth curve
PROB: A MOSFET's body diode has a K-factor of −2.0 mV/K at 10 mA. Heated with 10 W, its sense voltage after the power is removed reads 0.450 V; at the ambient temperature it reads 0.650 V. Find the temperature rise and Zth.
SOL: ΔT = (0.650 − 0.450) V/2.0 mV/K = 100 K. Zth = 100 K/10 W = 10 K/W at the heating time used. Repeating with shorter heating pulses, or reading the whole cooling curve, gives Zth against time. The first microseconds after switch-off are disturbed by the electrical transient, so testers extrapolate back to the moment of switch-off, a source of uncertainty worth stating.
H2: 8.7 From measured data to a SPICE model
P: A SPICE model is an approximate equation with adjustable parameters. Fitting it to measurements makes it agree with those measurements, in the range where it was fitted, and nowhere else is it guaranteed to agree. Physics explains why the equation has its shape (Book 3), but the fitted numbers are best-fit values, and some of them absorb effects the equation does not contain. The workflow that keeps a model honest: (1) measure, with the conditions recorded; (2) choose the fit range and state it; (3) fit; (4) plot the residuals, measured minus model, across the fit range, and look for structure as well as size; (5) validate against data that were not used in the fit; (6) document the parameters, the fit range, the conditions, the residuals, and what the model must not be used for; and (7) simulate the model in the circuit simulator and compare once more.

WE: Worked Example 8.6: Fitting a diode, honestly
PROB: Listing 8.1 fits a diode whose measured curve contains two current mechanisms (ideality 1 and 2), 0.6 Ω of series resistance, and 0.3 mV of meter noise, with a single SPICE diode (IS, N, RS) over 1 µA to 20 mA. What does the fit return, how good is it inside the range, and how good outside?
SOL: The fit returns N = 1.046, IS = 6.38 × 10⁻¹⁵ A, and RS = 0.323 Ω, with residuals of 1.25 mV rms and 3.69 mV maximum over 1 µA to 20 mA: an excellent fit. Two of those numbers are not the physics. N is a little above 1 because the fit averages a range where the second mechanism still contributes, and RS is about half the true 0.6 Ω because the raised N already supplies part of the high-current voltage. Outside the fit range the model fails: at 10 to 100 nA, where the ideality-2 current dominates, it is up to 67 mV wrong, and at 30 to 100 mA up to 22 mV. The C-V fit over 0 to 20 V returns CJO = 3.99 pF, VJ = 0.61 V, and M = 0.452. The model line is documented with its range: forward 1 µA to 20 mA at 25 °C, reverse capacitance 0 to 20 V; not for leakage, breakdown, or temperature. Simulators that offer separate recombination-current parameters can extend the low end, at the cost of two more parameters to fit.

MIS: A model that fits well is a true description of the device. A good fit says the equation can be bent to the data in that range; the parameters may compensate for each other and for missing effects, as RS did in Worked Example 8.6. Use a model inside its documented range, and ask for that range when a vendor gives you a model.
BENCH: When the simulation of a power stage and the bench disagree, check the model's conditions before the circuit: at what Vds were its capacitances fitted, at what di/dt its recovery, at what temperature its Rds(on)? A model fitted at 25 °C and low current will not predict a 100 °C hard-switched stage, and the datasheet curves at the right conditions are often the better guide.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) A MOSFET reads Ciss = 2400 pF, Coss = 380 pF, Crss = 60 pF. Find Cgs, Cgd, and Cds.
PP: 2. (Core) A gate-charge test at 2 mA gate current reaches 10 V after 25 µs. What is Qg?
PP: 3. (Core) A double-pulse test runs at 600 V with a 200 µH inductor. How long must the first pulse be for 30 A?
PP: 4. (Extension) A diode's recovery current peaks at 12 A and lasts 80 ns, roughly a triangle, after 30 A of forward current. Estimate Qrr and a first Tt.
PP: 5. (Extension) A K-factor is −2.2 mV/K. After a 25 W heating pulse the sense voltage has fallen by 330 mV. Find ΔT and Zth.
PP: 6. (Extension) A junction capacitance follows Cjo = 10 pF, Vj = 0.7 V, M = 0.5. What is the capacitance at 6.3 V reverse?
PP: 7. (Challenge) In Worked Example 8.3, how much skew in picoseconds would keep the Eoff error below 1 %, assuming the error grows in proportion to the skew?
PP: 8. (Challenge) A colleague fits a MOSFET's Id-Vgs curve from 1 µA to 1 A and reports that the model's subthreshold slope "proves" the slope factor is 1.9. Write the three questions you ask before using the model for a low-power sensor bias circuit.
ANS: 1. Cgd = Crss = 60 pF; Cgs = 2400 − 60 = 2340 pF; Cds = 380 − 60 = 320 pF. The usual mistake is to take Coss as Cds and forget that it includes Cgd.
ANS: 2. Qg = 2 mA × 25 µs = 50 nC. The usual mistake is to integrate the voltage instead of using Q = I t at constant current.
ANS: 3. t₁ = L I/V = 200 µH × 30 A/600 V = 10 µs. The usual mistake is to use V/L × I, which has the wrong units.
ANS: 4. Qrr ≈ ½ × 12 A × 80 ns = 480 nC; Tt ≈ 480 nC/30 A = 16 ns, as a starting value only, valid near this di/dt. The usual mistake is to forget the ½ for the triangle (960 nC).
ANS: 5. ΔT = 330 mV/2.2 mV/K = 150 K; Zth = 150 K/25 W = 6 K/W at that pulse length. The usual mistake is to drop the sign of the K-factor and get a temperature fall.
ANS: 6. C = 10 pF/(1 + 6.3/0.7)^0.5 = 10/√10 = 3.16 pF. The usual mistake is to forget the 1 + and compute (9)^0.5 = 3, giving 3.33 pF.
ANS: 7. 2 ns caused 13.3 %, so 1 % allows about 2 ns × 1/13.3 = 150 ps. The usual mistake is to think the scope's sample interval sets the limit; deskew can be set much finer than one sample with interpolation, and it must be.
ANS: 8. Over which range was the fit made, and what are the residuals in the subthreshold part (a fit dominated by the many points above threshold can bend the subthreshold slope to reduce the error there)? Was the model validated against measured data at the currents the sensor bias will use? At what temperature and drain voltage was it measured, and does the circuit operate there? The usual mistake is to read a fitted parameter as a physical property of the device, outside the range where it was fitted.
