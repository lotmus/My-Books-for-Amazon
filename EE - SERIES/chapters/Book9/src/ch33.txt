PART: Part IX. Beyond the Wire: Optical, Thermal, and EMC Instruments
PINTRO: Some of the most useful instruments on an electrical bench do not measure volts at all. This part, a single chapter, covers the optical power meter, OTDR, and optical spectrum analyzer that fiber links need; the thermal camera that finds the hot component before it fails; and the precompliance kit (near-field probes, LISN, antenna, and analyzer) that tells you whether a product will pass EMC testing before you pay the test lab. Each gets the same treatment as the electrical instruments: principle, specifications, pitfalls, and a worked example.
H1: Chapter 33. Optical, Thermal, and EMC Precompliance Instruments
P: The product fails radiated emissions at 150 MHz by 4 dB, at a lab that charges by the day. Back at the bench, a home-made loop probe on a cheap analyzer finds the 150 MHz harmonic strongest on the USB cable's shield; a clip-on ferrite and a better ground at the connector bring it down by 9 dB. The second lab visit passes. Precompliance does not replace the lab; it makes the lab a formality.
MEET: Fiber-optic installation and data-center links, telecom equipment, power-electronics and PCB thermal design, reliability and failure analysis, building and electrical inspection, and every product that must meet FCC or CISPR emission limits.
H2: 33.1 Optical power meters, light sources, and OTDRs
P: An optical power meter is a calibrated photodiode (silicon for 400 to 1100 nm, InGaAs for 800 to 1700 nm) followed by a transimpedance amplifier and a log converter, reading in dBm or W. Its responsivity depends on the wavelength, so the meter must be set to the source's wavelength; typical uncertainty is ±3 to ±5 % (about ±0.2 dB) at the calibration wavelengths. With a stable light source it measures link loss (insertion loss); the loss budget adds fiber attenuation (about 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm and 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm for standard single-mode fiber), connectors (0.75 dB maximum each in TIA-568, 0.3 dB typical), and splices.
P: An optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) is the fiber's TDR (Chapter 18): it sends short pulses and records the Rayleigh backscatter and reflections against distance, showing fiber attenuation as a slope and connectors, splices, bends, and breaks as events. Its pulse width sets the resolution (10 ns in glass with group index 1.468 is about 1 m) and the dead zones after a reflection; longer pulses reach farther with less resolution. A launch fiber of a few hundred meters lets the OTDR see the first connector.
P: An optical spectrum analyzer (OSA) uses a diffraction grating (or a tunable filter) as its RBW filter, 0.02 to 1 nm wide, to show power against wavelength: laser side-mode suppression, WDM channel powers, and OSNR. OSNR is quoted in a 0.1 nm reference bandwidth, 12.5 GHz at 1550 nm.
WE: Worked Example 33.1: A fiber link budget
PROB: A 10 km single-mode link at 1310 nm has two connector pairs (0.5 dB each, measured) and four fusion splices (0.1 dB each). The transmitter launches −3 dBm and the receiver needs −20 dBm. What is the margin? The meter was left set to 1550 nm; how large an error can that cause on an InGaAs meter?
SOL: Loss = 10 × 0.35 + 2 × 0.5 + 4 × 0.1 = 4.9 dB; received −7.9 dBm; margin 12.1 dB. InGaAs responsivity is about 0.9 A/W at 1310 nm and 1.0 A/W at 1550 nm; a meter set to 1550 nm divides the 1310 nm photocurrent by the 1550 nm responsivity and reads 10 log(1.0/0.9) = 0.46 dB low. Small against the margin, but as large as the connectors being judged.
H2: 33.2 Thermal cameras
P: A thermal camera images infrared radiance, usually 8 to 14 µm, on a microbolometer array (an array of tiny resistors heated by the radiation), and converts radiance to temperature with three settings: emissivity of the surface, reflected apparent temperature (what the surface mirrors), and distance and humidity (for the atmosphere). Specifications: resolution (160 × 120 to 640 × 480 and beyond), field of view, NETD (the noise-equivalent temperature difference, 30 to 70 mK), and accuracy (typically ±2 °C or ±2 % of reading). The instantaneous field of view (IFOV, the angle of one pixel) and the distance give the spot size; reliable readings need a target at least about three pixels across.
P: Emissivity is the dominant error. Matte paint, solder mask, and most plastics are 0.9 to 0.95; bare copper, aluminum, and tinned leads can be 0.05 to 0.2 and mostly reflect the room. Put a dot of matte black paint or Kapton tape (ε about 0.95) on shiny parts, or measure ε by comparison with a thermocouple. Cameras also cannot see through glass or most plastic windows, which are opaque at 8 to 14 µm.

WE: Worked Example 33.2: Emissivity and spot size
PROB: A camera set to ε = 1 reads 60.0 °C on a component with ε = 0.85 in a 25 °C room. What is its temperature? The camera has 640 pixels across a 25° field of view; at 0.5 m, what is the smallest part it measures reliably?
SOL: In the camera's 8 to 14 µm band, the listing gives 65.3 °C (the T⁴ approximation gives 65.1 °C): the reading was 5 °C low. IFOV = 25° in radians/640 = 0.4363/640 = 0.68 mrad; at 0.5 m one pixel covers 0.34 mm, and three pixels about 1.0 mm. A 0402 resistor (1.0 × 0.5 mm) is at the limit: use a close-up lens or move closer.

H2: 33.3 EMC precompliance
P: Formal EMC tests use an EMI receiver with CISPR detectors (Chapter 22), calibrated antennas in a semi-anechoic chamber or open-area test site, and a LISN for conducted emissions. Precompliance uses the same principles with cheaper tools: a spectrum analyzer with a peak detector (and quasi-peak if available), a LISN (50 Ω/50 µH, CISPR 16-1-2), near-field H and E probes to find sources on the board, a current clamp to measure common-mode current on cables, and a small biconical or log-periodic antenna at 3 m in an ordinary room. The goal is relative: find the sources, compare fixes, and correlate with one lab result.
P: The arithmetic: dBµV = dBm + 107 (50 Ω); field strength dBµV/m = reading in dBµV + antenna factor (dB/m) + cable loss − preamplifier gain. CISPR 32 Class B radiated limits at 3 m are 40 dBµV/m (30 to 230 MHz) and 47 dBµV/m (230 MHz to 1 GHz), quasi-peak; at 10 m they are 10 dB lower. Peak readings are at least as high as quasi-peak, so a peak scan that passes with margin passes; frequencies within about 6 dB need a quasi-peak remeasurement. Common-mode current on cables is the usual radiator: a cable of length L carrying common-mode current I at frequency f radiates about E = 1.257e−6 × I × f × L/d (V/m, at distance d, for a short cable; Ott's estimate).

WE: Worked Example 33.3: Field strength, and the cable that fails
PROB: At 150 MHz the analyzer reads −82.0 dBm; the antenna factor is 10.6 dB/m and the cable loss 1.6 dB. What is the field strength and the margin to Class B at 3 m? Then, a current clamp measures 5 µA of common-mode current at 100 MHz on a 1 m USB cable: estimate the field at 3 m.
SOL: −82.0 + 107 = 25.0 dBµV; field 25.0 + 10.6 + 1.6 = 37.2 dBµV/m; margin 40 − 37.2 = 2.8 dB, inside the 6 dB precompliance margin: remeasure with quasi-peak and fix it. The cable: E = 1.257e−6 × 5e−6 × 1e8 × 1/3 = 2.1e−4 V/m = 46.4 dBµV/m, 6.4 dB over the limit. Five microamperes is enough to fail; a ferrite with 10 to 15 dB of common-mode impedance at 100 MHz, or a better shield termination at the connector, is the usual fix.

JOB: A hardware engineer at a consumer-electronics or industrial-controls company is the team's EMC person: running precompliance scans on every board spin, chasing emissions with near-field probes and current clamps, booking and attending the formal lab tests, and writing the fix list. A typical issue: a product passes at the lab in March and fails in June after a "cost-down" that changed the Ethernet magnetics; the precompliance setup, kept unchanged with a reference product, shows the 125 MHz rise in an hour. Tools: a spectrum analyzer or EMI receiver with QP, a LISN, near-field probe sets, a current clamp, a biconilog antenna, a thermal camera, and a log of reference scans.
MIS: Precompliance results are absolute. An ordinary room has reflections, ambient signals (FM radio, cellular), and no calibrated ground plane; readings can be 6 dB or more off. Use precompliance for comparisons (before and after a fix, this board against the one that passed) and correlate with one lab measurement.
BENCH: Scan the room before switching the product on. Save that ambient trace: FM broadcast, cellular, Wi-Fi, and the bench's own switching supplies will be in every later scan, and you want to know which spikes are yours.

PP: 1. (Core) Convert −60 dBm (50 Ω) to dBµV.
PP: 2. (Core) What is 0.1 nm in GHz at 1550 nm?
PP: 3. (Core) An OTDR uses 100 ns pulses in fiber with group index 1.468. Approximate two-point resolution?
PP: 4. (Extension) A camera set to ε = 0.95 measures a solder-mask surface (ε 0.95) correctly at 61.6 °C. A neighbor measures the bare copper next to it with the same setting. Why will it read near the room temperature?
PP: 5. (Extension) Class B 3 m limit 40 dBµV/m at 100 MHz; antenna factor 9 dB/m, cable 1.5 dB. What is the highest analyzer reading in dBm that keeps a 6 dB margin?
PP: 6. (Challenge) Plan a one-day precompliance session for a new board with a USB-C connector and a switching regulator: setup, order of measurements, and what you record so that results are comparable later.
ANS: 1. −60 + 107 = 47 dBµV. The usual mistake is to use +90 (dBm to dBmV is +47).
ANS: 2. Δf = cΔλ/λ² = 3e8 × 0.1e−9/(1550e−9)² = 12.5 GHz. The usual mistake is to use the wavelength instead of its square.
ANS: 3. Distance per pulse length: 3e8/1.468 × 100e−9/2 = 10.2 m. The usual mistake is to forget the factor 2 (round trip).
ANS: 4. Bare copper has ε of about 0.05 to 0.1 and reflects the surroundings; with ε set to 0.95, the camera interprets the reflected room radiation as the object's own and reads close to the room, even if the copper is at 60 °C. The usual mistake is to conclude that the copper is cool.
ANS: 5. Target field 40 − 6 = 34 dBµV/m; reading 34 − 9 − 1.5 = 23.5 dBµV = 23.5 − 107 = −83.5 dBm. The usual mistake is to subtract the antenna factor with the wrong sign.
ANS: 6. Morning: ambient scans (radiated and conducted, product off), then the reference product if one exists. Conducted emissions with the LISN from 150 kHz to 30 MHz, peak and average, product in its worst mode. Radiated at 3 m (antenna height and polarization fixed and recorded), 30 MHz to 1 GHz, peak. Afternoon: near-field probe the regulator's switching loop and the USB-C area, and clamp the cables to find the common-mode currents behind the worst peaks; try fixes one at a time and rescan. Record the setup (photos, cable routing, antenna position, analyzer settings, firmware and operating mode) and save traces with names, so the next spin repeats the same conditions. The usual mistake is to move the cables between scans and attribute the change to a fix.
