H1: Chapter 38. Building the Home Lab, and Instruments You Make Yourself
P: The best bench in the house is the one that tells you when it is wrong. A collection of good instruments with no references is a collection of opinions: the DMM says 2.499 V, the scope says 2.52 V, and the supply's display says 2.50 V. A $40 voltage reference, a GPS-disciplined 10 MHz oscillator, and a few precision resistors turn those opinions into measurements, and a weekend with a microcontroller adds a curve tracer that no catalog sells at that price.
MEET: Hobby and maker benches, home offices of hardware engineers, small consultancies, school and university teaching labs, and anyone who has to justify each instrument from their own pocket.
H2: 38.1 Safety and the bench itself
P: Start with the parts that prevent harm (Chapter 4). Power the bench from a circuit with a residual-current device (a GFCI in the US, which trips at about 5 mA); add an isolation transformer for any work on mains-connected circuits, so a grounded scope probe does not short the line (Chapter 17), and remember that a variac alone does not isolate. Use current-limited supplies with the limit set before connecting, a fused and switched outlet strip within reach, safety glasses when anything charged or spinning is on the bench, an ESD mat and wrist strap (1 MΩ to ground), good light, and a fire extinguisher rated for electrical fires. Lithium cells and capacitor banks get their own containers.
H2: 38.2 What to buy, in what order
P: Buy by what you measure. A first tier (about US $300 to $600 in 2026) is a CAT III true-rms handheld DMM, a 12-bit 70 MHz four-channel scope (Chapter 37), and a basic linear bench supply. A second tier (about $1500 to $2500 in total) adds a programmable triple-output supply with LAN, a two-channel function generator, a handheld LCR meter, a NanoVNA or LiteVNA, a tinySA, an SDR dongle, and a set of references. A third tier (about $5000 to $10 000) is mostly used lab equipment: a 6½-digit bench DMM, a spectrum analyzer with tracking generator, an SMU, and a 6 GHz VNA. Used instruments are the best value in this field; buy from sellers who show the self-test passing, check the instrument against your references on arrival, and budget for one that needs repair.
H2: 38.3 References: the bench's calibration lab
P: Three references cover most of a home bench. A voltage reference module (an ADR4525 or LM399-class reference with a stated initial accuracy, around ±0.02 % for a good one) checks DMMs and scope gains. A GPS-disciplined oscillator (GPSDO) gives a 10 MHz signal accurate to about 1e−11 after averaging, for counters, analyzers, and SDRs (Chapter 15). A set of precision resistors (0.01 % to 0.1 %, low temperature coefficient, with four-terminal connections for the low values) checks resistance ranges and LCR meters. None of these is a traceable calibration, but each turns "I trust it" into "I checked it against X on date Y," which is what a calibration record says too (Chapter 2).
WE: Worked Example 38.1: Checking the DMM and the counter
PROB: A 4½-digit DMM is specified at ±(0.05 % of reading + 3 counts) on its 6 V range (0.1 mV resolution). It reads 2.4991 V on a 2.5000 V reference of ±0.02 %. Does it pass, and what is the test uncertainty ratio? Then a counter with a TCXO timebase reads the GPSDO's 10 MHz as 10.000 012 MHz: what is its timebase error, and what does it read on a 100 MHz signal?
SOL: DMM tolerance ±(1.25 + 0.3) mV = ±1.55 mV; reference ±0.5 mV; test uncertainty ratio 1.55/0.5 = 3.1, below the usual 4, so guard-band (Chapter 2): accept if |error| ≤ 1.55 − 0.5 = 1.05 mV. The error is −0.9 mV: pass. The counter reads 12 Hz high on 10 MHz, +1.2 ppm; on 100 MHz it reads 120 Hz high. Use the GPSDO as the counter's external reference, and the error disappears.
H2: 38.4 The mini curve tracer
P: Chapter 7 promised a reader-lab curve tracer. Listing 38.1 is its firmware and host logic. A Raspberry Pi Pico or an ESP32-S3 (neither has a usable DAC of its own) drives an MCP4728 quad 12-bit DAC with its internal reference and gain 2, so one code is 1 mV and full scale 4.095 V; channel A drives the collector or drain through a rail-to-rail op-amp and an emitter follower (a BD139) and a 10 Ω sense resistor; channel B drives the base through 10 kΩ (or the gate directly). An ADS1115 16-bit ADC on the ±4.096 V range (125 µV per code) reads the drop across the sense resistor, the collector voltage, and the base voltage. Each point is pulsed, a few milliseconds on and 50 ms off, so a TO-92 transistor stays near room temperature (Chapter 7's self-heating lesson). The output is the CSV that Listing 7.1 reads, and the same Tracer class runs against a simulated transistor on a PC, which is how this book checked it.

WE: Worked Example 38.2: What the mini tracer can and cannot do
PROB: Find the current resolution, the highest collector voltage at 60 mA, and the peak power in the device at that point. The Early-voltage fit over 1.0 to 3.0 V gives 76 V at Ib ≈ 200 µA and predicts 41.85 mA at 3.48 V, where the tracer measures 41.88 mA. State the model's valid range.
SOL: Current resolution 125 µV/10 Ω = 12.5 µA. At 60 mA the sense resistor drops 0.6 V, so Vce reaches 4.0 − 0.6 = 3.4 V; peak power 3.4 V × 60 mA = 0.20 W, about 0.02 W on average at 10 % duty, well inside a TO-92 rating. The validation point differs by 0.03 mA, 0.06 %, so the straight-line output model holds where it was fitted and slightly beyond: valid for Vce from 1.0 to 3.5 V, Ic up to about 63 mA, at room temperature, for this transistor. It says nothing about breakdown, high current, or temperature; for those, Chapter 7's instruments are needed.

H2: 38.5 More instruments worth building
P: A Kelvin milliohm adapter: a bench supply set to a 1.000 A constant current through the unknown, and a DMM measuring the voltage on separate sense leads; 1 µV of DMM resolution is 1 µΩ, and reversing the current and averaging removes thermal EMFs (Chapter 14). A return-loss bridge (a resistive directional bridge) turns a tinySA's tracking output or a noise source into a scalar network analyzer for filters and antennas. A noise source (a reverse-biased transistor junction in avalanche, followed by an amplifier) shows a filter's response on a tinySA or SDR in one sweep. A 10 MHz distribution amplifier shares the GPSDO among instruments. Each is a weekend project with published designs, and each comes with the same rule as the tracer: measure something known before trusting it.
JOB: A laboratory engineer at a university or technical college runs the electronics teaching labs: specifying 30 identical benches on a fixed budget, keeping them calibrated and repaired, writing the lab exercises, and teaching students to doubt their instruments. A typical issue: one bench's scope reads every amplitude 8 % low; the reference check at the start of the semester catches it, and the fault is a probe set to 10:1 on a scope channel set to 1:1. Tools: low-cost 12-bit scopes, function generators, and supplies with SCPI for automated checks, voltage references and a GPSDO, and a spreadsheet of every instrument's last check.
MIS: A home-built instrument is as good as its parts' datasheets. It is as good as its check against a known reference; the datasheets say what is possible, the check says what you have.
BENCH: Label every instrument with the date and result of its last reference check, and repeat the check whenever a reading surprises you. Most "the circuit is wrong" moments on a home bench are "the probe was at 10:1" moments.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) The mini tracer with a 4.7 Ω sense resistor: current resolution?
PP: 2. (Core) With the 10 Ω sense resistor and a 4.0 V drive, what is the highest Vce at 80 mA?
PP: 3. (Core) Why does a variac not protect you when probing a mains circuit?
PP: 4. (Extension) A counter reads a GPSDO's 10 MHz as 10.000 023 MHz. Timebase error in ppm, and the reading error on a 433.92 MHz signal?
PP: 5. (Extension) A Kelvin adapter drives 1.000 A through a shunt and the DMM reads 4.73 mV forward and −4.71 mV reversed. Resistance and thermal EMF?
PP: 6. (Challenge) Plan a $1500 home bench for a reader who repairs audio amplifiers and builds 433 MHz IoT sensors. List the instruments, the references, the safety items, and the order of purchase.
ANS: 1. 125 µV/4.7 Ω = 26.6 µA, with a higher current range (up to about 0.2 A before the drive runs out). The usual mistake is to think a smaller sense resistor improves resolution.
ANS: 2. 4.0 − 0.08 × 10 = 3.2 V. The usual mistake is to forget the drop across the sense resistor.
ANS: 3. A variac is an autotransformer: its output is connected to its input, so the circuit is still referenced to the mains neutral and earth, and a grounded scope probe still shorts whatever it touches to earth. Only an isolation transformer (with the scope still grounded) breaks that path. The usual mistake is to treat "adjustable" as "isolated."
ANS: 4. 23 Hz in 10 MHz is +2.3 ppm; at 433.92 MHz the reading is 2.3e−6 × 433.92e6 = 998 Hz high, about 1 kHz. The usual mistake is to apply the 23 Hz offset at every frequency.
ANS: 5. R = (4.73 + 4.71)/2/1.000 A = 4.72 mΩ; thermal EMF (4.73 − 4.71)/2 = 10 µV. The usual mistake is to report the forward reading only.
ANS: 6. First: a CAT III true-rms DMM, a 12-bit 70 MHz scope with 10:1 probes, a 0 to 30 V, 3 A linear supply with current limit, an isolation transformer for amplifier repair, a GFCI outlet strip, and an ESD mat. Second: a function generator (audio distortion and frequency response), a dummy load for amplifiers (resistive, rated for the power), a tinySA and a NanoVNA for the 433 MHz work, an RTL-SDR for watching transmissions. Third: references (a voltage reference module and, for the IoT radios' frequency accuracy, a GPSDO), and a handheld LCR meter. Keep a spreadsheet of checks. The usual mistake is to buy a 200 MHz scope and no dummy load or isolation transformer.
