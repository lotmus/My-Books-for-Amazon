H1: Chapter 38. Building the Home Lab, and Instruments You Make Yourself
P: The best bench in the house is the one that tells you when it is wrong. A collection of good instruments with no references is a collection of opinions: the DMM says 2.499 V, the scope says 2.52 V, and the supply's display says 2.50 V. A $40 voltage reference, a GPS-disciplined 10 MHz oscillator, and a few precision resistors turn those opinions into measurements. The chapter also walks through the code and circuit limits for a low-voltage curve tracer; its curves are simulated, and the hardware still needs validation before a device is connected.
P: Where you meet this: Hobby and maker benches, home offices of hardware engineers, small consultancies, school and university teaching labs, and anyone who has to justify each instrument from their own pocket.
H2: 38.1 Safety and the bench itself
P: Start with the parts that prevent harm (Chapter 4). Power the bench from a circuit with a residual-current device (a GFCI in the US, which trips at about 5 mA); for a node not at earth potential, use a correctly rated differential or isolated probe or a scope designed for isolated-channel measurements (Chapters 4 and 17); keep the bench scope protective-earth connected. An isolation transformer does not make live circuitry safe to touch, and a variac alone does not isolate. Use current-limited supplies with the limit set before connecting, a fused and switched outlet strip within reach, safety glasses when anything charged or spinning is on the bench, an ESD mat and wrist strap (1 MΩ to ground), good light, and a fire extinguisher rated for electrical fires. Lithium cells and capacitor banks get their own containers.
H2: 38.2 What to buy, in what order
P: Buy by what you measure. A first tier (about US $300 to $600 in 2026) is a CAT III true-rms handheld DMM, a 12-bit 70 MHz four-channel scope (Chapter 37), and a basic linear bench supply. A second tier (about $1500 to $2500 in total) adds a programmable triple-output supply with LAN, a two-channel function generator, a handheld LCR meter, a NanoVNA or LiteVNA, a tinySA, an SDR dongle, and a set of references. A third tier (about $5000 to $10 000) is mostly used lab equipment: a 6½-digit bench DMM, a spectrum analyzer with tracking generator, an SMU, and a 6 GHz VNA. Used instruments are the best value in this field; buy from sellers who show the self-test passing, check the instrument against your references on arrival, and budget for one that needs repair.
H2: 38.3 References: the bench's calibration lab
P: Three references cover most of a home bench. A voltage reference module (an ADR4525 or LM399-class reference with a stated initial accuracy, around ±0.02 % for a good one) checks DMMs and scope gains. A GPS-disciplined oscillator (GPSDO) gives a 10 MHz signal accurate to about 1e−11 after averaging, for counters, analyzers, and SDRs (Chapter 15). A set of precision resistors (0.01 % to 0.1 %, low temperature coefficient, with four-terminal connections for the low values) checks resistance ranges and LCR meters. None of these is a traceable calibration, but each turns "I trust it" into "I checked it against X on date Y," which is what a calibration record says too (Chapter 2).
H2: Worked Example 38.1: Checking the DMM and the counter
P: Problem. A 4½-digit DMM is specified at ±(0.05 % of reading + 3 counts) on its 6 V range (0.1 mV resolution). It reads 2.4991 V on a 2.5000 V reference of ±0.02 %. Does it pass, and what is the test uncertainty ratio? Then a counter with a TCXO timebase reads the GPSDO's 10 MHz as 10.000 012 MHz: what is its timebase error, and what does it read on a 100 MHz signal?
P: Solution. DMM tolerance ±(1.25 + 0.3) mV = ±1.55 mV; reference ±0.5 mV; test uncertainty ratio 1.55/0.5 = 3.1, below the usual 4, so guard-band (Chapter 2): accept if |error| ≤ 1.55 − 0.5 = 1.05 mV. The error is −0.9 mV: pass. The counter reads 12 Hz high on 10 MHz, +1.2 ppm; on 100 MHz it reads 120 Hz high. Use the GPSDO as the counter's external reference, and the error disappears.
H2: 38.4 The mini curve tracer
P: Chapter 7 promised a reader-lab curve tracer. Listing 38.1 is a low-voltage NPN BJT demonstration for a Raspberry Pi Pico or ESP32-S3. Channel A of the MCP4728 drives U1, a TI OPA310-Q1 in the DBV five-pin SOT-23 package, as a unity-gain buffer: VOUTA to IN+ (pin 3), OUT (pin 1) directly to IN- (pin 4), V+ (pin 5) to the regulated 5.0 V rail, and V- (pin 2) to common analog ground. Bypass U1 with 100 nF at its supply pins. From U1 OUT, wire two independent 120 ohm, 1%, 1 W resistors in series, then a 47 ohm, 1%, 1 W sense resistor, then the DUT collector; ground the DUT emitter. Channel B drives the DUT base through 100 kohm.
P: Connect ADS1115 AIN0 and AIN1 as Kelvin taps across the sense resistor (AIN0 on its high side, AIN1 at the collector); AIN1-to-ground measures Vce and AIN2-to-ground measures Vbe. Power the MCP4728, ADS1115, and U1 from a regulated 5.0 V rail whose full tolerance range is 4.75 to 5.25 V; use a bidirectional I2C level shifter between the 5 V analog bus and the 3.3 V controller. The ADS1115 +/-4.096 V setting is its differential PGA range, not its pin-voltage limit: keep each analog input between GND and VDD.
P: The CSV reports collector current from the voltage across the 47 ohm sense resistor. Its base-current column is estimated as (DAC B setpoint minus measured VBE)/100 kohm; the circuit does not measure base current directly. Treat it as approximate unless the DAC output and base resistor have been calibrated.
P: With both 120 ohm limiters and the sense resistor at minimum tolerance, a 5.25 V stuck output is limited to 18.5 mA. If either limiter shorts, the remaining limiter and sense resistor still limit current to 31.8 mA. The 1 W parts have ample dissipation margin at these bounds. The DAC's 4.095 V maximum command limits normal current into a shorted DUT to 14.4 mA. These passive bounds do not establish a DUT safe operating area or safe junction temperature. Check the selected transistor data sheet and validate the output and pulse waveform on hardware before use. Do not use the MOSFET gate option without a separately designed and validated drain fixture.
P: On initialization and in every point cleanup path, firmware zeros the base DAC before the collector DAC; during acquisition it establishes collector voltage before applying base bias. Board execution is disabled by default; enable HARDWARE_MODE only after completing the circuit and bench checks. The PC backend uses a synthetic model, not the physical circuit: it omits U1 output swing and settling, analog errors, wiring effects, and device variation. Its curves check the analysis path only. Before connecting a DUT, test the assembled buffer across its commanded range with a dummy load, measure collector-node range and peak current, verify ADC pin voltages, and capture the pulse waveform. Then check the selected DUT's pulse SOA and temperature.

H2: Worked Example 38.2: What the mini tracer can and cannot do
P: Problem. In the seeded simulation, the 30 uA base-current curve has a measured point near Vce = 2.23 V and Ic = 6.180 mA. Find the current resolution, peak DUT power at that point, and illustrative average power at the stated pulse duty. Compare the multi-point Early-voltage fit with the known synthetic model parameter VA = 75 V.
P: Solution. Current resolution is 125 uV/47 ohm = 2.66 uA/count. Peak DUT power at the stated point is 2.23 V x 6.180 mA = 13.8 mW. Each point has about 31 ms of drive followed by 150 ms with both DAC outputs at zero, so its illustrative duty is 31/(31 + 150) = 17.1 % and average DUT power is about 2.36 mW. The 30 uA curve gives a seeded multi-point fit of VA = 77 V; the synthetic model is defined with VA = 75 V. Near 2.23 V, the simulated current is 6.180 mA versus about 6.177 mA predicted by the fit. This simulated agreement does not establish hardware accuracy, safe junction temperature, or compliance with a real DUT pulse safe-operating-area curve; verify those on the actual circuit and selected device.

H2: 38.5 More instruments worth building
P: A Kelvin milliohm adapter: a bench supply set to a 1.000 A constant current through the unknown, and a DMM measuring the voltage on separate sense leads; 1 µV of DMM resolution is 1 µΩ, and reversing the current and averaging removes thermal EMFs (Chapter 14). A return-loss bridge (a resistive directional bridge) turns a tinySA's tracking output or a noise source into a scalar network analyzer for filters and antennas. A noise source (a reverse-biased transistor junction in avalanche, followed by an amplifier) shows a filter's response on a tinySA or SDR in one sweep. A 10 MHz distribution amplifier shares the GPSDO among instruments. Each is a weekend project with published designs, and each comes with the same rule as the tracer: measure something known before trusting it.
P: On the Job
P: A laboratory engineer at a university or technical college runs the electronics teaching labs: specifying 30 identical benches on a fixed budget, keeping them calibrated and repaired, writing the lab exercises, and teaching students to doubt their instruments. A typical issue: one bench's scope reads every amplitude 8 % low; the reference check at the start of the semester catches it, and the fault is a probe set to 10:1 on a scope channel set to 1:1. Tools: low-cost 12-bit scopes, function generators, and supplies with SCPI for automated checks, voltage references and a GPSDO, and a spreadsheet of every instrument's last check.
P: Common misconception. A home-built instrument is as good as its parts' datasheets. It is as good as its check against a known reference; the datasheets say what is possible, the check says what you have.
BENCH: Label every instrument with the date and result of its last reference check, and repeat the check whenever a reading surprises you. Most "the circuit is wrong" moments on a home bench are "the probe was at 10:1" moments. Before connecting a DUT to the mini tracer, replace it with a 1.00 ohm, 1%, 0.5 W resistor, command 4.0 V on channel A with base drive off, and measure U1 output voltage and collector-path current. With both 120 ohm limiters intact, expect about 14.0 mA at a measured 4.0 V. For the single-fault bound, assume one limiter has shorted: the minimum path resistance is 118.8 + 46.53 + 0.99 = 166.32 ohm, so the current at 4.0 V is at most 24.1 mA. Scale that bound using the measured output voltage, and investigate any higher reading. Confirm the analog rail never exceeds 5.25 V and record the measured voltage and resistor values. This verifies the passive cap in that setup; it does not qualify a DUT pulse safe operating area or temperature.
