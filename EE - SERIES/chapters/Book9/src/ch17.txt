P: Where you meet this: Every scope measurement, switching power stages, gate drives, high-speed digital, power-rail ripple, current in motors and inductors, and double-pulse tests (Chapter 8).
H2: 17.1 The passive probe
P: The standard 10× passive probe has a 9 MΩ tip resistor with a compensating capacitor, forming a divider with the scope's 1 MΩ input, so the circuit sees 10 MΩ in parallel with roughly 10 to 15 pF. The resistive load is negligible; the capacitance is not. At 100 MHz, 11 pF is a reactance of 145 Ω, which loads a 50 Ω source noticeably and slows its edges. Compensation adjusts the probe's capacitor so the divider is flat with frequency: connect the probe to the scope's square-wave calibrator and turn the trimmer until the corners are square, every time the probe moves to another channel or scope. 1× passive probes have a much higher capacitance (often tens of pF) and low bandwidth, and are for small, slow signals only.
H2: Worked Example 17.1: Probe loading at 100 MHz
P: Problem. A 10× probe with 11 pF loads a node driven from a 50 Ω source impedance. What is the amplitude error at 100 MHz, and what rise time does the probe add to an edge?
P: Solution. Xc = 1/(2π × 100 MHz × 11 pF) = 145 Ω. The divider is |−j145/(50 − j145)| = 145/153.4 = 0.945: the node's 100 MHz component drops 5.5 % (and shifts in phase), with the probe connected. The RC of 50 Ω and 11 pF is 0.55 ns, a 10 % to 90 % rise of 2.2 RC = 1.21 ns added to the node's own edge. An active probe with 0.9 pF has a reactance of 1768 Ω at 100 MHz and loads the node by 0.04 %. In a release check with a 97 mV limit for that component, a real 100 mV signal would display about 94.5 mV and could pass falsely. The probe has changed the quantity being judged.
H2: 17.2 Ground leads
P: The probe's ground lead is an inductor, roughly 1 nH per millimeter, and with the probe's tip capacitance it forms a series resonant circuit excited by every fast edge. A 15 cm lead (about 150 nH) with 11 pF resonates near 124 MHz; a ground spring of about 10 nH moves the resonance to 480 MHz and lowers its Q below one. The lead also forms a loop that picks up magnetic fields from nearby switching currents, which appears as "ringing" or noise that is not in the circuit: touch the probe tip to its own ground clip at the same point and look; whatever remains is pickup.
H2: Worked Example 17.2: The ground lead's resonance
P: Problem. Find the resonance and Q of a 150 nH ground lead with an 11 pF probe, driven from 50 Ω, and of a 10 nH spring.
P: Solution. f = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π√(150 nH × 11 pF)) = 124 MHz, with Q = √(L/C)/R = √(150 nH/11 pF)/50 Ω = 117/50 = 2.3, so edges ring visibly. With 10 nH: f = 480 MHz and Q = 30/50 = 0.60, overdamped, no ringing; and 480 MHz is above most 350 MHz scopes' bandwidth.

H2: 17.3 Active, differential, and isolated probes
P: An active probe puts a small amplifier at the tip, so the circuit sees well under 1 pF and typically 1 MΩ or more; bandwidths run from 1 GHz to beyond 30 GHz, and the input range is small (a few volts). Differential probes measure the voltage between two points that are both away from ground, such as a high-side gate or a shunt in the supply line, and their quality figure is the common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR), which falls with frequency. Optically isolated probes (Tektronix's IsoVu is an example) and isolated-channel scopes remove the ground connection entirely and keep high CMRR at high frequency; they are the tool for GaN and SiC switch nodes. Power-rail probes measure millivolts of ripple on a DC rail with a 1:1 path, a built-in offset, and 50 kΩ DC loading. Never float a scope by disconnecting its protective earth (Chapter 4).
H2: Worked Example 17.3: CMRR on a half-bridge
P: Problem. A differential probe with 60 dB CMRR at 1 MHz and 30 dB at 100 MHz measures the high-side gate voltage of a 400 V half-bridge whose switch node slews in a few nanoseconds. What error can the common mode produce?
P: Solution. The switch node swings 400 V of common mode. At 1 MHz components, 60 dB is a factor of 1000: 0.4 V of error. The fast edge's energy is near 100 MHz, where 30 dB is a factor of 31.6: up to 12.6 V of false signal on a gate drive of 15 V. The gate waveform during switching is then the probe's, not the gate's. An isolated probe or a measurement referenced to the source pin with a short, isolated connection is required.
H2: 17.4 Current probes
P: Clamp-on current probes combine a Hall sensor for DC and low frequencies with a current transformer for high frequencies, reaching about 50 to 120 MHz and tens of amperes; they need degaussing and zeroing before use and add a small insertion impedance. Rogowski coils are flexible, do not saturate, and fit around a TO-247 leg, but have no DC response. Current transformers and coaxial shunts serve double-pulse tests (Chapter 8). Every current probe has a propagation delay different from the voltage probe's, so power measurements need deskew.
P: Listing 17.1 — Deskew of a voltage and a current probe: cross-correlate the two channels' records of a common edge, find the delay to sub-sample resolution, and report the energy error it would cause; a simulation mode uses the edge of Chapter 8 (Python with NumPy).
P: """Deskew a voltage and a current channel: find the delay between their records of a common edge bycross-correlating the derivatives, refine it to a fraction of a sample with a parabola, and show whatthe uncorrected skew does to a switching-energy integral.Run:  python l17_1_deskew.py      (simulation: the turn-off edge of Chapter 8, current probe 2.0 ns late)With real data, load the two channels from the scope (Listing 16.1) instead of make_records()."""import numpy as npdef make_records(skew=2.0e-9, dt=0.1e-9):    t = np.arange(-10e-9, 50e-9, dt)    v = np.clip(t / 15e-9, 0, 1) * 400                       # drain voltage 0 -> 400 V in 15 ns    i = lambda x: 20 * np.clip(1 - (x - 15e-9) / 15e-9, 0, 1)    return t, v, i(t - skew), i(t)def delay(t, a, b):    """Delay of b relative to a, in seconds, from the cross-correlation of their derivatives."""    da, db = np.gradient(a), np.gradient(b)    c = np.correlate(db, da, mode='full')    k = int(np.argmax(np.abs(c)))    if 0 < k < c.size - 1:                                    # parabolic refinement        y0, y1, y2 = abs(c[k - 1]), abs(c[k]), abs(c[k + 1])        k = k + 0.5 * (y0 - y2) / (y0 - 2 * y1 + y2)    return (k - (a.size - 1)) * (t[1] - t[0])def energy(t, v, i):    return float(np.sum(v * i) * (t[1] - t[0]))if __name__ == '__main__':    t, v, i_meas, i_true = make_records()    # the probes are deskewed on a fixture where both see the same edge: here the current edge itself    d = delay(t, i_true, i_meas)    print('measured skew of the current channel: %.2f ns' % (d * 1e9))    i_fixed = np.interp(t + d, t, i_meas)    print('Eoff uncorrected %.1f uJ, deskewed %.1f uJ, true %.1f uJ'          % (energy(t, v, i_meas) * 1e6, energy(t, v, i_fixed) * 1e6, energy(t, v, i_true) * 1e6))
P: Common misconception. A 10 MΩ probe does not load the circuit. Its resistance does not; its 10 to 15 pF of capacitance does at every frequency above a few megahertz, as Worked Example 17.1 showed.
BENCH: Keep a probe-tip kit next to the scope: ground springs, a tip adapter soldered into the board at critical nodes (a short coaxial or pin pair), and a spare compensated probe. Solder the measurement point into the design: two pins 2.54 mm apart at the switch node and the gate save more time than any scope feature. Record probe model, attenuation and grounding for every limit decision. If the 97 mV threshold passes only with the 10× passive probe, repeat with a lower-capacitance probe before release.
