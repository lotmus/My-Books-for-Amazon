H1: Chapter 13. LCR Meters and Impedance Analyzers
P: The 10 µF ceramic capacitor on the bill of materials measures 10.1 µF on the LCR meter at 1 kHz and 1 V. On the board, at 5 V of DC bias and 500 kHz, it behaves like 3.5 µF with an inductor in series. Both numbers are right. A capacitor is not a number; it is an impedance that depends on frequency, level, bias, and temperature, and an LCR meter tells you its value only at the conditions you set.

H2: 13.1 What the meter measures
P: An LCR meter applies a sine voltage at a set frequency and level, measures the current through the device and its phase, and computes the complex impedance Z = V/I. Everything else on its display, C, L, R, D, Q, ESR, and the phase angle, is derived from Z through a circuit model: a series model (a capacitor with ESR in series) or a parallel model (with a leakage resistance in parallel). Inside, most meters up to a few megahertz use an auto-balancing bridge: the device's low terminal is held at a virtual ground by an amplifier, which forces the device current through a range resistor where it is measured. Impedance analyzers extend the idea to tens or hundreds of megahertz, and above that the RF I-V method and the VNA's reflection measurement (Chapter 25) take over. Bench examples: Keysight's E4980A (20 Hz to 2 MHz) and E4990A (to 120 MHz), and LCR meters from Hioki, GW Instek, Rigol, and Siglent, plus handheld LCR meters at 100 kHz.
P: Connections decide accuracy. The four-terminal-pair connection carries current and voltage on separate coaxial cables whose shields return the current, so the cable's own field does not couple into the measurement. Open and short compensation measures the fixture with nothing in it and with a shorting bar, and subtracts its stray admittance and residual impedance. Low impedances (milliohms, microhenries) need short compensation; high impedances (picofarads, megohms) need open compensation.
WE: Worked Example 13.1: ESR, D, and Q of an electrolytic capacitor
PROB: A 10 µF capacitor measures, in the series model at 1 kHz, Cs = 10.0 µF and ESR = 0.08 Ω. Find the reactance, the dissipation factor D, and Q.
SOL: Xc = 1/(2π × 1000 × 10 µF) = 15.92 Ω. D = ESR/Xc = 0.08/15.92 = 0.0050, and Q = 1/D = 199. D is the ratio of loss to stored energy; for a capacitor it is the usual quality figure, while inductors are specified by Q.
WE: Worked Example 13.2: What the fixture adds
PROB: A 1 µH inductor is measured at 100 kHz in a fixture with 15 nH of residual inductance, without short compensation. A 10 pF capacitor is measured at 1 MHz in a fixture with 0.5 pF of stray capacitance, without open compensation. Find both errors.
SOL: The inductor's reactance is 2π × 100 kHz × 1 µH = 0.628 Ω; the fixture adds 2π × 100 kHz × 15 nH = 0.0094 Ω in series, a 1.5 % error. The capacitor reads 10.5 pF, a 5 % error, because the stray capacitance is in parallel. Compensation reduces both to the repeatability of the fixture, typically a few tenths of a percent.
H2: 13.2 Series or parallel, and why the numbers differ
P: The meter's model is a choice, not a measurement: one complex impedance can be written as a series or a parallel circuit, and the two give different capacitances whenever the loss is not negligible: Cs = Cp(1 + D²). Choose the model that matches the dominant loss. Large capacitors and low impedances: series (ESR dominates). Small capacitors and high impedances: parallel (leakage dominates). Inductors with core loss: parallel; air-core inductors: series.
WE: Worked Example 13.3: The same part, two capacitances
PROB: A 10 nF capacitor with 100 kΩ of parallel leakage (a lossy film or a sensor element) is measured at 1 kHz. What do the parallel and series models report?
SOL: Xc = 1/(2π × 1000 × 10 nF) = 15.92 kΩ. In the parallel model Cp = 10 nF and D = Xc/Rp = 15.92/100 = 0.159. The series model reports Cs = Cp(1 + D²) = 10 × (1 + 0.0253) = 10.25 nF. Two technicians with two settings disagree by 2.5 % on the same part; the report must name the model.
H2: 13.3 Frequency, level, and bias
P: Ceramic capacitors of class 2 dielectrics (X7R, X5R) lose capacitance with DC bias, often more than half at rated voltage in small cases, and change with the AC level too; datasheet curves or a meter with DC bias show it. Inductors with cores saturate with DC current, which needs a bias current source. Every real component also has parasitics: a capacitor has ESL in series, and above its self-resonant frequency (SRF) it behaves as an inductor. An impedance analyzer's sweep of |Z| against frequency shows all of this at once, and it is the first measurement to make on a decoupling capacitor, a ferrite, or a choke.

WE: Worked Example 13.4: Self-resonance
PROB: Find the self-resonant frequency of a 10 µF capacitor with 1 nH of ESL and 10 mΩ of ESR, and |Z| at 100 kHz and 10 MHz.
SOL: SRF = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π√(1 nH × 10 µF)) = 1.59 MHz, where |Z| = ESR = 10 mΩ. At 100 kHz, Xc = 0.159 Ω and XL = 0.6 mΩ, so |Z| ≈ 0.159 Ω. At 10 MHz, XL = 62.8 mΩ and Xc = 1.6 mΩ, so the net reactance is 61 mΩ and, with the ESR, |Z| = 62 mΩ: the capacitor is now a small inductor, and adding more of the same value in parallel helps less than lowering the mounting inductance.

MIS: An LCR meter reads the capacitor's value. It reads the impedance at the frequency, level, and bias you set, in the model you chose. A capacitor's "value" on the board can be half of what the meter showed at 1 kHz and 1 V.
BENCH: After compensation, measure a known part (a precision capacitor, or a short and an open again) before the real parts. If the "short" reads 50 mΩ, the compensation was done with the wrong cable position or the fixture's screw was loose. Keep the cables still between compensation and measurement.

PP: 3. (Core) A part has Cp = 100 pF and D = 0.3. What does the series model report?
PP: 4. (Extension) A 100 nF capacitor with 0.8 nH ESL: what is its SRF?
PP: 5. (Extension) An X7R capacitor reads 22 µF at 0 V and 9 µF at 5 V bias. A circuit needs at least 10 µF at 5 V. How many in parallel?
PP: 6. (Challenge) You must measure a 50 mΩ, 2 µH power inductor's inductance at its rated 10 A. Describe the setup, the model, and what changes with current.
ANS: 1. Xc = 1/(2π × 1000 × 4.7 µF) = 33.9 Ω; D = 0.2/33.9 = 0.0059. The usual mistake is to divide ESR by the capacitance instead of the reactance.
ANS: 2. Open for the 2 pF capacitor (the stray capacitance is in parallel and comparable); short for the 100 nH inductor (the residual inductance is in series). The usual mistake is to do only one of them for all parts; do both, always.
ANS: 3. Cs = Cp(1 + D²) = 100 × 1.09 = 109 pF. The usual mistake is to think the two models only differ in the resistor, not the capacitance.
ANS: 4. 1/(2π√(0.8 nH × 100 nF)) = 17.8 MHz. The usual mistake is to use nH and nF without converting units.
ANS: 5. 10/9 = 1.11, so two in parallel (18 µF at 5 V). The usual mistake is to choose one, from the 0 V value.
