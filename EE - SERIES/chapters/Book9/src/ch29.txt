H1: Chapter 29. Digital Modulation Quality: Constellations, EVM, MER, and Eyes
P: The 16-QAM link works at the bench and drops packets on the production line. The test station reports "EVM 3.7 %, pass" on every unit, and the limit is 5 %. Nobody had asked where the 3.7 % came from. Split into its parts, it is 3.2 % of noise, 1.4 % of phase noise, and 1.2 % of I/Q gain imbalance, and the production synthesizer had a different loop filter. A single EVM number passes or fails a product; its budget tells you why.
MEET: Modem and transmitter development, cable and broadcast head ends (MER), satellite links, IoT radio production test, serial links (eyes), and any receiver or transmitter specified by a modulation-quality limit.
H2: 29.1 Constellations and what they show
P: After the VSA recovers carrier, symbol timing, and amplitude, it plots each symbol's I and Q at the decision instant: the constellation. Each impairment has a signature. Noise makes round clouds; phase noise smears the clouds into arcs that grow with distance from the center; I/Q gain imbalance stretches the grid into a rectangle; quadrature skew bends it into a parallelogram; LO leakage shifts the whole grid; compression pulls the outer corner points inward; a spur rotates a small circle around each point. Learn to read them before trusting any number.
H2: 29.2 EVM and MER
P: The error vector is the difference between the measured symbol and the ideal one. EVM is its rms magnitude relative to a reference, in % or dB (20 log of the ratio). The analyzer first fits gain, phase, and frequency (and, depending on settings, I/Q offset), so those are not counted as error unless you tell it to. Two conventions matter: normalization to the average constellation power (most wireless standards) or to the peak (some older ones), and whether the error is computed before or after equalization. MER (modulation error ratio, used in cable and broadcast) is the ratio of average signal power to average error power in dB; with average normalization MER = −20 log(EVM). Independent impairments add in power, so an EVM budget is a root-sum-square: noise contributes 1/√SNR, phase noise contributes the rms phase in radians, and gain imbalance contributes half the split.
WE: Worked Example 29.1: Building the EVM budget
PROB: A 16-QAM transmitter has an SNR of 30 dB, 0.81° rms phase noise (from the synthesizer's integrated phase noise, Chapter 20), and 0.2 dB I/Q gain imbalance. Predict the EVM, and compare it with the listing's measurement.
SOL: Noise: 1/√1000 = 3.16 %. Phase noise: 0.81° = 0.0141 rad, 1.41 %. Gain imbalance: 10^(0.2/20) = 1.023; √1.023 − 1/√1.023 = 0.0230, half of it 1.15 %. Total √(3.16² + 1.41² + 1.15²) = 3.65 %, −28.8 dB, MER 28.8 dB. The listing measures 3.66 % (−28.7 dB) on 20 000 symbols. Fix the noise first: it is three quarters of the error power.

WE: Worked Example 29.2: Normalization and bandwidth
PROB: A 16-QAM datasheet states EVM 3.0 % normalized to the peak symbol; your analyzer normalizes to the average. What does it read? Then, the signal runs 1 Msym/s with a root-raised-cosine filter of roll-off 0.35: what span and what occupied bandwidth?
SOL: 16-QAM's corner symbols have 1.8 times the average power (2.55 dB), so the peak amplitude is √1.8 = 1.342 times the rms amplitude. An error that is 3.0 % of the peak amplitude is 3.0 × 1.342 = 4.0 % of the rms amplitude: your analyzer reads 4.0 %, which looks like a failure against a 3 % expectation but is the same signal. Occupied bandwidth Rs(1 + α) = 1.35 MHz; set the span to 2 to 3 MHz so the filter skirts are inside.

H2: 29.3 The measuring receiver's own EVM
P: The analyzer adds its own noise, phase noise, and imbalance. Its residual EVM (from the datasheet, or measured on a clean reference signal) subtracts in power: device EVM = √(measured² − residual²). The correction is meaningful while the residual is less than about half the measured value; beyond that, use a better analyzer or average. Mixer level matters as in Chapter 21: too low raises noise, too high adds compression, and the optimum for a 16-QAM signal with a 2.55 dB peak-to-average ratio sits a few dB below the analyzer's recommended level for CW.
H2: 29.4 Eye diagrams and Q
P: For baseband and serial signals, the oscilloscope (Chapter 16) or a VSA folds the waveform on the symbol clock to make an eye. The vertical opening at the sampling instant, compared with the noise on each rail, is the Q factor: Q = (μ1 − μ0)/(σ1 + σ0). For Gaussian noise the bit error ratio is ½ erfc(Q/√2): Q = 6 gives about 1e−9, Q = 7 about 1.3e−12. Jitter closes the eye horizontally; serial standards specify masks and total jitter at a BER (bathtub curves), measured with the scope's jitter tools or a bit error ratio tester.
MIS: EVM in dB and MER are always the same number with the sign changed. Only with average normalization; with peak normalization the EVM figure is better by the peak-to-average ratio of the constellation (2.55 dB for 16-QAM, 3.68 dB for 64-QAM).
BENCH: Keep a known-good reference: a generator playing the same standard waveform, measured through the same cables, at the same level. If its EVM is not as good as the generator's datasheet value, the problem is in your setup (level, span, filter, trigger), not in the device.
