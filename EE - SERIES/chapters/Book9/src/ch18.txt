H1: Chapter 18. Triggers, Mixed Signals, Logic and Protocol Analyzers, and TDR
P: The board resets once a day. The scope, triggered on the reset line, captures the moment, but the cause happened 400 µs earlier, outside the window, on an I2C bus that was hanging. A runt trigger on the clock, a protocol trigger on the address, and a deep pre-trigger window catch it in an afternoon. Triggering is how you ask the instrument a question, and most of the skill in time-domain measurement is asking the right one.
P: Where you meet this: Embedded debugging, intermittent faults, bus timing (I2C, SPI, UART, CAN, USB), FPGA and memory interfaces, cable and PCB impedance checks, and connector faults.
H2: 18.1 Triggers beyond the edge
P: The edge trigger fires when the signal crosses a level in a chosen direction, with holdoff to ignore further edges for a set time. Advanced triggers catch the events that cause trouble: pulse width (shorter or longer than a limit, for glitches), runt (a pulse that crosses one threshold but not the other), setup and hold (data changing too close to a clock edge), timeout, window, pattern across several channels, and serial triggers that fire on a decoded address or data value. Segmented memory captures many short windows around repeated triggers with very small dead time and timestamps each, which turns a rare event into a statistic. Mask testing compares each acquisition with a template and stops on a violation.
H2: Worked Example 18.1: Segmented memory for a rare event
P: Problem. A glitch appears on one packet in a few thousand. The scope has 10 Mpoints of memory at 2.5 GS/s and segmented mode. Each packet lasts 8 µs, and the trigger rearms in 1 µs. How many packets fit in memory per capture, and how long does that cover at 1000 packets per second?
P: Solution. One segment of 8 µs at 2.5 GS/s is 20 000 points (plus a small overhead), so 10 Mpoints hold 500 segments. At 1000 packets per second the capture spans 0.5 s of traffic, and the rearm time of 1 µs is far below the 1 ms between packets, so no packet is missed. A normal long capture of 0.5 s would need 1.25 Gpoints at the same rate, mostly empty.
H2: 18.2 Mixed-signal oscilloscopes and logic analyzers
P: A mixed-signal oscilloscope (MSO) adds 8 to 16 digital channels to the analog ones, sharing the trigger and time base, so a decoded SPI word and the analog supply dip appear on the same screen. A logic analyzer has many more channels (34 to hundreds) and two modes: timing (asynchronous sampling at a high rate, for glitches and timing) and state (sampling on the target's own clock, for bus contents). USB logic analyzers with 8 to 16 channels at 100 to 500 MS/s, with open-source software such as sigrok PulseView or vendor software, cover most embedded work for little money. Timing resolution is the sample interval: to see a 1 ns setup violation you need well under 1 ns, which only a dedicated analyzer or a fast scope provides.
H2: 18.3 Protocol decoding
P: Decoding turns sampled waveforms into frames: I2C addresses, ACKs and NACKs; SPI words; UART characters; CAN identifiers and error frames; USB packets. The decoder must be told the protocol's parameters (baud rate, bit order, clock polarity and phase), and it decodes only what was captured at a sufficient sample rate: at least four to ten samples per bit. A protocol analyzer as a dedicated instrument (for USB, PCIe, or Ethernet) sits in line and captures every packet at the link level, with timestamps, which a scope cannot do for long.
H2: Worked Example 18.2: A UART with the wrong clock
P: Problem. A device's UART runs from an internal oscillator that is 3.5 % fast at 115 200 baud. The receiver samples each bit in its middle. After how many bits does the sampling point reach the bit edge, and does an 8N1 frame survive?
P: Solution. Each bit is 3.5 % short, so the sampling point drifts by 0.035 bit per bit. Starting at the middle (0.5 bit from the edge), it reaches the edge after 0.5/0.035 = 14.3 bits. An 8N1 frame is 10 bits, ending at 0.35 bit of drift for the stop bit, so it survives on paper; with the receiver's own error and edge jitter, the margin is thin. A decoder on the scope shows framing errors only on some characters, and measuring the bit width with cursors (8.68 µs nominal, 8.39 µs here) finds the cause.
H2: 18.4 Time-domain reflectometry
P: A TDR sends a fast step into a line and watches the reflections: a higher impedance reflects a positive step, a lower impedance a negative one, an open doubles the voltage, and a short pulls it to zero. The reflection coefficient is ρ = (Z − Z0)/(Z + Z0), and the position of each feature in time gives its distance: d = v t/2, with v the propagation velocity. Resolution is set by the system rise time: two discontinuities closer than about v × tr/2 merge. Dedicated TDR modules on sampling scopes reach rise times of tens of picoseconds; VNAs compute the same view from S-parameters (Chapter 26); and cable fault locators do it for meters to kilometers.
H2: Worked Example 18.3: Reading a TDR trace
P: Problem. A 50 Ω TDR with a 35 ps rise time looks into a PCB trace (microstrip, effective permittivity 3.4). A step of ρ = +0.091 appears 1.23 ns after the launch. What impedance, how far, and what resolution? A 30 m coaxial cable with velocity factor 0.66 shows an open: when does it appear?
P: Solution. Z = Z0(1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ) = 50 × 1.091/0.909 = 60 Ω. v = c/√3.4 = 1.63 × 10⁸ m/s, so d = 1.63 × 10⁸ × 1.23 ns/2 = 0.100 m: the trace becomes 60 Ω 10 cm from the launch. Resolution ≈ v tr/2 = 2.8 mm. The cable's round trip is 2 × 30/(0.66 × 3.0 × 10⁸) = 303 ns, where the voltage doubles.

P: On the Job
P: A signal-integrity and validation engineer at a server or networking company spends the week with a high-bandwidth scope, a TDR module or VNA, and protocol analyzers: measuring channel impedance on new PCB stack-ups, debugging a DDR interface whose eye closes at temperature, and running compliance tests for PCIe or USB with the scope vendor's automated software. A typical finding: a 42 Ω via field that the TDR shows as a 3 mm dip, fixed by enlarging the anti-pads in the next board spin. Tools: Keysight, Tektronix, or Teledyne LeCroy real-time and sampling scopes, R&S or Keysight VNAs, and Teledyne LeCroy or Keysight protocol analyzers.
P: Common misconception. A logic analyzer shows the logic levels as they are. It shows them as thresholds sampled at its rate: a slow edge, a runt, or a ringing line can be shown as clean ones and zeros. When the logic view and the system disagree, look at the same lines with the analog channels.
BENCH: Set up triggers so they fire on the fault, not on the symptom: when a reset fires, the cause is usually earlier. Use a long pre-trigger (90 % of the window before the trigger), and trigger on the earliest abnormal signal you can define: a NACK, a runt, a timeout.
H2: Practice
P: 1. (Core) Which trigger catches a pulse that crosses the low threshold but not the high one?
P: 2. (Core) A TDR sees ρ = −0.2 in a 50 Ω system. What impedance?
P: 3. (Core) How many samples per bit does a 1 Mbit/s CAN bus get at 10 MS/s?
P: 4. (Extension) A coaxial cable with velocity factor 0.8 shows an open 250 ns after the launch. How long is it?
P: 5. (Extension) 10 Mpoints of segmented memory at 1 GS/s, segments of 50 µs. How many segments?
P: 6. (Challenge) An I2C bus at 400 kHz fails occasionally with a stuck SDA line. Plan the capture: instrument, channels, trigger, sample rate, and window, and the measurements that would identify a target device holding SDA or a rise-time problem.
