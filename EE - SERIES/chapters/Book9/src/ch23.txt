H1: Chapter 23. RF Power Meters and Sensors
P: The amplifier delivers 30 dBm by the spectrum analyzer, 29.2 dBm by the power meter, and the datasheet promised 30 dBm ±0.5 dB. The analyzer's level accuracy at that frequency is ±0.8 dB; the power meter's sensor is calibrated to ±0.1 dB plus mismatch. When the numbers disagree, the power meter wins, and the analyzer's amplitude is calibrated against it.
MEET: Transmitter output power, amplifier gain and compression, path-loss calibration of test systems (Chapter 20), radar pulses, power control loops, and the calibration of every other RF instrument.
H2: 23.1 Sensor types
P: Thermal sensors (thermocouple or thermistor) heat a load and measure the temperature: they respond to true average power for any waveform, with about 50 dB of range (−35 to +20 dBm) and slow response. Diode sensors rectify the signal: below about −20 dBm they operate in the square-law region, where the output is proportional to power for any waveform; above it they become peak detectors, and their CW calibration no longer holds for modulated signals. Wide-dynamic-range average sensors combine several diode paths to stay in square law from −60 to +20 dBm. Peak and envelope sensors have video bandwidths of tens of megahertz and measure pulse power, rise time, and the crest factor of modulated signals. USB and LAN sensors put the meter inside the sensor. Examples: Keysight's U2000, U8480, and N1920 series, R&S's NRP series, Boonton's peak power sensors, and Anritsu's sensors.
H2: 23.2 Accuracy: calibration factor, zeroing, mismatch
P: Every sensor carries a calibration factor (CF) table against frequency, which the meter applies; a 97 % factor at 10 GHz is a correction of −0.13 dB. Zeroing removes the sensor's offset and must be repeated after warm-up and temperature changes, with no signal applied. Averaging reduces noise at low levels. And mismatch between source and sensor (Chapter 20) is usually the largest single term of the uncertainty budget: sensors have VSWRs of about 1.1 to 1.3, sources often 1.5 or worse. Higher powers need a calibrated attenuator or directional coupler in front of the sensor.
WE: Worked Example 23.1: A power-measurement budget
PROB: A sensor with VSWR 1.15 measures a source with VSWR 1.5. Its calibration factor uncertainty is a limit of ±1.5 % and the meter's linearity a limit of ±1 %, both treated as rectangular. Find the mismatch limits and an expanded uncertainty.
SOL: |Γs| = 0.5/2.5 = 0.2, |Γl| = 0.15/2.15 = 0.0698; product 0.0140: mismatch limits +0.120/−0.122 dB, about ±2.8 % in power. Mismatch follows a U-shaped distribution, u = 2.81 %/√2 = 1.99 %; CF u = 1.5 %/√3 = 0.87 %; linearity 1 %/√3 = 0.58 %. Combined u = √(1.99² + 0.87² + 0.58²) = 2.24 %, expanded U = 4.5 % (k = 2), ±0.19 dB, as Listing 23.1 prints. Mismatch dominates; a better-matched source (an attenuator pad) is the cure.
H2: 23.3 Pulses and modulation
P: An average sensor reports the average power. For a pulsed signal, the pulse power is the average divided by the duty cycle, as long as the pulse is flat; a peak sensor measures it directly, with the pulse shape. For modulated signals with high crest factors (OFDM with 10 dB or more, Chapter 30), a diode sensor in its CW-calibrated mode reads wrong outside square law; use a thermal sensor, a multi-path diode sensor, or a peak sensor specified for the modulation bandwidth.
WE: Worked Example 23.2: A radar pulse and a coupler
PROB: A radar transmitter emits 1 µs pulses at 1 kHz repetition rate, and a 40 dB directional coupler feeds the power sensor, which reads an average of −3 dBm. What are the average and the pulse power at the transmitter?
SOL: Duty cycle = 1 µs × 1 kHz = 0.001, or −30 dB. Average at the transmitter: −3 + 40 = 37 dBm (5 W). Pulse power: 37 + 30 = 67 dBm (5 kW), if the pulse is rectangular. The coupler's coupling factor must be calibrated at the frequency (often ±0.5 dB nominal), and a peak sensor confirms the pulse shape and droop.

MIS: A power meter measures the power the source would deliver into a matched load. It measures the power delivered into the sensor, which depends on both reflections; without the mismatch term the uncertainty is understated, often by a factor of two.
BENCH: Zero the sensor after it has warmed up for 30 minutes, with the source switched off, not disconnected; and never apply more than the sensor's damage level: a +20 dBm sensor on a 10 W amplifier dies in milliseconds. Put the attenuator on the sensor before the amplifier is switched on.

PP: 1. (Core) A calibration factor of 95 % corresponds to how many dB?
PP: 2. (Core) An average power of 10 dBm with a duty cycle of 2 % means what pulse power?
PP: 3. (Core) Why is a diode sensor read wrongly on a modulated signal at +10 dBm?
PP: 4. (Extension) Source VSWR 1.3, sensor VSWR 1.1: what are the mismatch limits?
PP: 5. (Extension) A 100 W (50 dBm) transmitter is measured through a 30 dB attenuator rated 10 W. Is that safe, and what does the sensor see?
PP: 6. (Challenge) Write the uncertainty budget for an amplifier's 30 dBm output measured through a 20 dB attenuator (±0.1 dB, VSWR 1.1), with a sensor of VSWR 1.15 and the amplifier output VSWR 2.0.
ANS: 1. 10 log 0.95 = −0.22 dB. The usual mistake is to apply the factor twice (meter and spreadsheet).
ANS: 2. 0.02 is −17.0 dB: 10 + 17.0 = 27.0 dBm (0.5 W). The usual mistake is to multiply by the duty cycle instead of dividing.
ANS: 3. At +10 dBm the diode is no longer in its square-law region and responds to the peaks of the envelope; its CW calibration does not apply to a signal with a different peak-to-average ratio. The usual mistake is to trust a reading that looks stable.
ANS: 4. |Γs| = 0.3/2.3 = 0.130, |Γl| = 0.1/2.1 = 0.0476; product 0.0062: ±0.054 dB. The usual mistake is to forget that two good matches still leave a small term.
ANS: 5. 50 dBm − 30 dB = 20 dBm at the sensor, at its maximum for many sensors; the attenuator must dissipate almost 100 W but is rated 10 W, so it fails. Use a high-power attenuator or a directional coupler rated for 100 W and add attenuation so the sensor sees about 0 dBm. The usual mistake is to check only the sensor's level and not the attenuator's power rating.
ANS: 6. Terms: attenuator ±0.1 dB (rectangular, u = 0.058 dB); mismatch amplifier to attenuator |Γ| 0.333 × 0.0476 = 0.0159 (±0.14 dB, U-shaped, u = 0.097 dB); attenuator to sensor 0.0476 × 0.0698 = 0.0033 (±0.03 dB, u = 0.02 dB); sensor CF and linearity from its datasheet (for example u = 0.05 dB).
