H1: Chapter 7. The Curve Tracer at Work: Diodes, Bipolar Transistors, and MOSFETs
P: Two bags of "the same" MOSFET arrive from two distributors. On the curve tracer, the first bag turns on at 1.6 V and carries 1.5 A at 3 V on the gate. The second turns on at 2.4 V, carries a third of that current, and breaks down at 41 V instead of beyond 60 V. The markings are identical. The curves are not, and a datasheet is a promise about curves. This chapter shows how to read a device's parameters off its curves, and how to catch the part that breaks the promise.
MEET: Incoming inspection, failure analysis, matching parts for amplifiers and current mirrors, characterization labs, model extraction (Chapter 8), and the hobby bench sorting a drawer of unmarked transistors.
H2: 7.1 The curve tracer, classic and modern
P: The classic curve tracer, of which Tektronix's 576 is the best-known ancestor, applies a rectified sine sweep to the collector or drain through a series resistor, steps the base current or gate voltage once per sweep, and draws current against voltage on a CRT, so a whole output family appears at once. Its knobs are protection: the series resistor and a peak-power setting limit what the sweep can deliver, and a high-voltage interlock covers the test fixture. The modern equivalents are SMU-based: a power device analyzer or curve tracer built from high-power and high-voltage SMUs (Keysight's B1505A and Keithley's 2600-PCT systems are examples), or two bench SMUs (Chapter 6) programmed as a step generator and a sweep. They add pulsed sweeps, accurate low-current ranges, Kelvin sensing, and exportable data, which is what parameter extraction needs.
P: The settings that matter on every tracer: the sweep range and polarity, the compliance or series resistance, the peak-power limit, the step size and number of steps, pulse width and duty cycle, and the connection: Kelvin (force and sense to each terminal) for anything above about 100 mA or below about 1 Ω. Set the limits before the part is in the socket. A curve tracer can destroy a small transistor faster than you can read its screen.
BOXH: Safe limits before every sweep
BOXL: Voltage below the part's absolute maximum (or the breakdown you intend to find, with the current limited).
BOXL: Current compliance or series resistance below the maximum continuous current; a low limit for breakdown sweeps (100 µA to 1 mA).
BOXL: Peak power below what the package survives for the pulse width; pulsed sweeps for anything above about 0.5 W.
BOXL: Gate drive within the gate's ±V rating; a gate resistor (10 to 100 Ω) at the socket against oscillation.
BOXL: High-voltage interlock closed; never touch the fixture with the sweep enabled.
H2: 7.2 The diode
P: A diode needs four views. The forward curve on a logarithmic current axis shows the ideal exponential as a straight line, whose slope gives the ideality factor n and whose extrapolation to zero volts gives the saturation current Is; at high current the curve bends right as the series resistance Rs adds its IR drop. The forward voltage Vf is read at the datasheet's test current. The reverse curve at low voltage gives the leakage current, a nanoampere-level number that needs an SMU's low ranges (Chapter 6). The reverse curve at high voltage shows breakdown; for a Zener it is the operating point, with its voltage Vz at a test current and its dynamic resistance rz = ΔV/ΔI as the slope. The physics behind each of these numbers is Book 3's (Chapters 5 and 6).
WE: Worked Example 7.1: A switching diode on the log plot
PROB: A small-signal switching diode (simulated with the SPICE parameters of the common 1N4148 model, Is = 2.682 nA, N = 1.836, Rs = 0.5664 Ω) is swept from 1 nA to 100 mA. Walk through reading Vf at 10 mA, n, Is, and Rs from the curve, as Listing 7.1 does.
SOL: Vf at 10 mA reads 0.724 V. On the log plot between 1 µA and 100 µA the curve is straight, and it rises 109.3 mV per decade of current. The ideal slope for n = 1 is Vt ln 10 = 59.5 mV per decade at 300 K, so n = 109.3/59.5 = 1.836. Extending that straight line down to 0 V meets the current axis at Is ≈ 2.7 nA. At 100 mA the curve sits 56.6 mV to the right of the extended line: Rs = 56.6 mV/100 mA = 0.566 Ω. Listing 7.1 makes the same three reads by least squares and returns n = 1.836, Is = 2.67 nA, and Rs = 0.567 Ω. The fit window matters: below about 100 nA, leakage and the "+1" of the diode law bend the line; above about 1 mA, Rs does. Fitting n across the whole sweep would give a meaningless number.

WE: Worked Example 7.2: Leakage, breakdown, and a Zener's dynamic resistance
PROB: The diode's reverse current at 20 V reads 4 nA on the SMU's 10 nA range; a sweep to 120 V with a 100 µA compliance shows no breakdown. A 5.1 V Zener reads 5.040 V at 1 mA, 5.100 V at 5 mA, and 5.175 V at 10 mA. Interpret both.
SOL: 4 nA at 20 V is well inside a typical 25 nA datasheet limit for this class of diode; at that level, check that the fixture's own leakage (an empty socket at 20 V) is far below 4 nA first (Chapter 14). No breakdown up to 120 V at 100 µA means a rated 100 V part has margin, and the low compliance kept the test non-destructive. For the Zener, Vz = 5.100 V at the 5 mA test current, and rz = (5.175 − 5.040) V/(10 − 1) mA = 15 Ω. A regulator design that assumed an ideal 5.1 V would see 135 mV of change across that current range.
H2: 7.3 The bipolar transistor
P: The output family is the classic picture: collector current against collector-emitter voltage, one curve per base-current step. Its views: the current gain hFE = Ic/Ib at each point, and its variation with Ic (a separate sweep of Ic at fixed VCE, with hFE plotted on a log Ic axis, peaking in the middle and falling at both ends); the saturation voltage VCE(sat), read at a forced gain such as Ic/Ib = 10; the Early voltage, from the slope of the curves in the active region extrapolated back to Ic = 0, where they meet near −VA; and the breakdown BVCEO, swept with the base open and the current limited. Book 3 (Chapters 8 and 9) explains each effect; the curve tracer measures it.
WE: Worked Example 7.3: Gain, Early voltage, and output resistance
PROB: An NPN transistor's output family is traced with base steps of 10 µA. On the 20 µA curve, Ic reads 4.107 mA at 2 V and 4.533 mA at 10 V (and 4.267 mA at 5 V). Find hFE at 5 V, the Early voltage, and the output resistance.
SOL: hFE = 4.267 mA/20 µA = 213 at 5 V. The slope between 2 V and 10 V is (4.533 − 4.107) mA/8 V = 53.3 µS, an output resistance of 18.8 kΩ. Extrapolating the line back from 2 V: the current reaches zero 4.107 mA/53.3 µS = 77 V further left, at VCE = 2 − 77 = −75 V, so VA = 75 V. Every curve of the family extrapolates to about the same point, and a family whose curves do not is either heating (Section 7.5) or not a single bipolar device. Note that hFE read at 5 V includes the Early term: at 10 V the same transistor reads 227, which is why datasheets state VCE with every hFE.

WE: Worked Example 7.4: hFE against current, saturation, and breakdown
PROB: A sweep of the same transistor at VCE = 5 V reads hFE = 160 at 0.1 mA, 210 at 10 mA, and 110 at 100 mA. VCE(sat) reads 0.10 V at Ic = 10 mA, Ib = 1 mA. With the base open and a 1 mA limit, the collector breaks down at 62 V. The part is sold as a 2N3904 (datasheet: VCE(sat) at most 0.2 V at 10 mA and 1 mA, BVCEO at least 40 V). Interpret.
SOL: The gain peaks in the middle of the current range and falls at both ends, as every bipolar transistor does: at low currents recombination in the base-emitter junction steals base current, at high currents high-level injection and the series resistances reduce the gain (Book 3, Chapter 9). Design with the gain at your operating current, not the headline value. VCE(sat) = 0.10 V is inside the 0.2 V limit, read with a forced gain of 10, much lower than the active-region gain, which is the condition a switch should be designed for. BVCEO = 62 V is above the 40 V minimum; a breakdown below the minimum, or a soft knee that starts drawing microamperes long before the edge, would mark a damaged or counterfeit part.
H2: 7.4 The MOSFET
P: A MOSFET's transfer curve, drain current against gate-source voltage, gives the threshold voltage, the transconductance gm = dId/dVgs, and on a log current axis the subthreshold slope S, the gate voltage per decade of current below threshold. The threshold depends on the method, and a datasheet's Vgs(th) is defined by a constant current (often 250 µA, with the drain tied to the gate), while device engineers often extrapolate √Id (saturation) or Id (at a small Vds) back to zero. The on-resistance Rds(on) is Vds/Id at a small Vds, measured at several gate voltages and with Kelvin connections, because a milliohm part is lost in the resistance of the leads. The output family shows the linear and saturation regions; the breakdown BVDSS is swept with the gate shorted and a current limit (often 250 µA); and the body diode is traced like any diode, with the gate shorted, current flowing from source to drain.

WE: Worked Example 7.5: Threshold two ways, subthreshold slope, and gm
PROB: A small logic-level MOSFET is swept with drain tied to gate (saturation) from 0.5 V to 5 V. Listing 7.1's model has a threshold parameter of 1.6 V. What do the √Id extrapolation and the 250 µA constant-current method return, and what are the subthreshold slope and gm at Vgs = 3 V, Vds = 10 V?
SOL: The √Id line, fitted where it is steepest, extrapolates to 1.654 V; the constant-current method gives 1.511 V. Neither is wrong: they answer different questions, and a 0.14 V difference between methods is normal. Compare a part with its datasheet only by the datasheet's own method and current. Below threshold the current rises one decade per 83.4 mV, which corresponds to a slope factor of n = 83.4/59.5 = 1.40. At Vgs = 3 V and Vds = 10 V the drain carries 1.540 A, and a 10 mV gate step changes it by 22.1 mA: gm = 2.21 S.

WE: Worked Example 7.6: Rds(on), Kelvin connections, and a counterfeit
PROB: At Vds = 0.1 V the same MOSFET reads Rds(on) = 176.5 mΩ at Vgs = 4.5 V and 60.0 mΩ at 10 V with Kelvin connections. Without Kelvin connections, the two test leads add 25 mΩ each. The second bag of the hook reads 0.52 A at Vgs = 3 V, Vds = 10 V, a threshold of 2.4 V, and BVDSS = 41 V. What do you conclude?
SOL: Rds(on) falls with gate drive, by a factor of almost three from 4.5 V to 10 V, so a logic-level design must use the 4.5 V figure. Two-wire, the 10 V reading would be 60 + 50 = 110 mΩ, an 83 % error, entirely from the leads. The second bag fails three tests at once: the threshold is 0.8 V high, the current at 3 V is a third of the reference part's (1.540 A), and the breakdown is below a 60 V rating. A different, smaller die in the same package is the usual explanation of such a pattern; quarantine the lot and report it to the distributor.
H2: 7.5 Pulsed versus DC sweeps
P: A curve tracer's DC or slow sweep heats the device at every point where Vds × Id is large, and a hot MOSFET carries less current at a high gate voltage, as its mobility falls with temperature. The result is an output family whose curves droop or even fall with rising Vds, which is a thermal effect, not a property of the cold device. A pulsed sweep (Chapter 6) keeps the junction near the case temperature. Compare the two whenever the curves look strange at high power; if they differ, report the pulsed curves with the pulse width, and the DC curves with the heatsink and the measured case temperature.
WE: Worked Example 7.7: How much a DC sweep heats
PROB: The MOSFET of Worked Example 7.5 sits on a heatsink with 10 K/W from junction to ambient. At Vgs = 3.5 V it is swept to Vds = 10 V. Compare the pulsed current with the DC current.
SOL: Pulsed (junction at 300 K): Id = 2.836 A at 10 V. In DC the device dissipates about 27 W, the junction settles 266 K above ambient (the model's threshold falls by 4 mV/K and its mobility by T^−1.5), and Id is 2.664 A, 6 % lower; at Vds = 2 V the junction rises only 51 K and the two curves differ by about 3 %. At lower gate voltage the threshold shift wins and the hot device carries more current: at Vgs = 2.6 V and 10 V, 1.000 A in DC against 0.786 A pulsed. That crossover is the temperature-stable point of a MOSFET, and it is why a DC curve family of a power device can show any shape. A junction 266 K above ambient also far exceeds the part's ratings, which is the other reason to pulse.

H2: 7.6 Fault finding, matching, and the hobby route
P: The curve tracer is the fastest failure-analysis tool on the bench. A good junction has a sharp breakdown knee and nanoamperes below it; an ESD-damaged junction leaks microamperes with a soft, resistive curve long before the knee. A MOSFET whose gate draws current has a damaged oxide. A curve that changes from sweep to sweep (walkout) points to charge trapping or a cracked die. Matching is the other daily job: sort transistors by hFE or Vgs at the operating current for differential pairs and current mirrors, within a few percent, with all parts at the same temperature. And comparing a suspect lot with a known-good reference part, curve for curve, is the most effective counterfeit check that does not need a microscope.
P: The hobby route has three levels. The low-cost component testers built on an AVR microcontroller (from the open-source design by Markus Frejek, extended by Karl-Heinz Kübbeler) identify the pins and type of a part and report hFE, Vf, Vth, and capacitances at small currents for a few dollars; they are excellent for sorting a drawer, not for parameters at a datasheet's test conditions. An oscilloscope in X-Y mode with a transformer and a series resistor (the "octopus" tester) draws a part's V-I curve and is a classic fault finder for comparing a board against a good one. And a reader-lab mini tracer is a small project: an ESP32-S3 or Pico drives the collector or drain through a resistor from a DAC and an op-amp buffer, a second channel sets the base current or gate voltage, and the ADC measures both, giving a few volts and up to about 100 mA, enough to trace small transistors and LEDs and to run Listing 7.1 on the exported data (Chapter 38).
JOB: In a semiconductor characterization lab, a device engineer spends the day with a parameter analyzer, a power-device curve tracer, and a wafer prober: I-V and C-V curves of test structures across the wafer, threshold and leakage maps, breakdown at temperature, and the data that sets the parameters of the compact model (Book 3, Chapter 22, and Chapter 8 of this book). A typical task: a power MOSFET lot shows 3 % of parts with Rds(on) 20 % high. The engineer traces good and bad parts pulsed with Kelvin connections, finds the difference only above 20 A, measures it at two temperatures, and shows that a source-bond resistance, not the die, is responsible, which sends the problem to the packaging line. Tools: Keysight B1500A or B1505A, Keithley 4200A-SCS or 2600-PCT class systems, FormFactor or MPI probers, thermal chucks, and Python or vendor software for the maps.
MIS: The threshold voltage is a property of the transistor. It is a property of the transistor and a definition: the constant-current, √Id-extrapolation, and linear-extrapolation methods give different numbers for the same part, and only the same method and test current make two thresholds comparable.
BENCH: Keep a known-good reference part of every type you trace often, labeled and stored in an antistatic bag. When a curve looks odd, trace the reference with the same settings first. If the reference looks odd too, the setup is wrong; if it does not, the part is.
