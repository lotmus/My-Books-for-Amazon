PART: Part VII. Network Analysis
PINTRO: A network analyzer measures how a device answers a known stimulus: how much is reflected, how much passes, with what phase and delay, across frequency. Chapter 25 explains the vector network analyzer (VNA) and its displays, Chapter 26 the calibration that makes it accurate and the de-embedding and time-domain gating that remove fixtures and connectors, and Chapter 27 the scalar analyzers, amplifier tests, and load-pull systems used on active devices. S-parameter theory and the Smith chart are in Book 4; here we measure.
H1: Chapter 25. The Vector Network Analyzer
P: The antenna's return loss is −8 dB at the design frequency on the bench and −18 dB in the simulation. The engineer moves the cable, and the return loss changes by 3 dB. Before the antenna is wrong, the measurement plane is: the analyzer was calibrated at its own ports, and the 1 m cable, its adapter, and the PCB connector are all part of the "antenna." A VNA measures exactly what is between its calibration planes, and nothing tells it where you meant those planes to be.
MEET: Filter, amplifier, and antenna design and production test, cable and connector qualification, PCB channel characterization, matching networks, material measurements, and every RF lab.
H2: 25.1 How a VNA works
P: A VNA has a swept source, a switch that sends it to one port or another, directional couplers or bridges that separate the incident and reflected waves at each port, and receivers that measure those waves in amplitude and phase relative to the reference channel (a narrow IF filter, the IF bandwidth, sets the noise). The ratios give the S-parameters: S11 and S22 are reflections, S21 and S12 transmissions, each a complex number at every frequency. Two-port analyzers are the standard; four-port analyzers measure differential and mixed-mode parameters. Examples: Keysight's ENA and PNA, R&S's ZNA and ZNB, Anritsu's ShockLine and VectorStar, Copper Mountain's USB VNAs, and the NanoVNA at the hobby end (Chapter 37).
H2: 25.2 Reading the displays
P: The same S-parameter can be shown in several formats. Log magnitude (dB) for return loss and insertion loss; phase; group delay, −dφ/dω, for filters and cables; the Smith chart for reflection as impedance; and polar plots. Return loss RL = −20 log |Γ| (a positive number of dB by the strict definition, often shown as a negative S11); VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|). Markers read values and convert formats; marker search finds the −3 dB points of a filter.
WE: Worked Example 25.1: Return loss, VSWR, and impedance
PROB: An S11 of −20 dB and one of −10 dB: what are |Γ| and the VSWR? A load reads 25 + j25 Ω on the Smith chart; find Γ, its return loss, and VSWR.
SOL: −20 dB: |Γ| = 0.1, VSWR = 1.1/0.9 = 1.22. −10 dB: |Γ| = 0.316, VSWR = 1.92. For 25 + j25 Ω: Γ = (Z − 50)/(Z + 50) = (−25 + j25)/(75 + j25) = −0.20 + j0.40, |Γ| = 0.447 at 116.6°, a return loss of 7.0 dB and a VSWR of 2.62. A series capacitor of −j25 Ω would bring it to 25 Ω, and a quarter-wave transformer or an L-network the rest of the way (Book 4).

WE: Worked Example 25.2: Group delay of a filter
PROB: A band-pass filter's S21 phase falls by 36° between 1999.5 and 2000.5 MHz. What is its group delay? If the IF bandwidth is reduced from 10 kHz to 100 Hz, how do the trace noise and sweep time change?
SOL: Group delay = −Δφ/(360° × Δf) = 36°/(360° × 1 MHz) = 100 ns. Group delay is a derivative, so its trace is noisy; the aperture (the Δf over which it is computed) trades noise for resolution. Reducing the IF bandwidth by 100 lowers the noise floor by 20 dB (and the trace noise by a factor of 10) and makes each point about 100 times slower.
H2: 25.3 Specifications that matter
P: Frequency range; dynamic range (the ratio between the maximum power at the receiver and the noise floor at a stated IF bandwidth, often 120 to 140 dB at 10 Hz), which decides how deep a filter's stopband you can see; trace noise; output power range (for amplifiers and for compression measurements); raw directivity and source match (before calibration), and the corrected values after calibration (Chapter 26), which set the measurement uncertainty; and stability of the calibration with temperature and time.

MIS: Return loss is the same thing as S11 in dB. It is the negative of it: a good match has a high return loss (20 dB) and a low S11 (−20 dB). Mixing the two signs is the most common error in RF reports.
BENCH: Before measuring the device, measure the calibration: connect a load to each port and check the return loss (better than about 40 dB after a good calibration), connect a short and check it sits at the left edge of the Smith chart, and connect a through and check S21 is 0 dB ±0.05 dB with flat phase. Five minutes here saves a day of chasing the device.
H2: Practice
PP: 1. (Core) What return loss and VSWR correspond to |Γ| = 0.2?
PP: 2. (Core) Which S-parameter is the gain of an amplifier driven at port 1?
PP: 3. (Core) Γ for a 100 Ω resistive load in a 50 Ω system?
PP: 4. (Extension) A cable's S21 phase changes by 720° over 1 GHz. What is its delay?
PP: 5. (Extension) A filter's stopband is 90 dB below the passband. The VNA has 110 dB of dynamic range at 1 kHz IFBW and a 0 dBm source. What IFBW gives a 20 dB margin on the stopband reading, and why?
PP: 6. (Challenge) Plan the measurement of a 2.4 GHz chip antenna on an evaluation board with an SMA connector: calibration plane, cable, fixture, and how you distinguish the antenna's match from the cable's.
ANS: 1. RL = −20 log 0.2 = 14.0 dB; VSWR = 1.2/0.8 = 1.5. The usual mistake is to give RL as −14 dB.
ANS: 2. S21, the transmission from port 1 to port 2. The usual mistake is to use S12, the reverse isolation.
ANS: 3. Γ = (100 − 50)/(100 + 50) = 0.333, VSWR 2.0. The usual mistake is to compute 50/100.
ANS: 4. Delay = 720°/(360° × 1 GHz) = 2 ns. The usual mistake is to read the wrapped phase on screen, which hides the full turns.
ANS: 5. The noise floor must be 110 dB below the passband for 20 dB of margin below a 90 dB stopband: exactly the 110 dB available at 1 kHz, so use 1 kHz or less; at 100 Hz the floor drops 10 dB for 30 dB of margin. The reading of a stopband close to the noise floor is the noise floor, not the filter. The usual mistake is to measure the stopband at the default IFBW and report the noise as rejection.
ANS: 6. Calibrate at the end of the cable that will connect to the board (SOLT with a calibration kit or an ECal module), use a phase-stable cable that does not move during the measurement, and include the board's SMA and launch as part of the antenna or remove them by port extension (Chapter 26). Check by moving the cable and watching the trace (a stable result means the cable is not radiating or detuning), and add a ferrite on the cable to suppress common-mode currents on its shield, which otherwise make the cable part of the antenna. The usual mistake is to calibrate at the analyzer's ports and blame the antenna for the cable.
