H1: Chapter 15. Uninterruptible Power and Hold-Up
P: A data center can drop its load during a test of its own backup. The utility feed opens, the batteries carry the load, and the generators start; if one transfer switch, one setting, or one shared component fails, the servers go dark. Their own supplies bridge only the 10 to 20 ms that their bulk capacitors can hold, so everything beyond that has to come from the UPS.
P: Where you meet this: Every UPS data sheet, every server supply's hold-up specification, every generator test, every hospital's emergency power system, and every availability or tier rating of a facility.
P: The grid fails, briefly or for hours, and the loads that matter most, data centers, hospitals, telecom sites, and factory controls, must not notice. Between the utility and those loads sit the uninterruptible power supply, its batteries, and the generator that takes over for long outages, and inside every power supply sits a capacitor whose stored energy rides through the gap. This chapter sizes each of them and compares the UPS topologies by the waste formula of Chapter 1.
H2: 15.1 Three UPS topologies
P: An offline, or standby, UPS passes the utility straight to the load and switches to its inverter when the utility fails, with a transfer of a few milliseconds. A line-interactive UPS adds a transformer or converter that corrects moderate sags and swells without going to battery. A double-conversion, or online, UPS rectifies every watt to a DC bus that also charges the battery and inverts it back, so the load never sees the utility's disturbances and there is no transfer at all. Double conversion gives the best protection but pays for it twice in conversion loss; its eco mode bypasses the converters while the utility is healthy and switches back within a few milliseconds when it is not.
H2: Worked Example 15.1
P: Problem. A data hall's UPS carries 400 kW. Compare the waste in double conversion at 96% and in eco mode at 99%, and find the energy and cost difference over a year at $0.10 per kWh.
P: Solution. Double conversion wastes 400 × (1/0.96 − 1) = 16.7 kW; eco mode wastes 400 × (1/0.99 − 1) = 4.0 kW. The difference is 12.6 kW, or 110.6 MWh per year, about $11,000 in electricity, plus the cooling needed to remove the extra heat. The operator weighs that against the brief exposure to utility disturbances that eco mode allows.
H2: 15.2 Sizing the battery
P: The UPS battery bridges the time until the generator starts and takes the load, usually tens of seconds, plus a margin; designs commonly provide five to fifteen minutes. The energy required is the load times the runtime, divided by the inverter's efficiency and by the usable fraction of the battery's capacity, its depth of discharge. Lithium-ion batteries have largely replaced valve-regulated lead-acid in new installations because they are smaller, last longer, and tolerate deeper discharge, but they bring the battery-management and fire-safety requirements of Chapter 12.
EQ: Ebattery = Pload × truntime / (ηinv × DoD)
H2: Worked Example 15.2
P: Problem. The 400 kW load needs 5 minutes of runtime through a 96% inverter, using 80% of the battery's capacity. Find the battery energy.
P: Solution. The load needs 400 × 5/60 = 33.3 kWh. Dividing by 0.96 and by 0.8 gives 43.4 kWh of installed battery capacity. A generator that starts in 15 s uses only a small part of that; the rest covers a failed first start.
P: Key idea. Backup power is a chain of energy stores sized by time: the supply's bulk capacitor rides through milliseconds, the UPS battery through minutes, and the generator through hours. Each must cover the gap until the next one takes over.
H2: 15.3 Hold-up time in a power supply
P: A server power supply must keep its output in regulation for a specified hold-up time, often 10 to 20 ms, after the AC input disappears, long enough to ride through a UPS or transfer-switch transition. The energy comes from the bulk capacitor on the high-voltage DC bus after the power-factor stage of Chapter 7. The converter after it keeps regulating as the bus falls from its normal value to the minimum at which it still works. In the first-cut energy balance, P is the average power drawn from the DC bus, including downstream conversion loss; if only output power is known, use Pbus = Pout/η. The usable capacitor energy is ½C(V1² − V2²), and the capacitance follows directly.
P: This ideal energy calculation assumes the capacitor can deliver the required current without a significant terminal drop. ESR adds an immediate I·ESR drop and I²·ESR heating; ESL and busbar inductance add a short transient drop at the load step. If the bus crosses the converter’s undervoltage threshold early, the nominal energy calculation overstates ride-through and the server can reset before the UPS transfer completes. Check the capacitor bank’s ripple-current and impedance data at temperature and end of life, then verify bus voltage during the specified transfer test.
P: For scale, if the 3 kW example draws about 10 A from its 300 V bus and the bank has 50 mΩ ESR, the instantaneous ESR drop is 0.5 V and the dissipation is 5 W while that current flows. The energy estimate still needs the ESR, wiring inductance, temperature, and end-of-life checks; even a small drop matters if the converter is already near its undervoltage threshold.
EQ: C = 2 P thold / (V1² − V2²)

H2: Worked Example 15.3
P: Problem. A server supply drawing 3 kW from its DC bus needs 10 ms of hold-up. Its bus normally sits at 400 V and the converter works down to 300 V. Find the bulk capacitance.
P: Solution. C = 2 × 3000 × 0.010 / (400² − 300²) = 60/70,000 = 857 µF. Allowing the converter to work down to 250 V would reduce it to 615 µF, which is why hold-up requirements shape the wide-input design of the DC-DC stage.
H2: 15.4 Generators and transfer
P: Diesel or gas generators carry long outages. An automatic transfer switch moves the load from utility to generator once the generator is stable, and back when the utility returns; the UPS covers the transfer. Large sites test generators monthly under load, and they are increasingly supplemented or replaced by battery energy storage that also provides grid services when the utility is healthy, a link to the grid codes of Chapter 16.
H2: 15.5 Lithium-ion versus lead-acid
P: For decades, UPS batteries were valve-regulated lead-acid, VRLA: cheap, well understood, and tolerant of float charging, but heavy, bulky, and short-lived in warm rooms. Their life halves for roughly every 8 to 10 °C above 25 °C, so a battery designed for ten years lasts five in a room at 33 to 35 °C. Lithium-ion, usually lithium iron phosphate for its safety and cycle life, now dominates new installations: about a third of the weight and floor space, 10 to 15 years of life, thousands of cycles instead of hundreds, and a BMS that reports every cell's state. Its higher price is recovered over the replacement cycles it avoids. Its fire risk, managed by the chemistry choice, cell-level monitoring, and the testing of UL 9540A, is the subject of fire codes that every data-hall design must follow.
H2: 15.6 Supercapacitors and flywheels
P: When the backup only needs to bridge seconds, not minutes, energy density matters less than power and cycle life. Supercapacitors store energy as ½CV² in an electrochemical double layer, hundreds to thousands of farads per cell at 2.7 to 3 V, and deliver it at high power for millions of cycles. Usable energy depends on the voltage window: discharging from V1 to V2 releases ½C(V1² − V2²), and a converter must accept the falling voltage. Flywheel UPS systems store kinetic energy in a rotor spinning at thousands to tens of thousands of rpm and deliver hundreds of kilowatts for 10 to 30 seconds, enough to start a generator. Both serve where batteries would be cycled too often, as in smoothing the power swings of AI training racks.
EQ: E = ½ C (V1² − V2²)
H2: Worked Example 15.4
P: Problem. A 48 V shelf must ride through 10 s at 500 W from a supercapacitor bank, discharging from 48 V to 36 V through a converter. Find the capacitance, ignoring converter loss.
P: Solution. E = 500 W × 10 s = 5000 J. C = 2E/(V1² − V2²) = 10 000/(2304 − 1296) = 9.9 F at 48 V, built from about eighteen 2.7 V cells of 180 F in series, each cell's capacitance divided by eighteen. The converter's loss, typically 5 %, adds about 0.5 F.
H2: Worked Example 15.5
P: Problem. A VRLA battery has a 10-year design life at 25 °C, and its life halves for every 8 °C above that. Estimate its life in a room at 33 °C and at 41 °C.
P: Solution. At 33 °C, 10/2 = 5 years; at 41 °C, 10/4 = 2.5 years. A lithium-ion battery in the same room loses capacity faster than at 25 °C too, but far less steeply, which, with the reduced cooling the room then needs, is part of its economic case.
H2: 15.7 Redundancy and availability
P: A single UPS, however good, is a single point of failure, so critical facilities duplicate. N+1 adds one module more than the load needs, so any single module can fail or be serviced; 2N duplicates the entire path from the utility to dual-corded servers, each with two power supplies fed from separate A and B systems. The arithmetic of availability explains why: if one path is available a fraction A of the time and failures are independent, two parallel paths are unavailable only when both fail, a fraction (1 − A)². In practice failures are not entirely independent, common-mode faults such as a shared switchboard or a software bug defeat redundancy, and the Uptime Institute's tier classification therefore asks for physically separate paths, not just extra modules.
EQ: A_parallel = 1 − (1 − A)²
H2: Worked Example 15.6
P: Problem. A UPS path is available 99.9 % of the time, about 8.8 hours of downtime a year. Find the availability and expected downtime of two independent paths in parallel.
P: Solution. 1 − 0.001² = 0.999999, or 99.9999 %, about 32 seconds of downtime a year. If a shared component with 99.99 % availability sits in series with both paths, the system's availability falls to about 99.99 %, 53 minutes a year: the common element dominates.
P: The UPS and its storage protect the load from the grid's failures. Chapter 16 turns the question around: how the grid itself stays stable as inverter-based plants replace the machines that once held it together.
BENCH: Test the backup the way it will fail: open the upstream breaker rather than sending a control signal, and log a server supply's bus voltage on a scope through the transfer. Measure a battery string's runtime under real load every year, because capacity fades with age and temperature long before a cell fails outright.
