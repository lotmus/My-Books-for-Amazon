H1: Chapter 7. Rectifiers, Power Factor, and the Grid
P: A building full of desktop computers can overheat a neutral conductor that carries no fundamental current at all. Each computer's rectifier draws short pulses at the peaks of the mains, rich in third harmonic, and in a three-phase system the third harmonics of the three phases add in the neutral instead of canceling.
P: Where you meet this: Every power supply with a power-factor rating, every 'PFC' block in a server or LED-lighting supply, every harmonic limit in IEC 61000-3-2, and every grid-code requirement for an inverter.
P: Most converters ultimately draw their power from the alternating-current grid, and many now feed power back into it, from solar panels, batteries, and electric vehicles. At that boundary, a converter is no longer only a circuit: it is a load or a generator on a shared network, and the network's operators set rules for how it may behave. This chapter covers the rectifier and the power-factor corrector that sit at the front of every mains-powered supply, the harmonic currents they must limit, the three-phase arithmetic of larger installations, and the grid codes that govern inverters feeding the grid.
H2: 7.1 The rectifier and its harmonics
P: The simplest front end is a diode bridge followed by a large capacitor. The capacitor charges only near the peaks of the mains waveform, so the current flows in short, tall pulses rather than a sine wave. Those pulses are rich in odd harmonics, the third, fifth, seventh, and higher, which carry no average power but heat wiring and transformers, distort the voltage for other users, and in three-phase systems add up in the neutral conductor rather than canceling. The power factor is the ratio of real power to the product of rms voltage and rms current. For a sinusoidal voltage, it is the product of a displacement factor, the cosine of the phase angle of the fundamental current, and a distortion factor set by the total harmonic distortion of the current.
EQ: PF = cos φ × 1/√(1 + THD²)

H2: Worked Example 7.1
P: Problem. A 1 kW load on a 230 V supply draws current with 100% THD and no phase shift of the fundamental. Find its power factor and rms current, and compare it with a load of unity power factor.
P: Solution. PF = 1/√(1 + 1) = 0.707. The rms current is 1000/(230 × 0.707) = 6.15 A, against 4.35 A at unity power factor. The extra 1.8 A heats the wiring and limits how many such loads a 16 A circuit can carry: at PF 0.6, a 16 A, 230 V circuit delivers only 2.21 kW of real power; at PF 0.99, it delivers 3.64 kW.
H2: 7.2 Active power-factor correction
P: IEC 61000-3-2 limits harmonic current emissions from electrical and electronic equipment with a rated input current up to and including 16 A per phase when it is intended for connection to a public low-voltage distribution system. Its scope and limits depend on the equipment category, class, and specified test conditions; 75 W is not a universal threshold for every product. The standard solution for many mains supplies is a boost converter, Chapter 4’s topology, placed after the bridge. Its controller shapes the inductor current to follow the rectified sine wave, so the line current is nearly sinusoidal and in phase with the voltage, and it delivers a DC bus of about 400 V to the isolated converter that follows. At higher powers, interleaved and bridgeless totem-pole PFC stages, made practical by gallium-nitride and silicon-carbide switches, remove the bridge’s diode losses; actual efficiency depends on the design and operating point.
P: Key idea. A converter on the grid is judged by the current it draws, not only by the power it delivers. A power-factor corrector makes that current a sine wave in phase with the voltage, so the wiring carries power and not just current.
H2: 7.3 Three-phase power
P: Larger loads and every data center take three-phase power. For a balanced load with line-to-line voltage VL and line current IL, the real power is √3 VL IL PF. The three phases' fundamental currents sum to zero in the neutral, but the third harmonics, and all multiples of three, are in phase on all three phases and add: if each phase carries a 30% third harmonic, the neutral carries 90% of the fundamental phase current, and an undersized neutral can overheat. A three-phase inverter or rectifier with an active front end needs a DC link above the peak line-to-line voltage, √2 × 400 = 566 V for a 400 V system, which is one reason 800 V DC links and 1200 V switches are common.
EQ: P = √3 VL IL PF
H2: Worked Example 7.2
P: Problem. A 100 kW rack row is fed at 400 V three-phase with a power factor of 0.99. Find the line current, and the minimum DC link voltage for an active front end.
P: Solution. IL = 100,000/(√3 × 400 × 0.99) = 145.8 A per phase. The peak of the line-to-line voltage is √2 × 400 = 566 V, so the DC link must exceed that with margin for voltage tolerance and control, typically 650 to 800 V.
H2: 7.4 The grid's frequencies and codes
P: The grid frequency is 60 Hz in North America, Brazil, Korea, and Taiwan; 50 Hz in Europe, China, India, most of Africa, Australia, and much of South America; and split in Japan, with eastern Japan at 50 Hz and western Japan at 60 Hz, joined by frequency converters. Equipment that will be sold worldwide must work at both. Inverters that feed the grid must also meet grid codes, which differ by country and do not travel with a converter design: IEEE 1547-2018 in the United States, the EN 50549 series in Europe, and national rules such as VDE-AR-N 4105 in Germany. The codes require inverters to stay connected and support the grid through short voltage and frequency disturbances, called ride-through, to supply or absorb reactive power to help regulate voltage, to reduce real power when frequency rises, and to disconnect cleanly when the grid is lost, preventing an unintentional island that could endanger line workers.
H2: Worked Example 7.3
P: Problem. A 10 kW solar inverter converts at 97.5%. Find its waste. If the grid code requires it to supply reactive power at a power factor of 0.9 while delivering 10 kW, what apparent power must it be rated for?
P: Solution. The waste is 10 × (1/0.975 − 1) = 0.256 kW, 256 W. At PF 0.9, the apparent power is 10/0.9 = 11.1 kVA, so the inverter must be rated for 11.1 kVA, not 10 kVA: grid support costs semiconductor and filter capacity, even though it carries no extra real power.
H2: 7.5 Designing a boost PFC stage
P: A boost power-factor corrector draws a sinusoidal current from the rectified mains and delivers it into a bulk capacitor at about 400 V. Its instantaneous input power follows sin²(ωt), so it pulses at twice the line frequency, while the load draws steady power. The bulk capacitor absorbs the difference, and its voltage ripples at 100 or 120 Hz with an amplitude set by the capacitance. The voltage loop of the PFC must be slow, crossing over at 10 to 20 Hz, or it would try to correct this ripple and distort the input current. The inductor is sized at the peak of the low-line sinusoid, where the current is largest, and in continuous conduction for powers above a few hundred watts.
EQ: ΔVpp ≈ P / (2π fline C Vbus)
H2: 7.6 The bridgeless totem-pole PFC
P: In a conventional PFC the current passes through two diodes of the bridge rectifier and then through the boost switch or diode, three semiconductor drops in series. At low line, the bridge alone costs about 1 % of the power. The totem-pole PFC removes the bridge. One half-bridge of fast switches, GaN or SiC, operates at high frequency as the boost switch and synchronous rectifier, swapping roles every half cycle of the line; the other half-bridge, of slow, low-resistance MOSFETs, switches at the line frequency to steer the return current. The fast half-bridge must survive hard switching in continuous conduction, which silicon MOSFETs cannot because of their body diode's reverse recovery, so the topology became practical only with wide-bandgap switches. It is what lets 80 PLUS Titanium server supplies reach 99 % in their PFC stage.
H2: Worked Example 7.4
P: Problem. A 1 kW PFC stage holds a 400 V bus on 50 Hz mains. Find the bulk capacitance for 20 V peak-to-peak ripple at the bus.
P: Solution. C = P/(2π fline Vbus ΔV) = 1000/(2π × 50 × 400 × 20) = 398 µF; a standard 470 µF capacitor gives 17 V. The hold-up time of Chapter 15 often demands more, and then sets the value.
H2: Worked Example 7.5
P: Problem. A 1 kW PFC runs from 230 VAC. Estimate the loss in a diode bridge with 0.9 V per diode, and the loss in the slow leg of a totem pole built from 40 mΩ MOSFETs.
P: Solution. The RMS input current is 1000/230 = 4.35 A and its rectified average 0.9 × 4.35 = 3.91 A. Two diodes conduct at a time, so the bridge loses 2 × 0.9 × 3.91 = 7.0 W, 0.7 % of the power. In the slow leg one MOSFET conducts each half cycle, so the loss is 4.35² × 0.04 = 0.76 W. Removing the bridge saves about 6 W, the difference between 98.3 and 98.9 % efficiency.
H2: 7.7 The harmonic limits
P: IEC 61000-3-2 and related national or regional requirements limit harmonic currents for equipment within their stated scope; they did not impose one universal power-factor-correction rule on every PC and television. Under the current IEC standard, rated input current, intended supply connection, equipment category, class, and test conditions determine which limits apply. Class D includes defined categories such as personal computers, monitors, and television receivers; its limits are specified by harmonic and, for some components, in relation to power. A capacitor-input rectifier draws current in short peaks and may exceed the applicable limits, which is why many supplies use active power-factor correction. Check the current standard and product requirements for a particular design rather than treating 75 W as a universal legal trigger.
H2: Worked Example 7.6
P: Problem. A 300 W computer supply falls in Class D. Find its third-harmonic limit, and compare it with the third harmonic of a capacitor-input rectifier drawing 300 W at 230 V with a third harmonic equal to 80 % of its fundamental.
P: Solution. The limit is 3.4 mA/W × 300 W = 1.02 A. The rectifier's fundamental is about 300/230 = 1.30 A at unity displacement, so its third harmonic is 0.8 × 1.30 = 1.04 A, at the limit with no margin; in practice its peaky current gives an even larger third harmonic, and the fifth and seventh fail too. Active PFC reduces the third harmonic to a few percent.
P: Power-factor correction makes a converter a good citizen of the grid. Chapter 8 turns to converters at the other extreme of voltage, where insulation and stored energy dominate the design.
BENCH: Measure power factor and harmonics with a power analyzer, not by multiplying rms readings from separate meters, and use a true-rms clamp meter: an averaging meter misreads the pulse current of a rectifier. When a PFC stage's current shows a notch or a flat top near the zero crossings, check the current loop's bandwidth and whether the boost inductor saturates at the line peak.
