H1: Chapter 3. The Buck Converter
P: A buck converter that turns 12 V into 1 V switches at 500 kHz, so each cycle lasts 2 µs. Its high-side switch is on for only 167 ns of that, a twelfth of the time, yet the converter delivers 20 A at 89 % efficiency. The inductor and capacitor account for the remaining 1.833 µs. How this works is the subject of this chapter.
P: Where you meet this: Every point-of-load converter on a motherboard, every phone's power-management chip, the battery eliminator circuit (BEC) that powers a radio-control receiver and servos, and every 'buck' in a regulator data sheet's typical application.
P: A switching converter avoids the linear regulator's loss by never operating its transistor as a resistor. The switch is either fully on, with little voltage across it, or fully off, with no current through it, and an inductor and capacitor smooth the resulting pulses into a steady output. The buck converter steps a voltage down. It is the most widely used switching converter, from the regulators beside a processor to the chargers in phones, and it introduces the ideas of duty cycle, ripple, and loss that every other topology reuses. Robert Erickson and Dragan Maksimović's Fundamentals of Power Electronics is the standard reference for its analysis.
P: Model limit. The ideal duty-cycle and ripple equations are a starting point. Inductor winding resistance adds I²R loss; core loss depends on the actual flux waveform and temperature; capacitor ESR adds ripple heating, while ESL and layout inductance shape fast spikes. Use the component data at the operating point and confirm the waveform on the assembled board (Chapter 9).
H2: 3.1 Duty cycle and volt-second balance
P: In a synchronous buck, a high-side switch connects the inductor to the input for a fraction D of each period, and a low-side switch connects it to ground for the rest. In steady state the inductor's average voltage must be zero, or its current would grow without limit. During the on-time it sees Vin − Vout, during the off-time −Vout; balancing the two gives the output voltage. The inductor current rises and falls by a ripple ΔI each cycle, set by the inductance and switching frequency, and the output capacitor absorbs the ripple, leaving a small voltage ripple.
EQ: Vout = D Vin,    ΔI = (Vin − Vout) D / (fsw L),    ΔV ≈ ΔI / (8 fsw C) + ΔI × ESR

H2: Worked Example 3.1
P: Problem. A buck converts 12 V to 1.0 V at 20 A, switching at 500 kHz with a 0.47 µH inductor and 200 µF of ceramic capacitance with 1 mΩ of total ESR. Find the duty cycle, the inductor ripple, and the output ripple.
P: Solution. D = 1.0/12 = 0.0833, an on-time of only 167 ns. ΔI = (12 − 1.0) × 0.0833/(500 × 10³ × 0.47 × 10⁻⁶) = 3.90 A, 19.5% of the load current, within the usual 20 to 40% guideline. The capacitive ripple is 3.90/(8 × 500 × 10³ × 200 × 10⁻⁶) = 4.9 mV and the ESR term is 3.9 mV; the two peak at different instants, so the total is less than their 8.8 mV sum, about 6.6 mV peak to peak, well under 1% of the output.
H2: 3.2 Where the losses are
P: The buck's losses fall into a few categories. Conduction loss is the rms current squared times the resistance of each switch, weighted by the fraction of time it conducts, plus the inductor's winding resistance. Switching loss occurs during each transition, when the high-side switch briefly carries current with voltage across it; a simple estimate is ½VinI(tr + tf)fsw. Gate-drive loss and the reverse-recovery or dead-time loss of the low-side switch add more. Conduction loss favors larger switches, and switching loss favors smaller ones, so each design has an optimum.
H2: Worked Example 3.2
P: Problem. For the converter of Example 3.1, the high-side switch has 2 mΩ, the low-side 1 mΩ, and the inductor 1 mΩ. The transition times total 10 ns. Estimate the conduction, switching, and inductor losses, and the efficiency.
P: Solution. The rms inductor current is √(20² + 3.90²/12) = 20.03 A. The high-side switch conducts for 8.3% of the time and the low-side for 91.7%, so conduction loss is 20.03² × (0.0833 × 0.002 + 0.917 × 0.001) = 0.43 W. The switching loss is ½ × 12 × 20 × 10 × 10⁻⁹ × 500 × 10³ = 0.60 W, and the inductor loses 20.03² × 0.001 = 0.40 W. The three modeled losses total 1.44 W. Dividing 20 W output by 21.44 W input gives 93.3% for this partial loss model, not the converter’s complete efficiency: gate-drive, output-capacitance, dead-time, controller, and other losses are omitted. Chapter 9 adds several switch losses to a more detailed estimate. At 0.8 V and higher currents, the same converter loses more of its efficiency, which is why processor regulators use many phases in parallel, each carrying 30 to 60 A.
P: Key idea. A switching converter is efficient because its switches are never resistors for long. Its losses are conduction, which grows with current squared, and switching, which grows with voltage, current, and frequency.
H2: 3.3 The hot loop
P: In a buck, the input capacitor, the high-side switch, and the low-side switch form a loop through which the current switches abruptly on every transition. The loop's inductance, even a nanohenry, generates a voltage spike L di/dt that adds to the switches' stress and radiates. A 20 A change in 2 ns across 1 nH gives 10 V, a large overshoot on a 12 V input. The first rule of buck layout is therefore to make this hot loop as small as possible, with the input capacitor placed directly across the switches and the return path on the adjacent layer. The output side, where current flows smoothly through the inductor, is far less critical. Emissions from the hot loop are measured in Book 7's chamber; this book's job is to keep the loop small enough that they do not arise.
H2: 3.4 Choosing the frequency
P: A higher switching frequency allows a smaller inductor and capacitor for the same ripple, and a faster response to load steps, at the cost of more switching loss. Silicon buck converters typically switch at 300 kHz to 2 MHz. Gallium-nitride switches, with lower capacitance and charge, allow several megahertz at the same loss. Converters for processors often switch near 1 MHz per phase and interleave many phases, so that the total ripple frequency is high while each phase switches slowly enough to stay efficient.
H2: 3.5 Choosing the inductor and the capacitors
P: The inductor is chosen for three currents. Its inductance sets the ripple, usually 20 to 40 % of the full-load current. Its saturation current must exceed the peak, the load current plus half the ripple plus margin for transients and current-limit overshoot, because a saturating inductor's current rises almost without limit. Its RMS rating, set by temperature rise, must exceed the RMS current. Molded powder inductors such as Coilcraft's XAL family saturate softly and suit most bucks; ferrite parts saturate abruptly but have lower core loss at high frequency. The output capacitor sets the ripple and the transient response, and ceramic capacitors lose much of their capacitance under DC bias, a 22 µF 6.3 V X5R part in 0805 may keep only 10 µF at 3.3 V, so the data sheet's DC-bias curve, not the nominal value, goes into the calculation.
P: The input capacitor is chosen for its RMS current, which is the most frequently overlooked stress in a buck. The input current is a pulse train of height Iout for a fraction D of the time, so the capacitor carries an RMS current of Iout√(D(1 − D)), at most half the load current at D = 0.5. Ceramic capacitors carry it easily; aluminum electrolytics overheat if they are sized for capacitance alone.
EQ: Icin,rms = Iout √(D(1 − D))
H2: Worked Example 3.3
P: Problem. Design the power stage of a 12 V to 3.3 V, 5 A buck at 500 kHz with 30 % ripple and 20 mV of capacitive output ripple. Find L, the inductor peak and saturation requirement, the output capacitance, and the input capacitor's RMS current.
P: Solution. D = 3.3/12 = 0.275 and ΔI = 1.5 A, so L = (12 − 3.3) × 0.275/(500 × 10³ × 1.5) = 3.19 µH; choose 3.3 µH. The peak current is 5 + 0.75 = 5.75 A; with margin for transients, specify a saturation current of at least 7 to 8 A. For 20 mV, C = ΔI/(8 fsw ΔV) = 1.5/(8 × 500 × 10³ × 0.02) = 18.8 µF of effective capacitance, so three 22 µF ceramics that each keep about 10 µF at 3.3 V. The input capacitor carries 5 × √(0.275 × 0.725) = 2.23 A RMS, which two 10 µF 25 V ceramics share comfortably.
H2: Worked Example 3.4
P: Problem. A buck runs at D = 0.5 with a 10 A load. Find the input capacitor's RMS current, and compare it with D = 0.1.
P: Solution. At D = 0.5, Icin = 10 × √0.25 = 5.0 A, the worst case. At D = 0.1, 10 × √0.09 = 3.0 A. A converter that operates over a wide input range must be checked at the duty cycle nearest 0.5.
H2: 3.6 Minimum on-time and the limits of step-down
P: A buck controller cannot turn its high-side switch on for less than a minimum time, set by the driver delays, the current-sense blanking, and the switches' own transitions: typically 40 to 100 ns for a silicon controller, 10 to 30 ns for some GaN designs. The duty cycle D = Vout/Vin therefore has a floor of ton,min × fsw, and a converter asked for less either skips pulses or regulates poorly. High step-down ratios at high frequency are where it bites: 48 V to 1 V is a duty cycle of 2 %, which at 1 MHz is a 21 ns on-time. Designers respond by lowering the frequency, using two stages, or choosing topologies with a transformer or switched capacitors that divide the voltage before the buck.
EQ: Vout,min = Vin × ton,min × fsw
H2: Worked Example 3.5
P: Problem. A controller has a minimum on-time of 50 ns. What is the lowest output it can regulate from 48 V at 2 MHz and at 400 kHz?
P: Solution. At 2 MHz, Dmin = 50 ns × 2 MHz = 0.10, so Vout,min = 4.8 V: a 3.3 V output is impossible. At 400 kHz, Dmin = 0.02 and Vout,min = 0.96 V, so 3.3 V works, at the price of a larger inductor and capacitor.
P: The buck steps voltage down. Chapter 4 rearranges the same switch, diode, and inductor to step it up or invert it, and then closes the loop that holds the output steady.
BENCH: Probe the switch node with a short ground spring on the probe tip, not the long ground lead, or the ringing you see is your probe. Measure output ripple with the tip and the ground barrel pressed directly across the output capacitor, with the scope's bandwidth limit set to 20 MHz. If the pulse widths jitter or the inductor whines, check whether the on-time is close to the controller's minimum.
P: On the Job
P: Almost every product with a circuit board has bucks on it, and most engineers who place them never design one from discrete parts: they choose an integrated converter from TI, Analog Devices, Infineon, Renesas, or MPS and prove that it works. The tools are the vendor's design calculator, LTspice, KiCad or Altium, an electronic load, and a scope with a spring-ground probe. A typical task: pick a buck for an STM32 board's 3.3 V rail from a 12 V input at 1 A, copy the vendor's layout around the hot loop, and verify ripple, load-step response, and switch-node ringing on the scope before the board goes to EMC testing. This chapter is what lets you read that data sheet critically.
