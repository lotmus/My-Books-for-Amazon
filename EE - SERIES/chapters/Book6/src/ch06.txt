H1: Chapter 6. Magnetics: Four Shelves
P: At 60 Hz, current spreads through 8.5 mm of copper, so a mains transformer can be wound with thick wire or bars. At 100 kHz it crowds into the outer 0.21 mm, and at 1 MHz into 66 µm, thinner than a sheet of office paper. At those frequencies the middle of a thick wire is copper that you paid for, that carries almost no current, and that still sits in the winding window.
P: Model limit. A core material name alone does not specify inductance, loss, temperature rise, or saturation margin. Use the exact core geometry, winding, bias, flux waveform, frequency, and temperature curves; the core’s catalog point is not the converter’s operating point.
P: Where you meet this: Every inductor and transformer data sheet, every Litz-wire specification, every ferrite manufacturer's core-loss chart, every planar transformer in a circuit board, and the coils of every wireless charger.
P: Almost every converter in this book depends on a magnetic component, and the magnetics usually decide its size, its loss, and its cost. They are also the least standardized part of the design: an inductor or transformer is often custom, and its designer must choose a core material, a shape, a winding, and a gap. It helps to sort magnetic components into four families, which this book calls shelves, because each uses different materials, rules, and references. Shelf A is line-frequency iron. Shelf B is switch-mode ferrite and powder. Shelf C is the pulse transformer of high-voltage and pulsed power, Chapter 8's subject. Shelf D is the RF transformer and balun, whose transmission-line behavior links to Book 4. A standard SMD inductor bought from a catalog is a circuit component, Book 2's subject; this chapter is about magnetics that are designed.
H2: 6.1 Skin depth
P: At high frequencies, current crowds toward the surface of a conductor, flowing mainly within a skin depth δ of it. For copper at room temperature, δ ≈ 66/√f millimeters, with f in hertz. At 60 Hz it is 8.5 mm, so a mains transformer can use thick wire or bars. At 100 kHz it is 0.21 mm, so a wire thicker than about 0.4 mm carries current only in its outer shell, and its AC resistance rises above its DC value. Adjacent conductors in a winding make it worse, through the proximity effect, in which each layer's field induces currents in the others. Switch-mode windings therefore use Litz wire, made of many fine insulated strands, thin foil, or planar windings in a circuit board.
EQ: δ ≈ 66 / √f  mm   (copper, f in Hz)

H2: Worked Example 6.1
P: Problem. Find the skin depth in copper at 60 Hz, 100 kHz, and 1 MHz. What strand diameter would a Litz wire use at 100 kHz?
P: Solution. 66/√60 = 8.5 mm, 66/√10⁵ = 0.21 mm, and 66/√10⁶ = 0.066 mm, or 66 µm. A Litz strand should be well under a skin depth, commonly a third to a half of it, so about 0.07 to 0.1 mm at 100 kHz; strands of 0.1 mm, about AWG 38, are a common choice there.
H2: 6.2 Shelf A: line-frequency iron
P: A transformer on the 50 or 60 Hz mains uses laminated silicon steel, which saturates at about 1.5 to 1.8 T, far higher than ferrite, but has high losses at high frequencies. For a sine-wave voltage, Faraday's law gives the number of turns needed to keep the peak flux density below a chosen limit. The low frequency forces a large core: at 50 Hz, a given transformer needs 20% more turns, or core area, than at 60 Hz for the same voltage. Line-frequency transformers survive in utility distribution, welding, and where robustness matters more than weight. Colonel Wm. T. McLyman's Transformer and Inductor Design Handbook is the standard reference for sizing.
EQ: Vrms = 4.44 f N Bpk Ae   (sine);   V = 4 f N Bpk Ae   (square wave)
H2: Worked Example 6.2
P: Problem. A 230 V, 50 Hz transformer uses a core of 10 cm² cross-section at a peak flux density of 1.5 T. Find the primary turns. How many are needed for the same voltage at 60 Hz?
P: Solution. N = 230/(4.44 × 50 × 1.5 × 10⁻³) = 691 turns. At 60 Hz, N = 230/(4.44 × 60 × 1.5 × 10⁻³) = 576 turns. A 115 V, 60 Hz primary on the same core would need only 288 turns.
H2: 6.3 Shelf B: switch-mode ferrite and powder
P: Ferrites have low losses from tens of kilohertz to a few megahertz but saturate at only about 0.3 to 0.5 T, and their saturation falls as they heat. A switch-mode transformer is therefore usually limited by core loss rather than saturation: the flux swing is chosen to keep loss acceptable, often near 0.1 T at 100 kHz. Core loss is described by the Steinmetz relation P = k f^α B^β, with β typically 2.5 to 3, so doubling the flux swing multiplies the loss by roughly six. An inductor or flyback transformer must store energy, and that energy is stored almost entirely in an air gap, because ferrite's high permeability stores very little. Powder cores of iron, iron-silicon, or other alloys have a distributed gap built in and saturate softly, which suits inductors with large DC currents.
EQ: N = L Ipk / (Bmax Ae),    lg ≈ μ0 N² Ae / L,    E = ½ L I²
H2: Worked Example 6.3
P: Problem. Design a 10 µH inductor for a 12 A peak current on a ferrite core with Ae = 50 mm², at a peak flux density of 0.3 T. Find the turns, the gap, and the stored energy. Find the turns for a 48 V, 100 kHz square-wave transformer winding on the same core at 0.1 T.
P: Solution. N = 10⁻⁵ × 12/(0.3 × 50 × 10⁻⁶) = 8 turns. The gap is lg = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 64 × 50 × 10⁻⁶/10⁻⁵ = 0.40 mm, ignoring fringing, which in practice requires a slightly larger gap. The stored energy is ½ × 10⁻⁵ × 144 = 720 µJ. For the transformer, N = 48/(4 × 10⁵ × 0.1 × 50 × 10⁻⁶) = 24 turns.
P: Key idea. Name the shelf before you name the core. Line iron, switch-mode ferrite, pulse transformers, and RF baluns each have their own materials, limits, and design rules, and an inductor's energy lives in its gap.
H2: 6.4 Shelves C and D
P: A pulse transformer, shelf C, must pass a fast rectangular pulse with little rise time, overshoot, or droop. Its rise time is set by leakage inductance and winding capacitance, and its droop by magnetizing inductance, as Chapter 8 calculates for a radar modulator. Its insulation must withstand tens of kilovolts. An RF transformer or balun, shelf D, works at megahertz to gigahertz frequencies and often uses its windings as transmission lines, so that the bandwidth is set by line lengths rather than core permeability; Jerry Sevick's books describe these transmission-line transformers. Marian Kazimierczuk's High-Frequency Magnetic Components explains how the behavior of all four shelves changes with frequency.
H2: 6.5 Sizing a core by its area product
P: An inductor must store its energy without saturating the core and carry its current without overheating the winding. The two requirements meet in the area product, the core's cross-section Ae times its window area Aw. Faraday's law limits the flux density, LIpk = N Bmax Ae; the window must hold N turns of wire carrying Irms at a current density J, with a fill factor Ku of about 0.3 to 0.5 for round wire. Eliminating N gives the area product the core must at least provide, and a core table, listing Ap for each size, gives the smallest core that works. J of 4 to 6 A/mm² suits natural convection; Bmax is about 0.25 to 0.3 T for ferrite at low frequency, less when core loss dominates, and up to 1 T or more for powder cores at moderate ripple.
EQ: Ap = Ae Aw ≥ L Ipk Irms / (Bmax J Ku)
H2: 6.6 Core loss and the Steinmetz equation
P: Core loss per unit volume follows the empirical Steinmetz equation, Pv = k f^α B^β, with α and β dependent on material, flux waveform, and temperature. Doubling flux swing can multiply loss by about six, while doubling frequency may multiply it by two to three, but neither exponent is a temperature coefficient. Ferrite loss can rise or fall with temperature; use the material’s loss-versus-temperature curve at the actual frequency and flux. TDK’s N87 data, for example, specifies 375 kW/m³ at 100 kHz, 200 mT, and 100 °C, and publishes the temperature curve. For non-sinusoidal flux, use a waveform-appropriate model such as iGSE, then check it against the manufacturer’s limits.
P: Thermal-budget example. At that published N87 point, a 10 cm³ magnetic volume dissipates 375,000 W/m³ × 10⁻⁵ m³ = 3.75 W. With an assumed 15 °C/W core-to-ambient path and 45 °C ambient, the first-pass temperature is about 101 °C, just over a 100 °C design limit; iterate against the actual temperature curve and geometry. TDK also lists N87 saturation flux density as 490 mT at 25 °C and 390 mT at 100 °C under its stated test conditions. A design relying on 450 mT because it is below the room-temperature figure loses that margin when hot; falling inductance steepens current rise, can trip current limit, and can pull the rail out of regulation. These are N87-specific published values, not universal ferrite constants.
P: Published source: TDK Electronics, SIFERRIT material N87 data sheet
P: The winding has its own limit: DC resistance adds I²R copper loss, while skin and proximity effects raise AC resistance as frequency and harmonic content rise. A datasheet DCR value alone therefore understates winding loss in a high-ripple inductor. Use the manufacturer’s frequency-dependent data or a waveform-appropriate model, then compare with temperature and loss measurements.
EQ: Pv = k f^α B^β
H2: Worked Example 6.4
P: Problem. Find the area product for a 10 µH inductor with Ipk = 12 A and Irms = 10 A, using Bmax = 0.3 T, J = 5 A/mm², and Ku = 0.4.
P: Solution. Ap = 10 × 10⁻⁶ × 12 × 10/(0.3 × 5 × 10⁶ × 0.4) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ m⁴, or 0.20 cm⁴. A core with Ae of about 0.5 cm² and a window of 0.4 cm² or more meets it. The turns follow from N = LIpk/(BmaxAe) = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴/(0.3 × 0.5 × 10⁻⁴) = 8 turns, and the gap is then set to give 10 µH with 8 turns.
H2: Worked Example 6.5
P: Problem. A ferrite transformer loses 1.0 W in its core at 100 kHz and 100 mT peak. With β = 2.6 and α = 1.4, estimate the loss if the designer doubles the frequency and halves the turns, which doubles the flux swing, or doubles the frequency and keeps the turns, which halves the flux.
P: Solution. Doubling f and B multiplies the loss by 2^1.4 × 2^2.6 = 2.64 × 6.06 = 16, to 16 W. Doubling f and halving B multiplies it by 2^1.4 × 2^(−2.6) = 2.64 × 0.165 = 0.44, to 0.44 W. Raising the frequency only helps if the flux swing falls with it.
H2: 6.7 Proximity effect and Litz wire
P: Skin depth, Section 6.1, describes one conductor alone. In a winding, each turn sits in the magnetic field of its neighbors, which induces eddy currents that crowd the current to one side of the wire. This proximity effect grows rapidly with the number of layers: in a multilayer winding, the AC resistance of the outer layers can be ten or more times their DC resistance even when the wire is only a little thicker than the skin depth. Dowell's analysis quantifies it as a function of the ratio of conductor thickness to skin depth and the number of layers. The cures are to use fewer layers, to interleave primary and secondary so that the field between them falls, to use foil or planar windings for low-voltage high-current windings, and to use Litz wire, many individually insulated strands twisted so that each strand spends equal time at every position in the bundle, with strand diameters well below the skin depth.
H2: Worked Example 6.6
P: Problem. A transformer designed for 100 kHz is to be redesigned for 500 kHz. Find the skin depth in copper at each frequency, and choose a Litz strand size for each.
P: Solution. δ = 66/√(100 × 10³) mm = 0.21 mm. Strands should be well under δ, commonly a third to a half of it at the operating frequency, so 0.1 mm strands, AWG 38, are a common choice; at 500 kHz, δ = 0.093 mm, and 0.05 mm strands, AWG 44, are needed, which raise the cost and lower the copper fill.
P: The magnetics, with the switches and capacitors, are the parts of a converter. Chapter 7 connects the whole converter to the AC grid, where the shape of the current it draws becomes the grid's problem.
BENCH: When an inductor runs hotter than its DC resistance predicts, suspect AC winding loss or core loss before blaming the current measurement. Read the core manufacturer's loss curves at your own flux swing and frequency, not at the catalog test point. To check for saturation, watch the inductor current at the highest input and full load: a slope that steepens near the peak means the core is running out. The catalogue test point is obliging; the converter is not.
