H1: Chapter 8. High Voltage and Pulsed Power
P: In the radars of the Second World War, each transmitted pulse came from a ladder of inductors and capacitors charged to many kilovolts and discharged through a spark gap or a hydrogen thyratron into a magnetron. That pulse-forming network, a lumped imitation of a transmission line, still shapes rectangular high-voltage pulses today.
P: Where you meet this: Every radar transmitter's modulator, every X-ray and electron-beam supply, every defibrillator's energy-storage capacitor, every creepage and clearance table in a safety standard, and every warning label on a high-voltage cabinet.
P: Most of this book deals with voltages below a kilovolt and continuous power. Two specialties go beyond that. High-voltage engineering deals with the insulation, spacing, and breakdown of systems at tens to hundreds of kilovolts: X-ray sources, electrostatic precipitators, particle accelerators, and the transmission grid. Pulsed power stores energy slowly and releases it in microseconds or nanoseconds, at peak powers of megawatts to terawatts, to drive radar tubes, lasers, and research machines. Both share a concern that ordinary converters can mostly ignore: the electric field, and what happens when it exceeds what the insulation can stand. Safety is the first consideration in both. Charged capacitors at these energies are lethal, and the work requires interlocks, grounding sticks, and training that no book replaces.
H2: 8.1 Breakdown and insulation
P: Air at atmospheric pressure breaks down at about 3 kV/mm in a uniform field, less in the concentrated field at a sharp point or edge, which also produces corona, a continuous partial discharge that erodes insulation and creates interference. Paschen's law describes how breakdown depends on the product of pressure and gap: there is a minimum, near 330 V for air, below which no gap breaks down, and the breakdown voltage rises at both lower and higher pressure-gap products. Solid insulation fails by partial discharge in voids, tracking along surfaces, and slow aging. Designs therefore set two distances: clearance through air, and creepage along surfaces, given by safety standards such as IEC 60664-1 for the voltage, pollution degree, and material group. Edges are rounded and fields graded, so that no point sees much more than the average field.

H2: Worked Example 8.1
P: Problem. What is the smallest uniform-field air gap that would withstand 25 kV, and why is a practical design much larger?
P: Solution. At 3 kV/mm, 25/3 = 8.3 mm. A practical design uses several times that, because real electrodes have field enhancement at edges, humidity and contamination lower the threshold, transients exceed the working voltage, and safety standards add margins. Rounding electrodes, adding solid barriers, or immersing the assembly in oil or a pressurized insulating gas reduces the size.
H2: 8.2 Storing and releasing energy
P: Pulsed power begins with a store, usually capacitors, charged over milliseconds or seconds, and a switch, a spark gap, thyratron, or stack of solid-state devices, that discharges it in microseconds. The energy in a capacitor is ½CV². A Marx generator charges n capacitors in parallel and then switches them in series, multiplying the voltage by n: ten stages charged to 30 kV give 300 kV. Pulse-forming networks shape the release into a flat-topped pulse.
EQ: E = ½ C V²,    VMarx ≈ n Vcharge
H2: Worked Example 8.2
P: Problem. Find the energy stored in a 1 µF capacitor at 10 kV and in a 2 µF capacitor at 20 kV. Find the output of an eight-stage Marx charged to 40 kV.
P: Solution. ½ × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁸ = 50 J and ½ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 4 × 10⁸ = 400 J. The Marx produces about 8 × 40 = 320 kV, less in practice because of stray capacitance and switch losses. 50 J delivered in 1 µs is 50 MW of peak power, from a supply that might be rated at only a few hundred watts.
P: Key idea. Pulsed power trades time for power. Energy is collected slowly at modest power and released quickly at enormous power, and the design problem is the switch, the pulse shape, and the field the insulation must withstand.
H2: 8.3 The line-type modulator
P: A radar transmitter built around a magnetron or klystron, Book 4's subject, needs a rectangular high-voltage pulse at each transmit instant. A line-type modulator charges a pulse-forming network, a ladder of inductors and capacitors that approximates a transmission line of impedance Z0 = √(L/C) and delay τ/2, to a voltage V, and discharges it through a switch into a matched load. The network delivers a pulse of width τ at V/2 across the load. Its total capacitance and inductance follow from the impedance and pulse width. A pulse transformer, shelf C of Chapter 6, steps the pulse up to the tube's voltage.
EQ: C_total = τ / (2 Z0),    L_total = τ Z0 / 2,    Vload = Vcharge / 2
H2: Worked Example 8.3
P: Problem. A magnetron needs 1 µs pulses at 50 kV and 50 A, which is 1 kΩ, at 1 kHz. A 1:10 pulse transformer feeds it from a pulse-forming network. Find the primary pulse voltage, current, and impedance, the network's charge voltage, total capacitance, and inductance, the energy per pulse, and the average power.
P: Solution. The 1:10 transformer reflects 1 kΩ as 1000/100 = 10 Ω, with a primary pulse of 5 kV and 500 A. The network must have Z0 = 10 Ω and is charged to 2 × 5 = 10 kV. Its capacitance is 10⁻⁶/(2 × 10) = 50 nF and its inductance 10⁻⁶ × 10/2 = 5 µH. The energy per pulse is ½ × 50 × 10⁻⁹ × 10⁸ = 2.5 J, which matches the 2.5 MW pulse power times 1 µs. At 1 kHz, the average power is 2.5 kW.

H2: 8.4 The pulse transformer's droop
P: During a pulse, the transformer's magnetizing current rises linearly and diverts current from the load, so the pulse top droops. For a load resistance R referred to the primary and magnetizing inductance Lm, the fractional droop is about Rτ/Lm. The leakage inductance and winding capacitance set the rise time and overshoot, so the designer must balance a large magnetizing inductance, which needs more turns, against small leakage and capacitance, which need fewer. The core must also be reset between pulses, so that its flux does not walk toward saturation.
EQ: droop ≈ R τ / Lm
H2: Worked Example 8.4
P: Problem. For the modulator of Example 8.3, what primary magnetizing inductance limits the droop to 5%? What is it referred to the secondary?
P: Solution. Lm ≥ Rτ/0.05 = 10 × 10⁻⁶/0.05 = 200 µH on the primary. Referred to the secondary, through the square of the 1:10 ratio, it is 20 mH, which is the same as computing with the 1 kΩ load directly: 1000 × 10⁻⁶/0.05 = 20 mH.
H2: 8.5 The Marx generator
P: When a pulse must reach hundreds of kilovolts, charging one capacitor to that voltage would require a supply and insulation at the full voltage the whole time. Erwin Marx's 1924 generator avoids it. N capacitors are charged in parallel through resistors to a moderate voltage V. Spark gaps, or in modern designs solid-state switches, then reconnect them in series, and the output momentarily reaches about NV. The erected capacitance is C/N, and the stored energy, N × ½CV², is the same before and after. Marx generators test insulation with standard lightning impulses, 1.2 µs rise and 50 µs fall, drive flash X-ray tubes and ion accelerators, and, built from MOSFETs or IGBTs with sub-microsecond switching, power pulsed electric-field food processing and medical ablation.
EQ: Vout ≈ N V,   Cerected = C / N,   E = N × ½ C V²
H2: 8.6 Creepage and clearance
P: Insulation in air fails in two ways. Through the air, the shortest distance between conductors, the clearance, must withstand transient overvoltages, and breakdown depends on the peak voltage, the field shape, and the altitude. Along a surface, the distance, the creepage, must withstand the working voltage for years while dust and moisture build up conductive tracks. IEC 60664-1 and product standards derived from it, such as IEC 62368-1 for IT equipment, coordinate these distances. Creepage is selected from steady-state working voltage, pollution degree, and the material group determined from comparative tracking index (CTI). Clearance is selected separately from rated impulse voltage, overvoltage category, field conditions, and altitude. As one creepage example, IEC 60664-1:2020+AMD1:2025 Table F.5 gives 5 mm for basic insulation at 1000 V RMS, pollution degree 2, using its printed-wiring-material column (which excludes material group IIIb); reinforced insulation requires 10 mm. This example does not establish the required clearance or qualify a particular PCB: apply the relevant product standard and verify the actual material and construction. A slot increases creepage only when its dimensions satisfy the applicable standard.
H2: Worked Example 8.5
P: Problem. A ten-stage Marx generator uses 0.1 µF capacitors charged to 30 kV. Find the erected voltage, the erected capacitance, and the stored energy, and the peak current into a 100 Ω load.
P: Solution. The output is about 10 × 30 kV = 300 kV, from 0.1 µF/10 = 10 nF, storing 10 × ½ × 0.1 × 10⁻⁶ × (30 × 10³)² = 450 J. Into 100 Ω the initial current is 300 kV/100 Ω = 3 kA, decaying with a time constant of 100 Ω × 10 nF = 1 µs, before the stray inductance of the stack, which slows the rise, is counted.
H2: Worked Example 8.6
P: Problem. A design at sea level has 3 mm of clearance, adequate for its impulse rating. The product is to be used at 4000 m, where IEC 60664-1 applies a clearance multiplication factor of about 1.29. What clearance is needed?
P: Solution. 3 mm × 1.29 = 3.9 mm. Thinner air breaks down at lower voltage, so clearances grow with altitude, while creepage, which depends on surface contamination, does not.
H2: 8.7 The energy hiding in a cable
P: A high-voltage system stores energy in places that do not look like capacitors. A shielded cable has a capacitance of about 100 pF per meter, depending on its size and insulation, and a long run charged to tens of kilovolts holds enough energy to kill. After the supply is switched off, that charge remains until it is bled away, and dielectric absorption in the insulation can bring part of it back after a brief short. Safe practice therefore treats every high-voltage conductor as charged until it has been grounded with a hot stick, keeps grounding straps on it while work proceeds, and fits permanent bleeder resistors sized to bring the voltage below 50 V within a defined time, often a minute or less.
H2: Worked Example 8.7
P: Problem. A 100 m high-voltage cable with 100 pF/m is charged to 50 kV. Find its stored energy, and the bleeder resistance that brings it below 50 V within 60 s.
P: Solution. C = 10 nF, and E = ½ × 10⁻⁸ × (5 × 10⁴)² = 12.5 J. For comparison, IEC 61010-1 uses a 350 mJ stored-energy limit in its accessible-part hazardous-live criteria above 15 kV for equipment in its scope (measurement, control, and laboratory equipment); this is not a universal safe-energy threshold for high-voltage cables. Here the cable stores about 36 times that amount. Decaying from 50 kV to 50 V takes ln(1000) = 6.9 time constants, so RC ≤ 60/6.9 = 8.7 s and R ≤ 870 MΩ. A bleeder chain must meet each resistor's voltage and power ratings; at 50 kV, 870 MΩ dissipates about 2.9 W. The bleeder is not a substitute for grounding, measuring, and maintaining a shorting lead.
P: High voltage concentrates the converter's problems into insulation and stored energy. Part II goes back inside the converter, starting with an accounting of where its watts are lost.
BENCH: Before touching a high-voltage circuit, discharge it with a grounding stick and then measure it; a bleeder resistor can fail open. Leave a shorting lead across capacitors and long cables while you work, because dielectric absorption lets a discharged capacitor recover part of its voltage. Look at a high-voltage assembly in a dark room: a faint glow or a hiss marks corona at an edge that needs rounding.
