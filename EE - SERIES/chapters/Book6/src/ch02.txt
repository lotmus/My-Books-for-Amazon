H1: Chapter 2. The Linear Regulator
P: A 3.3 V regulator on a crowded board takes its input from a 5 V rail, supplies 500 mA, and does exactly what its data sheet promises. It also runs at 106 °C inside a 55 °C enclosure, because it turns 0.85 W into heat in a package the size of a fingernail. Fed from a 3.8 V switcher instead, the same part runs at 70 °C.
P: Where you meet this: Every LDO on a schematic, the quiet supply of an ECG or pulse-oximeter front end, the radio rail of a Bluetooth or Wi-Fi module, the 3.3 V rail of a robot's microcontroller, and every TL431 in the feedback path of an isolated supply.
P: The simplest way to turn a higher voltage into a lower one is to drop the difference across a transistor that a feedback loop controls. That is a linear regulator, and the low-dropout version, the LDO, is the most common power-management circuit in electronics: nearly every chip, sensor, and radio has one or several. It is quiet, small, and cheap, and its efficiency is fixed by its input and output voltages. This chapter derives that efficiency, sets out the specifications that matter, dropout, power-supply rejection, and noise, and explains the stability question that every LDO designer and user must face.
H2: 2.1 The controlled resistor
P: An LDO is an error amplifier comparing a fraction of the output with a reference, driving a pass transistor between input and output. The pass device acts as a variable resistor that drops whatever voltage is needed to hold the output constant. The load current flows through it, plus a small quiescent current for the control circuit, so the efficiency can never exceed the voltage ratio, and the dissipation is the voltage difference times the current.
EQ: η ≈ Vout / Vin,    Pdiss ≈ (Vin − Vout) Iload
H2: Worked Example 2.1
P: Problem. An LDO supplies 0.5 A at 1.8 V from a 3.3 V rail. Find its efficiency and dissipation. Its package has a junction-to-ambient thermal resistance of 60 °C/W in 25 °C air. Find the junction temperature, and repeat for a small package of 200 °C/W.
P: Solution. η = 1.8/3.3 = 54.5%, and the dissipation is (3.3 − 1.8) × 0.5 = 0.75 W. In the 60 °C/W package the junction reaches 25 + 0.75 × 60 = 70 °C, which is acceptable. In the 200 °C/W package it would reach 175 °C, beyond the usual 125 to 150 °C limit. The regulator's efficiency is not the problem in this case; its heat is, and the remedy is either a better package or a switching converter ahead of it.
H2: 2.2 Dropout
P: The dropout voltage is the smallest input-to-output difference at which the regulator still regulates. With a PMOS or PNP pass device, which can be driven fully on, dropout is the on-resistance times the load current, or the saturation voltage, typically 100 to 300 mV. An NMOS pass device needs its gate above the output and therefore a charge pump or a separate bias rail, but it has lower resistance per area. A low dropout lets the regulator run close to its input, which raises its efficiency and lets a battery be used further into its discharge.
H2: Worked Example 2.2
P: Problem. A PMOS pass device has an on-resistance of 0.3 Ω. Find the dropout at 0.5 A. A lithium cell powers a 3.3 V LDO; at what cell voltage does the output fall out of regulation?
P: Solution. The dropout is 0.3 × 0.5 = 150 mV, so regulation is lost when the cell falls below 3.3 + 0.15 = 3.45 V. A lithium-ion cell spends much of its discharge between about 3.6 and 3.7 V, but it is below 3.45 V for the final part, so a 3.3 V system on one cell often uses a buck-boost converter, Chapter 4's subject, rather than an LDO.
H2: 2.3 Power-supply rejection and noise
P: An LDO's error amplifier also rejects ripple on its input. The power-supply rejection ratio is the ratio of input ripple to output ripple, in decibels, and it is high at low frequencies, where the loop gain is large, typically 60 to 80 dB. It falls at higher frequencies as the loop gain falls, often to 20 to 40 dB at 1 MHz, where a switching converter's ripple lies. The LDO's own output noise, mainly the reference's noise multiplied by the feedback ratio, matters for radios, converters, and clocks: a data converter's reference or a PLL's supply often needs a few microvolts rms, and dedicated low-noise LDOs filter their references to achieve it.
EQ: Vripple,out = Vripple,in × 10^(−PSRR/20)
H2: Worked Example 2.3
P: Problem. A switching converter delivers 3.3 V with 100 mV of ripple to an LDO. Find the output ripple if the LDO's PSRR is 60 dB at the ripple frequency, and if it is 30 dB.
P: Solution. At 60 dB, the ripple is divided by 1000, giving 0.1 mV. At 30 dB, it is divided by 31.6, giving 3.16 mV. The same LDO can be excellent at a 100 kHz ripple and nearly useless at 2 MHz. A ferrite bead and capacitor ahead of the LDO, or a switcher running at a frequency where the LDO's rejection is high, solve the problem.
P: Key idea. An LDO trades efficiency for quietness. Its efficiency is Vout/Vin, fixed by the voltages; its value is in rejection and low noise, which a switching converter cannot provide alone.
H2: 2.4 Stability
P: The LDO's feedback loop has a pole from its error amplifier and a pole at its output, set by the load and the output capacitor, which moves with the load current. Older LDOs with PNP pass devices needed an output capacitor with a specific equivalent series resistance, because the zero it creates gave the phase margin; a low-ESR ceramic capacitor could make them oscillate. Most modern LDOs are internally compensated to be stable with ceramic capacitors, and their datasheets give the allowed range of capacitance and ESR. Using a larger capacitor than the datasheet allows, or placing a long trace between the LDO and its capacitor, can still erode the margin, and Book 2's loop analysis applies directly.
H2: 2.5 Thermal design
P: A linear regulator's loss is easy to compute and hard to get rid of: (Vin − Vout) × Iout, plus a small ground-current term, all dissipated in a few square millimeters. The junction temperature is the ambient plus the power times the thermal resistance from junction to ambient, θJA, which depends far more on the board than on the package. A SOT-223 regulator on a minimal footprint might show 100 °C/W; on a square inch of 2-ounce copper with thermal vias, about 50 to 60 °C/W. The maximum junction temperature is usually 125 °C, and good practice keeps it below about 105 °C for life. A regulator that needs more dissipation than that needs a different package, a heatsink, a pre-regulator to reduce Vin, or a switching converter.
EQ: TJ = TA + (Vin − Vout) Iout θJA

H2: 2.6 The LDO after the switcher
P: The most common modern use of a linear regulator is to clean up the output of a switching converter for a noise-sensitive load: a clock synthesizer, an ADC reference, an RF front end. The quantity that matters is the power-supply rejection at the switching frequency, not at 120 Hz. A good conventional LDO rejects 60 to 70 dB at 1 kHz and only 20 to 40 dB at 1 MHz, where its loop gain has run out. Ultra-low-noise parts such as the Analog Devices LT3045 and the TI TPS7A94 use a current-reference architecture to keep 70 dB or more out to a megahertz, with output noise below 1 µV RMS from 10 Hz to 100 kHz. The rejection also falls as the dropout shrinks, so the headroom between the switcher's output and the LDO's must be chosen from the PSRR curve at the actual headroom, typically 300 to 500 mV, not from the headline number.
H2: Worked Example 2.4
P: Problem. A 5 V rail feeds a 3.3 V LDO in a SOT-223 that supplies 500 mA. The board gives θJA = 60 °C/W, and the ambient is 55 °C. Find the dissipation and the junction temperature.
P: Solution. P = (5 − 3.3) × 0.5 = 0.85 W, and TJ = 55 + 0.85 × 60 = 106 °C, right at the limit for long life. Feeding the LDO from a 3.8 V switcher output instead of 5 V cuts the loss to 0.25 W and the junction to 70 °C.
H2: Worked Example 2.5
P: Problem. A buck converter's 3.6 V output has 20 mV peak-to-peak ripple at 1 MHz. It feeds an LDO with 70 dB of rejection at 1 MHz at the chosen headroom. Find the ripple at the LDO's output, and repeat for a conventional LDO with 30 dB.
P: Solution. 70 dB is a factor of 3162, so the ripple falls to 20 mV/3162 = 6.3 µV peak to peak, below the LDO's own noise. With 30 dB, a factor of 31.6, the ripple is 0.63 mV, enough to show up as spurs at ±1 MHz on a synthesizer's output. A small LC filter before the LDO, with its resonance well below 1 MHz, would add the missing rejection.
H2: 2.7 The shunt regulator and the TL431
P: Not every regulator stands in series with the load. A shunt regulator sits across it and draws whatever current is needed to hold the voltage, with a series resistor dropping the rest; a Zener diode is the simplest example. The TL431, introduced by Texas Instruments in the 1970s and still made by the hundreds of millions, is an adjustable shunt regulator with a 2.495 V internal reference and an amplifier: it conducts as much current as needed to hold its reference pin at 2.495 V, so a divider sets the output to 2.495(1 + R1/R2). Its most common job today is not regulation but feedback: on the secondary of almost every offline flyback, it drives the LED of an optocoupler, which carries the error signal across the isolation barrier to the primary-side controller.
EQ: Vout = 2.495 V × (1 + R1/R2)
H2: Worked Example 2.6
P: Problem. A TL431 must hold the feedback point of a 12 V flyback output. With R2 = 10 kΩ, find R1, and the divider current.
P: Solution. R1 = R2(12/2.495 − 1) = 10 kΩ × 3.81 = 38.1 kΩ, and the divider draws 2.495 V/10 kΩ = 0.25 mA. The TL431 itself needs at least 1 mA of cathode current to regulate, which the optocoupler's LED path and a bias resistor provide, a detail that sets part of the converter's no-load power.
P: The linear regulator is quiet and simple, and its waste is set by the voltage it drops. Chapter 3 removes that waste by never letting the transistor sit between fully on and fully off.
BENCH: Before you choose an LDO, compute (Vin − Vout) × Iout and multiply it by the θJA of your own board, not the data sheet's test board. Check the output capacitor against the stable range in the data sheet, and remember that a small ceramic capacitor can lose half its capacitance at its rated DC voltage. For an analog front end, such as an ECG amplifier, read the PSRR at the switcher's frequency, not at 120 Hz.
