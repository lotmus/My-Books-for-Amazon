H1: Chapter 16. Grid Codes and Inverter-Based Resources
P: When a 1 GW power plant trips off a 50 GW grid, what holds the frequency up for the first seconds, before any controller acts? The spinning mass of every synchronous generator, which gives up stored energy at once. With an inertia constant of 4 s the frequency starts falling at 0.15 Hz/s; replace half of that spinning plant with inverters and it falls twice as fast.
P: Where you meet this: Every grid-code requirement on a solar, wind, or battery plant, every ride-through curve in IEEE 1547 or IEEE 2800, every news report of a frequency event, and every grid-forming inverter announcement.
P: Chapter 7 introduced the grid's frequencies and the rule that a generator connected to it must follow the grid's codes. Solar plants, wind farms, and battery systems connect through inverters like those of Chapter 13, and as their share grows they must do what spinning generators used to do automatically: support the frequency, hold up the voltage, and stay connected through disturbances. This chapter explains the physics behind the requirements and computes the main quantities: droop response, rate of change of frequency, and reactive support. In the United States the distribution-level rules are IEEE 1547-2018, and transmission-connected plants follow IEEE 2800-2022 and their operator's codes; Europe's network codes play the same role.
H2: 16.1 Frequency, inertia, and the first seconds
P: The grid's frequency is set by the balance of generation and load. When a large generator trips, the shortfall is first supplied by the kinetic energy of every synchronous machine's rotor, which slows them all down together. The inertia constant H, the stored kinetic energy divided by the machine rating, typically 2 to 9 seconds, sets how fast the frequency falls: the initial rate of change of frequency is ΔP f0 / (2 H S) for a power deficit ΔP in a system of rating S. Inverter-based resources have no rotor, so a grid dominated by them has less inertia and faster frequency swings unless the inverters are programmed to respond.
EQ: RoCoF = (ΔP / S) × f0 / (2H)
H2: Worked Example 16.1
P: Problem. A 1 GW plant trips in a 50 GW system at 60 Hz with an aggregate inertia constant of 4 s. Find the initial rate of change of frequency. What if displacing synchronous plant halves the inertia?
P: Solution. RoCoF = (1/50) × 60 / (2 × 4) = 0.15 Hz/s. With H = 2 s, it doubles to 0.30 Hz/s. Protection relays that trip on a high rate of change, and the time available for primary response before the frequency reaches its load-shedding threshold, are both set against this number.
H2: 16.2 Droop response
P: Within seconds, generators' governors raise output in proportion to the frequency drop, a behavior called droop: a 5% droop means the output would change by 100% of rating for a 5% change in frequency. Grid codes require inverter-based plants to provide the same frequency-watt response, raising output on underfrequency if they hold headroom, and reducing it on overfrequency, which every plant can do. A battery can respond within a fraction of a second, far faster than a steam turbine, which makes it valuable for fast frequency response.
EQ: ΔP/Prated = −(Δf/f0) / droop
H2: Worked Example 16.2
P: Problem. A 100 MW battery plant has 5% droop. The frequency falls by 0.3 Hz from 60 Hz. What extra power does it deliver?
P: Solution. Δf/f0 = 0.3/60 = 0.005; divided by 0.05, the response is 10% of rating, 10 MW. If the plant's droop were set to 2%, the same event would call for 25 MW.
P: Key idea. Grid codes ask inverters to replace what synchronous machines did by physics: respond to frequency with droop, support voltage with reactive power, and stay connected through faults. Their control software, not their hardware, now holds up much of the grid.
H2: 16.3 Voltage and reactive power
P: Voltage on a transmission line is held up by reactive power, as Chapter 7's power-factor discussion implied. An inverter can supply or absorb reactive power within its current rating: its apparent power S must cover both, S² = P² + Q². Grid codes require a plant to operate across a range of power factors, commonly 0.95 leading to lagging at full output, and to follow volt-var curves that raise reactive output as voltage falls. Sizing the inverter for reactive capability therefore costs real kVA.
EQ: S = √(P² + Q²),   at PF 0.95: Q = P tan(arccos 0.95) = 0.329 P
H2: Worked Example 16.3
P: Problem. A solar plant must deliver 50 MW at a power factor of 0.95. What inverter rating does it need, and how much reactive power does that provide?
P: Solution. S = 50/0.95 = 52.6 MVA, and Q = √(52.6² − 50²) = 16.4 Mvar. The plant must install about 5% more inverter capacity than its real-power rating to meet the requirement.

H2: 16.4 Ride-through and grid-forming control
P: Most inverter-based resources have traditionally used grid-following controls: they measure grid voltage with a phase-locked loop and inject synchronized current. Grid-forming controls regulate an internal voltage phasor and can support voltage and frequency under specified conditions. Some designs add fast frequency response or virtual-inertia behavior; grid-forming operation does not automatically provide synchronous inertia, and it does not by itself guarantee black-start capability. Those capabilities depend on the control design, available energy, protection, and system conditions.
H2: 16.5 Weak grids and the short-circuit ratio
P: An inverter that follows the grid measures the grid's voltage angle with a phase-locked loop and injects current in step with it. That works when the grid is stiff, when the voltage at the connection point barely moves as the inverter's current changes. The measure of stiffness is the short-circuit ratio, SCR: the fault level at the point of connection, in MVA, divided by the plant's rating in MW. Above about 3 the grid is strong; below 3 it is weak, and below about 1.5 grid-following control becomes unstable, because the inverter's own current moves the voltage its PLL is trying to follow. Remote wind and solar sites at the end of long lines often see SCRs near 2, and their grid codes require studies, synchronous condensers, or grid-forming inverters, Section 16.4, which set their own voltage instead of following it.
EQ: SCR = S_sc / P_plant
H2: 16.6 How much energy fast frequency response needs
P: Batteries have become the fastest responders to frequency events, delivering full power within a few hundred milliseconds. The energy they need is modest relative to their power: a frequency-containment service holds its power until slower reserves take over, typically for 15 to 30 minutes in the European rules, so a battery providing 100 MW of response needs roughly 25 to 50 MWh, plus margin for its state of charge to drift. Services that only cover the first seconds, the synthetic or virtual inertia of grid-forming inverters, need far less energy and far more speed, and can be provided by inverters with modest storage on their DC links.
H2: Worked Example 16.4
P: Problem. A 200 MW solar plant connects at a point whose fault level is 500 MVA. Find the SCR, and the fault level needed for an SCR of 3.
P: Solution. SCR = 500/200 = 2.5, a weak connection. An SCR of 3 needs 600 MVA, so a 100 MVA synchronous condenser, which adds roughly its rating divided by its subtransient reactance, perhaps 500 to 700 MVA of fault level, would more than cover it; alternatively part of the plant could be built grid-forming.
H2: Worked Example 16.5
P: Problem. A battery provides 100 MW of frequency-containment reserve that must be sustained for 15 minutes, and it must be able to do so in both directions from any operating point. Find the minimum energy capacity.
P: Solution. 100 MW × 0.25 h = 25 MWh in each direction, so the battery must hold at least 25 MWh of headroom above and 25 MWh of energy below its operating point, 50 MWh in total, with the state of charge managed to stay near the middle.
H2: 16.7 Current harmonics from inverters
P: Inverter-based plants must also inject clean current. IEEE 1547 for distributed resources in North America, IEEE 2800 for transmission-connected plants, and the European network codes set limits on harmonic current, expressed as a total demand distortion, TDD, the RMS of all harmonics relative to the rated current, typically 5 %, with tighter limits on individual harmonics. A PWM inverter's harmonics cluster around multiples of its switching frequency. An ideal LCL filter, two inductors and a capacitor, has asymptotic attenuation of about 60 dB per decade well above resonance; actual attenuation depends on damping, component parasitics, and grid impedance. The resonance must be damped, passively with a resistor or actively by the control loop, or it can amplify current near resonance.
EQ: f_res = (1/2π) √[(L1 + L2) / (L1 L2 Cf)]
H2: Worked Example 16.6
P: Problem. An inverter's LCL filter has L1 = 1 mH, L2 = 0.3 mH, and Cf = 20 µF. Find its resonant frequency, and check that it lies between ten times the 50 Hz line and half the 10 kHz switching frequency.
P: Solution. (L1 + L2)/(L1L2Cf) = 1.3 × 10⁻³/(10⁻³ × 0.3 × 10⁻³ × 20 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.17 × 10⁸, whose square root is 14 720 rad/s, or 2.34 kHz. This passes the stated 500 Hz to 5 kHz rule-of-thumb range, but that check alone does not establish separation from the implemented current-loop bandwidth or stability with control delay and grid-impedance variation. Those depend on the controller and damping method and must be checked in the design. The ideal high-frequency LCL model gives about 38 dB attenuation at 10 kHz; real attenuation depends on damping, parasitics, and grid impedance.
P: Grid codes govern how inverter-based plants behave. Chapter 17 covers the equipment that connects every building to that grid and protects it when something fails.
BENCH: When commissioning an inverter-based plant, check its frequency-watt and volt-var settings line by line against the grid code that applies at the site; the factory defaults are often for another region. Find the short-circuit ratio at the point of connection before tuning the controls: near 2, a weak grid, the current controller's gains need studies, not defaults.
