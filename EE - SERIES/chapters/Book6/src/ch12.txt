H1: Chapter 12. Batteries, Chargers, and Battery Management
P: It is widely believed that a lithium-ion battery charges at a steady pace, so the last 20 % should take a quarter as long as the first 80 %. It does not. At 0.5C, constant current puts in 80 % in about 1.6 h, but the constant-voltage phase that follows tapers the current away and can take most of another hour for the rest.
P: Where you meet this: Every laptop, phone, and power tool, every LiPo pack in a drone or radio-control car and its balance charger, every robot's battery, every battery-management IC, and every airline's 100 Wh rule.
P: Every portable device, electric vehicle, and data-center backup system has a battery and a converter that charges it. The battery is an electrochemical source with a voltage that changes with its state of charge, a resistance that heats it, and limits that, if exceeded, damage it or set it on fire. This chapter covers the lithium-ion cell as a circuit element, the series-parallel pack, the charger's constant-current and constant-voltage profile, and the battery-management system that keeps the pack safe. The chemistry itself is outside this series; the electrical behavior is this book's.
H2: 12.1 The cell as a circuit element
P: A lithium-ion cell with a graphite anode and a nickel-rich or cobalt-oxide cathode has a nominal voltage of about 3.6 to 3.7 V, a full-charge voltage of 4.2 V, and a discharge cutoff near 3.0 V; lithium iron phosphate cells sit lower, about 3.2 V nominal and 3.65 V full. Capacity is quoted in ampere-hours, and energy is capacity times nominal voltage. The C-rate expresses current relative to capacity: 1C discharges the cell in about an hour. The internal resistance, tens of milliohms for a cylindrical cell and well under one milliohm for a large prismatic cell, causes a voltage drop under load and I²R heating.

H2: 12.2 The pack
P: Cells are connected in series to raise the voltage and in parallel to raise the capacity, written as, for example, 3S2P: three groups in series, each of two cells in parallel. The pack's voltage window is the cell's window times the number in series, and its energy is the total cell count times each cell's energy. Every series group must be monitored, because the weakest group limits the whole pack and a group that overcharges is the one that fails.
H2: Worked Example 12.1
P: Problem. A laptop pack is 3S2P of 3.5 Ah cells at 3.6 V nominal with 50 mΩ internal resistance. Find its nominal voltage, voltage window, capacity, energy, and the heat in the cells at a 7 A discharge.
P: Solution. Nominal voltage 3 × 3.6 = 10.8 V; window 9.0 to 12.6 V; capacity 2 × 3.5 = 7 Ah; energy 10.8 × 7 = 75.6 Wh, under the 100 Wh limit airlines allow in the cabin without approval. At 7 A each cell carries 3.5 A, so the six cells dissipate 6 × 3.5² × 0.05 = 3.68 W, about 5% of the 75.6 W being drawn.
H2: 12.3 The charger
P: Lithium-ion cells are charged in two phases. In constant current, typically 0.5C to 1C, the voltage rises until it reaches the full-charge limit, usually 4.2 V per cell, within about 50 mV. The charger then holds that voltage, constant voltage, while the current tapers; it terminates when the current falls to a small fraction of C, often C/20. The constant-current phase delivers most of the charge, about 70% to 80%, so fast charging mainly shortens that phase, at the cost of heat and of cell aging. The charger itself is a buck or buck-boost converter of the kind in Chapters 3 and 4, with current and voltage loops, and its efficiency is counted by Chapter 1's waste formula.

H2: Worked Example 12.2
P: Problem. The pack of Example 12.1 is charged at 0.5C by a 65 W adapter with 92% efficiency. Find the charge current, the approximate duration of the constant-current phase if it delivers 80% of the charge, and the adapter's waste at full power.
P: Solution. 0.5C is 3.5 A. The constant-current phase delivers 0.8 × 7 Ah at 3.5 A, which takes 1.6 h, and the constant-voltage taper adds a further fraction of an hour. The adapter's waste is 65 × (1/0.92 − 1) = 5.65 W.
P: Key idea. A lithium-ion cell is a voltage source with a narrow allowed window and a resistance that heats it. The charger holds it inside the window with a constant-current then constant-voltage profile, and the battery-management system watches every series group.
H2: 12.4 The battery-management system
P: The battery-management system, BMS, measures every series group’s voltage, pack current, and temperature, estimates state of charge and health, and disconnects the pack on overvoltage, undervoltage, overcurrent, or overtemperature. Cells age at different rates, so groups drift apart; balancing corrects the mismatch. If one group reaches its voltage limit first, it can end discharge while energy remains in the other groups, or be pushed beyond its limit during charge. Passive balancing bleeds the fullest groups through resistors near the end of charge; active balancing moves charge between groups with small converters. Large packs, such as an electric vehicle’s 96S pack, use isolated monitoring chips daisy-chained along the stack.
H2: Worked Example 12.3
P: Problem. A passive balancer bleeds 100 mA from a group at 4.2 V. What resistor does it use, and how much power does it dissipate?
P: Solution. R = 4.2/0.1 = 42 Ω, and P = 4.2 × 0.1 = 0.42 W. Over an hour, it removes 0.1 Ah, enough to correct a 3% imbalance in a 3.5 Ah group. Larger imbalances in big packs make active balancing worth its cost.
H2: 12.5 State of charge: counting and correcting
P: A battery-management system must tell the user how much charge remains, and no sensor measures it directly. Coulomb counting integrates the current, which is accurate over hours but accumulates every offset in the current sensor: a 5 mA error is a full ampere-hour in eight days. The open-circuit voltage gives an absolute estimate, because a rested lithium-ion cell's voltage maps to its state of charge, but the cell must rest for tens of minutes, and the map is nearly flat for lithium iron phosphate between 20 and 80 %. Practical gauges, the TI Impedance Track and the Kalman filters in electric-vehicle BMSs, combine the two: they count charge continuously, correct with voltage whenever the cell rests or a model predicts the loaded voltage well, and learn the cell's capacity and resistance as it ages.
H2: 12.6 Cell balancing
P: The cells of a series string never stay identical. Their self-discharge, capacity, and temperature differ, and after many cycles one group may reach the charge or discharge limit first, setting the usable window for the entire pack. If the BMS misses that drift, the weakest cell can trigger a protective shutdown early; continued charging beyond its limit can damage the cell. Passive balancing bleeds the high cells through resistors near the top of charge, wasting a little energy; active balancing moves charge between cells at higher cost. Either way, the BMS needs each cell’s voltage within a few millivolts, as provided by multicell monitor chips such as the ADI ADBMS6830 or TI BQ79616.
H2: Worked Example 12.4
P: Problem. A gauge counts charge with a 5 mA offset on a 3000 mAh phone battery. How much state-of-charge error accumulates per day, and how often must it be corrected?
P: Solution. 5 mA × 24 h = 120 mAh per day, 4 % of the capacity. If the user's tolerance is 5 %, the gauge must correct within about a day, which a rest of half an hour overnight allows. An electric-vehicle pack whose LFP cells give a flat voltage curve must instead correct near the top of charge, where the curve steepens, which is why some cars ask for an occasional full charge.
H2: Worked Example 12.5
P: Problem. One cell of a 100 Ah pack holds 2 % more charge than the rest at the top of charge. How long does a 100 mA passive balancer take to remove the difference?
P: Solution. 2 % of 100 Ah is 2 Ah, and 2 Ah/0.1 A = 20 hours of balancing. If balancing runs only during the last hour of each charge, the pack needs about 20 charges to equalize, which is fine for slow drift but not for a newly replaced cell; that case calls for active balancing or a manual pre-charge.
H2: 12.7 How fast can a battery charge?
P: Charging speed is limited inside the cell, not by the charger. Lithium ions must diffuse into the graphite anode, and if they arrive faster than they can intercalate, especially in the cold, they plate as metallic lithium on the anode's surface, permanently losing capacity and, in the worst case, growing dendrites that short the cell. Charge rates are therefore expressed as C-rates, multiples of the current that would charge the cell in one hour, and fast-charge profiles taper the current as the state of charge rises and the anode's potential nears the plating threshold. Electric cars at 350 kW chargers reach 3 to 5 C briefly at low charge, preheat the pack before arrival, and spend the most time between 60 and 80 %, which is why charging times are quoted from 10 to 80 %.
H2: Worked Example 12.6
P: Problem. A 75 kWh pack accepts a peak of 350 kW and averages 200 kW from 10 to 80 %. Find the peak C-rate and the charging time over that window.
P: Solution. 350/75 = 4.7 C at the peak. The window is 70 % of 75 kWh, 52.5 kWh, so at 200 kW average the charge takes 52.5/200 h = 15.8 minutes. Charging from 80 to 100 % at the tapered rates that follow would take about as long again for a quarter of the energy.
H2: Worked Example 12.7
P: Problem. An e-bike's 10S4P pack uses 3.5 Ah cells at 3.6 V nominal. Its motor is rated 250 W, the European limit for assisted bicycles, and the motor and controller together are 80 % efficient. The bike computer logs an average of 9 Wh/km from the pack on a hilly commute. Find the pack's voltage, capacity, and energy, the range on 80 % of that energy, and the pack current, cell current, and range at a steady 250 W of motor output at 25 km/h.
P: Solution. The pack is 10 × 3.6 = 36 V and 4 × 3.5 = 14 Ah, so 504 Wh. On 80 % of it, 403 Wh, the commute gives 403/9 = 44.8 km. At 250 W out the pack supplies 250/0.8 = 312.5 W, or 8.68 A, 2.17 A per cell, 0.62C. At 25 km/h that is 12.5 Wh/km, and the range falls to 403/12.5 = 32.3 km. If the rider supplies an extra 100 W mechanically at the same speed, the motor needs 150 W; at 80 % efficiency the pack then supplies 187.5 W, or 7.5 Wh/km. The rider's contribution therefore saves 5 Wh/km from the pack, extending the 403 Wh usable range to about 53.7 km.
P: The battery stores energy as direct current. Chapter 13 turns direct current back into the alternating current that motors and the grid need.
BENCH: On the bench, put a fuse in series with any pack and a thermocouple on its cells. Watch each series cell's voltage, not only the pack's: the cell that is out of balance is the one that gets overcharged, which is why hobby LiPo chargers use the separate balance lead. A cell whose voltage sags more than its neighbors under the same load has higher resistance and is aging faster. The pack has no way to identify the cell that the spreadsheet averaged away.
