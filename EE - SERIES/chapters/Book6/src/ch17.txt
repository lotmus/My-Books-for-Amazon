H1: Chapter 17. Distribution Transformers, Fault Current, and Protection
P: In this chapter's example, a 2500 kVA transformer with 5 kW core loss and 25 kW rated-load copper loss reaches peak modeled efficiency at about 45 % load. That load fraction does not by itself establish the insulation-aging rate: the simplified aging calculation later in the chapter uses winding hot-spot temperature, while actual paper deterioration also depends on moisture and oxygen. Load, ambient temperature, cooling, harmonics, and operating history all affect the thermal conditions that the calculation needs. This chapter shows how to calculate the loss balance and estimate thermal aging from hot-spot temperature.
P: Where you meet this: Every transformer nameplate, every breaker and fuse in a panel, every arc-flash label, every GFCI outlet or RCD, and every utility's transformer loading guide.
P: Between the medium-voltage feeder and the low-voltage switchboard of every building and data hall sits a distribution transformer. It sets two numbers that every downstream engineer needs: how efficiently power is delivered at partial load, and how much current flows when something short-circuits. That second number decides the rating of every breaker, busbar, and fuse, and the hazard to anyone working on live equipment. This chapter works with the per-unit impedance, the transformer's loss model, and the basics of overcurrent protection. Shelf A's line-frequency magnetics, from Chapter 6, are the hardware.
H2: 17.1 Per-unit impedance and fault current
P: A transformer's impedance is quoted as a percentage, or per-unit value: the fraction of rated voltage needed to drive rated current through a shorted secondary. A typical distribution transformer has 5% to 6%. That number gives the available fault current directly: with a bolted short on the secondary and a stiff utility supply, the current is the rated current divided by the per-unit impedance. A 5.75% transformer therefore delivers about 17 times its rated current into a fault.
EQ: Irated = S / (√3 VLL),   Ifault ≈ Irated / Zpu
H2: Worked Example 17.1
P: Problem. A 2500 kVA transformer with 5.75% impedance feeds a 480 V switchboard. Find its rated current and the available fault current.
P: Solution. Irated = 2.5 × 10⁶ / (√3 × 480) = 3007 A. Ifault = 3007/0.0575 = 52.3 kA. Every breaker and busbar on that switchboard must be rated to interrupt or withstand at least that current, and the real value is slightly lower once the utility's own impedance is included, which is the conservative direction to ignore.
H2: 17.2 Losses and efficiency at partial load
P: A transformer has two kinds of loss. No-load, or core, loss is the hysteresis and eddy-current loss of Chapter 6, present whenever the transformer is energized and nearly independent of load. Load, or copper, loss is I²R in the windings and scales with the square of the load fraction. Efficiency therefore peaks at the load fraction where copper loss equals core loss, x = √(Pcore/Pcu,rated). Regulations, such as the US Department of Energy's efficiency standards, specify efficiency at a reference load, usually 50%, because that is closer to how distribution transformers actually operate.
EQ: Ploss(x) = Pcore + x² Pcu,rated,   η max at x = √(Pcore / Pcu,rated)

H2: Worked Example 17.2
P: Problem. The transformer of Example 17.1 has 5 kW of core loss and 25 kW of copper loss at rated load. Find its efficiency at 50% load and unity power factor, and the load at which efficiency peaks.
P: Solution. At 50% load the output is 1250 kW and the losses are 5 + 0.25 × 25 = 11.25 kW, so η = 1250/1261.25 = 99.11%. Efficiency peaks where 25x² = 5, at x = 0.447, about 45% load. A data hall that runs its transformers lightly loaded for redundancy should choose low-core-loss designs, such as amorphous-metal cores.
P: Key idea. A transformer's per-unit impedance sets the fault current every downstream device must survive, and its core and copper losses set efficiency, which peaks where the two are equal.
H2: 17.3 Overcurrent protection
P: Fuses and circuit breakers clear faults. Each has a time-current characteristic: it carries rated current indefinitely, trips on moderate overloads after seconds to minutes, and clears high fault currents within a cycle or less. Coordination, or selectivity, means arranging the characteristics so that the device nearest the fault opens first and the upstream ones stay closed, keeping the rest of the building powered. Current-limiting fuses clear so quickly that the fault current never reaches its prospective peak, reducing the let-through energy I²t that downstream equipment must withstand.
H2: 17.4 Arc flash
P: A fault that arcs through air rather than a bolted short releases its energy as heat, light, and pressure. The incident energy at a worker's position grows with the fault current and, almost linearly, with the clearing time, which is why reducing clearing time is the main mitigation. Standards such as IEEE 1584 give the calculation, and NFPA 70E in the United States requires labeling and protective equipment based on it. Maintenance switches that temporarily speed up the upstream breaker, and remote racking of breakers, keep workers out of the hazard. Higher-voltage DC distribution in data centers, Chapter 14's 800 V racks, brings new arc-flash and interruption problems because DC has no current zero to help extinguish an arc.
H2: Worked Example 17.3
P: Problem. A 1000 kVA, 208 V transformer has 5% impedance. Find the available fault current and compare it with the 2500 kVA, 480 V unit.
P: Solution. Irated = 10⁶/(√3 × 208) = 2776 A, so Ifault = 2776/0.05 = 55.5 kA. A smaller transformer at a lower voltage can deliver more fault current than a larger one at a higher voltage, because the current, not the power, is what the protection must handle.
H2: 17.5 Thermal aging of transformers
P: Transformer insulation aging depends on temperature and time, as well as moisture and oxygen. The IEEE C57.91-2011 thermal-aging model for mineral-oil-immersed transformers uses winding hottest-spot temperature and a 110 °C reference for the standard 65 °C-rise insulation system. IEEE has superseded that guide with C57.91-2025; this chapter uses the 2011 Arrhenius equation only as a teaching model, not as current loading guidance. It gives relative factors of 0.28 at 98 °C, 2.7 at 120 °C, and 7.0 at 130 °C; these do not estimate remaining transformer life. Load fraction alone is not a life reserve: overload capability depends on ambient temperature, cooling, load history, insulation condition, and equipment limits. Use the current guide and transformer-specific data for a loading decision. Data-center transformers with substantial harmonic heating may need K-factor ratings.
EQ: F_AA = exp[15 000/383 − 15 000/(θH + 273)]

H2: 17.6 Selectivity
P: When a fault occurs, only the protective device nearest to it should open, so that the rest of the installation keeps running. That requires the time-current characteristics of devices in series to be coordinated, the downstream one always clearing faster. For fuses of the same type, a ratio of rated currents of about 1.6 to 1 between upstream and downstream gives selectivity at all fault levels; for circuit breakers, manufacturers publish selectivity tables, and electronic trip units with short-time delays or zone-selective interlocking, a signal from the downstream breaker telling the upstream one to wait, achieve it at high fault currents. Selectivity and arc-flash protection pull in opposite directions, since delays that preserve selectivity also lengthen the arc, and the maintenance switch that temporarily removes the delay while people work on the gear is the common compromise.
H2: Worked Example 17.4
P: Problem. Find the aging acceleration factor at hot-spot temperatures of 98, 120, and 130 °C. How many hours at 130 °C produce the same modeled thermal aging as 24 hours at 110 °C?
P: Solution. F_AA = exp(39.16 − 15 000/(θ + 273)): at 98 °C, 0.28; at 120 °C, 2.7; at 130 °C, 7.0. Under this model, 24 hours at the 110 °C reference represents 24 equivalent hours at the reference aging rate; at 130 °C, 24/7.0 = 3.4 hours produces the same modeled thermal aging. Ten hours at 98 °C corresponds to 0.28 × 10 = 2.8 equivalent reference-temperature aging hours. These are relative model comparisons, not estimates of remaining transformer life.
H2: Worked Example 17.5
P: Problem. A 63 A gG fuse protects a branch circuit. What is the smallest gG fuse upstream that is selective with it, and why does a 100 A upstream breaker not automatically achieve the same?
P: Solution. 1.6 × 63 A = 101 A, so the next standard size, 125 A, is safe, and 100 A is borderline. A breaker's magnetic trip opens instantaneously above its threshold, so for high fault currents it may open together with the fuse; only the manufacturer's selectivity table tells whether a given pair is selective up to the available fault current.
H2: 17.7 Residual-current protection and grounding
P: Overcurrent devices primarily protect conductors and equipment; residual-current devices provide additional protection against some electric-shock hazards. An RCD, called a GFCI in North America, passes the line and neutral conductors through a toroidal current transformer and trips when the imbalance reaches its operating threshold, which means current is returning by some other path, possibly through a person. IEC-based installations often use 30 mA RCDs for additional protection; North American Class A GFCIs must trip at or before 6 mA and must not trip below 4 mA. These devices reduce risk, but do not make contact with live parts safe or guarantee prevention of injury. Shock outcome also depends on current path and duration; protective earthing or bonding and correct device selection remain essential. How well the protection works depends on the grounding system, which IEC standards call the earthing system. In a TT system, where the installation has its own earth electrode, the electrode's resistance must be low enough that a fault drives at least the RCD's trip current while keeping the touch voltage below 50 V. Power converters complicate RCD selection because their EMI filters leak current to earth continuously, and some inverter faults add DC residual current that can impair RCD types not designed to detect it. Use the protection arrangement specified for the inverter or charger and required by local code; depending on the equipment, this may be a Type B RCD or another DC-sensitive solution.
EQ: RA × IΔn ≤ 50 V
H2: Worked Example 17.6
P: Problem. A TT installation uses a 30 mA RCD. Find the highest earth-electrode resistance that satisfies the touch-voltage rule, and the leakage budget if each of ten server supplies leaks 1.5 mA to earth.
P: Solution. The simplified 50 V touch-voltage criterion gives RA ≤ 50 V/0.03 A = 1667 Ω. This is an arithmetic ceiling, not an installation acceptance target: the applicable code may require a much lower measured electrode resistance, and the required disconnection time must also be met. In the UK context, IET guidance says the electrode resistance should be as low as practicable and values above 200 Ω may not be stable; soil and seasonal conditions matter. The stated leakage budget is less than 30 mA/3 = 10 mA. The ten supplies total 15 mA, so they exceed it by 5 mA. Although a compliant RCD must not operate at half its rated residual current, this leaves no margin for other loads or changing leakage; divide the loads across appropriately protected circuits and verify the installation under local rules. Do not raise the RCD rating where 30 mA additional protection is required.

BENCH: Before opening a panel, read its arc-flash label and wear what it calls for; then verify that the circuit is dead with a tester that you have checked on a known live source a moment before. When two protective devices trip together, compare their time-current curves at the actual fault current, not at their ratings. When an RCD trips at random, measure the leakage of the loads it feeds before replacing it.
