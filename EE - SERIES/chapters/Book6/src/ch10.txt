H1: Chapter 10. Compensating the Loop
P: How fast should a power supply's control loop be? Too slow, and a 10 A load step pulls the output down by tens of millivolts; too fast, and the loop amplifies switching noise and runs out of phase margin. The 1 V, 20 A buck of Chapter 9, closed at 50 kHz with 78.7° of margin, dips about 32 mV for that step, and this chapter shows where each of those numbers comes from.
P: Where you meet this: Every COMP pin on a controller, every Type II and Type III network in an application note, every Bode-plot measurement with an injection transformer, and every digital power controller's loop settings.
P: Chapter 4 explained why a converter needs feedback and why the crossover frequency sets how fast it recovers from a load step. This chapter designs the compensator that sets that crossover. It follows the approach of Basso's Designing Control Loops for Linear and Switching Power Supplies: model the power stage's control-to-output response, choose a crossover and a phase margin, and place the compensator's poles and zeros to reach them. Feedback theory itself is Book 2's.
H2: 10.1 The power stage seen by the loop
P: In voltage-mode control the duty cycle drives the output through the LC filter, which contributes a double pole at f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)) and a phase drop of 180° above it; the output capacitor's equivalent series resistance adds a zero. In peak current-mode control an inner loop sets the inductor current directly, so the inductor drops out of the outer loop and the plant becomes a single pole formed by the load resistance and the output capacitance, plus the ESR zero, with a gain of R divided by the current-sense gain Ri. Current mode is simpler to compensate and limits current cycle by cycle, which is why most modern converters use it.
EQ: voltage mode: f0 = 1/(2π√(LC));   current mode: Gvc(s) ≈ (R/Ri)/(1 + s/ωp),  fp = 1/(2πRC)
H2: 10.2 Choosing the crossover and the margin
P: The crossover frequency fc is where the loop gain falls to 1. A higher fc gives a faster response, but the loop must cross well below the switching frequency, typically at a tenth or a fifth of it, because the converter is a sampled system, and below any right-half-plane zero, as Chapter 4 showed for the boost. The phase margin, how far the loop's phase is from −180° at fc, sets how much the response rings: 45° is the usual minimum, and 60° to 75° gives a well-damped step.
H2: 10.3 Type II and Type III compensators
P: A Type II compensator, built around an error amplifier with one capacitor-resistor network, provides an integrator for zero steady-state error, one zero, and one high-frequency pole. Placing the zero at or below the plant's pole cancels its phase drop, and placing the pole near the ESR zero or at half the switching frequency attenuates switching noise. It provides up to 90° of phase boost and suits current-mode control. A Type III compensator adds a second zero and pole, providing the extra phase boost needed to cross over above the LC double pole in voltage mode.
EQ: Type II: C(s) = (ωI/s)(1 + s/ωz)/(1 + s/ωp2);   with ωz = ωp, PM ≈ 90° − arctan(fc/fp2)
H2: Worked Example 10.1
P: Problem. The 1 V, 20 A buck of Chapter 9 uses peak current mode with a current-sense gain of 0.01 V/A, 1 mF of output capacitance, and a divider ratio of 0.6 to a 0.6 V reference. Its load is 0.05 Ω. Design a Type II compensator for a 50 kHz crossover, with its zero canceling the plant pole and its high-frequency pole at 250 kHz. Find the plant pole, the compensator gain needed at crossover, the phase margin, and the output dip for a 10 A load step.
P: Solution. The plant pole is at 1/(2π × 0.05 × 10⁻³) = 3.18 kHz. The DC gain R/Ri is 5, so at 50 kHz the plant gives 5/√(1 + (50/3.18)²) = 0.318, or −10.0 dB. With the 0.6 divider, the compensator must supply 1/(0.318 × 0.6) = 5.25, or 14.4 dB, at 50 kHz. With the zero canceling the plant pole, the loop is an integrator times the 250 kHz pole, so the phase margin is 90° − arctan(50/250) = 78.7°. The load-step dip, with the formula of Chapter 4, is about 10/(2π × 50 kHz × 1 mF) = 31.8 mV.

P: Key idea. Compensation is pole-zero placement: model the plant, choose a crossover well below the switching frequency, and place the compensator's zeros and poles to leave adequate phase margin. Current mode turns the LC double pole into a single pole and makes this much easier.
H2: 10.4 Measuring the loop
P: The model is only a starting point; parasitic ESR, capacitor derating with voltage, and layout change the real loop. A network analyzer measures gain and phase around the loop; a load-step test checks the time-domain response. A well-damped loop shows one small overshoot; a marginal one rings. If the rail crosses its undervoltage threshold, a Book 8 controller can brown out and reset; if it falls outside a Book 2 audio stage’s supply headroom, the amplifier can clip. Both measurements belong to design verification, alongside the EMC tests of Book 7.
H2: Worked Example 10.2
P: Problem. A voltage-mode version of the same buck uses a 0.47 µH inductor. Where is the LC double pole, and why does a 50 kHz crossover need a Type III compensator?
P: Solution. f0 = 1/(2π√(0.47 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁻³)) = 7.34 kHz. Above it the plant's phase falls toward −180°, so at 50 kHz the plant alone has almost no phase left. A Type II compensator, whose integrator already costs 90°, cannot restore it; the Type III's two zeros, placed near f0, supply the extra boost needed for a 45° to 60° margin.
H2: 10.5 Compensating a current-mode converter
P: Peak current-mode control, Section 4.7, reduces the buck's power stage to a single low-frequency pole formed by the output capacitor and the load, plus the capacitor's ESR zero, plus a pair of poles near half the switching frequency from the sampling of the current. A Type II compensator, an integrator with one zero and one pole, is then enough. The usual recipe sets the crossover at a tenth to a fifth of the switching frequency, places the zero at or below the power stage's pole to recover phase, places the pole at the ESR zero or at half the switching frequency to attenuate switching noise, and sets the gain so that the loop crosses at the chosen frequency. Phase margins of 50 to 65° result without the precise placement that voltage mode's Type III demands.
H2: 10.6 Digital control and its delay
P: More and more converters are controlled by a microcontroller or a dedicated digital controller. Digital control brings programmability, telemetry, and adaptive tuning, and the PMBus interface of server and processor regulators depends on it. It also brings delay. The ADC samples once per switching period, the processor computes the new duty cycle, and the PWM applies it at the next update, so between sampling and action lies typically one to one and a half switching periods. A pure delay Td costs a phase of 360° × fc × Td at the crossover frequency fc without changing the gain, and that phase comes straight out of the margin. Digital loops therefore cross over somewhat lower than analog ones at the same switching frequency, or sample several times per period.
EQ: φdelay = 360° × fc × Td
H2: Worked Example 10.3
P: Problem. A digitally controlled buck switches at 500 kHz and has a total loop delay of 1.5 switching periods. Find the phase lost at crossover frequencies of 50 kHz and 25 kHz.
P: Solution. Td = 1.5 × 2 µs = 3 µs. At 50 kHz, φ = 360 × 50 × 10³ × 3 × 10⁻⁶ = 54°, most of a typical phase margin. At 25 kHz the loss is 27°, which a Type II or Type III compensator can make up. A controller that samples twice per period and updates immediately cuts the delay to about 0.75 periods and the loss at 50 kHz to 27°.
H2: Worked Example 10.4
P: Problem. A current-mode buck at 1 MHz has its output-capacitor pole at 2 kHz and its ESR zero at 400 kHz. Place the crossover, the compensator zero, and the compensator pole.
P: Solution. A crossover at a tenth of 1 MHz, 100 kHz, is safe. The zero goes at or below the 2 kHz pole, say 2 kHz, to cancel it; the pole at half the switching frequency, 500 kHz, close to the ESR zero it cancels. The gain is then set so that the loop gain is unity at 100 kHz. The result has about 60 to 70° of phase margin.
H2: 10.7 Output impedance and the load step
P: A loop's crossover frequency translates directly into how far the output dips when the load steps. Above crossover the loop cannot help, and the output capacitor alone supplies the difference; below it the loop holds the output. To a good approximation, the closed-loop output impedance peaks near crossover at about the capacitor's impedance there, 1/(2π fc C), so a load step ΔI produces a deviation of about ΔI/(2π fc C). Doubling the crossover halves the dip, or halves the capacitance needed for the same dip. This is the deepest reason designers push crossover as high as the switching frequency and the phase margin allow, and why processor regulators, whose load steps are hundreds of amperes in a microsecond, add the load-line technique of Chapter 18 on top.
EQ: ΔV ≈ ΔI / (2π fc Cout)
H2: Worked Example 10.5
P: Problem. A regulator with 1 mF of output capacitance crosses over at 100 kHz. Estimate the output deviation for a 100 A load step, and the capacitance needed to halve it at the same crossover.
P: Solution. Zout ≈ 1/(2π × 10⁵ × 10⁻³) = 1.6 mΩ, so ΔV ≈ 100 × 0.0016 = 160 mV, too much for a 0.8 V processor rail with a ±3 % window of 24 mV. Halving it needs 2 mF at the same crossover; meeting 24 mV needs about 6.6 mF, or a higher crossover with more phases, or the load line.
P: The loop holds the output at full load. At light load the inductor current reverses or stops, the power stage changes its behavior, and efficiency falls. On a battery-powered sensor that fixed loss consumes runtime even while the radio sleeps; in a server hall, the idle loss still occupies electrical and cooling capacity. Chapter 11 takes up the changed light-load dynamics.
BENCH: Measure the loop: inject a small signal across a 10 to 50 Ω resistor in the feedback path and read gain and phase with a frequency-response analyzer, keeping the injection small enough that the output stays linear. A load-step test shows the same margin in the time domain: one clean undershoot means plenty of margin, several rings mean little.
