H1: Chapter 19. Gate Drivers and Isolation
P: A half bridge can destroy itself with nothing wrong in its controller. When one switch turns on fast, the other's drain jumps by hundreds of volts in nanoseconds; that dv/dt drives current through the idle switch's Miller capacitance into its gate, lifts the gate to threshold, and both switches conduct at once. The cure lives in the gate driver.
P: Where you meet this: Every gate-driver IC, every bootstrap diode and capacitor, every isolated driver's CMTI rating, every desaturation pin, and the isolation barrier between a patient-connected medical sensor and the mains-powered electronics behind it.
P: A power switch is only as good as the circuit that drives its gate. The driver must deliver amperes of gate current for nanoseconds, hold the switch firmly off while its drain swings by hundreds of volts, and, for the high-side switch of a half bridge, do all this from a supply that floats with the switch node. Where safety demands, the control signal must also cross an isolation barrier. This chapter sizes the bootstrap supply, explains the dv/dt hazards of Miller turn-on and common-mode transients, and introduces the isolation that safety standards require. The devices are Book 3's.
H2: 19.1 Gate charge and drive current
P: A MOSFET or GaN gate is a capacitor that must be charged by its total gate charge Qg to turn the device on. Switching in a time t requires an average current of Qg/t, and the driver's peak current, limited by its output resistance and an external gate resistor, sets the switching speed and with it the overlap loss of Chapter 9. Faster is not always better: faster edges ring with the loop inductance and radiate the emissions that Book 7 measures, so gate resistors are often chosen to slow the edges deliberately, separately for turn-on and turn-off.
EQ: Igate,avg = Qg / tsw,   Pdrive = Qg Vdrive fs
H2: 19.2 The bootstrap supply
P: The high-side switch's source sits at the switch node, which swings between ground and the input voltage. A bootstrap capacitor, charged through a diode from the low-side supply whenever the switch node is low, provides the floating supply for the high-side driver. Each turn-on draws Qg from it, and its voltage must not droop below the gate's required drive. The capacitance must therefore be many times Qg/ΔV; a factor of ten is a common margin, also covering the driver's quiescent current and leakage.
EQ: Cboot ≥ Qg / ΔV  (× margin)
H2: Worked Example 19.1
P: Problem. A high-side switch has Qg = 20 nC, and the bootstrap may droop by 0.5 V. Find the minimum capacitance and a practical value with a factor-of-ten margin.
P: Solution. Qg/ΔV = 20 nC/0.5 V = 40 nF; with the margin, about 400 nF, so a 0.47 µF ceramic capacitor. At very low duty cycles or in long high-side on-times, the bootstrap cannot recharge and needs a charge pump or an isolated supply instead.
H2: 19.3 dv/dt: Miller turn-on and common-mode transients
P: When the switch node slews, the drain-gate capacitance Cgd of an off device injects a current Cgd dv/dt into its gate. If that current, flowing through the gate resistance and driver, raises the gate voltage above threshold, the device turns on briefly while its partner is on, a shoot-through called Miller turn-on. The cures are a low-impedance turn-off path, a negative off-state gate voltage, and an active Miller clamp. The same dv/dt couples through the parasitic capacitance of any isolation barrier, so isolated drivers specify a common-mode transient immunity, CMTI, in kilovolts per microsecond or volts per nanosecond, that must exceed the switch node's slew with margin.
EQ: IMiller = Cgd dv/dt,   Icm = Cbarrier dv/dt
H2: Worked Example 19.2
P: Problem. A switch with Cgd = 10 pF sees a 50 V/ns edge while held off through 2 Ω. Find the injected current and the gate bump. Then find the common-mode current through a 1 pF barrier at 100 V/ns.
P: Solution. IMiller = 10 pF × 50 V/ns = 0.5 A, and through 2 Ω it lifts the gate by 1 V, uncomfortably close to a GaN device's threshold of 1.5 to 2 V; a negative off-voltage or a lower-impedance path is needed. Through the 1 pF barrier, 100 V/ns drives 0.1 A of common-mode current, which the isolator's receiver must reject without a false transition.
P: Key idea. The gate driver's job is to switch fast and stay off. The faster the switch node slews, the more current its parasitic capacitances inject, so drivers fight dv/dt with low-impedance hold-off, negative bias, and isolators with high common-mode immunity.
H2: 19.4 Isolation and safety
P: Accessible circuits in mains-connected equipment must be protected from hazardous energy by the safeguards required by the applicable product standard. Depending on the equipment and its protective architecture, the requirements may use combinations of basic and supplementary safeguards, double insulation, or reinforced insulation; the same insulation class is not required for every product. A gate-driver isolation barrier is only one part of the protection path. If its insulation system or board spacing is inadequate, a mains or DC-bus fault can reach an accessible circuit and expose a person to hazardous voltage. Separately, inadequate CMTI can let a fast switch-node edge create a false gate command and shoot-through, potentially destroying the bridge. Check its certified insulation classification, working and transient voltages, and CMTI, then determine the board's creepage and clearance from the finished product's standard, pollution degree, material group, and altitude. IEC 62368-1 applies to AV/ICT/office equipment; IEC 61800-5-1 covers adjustable-speed power drive systems, excluding EV traction drives. Use the standard applicable to the finished equipment and verify the complete assembly.
P: Isolators use optical, magnetic, or capacitive coupling. Some digital isolators offer high data rates, more consistent timing, or higher common-mode transient immunity than many optocouplers, and they avoid the optocoupler LED aging mechanism. Both technologies remain in use. Choose by the required insulation class, working voltage and lifetime, surge withstand, CMTI, temperature range, signal type, and product certification; do not infer safety from the technology name alone.
H2: 19.5 Gate loops, ringing, and negative bias
P: The gate driver, the gate resistor, the transistor's input capacitance, and the inductance of the loop between them form a series resonant circuit. With 10 nH of loop inductance and 2 nF of input capacitance it rings at about 36 MHz, and unless the resistance damps it, the gate voltage overshoots its rating on turn-on and rings back toward the threshold on turn-off. Critical damping needs a total resistance near 2√(L/C), about 4.5 Ω here, which also slows the switching; the real cure is a small loop, with the driver next to the transistor and a Kelvin source connection that keeps the power current out of the gate return. SiC MOSFETs, whose gates tolerate about −8 to +22 V and whose thresholds can fall below 2 V when hot, are usually driven at +15 to +18 V on and −3 to −5 V off, so that the Miller current of Section 19.3 cannot lift the gate to its threshold. Enhancement-mode GaN, with a gate rated only about +6 V or +7 V and a threshold near 1.5 V, needs even tighter loops.
EQ: f_ring = 1/(2π√(L C)),   R_crit ≈ 2√(L/C)

H2: 19.6 Short-circuit protection: desaturation
P: When a load or a bridge shorts, the transistor carries many times its rated current with the full bus across it, and it survives only for its short-circuit withstand time: about 10 µs for industrial IGBTs, 2 to 3 µs for many SiC MOSFETs, and less for GaN. The driver detects the fault by watching the drain or collector voltage while the device is commanded on: if it rises above a threshold, typically 7 to 9 V for an IGBT, after a blanking time that covers the normal turn-on, the device has left saturation. The driver then turns it off slowly, a soft turn-off, because cutting a fault current of thousands of amperes quickly would produce a destructive L di/dt spike. Isolated drivers such as the TI UCC21750 and the Infineon 1ED34 family integrate desaturation detection, soft turn-off, and a fault signal back across the barrier.
H2: Worked Example 19.3
P: Problem. A SiC MOSFET with 2 nF of input capacitance is driven through a gate loop of 10 nH. Find the ringing frequency, the resistance for critical damping, and the gate resistor needed if the driver and internal gate resistance together contribute 2 Ω.
P: Solution. f = 1/(2π√(10 × 10⁻⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁹)) = 35.6 MHz. Critical damping needs 2√(10 nH/2 nF) = 4.5 Ω, so an external resistor of about 2.5 Ω. Halving the loop inductance to 5 nH lowers the required total to 3.2 Ω and allows faster switching with the same damping.
H2: Worked Example 19.4
P: Problem. A SiC MOSFET has a short-circuit withstand time of 2.5 µs. The driver's desaturation blanking time is 1.0 µs and its detection and soft turn-off take 0.8 µs. Is the margin adequate, and how would an IGBT with 10 µs compare?
P: Solution. The device is off after 1.8 µs, leaving 0.7 µs of margin, about 28 %, adequate but not generous; designers often shorten the blanking time for SiC to 0.5 µs or less. An IGBT with 10 µs would leave 8.2 µs of margin, which is why IGBT protection could be slower and simpler.
H2: 19.7 Common-mode transient immunity and isolated bias
P: An isolated gate driver for a high-side switch rides on the switch node, which slews by hundreds of volts in nanoseconds with SiC and GaN. Any capacitance across the isolation barrier, in the driver chip itself or in the isolated DC-DC converter that supplies its bias, carries a current C dv/dt, which flows through the circuit's grounds and can corrupt the driver's input signal. Drivers are therefore rated for common-mode transient immunity, CMTI, in kV/µs; 100 to 200 kV/µs is typical in 2026 for SiC-rated parts such as the Silicon Labs Si823x successors and the TI UCC5x family. The isolated bias supply matters as much: its transformer's interwinding capacitance should be a few picofarads, which is why dedicated low-capacitance modules are sold for gate-drive bias.
EQ: I_cm = C_iso × dv/dt
H2: Worked Example 19.5
P: Problem. A half-bridge switch node slews at 100 V/ns. The isolated bias supply has 5 pF of barrier capacitance and the driver 1 pF. Find the common-mode current pulse.
P: Solution. dv/dt = 100 V/ns = 10¹¹ V/s, so the bias supply passes 5 × 10⁻¹² × 10¹¹ = 0.5 A and the driver 0.1 A, for the duration of the edge, a few nanoseconds. Half an ampere in the controller's ground is enough to flip a logic input, which is why the bias supply's capacitance, not just the driver's rating, decides whether a fast design works.
P: The gate driver switches power devices at a few hundred volts to a few kilovolts. Chapter 20 takes the same devices into series stacks for pulsed power at tens of kilovolts.
BENCH: Measure gate voltage at the transistor's own pins, with the probe's ground on the Kelvin source and the shortest possible connection. Look for ringing at turn-on and for a bump on the idle switch's gate when its partner switches. Test desaturation protection with a deliberate short from a current-limited supply before trusting it at full voltage.
P: On the Job
P: Engineers in EV traction inverters, chargers, solar inverters, and industrial drives work with SiC MOSFETs from Wolfspeed, Infineon, onsemi, and STMicroelectronics, isolated drivers such as TI's UCC21750 or Infineon's 1ED34, and digital-power controllers such as TI's C2000 or the STM32G4. Their bench has isolated differential probes, Rogowski current probes, and a double-pulse test fixture. A typical task: run double-pulse tests at full voltage and current to measure switching energy and overshoot, then choose the turn-on and turn-off gate resistors that balance loss against ringing and EMI.
