H1: Chapter 37 — The Smith Chart, Impedance Matching, and Bias Networks
P: A Wi-Fi router's radio chip, the cable and the antenna all have to agree on an impedance, usually 50 Ω, or part of the signal bounces back instead of radiating. The Smith chart is the map engineers use to make them agree. Phillip Smith published it in 1939, and it remains a common display on vector network analyzers.
P: Where you meet this: Antenna matching, RF amplifier design, vector network analyzer displays, and the matching networks between a phone's power amplifier and its antenna (Book 4).
P: This chapter is a preview of where the mathematics of complex impedance leads once RF engineering takes it over — not a transmission-line design course compressed into a foundations book. You will come away able to design a basic single-frequency L-network by hand, using the reasoning below; the deeper skills — bandwidth, multi-section networks, and real component non-idealities — are what this series' dedicated RF and microwave volume builds on top of this foundation.
H2: 37.1 Why Matching Matters: The Conjugate-Match Condition
P: Chapter 23 introduced impedance and showed that a linear circuit's behavior at one frequency is fully captured by a complex number. A question follows immediately once any real source feeds any real load: how much of the available power actually reaches the load, and what can be done about it if the answer is “not much”? Chapter 30 answered this for purely resistive networks — maximum power transfer requires R_load = R_source. The complex generalization, needed the moment either side has any reactance, is the conjugate-match condition:
EQ: Zload = Zsource*
P: That is, the load impedance must equal the complex conjugate of the source impedance — same resistance, equal and opposite reactance. A source with impedance 50 + j30 Ω delivers maximum available power to a load of exactly 50 − j30 Ω, because the load's negative reactance exactly cancels the source's positive reactance (leaving a purely resistive series combination), while the matched resistances split available power exactly as the DC theorem already established. A matching network is nothing more than a lossless circuit — built from reactive elements, which absorb no real power themselves — inserted between an arbitrary source and load specifically to present this conjugate-matched impedance to each side.
H2: 37.2 The Reflection Coefficient
P: Working directly with impedance is awkward once transmission lines enter the picture, because impedance transforms in a complicated way along a line. The reflection coefficient Γ, defined relative to a reference impedance Z₀ (almost always 50 Ω in RF work), is better behaved:
EQ: Γ = (Z − Z0) / (Z + Z0)
P: Equivalently, working with the normalized impedance z = Z/Z₀: Γ = (z − 1)/(z + 1). Γ = 0 means a perfect match (all power delivered, nothing reflected); |Γ| = 1 means total reflection (an open circuit, a short circuit, or any purely reactive load). For any passive load, |Γ| ≤ 1 always.
H2: 37.3 The Smith Chart
P: The Smith chart is a graphical picture of the relationship between Γ and z, plotted on the complex Γ-plane, restricted to the unit disk (since |Γ| ≤ 1 for passive loads). Overlaid on that disk are two families of curves: circles of constant normalized resistance r, and arcs of constant normalized reactance x. Every point inside the chart corresponds to exactly one normalized impedance z = r + jx, and reading r and x directly off the chart — rather than doing the complex algebra of the Γ ↔ z conversion by hand every time — is the entire practical value of the chart.
P: The constant-r circles all pass through the point Γ = 1 (the open-circuit point, z → ∞), with the r = 0 circle being the chart's own outer boundary (the case of a purely reactive load, |Γ| = 1). The constant-x arcs are centered off-chart, above the real axis for inductive reactance (positive x) and below it for capacitive reactance (negative x), with the entire real axis itself forming the x = 0 line — purely resistive impedances.

H2: 37.4 VSWR and Return Loss
P: Two practical numbers are computed directly from |Γ| and appear on every piece of RF test equipment. The voltage standing wave ratio:
EQ: VSWR = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|)
P: runs from 1 (perfect match) to infinity (total reflection), and is the ratio most commonly quoted for antennas and RF connectors. Return loss, expressed in decibels, is the other common figure of merit:
EQ: Return Loss (dB) = −20 log10|Γ|
P: with a larger return loss (in dB) meaning a better match — the opposite sense from VSWR, which is worth keeping straight, since both numbers describe exactly the same underlying |Γ|.
H2: 37.5 Moving on the Chart: Series and Shunt Elements
P: Adding a series reactance to a load moves its point along a constant-r circle — a series element changes only x, leaving r (and hence the circle it sits on) unchanged, whether the added reactance is inductive (moving clockwise, toward more positive x) or capacitive (moving counterclockwise). Adding a shunt element — one placed in parallel, rather than in series — is most naturally described in admittance terms: y = 1/z = g + jb, where a shunt reactance changes only b, moving the point along a constant-g circle. The admittance grid is the impedance grid rotated 180° about the chart's center (since y = 1/z corresponds to Γ → −Γ), so the same physical chart serves both purposes — a shunt element moves a point along whichever constant-g circle is the point-reflection, through the center, of the corresponding constant-r circle.
KEY: Series elements move you along a constant-r circle; shunt elements move you along a constant-g circle. A two-element L-network match is nothing more than finding one circle of each kind that intersect — one step to reach the intersection, one more to reach the center (the matched point, Γ = 0).
H2: 37.6 Lumped-Element (L-Network) Matching
P: The simplest matching network — an L-network — uses exactly two reactive elements, one in series and one in shunt, arranged so the load's impedance is transformed to Z₀. Given the discussion above, the design procedure is direct: pick whichever element (series or shunt) moves the load's own r-circle (or g-circle) so that it crosses the g = 1 circle (or r = 1 circle), then pick the second element to complete the trip to the center.
WE: Worked Example 37.1
P: Match a purely resistive load R_L = 25 Ω to a Z₀ = 50 Ω system at f = 1.0 GHz using an L-network.
P: Normalized load: z_L = 25/50 = 0.5. Since R_L < Z₀, add a series reactance first. Solving for the series reactance X_s that lands exactly on the g = 1 circle (algebraically, X_s² = R_L(Z₀ − R_L) = 25(50 − 25) = 625, so X_s = 25 Ω): the point moves from z = 0.5 to z = 0.5 + j0.5 — and a direct check confirms this normalized admittance is y = 1 − j1, i.e., g = 1 exactly, as required.
P: A series inductor supplies this positive reactance: L = X_s/ω = 25/(2π×10⁹) ≈ 3.98 nH. The load's residual susceptance of −1 (normalized) is canceled by a shunt capacitor supplying b = +1 (normalized) = 0.02 S (actual): C = 0.02/ω ≈ 3.18 pF. With both elements in place, the load presents precisely 50 + j0 Ω to the source.
WE: Worked Example 37.2
P: A load impedance of Z_L = 25 + j25 Ω (not purely resistive) is to be matched to the same 50 Ω system, also at 1.0 GHz. How many elements are needed?
P: Normalized: z_L = 0.5 + j0.5 — which is exactly the intermediate point reached in Worked Example 37.1 after its series inductor was added. This load's own reactance already does the job a series element would otherwise have to do. Only the shunt capacitor is needed: C ≈ 3.18 pF, exactly as before, bringing the load to a perfect match with a single component.
MIS: It is tempting to assume an L-network always needs exactly one inductor and one capacitor. It needs one series and one shunt reactance, of whichever sign the specific match requires — Worked Example 37.1 happened to need a series L and a shunt C, but the dual solution (series C, shunt L) is equally valid for a resistive load, and which pair of element types is actually needed depends on the load's own reactance, exactly as Worked Example 37.2 shows: a load with the right amount of its own series reactance already present needs only a single shunt element to finish the job.
H2: 37.7 Distributed Matching: Microstrip Lines and Stubs
P: At the frequencies where microstrip circuit boards operate, a short length of transmission line is often a more practical matching element than a discrete inductor, especially once the frequency is high enough that a lumped component's own parasitic behavior becomes significant. The single-stub tuning technique replicates the L-network idea using distributed elements: a length d of line (of the board's own characteristic impedance Z₀) is inserted between the load and a shunt stub, chosen so that at distance d from the load, the line's input admittance has real part exactly Y₀ = 1/Z₀ — placing the point on the g = 1 circle, exactly as a series lumped element did in Section 37.6. A stub of length l, then, cancels the remaining susceptance, exactly as the shunt capacitor did.
WE: Worked Example 37.3
P: A purely resistive load Z_L = 100 Ω is to be matched to a Z₀ = 50 Ω microstrip line using a single short-circuited shunt stub. Find the required distance d from the load to the stub, and the stub length l, both as fractions of a wavelength.
P: Normalized load: z_L = 2.0. Solving the standard single-stub distance equation for a real load gives two valid solutions within one half-wavelength; the shorter one works out to d ≈ 0.152λ. At that point, the line's input impedance is Z_in ≈ 33.3 − j23.6 Ω, so its admittance is Y_in ≈ 0.020 + j0.0141 S — the real part matches Y₀ = 1/50 = 0.020 S exactly, confirming the point sits on the g = 1 circle as intended.
P: A short-circuited stub of length l presents an input susceptance of −Y₀cot(βl); setting this equal to −0.0141 S (to cancel the remaining +j0.0141 S) gives βl ≈ 54.7°, or l ≈ 0.152λ. The second valid solution, d ≈ 0.348λ with a stub of l ≈ 0.348λ, is equally correct — in practice, whichever pair gives the more convenient physical length on the actual board is used.
P: An open-circuited stub can be used in place of a short-circuited one — often preferred in microstrip specifically because an open stub needs no via to ground, which a short-circuited stub does — at the cost of a generally different (and here, longer) required stub length for the same match, since an open stub's input susceptance is +Y₀tan(βl) rather than −Y₀cot(βl).
H2: 37.8 Fine-Tuning a Match with SMD Components
P: A matching network built from fixed-value components — whether lumped or microstrip — rarely lands exactly on the intended match once real component tolerances, board parasitics, and assembly variation are accounted for. A small additional shunt capacitance, realized as a surface-mount trimmer or a selectable small SMD pad, is the standard way to correct a small residual mismatch after assembly, precisely because shunt capacitive susceptances add: adding a second capacitor in parallel with the first increases the total susceptance by exactly ωΔC, with no interaction between the two.
WE: Worked Example 37.4
P: A matching network designed for a perfect match at 1.0 GHz is measured, after assembly, to present an input admittance of Y_in = 0.020 − j0.0010 S (the real part is correct; a small residual inductive-looking susceptance remains, most likely from stray inductance in the shunt capacitor's own PCB pad). Find the additional SMD shunt capacitance needed to null out this residual susceptance.
P: The residual susceptance to cancel is +0.0010 S (a shunt capacitor's susceptance is positive, ωC, so it can only add positive susceptance — exactly what is needed here to cancel a negative residual). ΔC = 0.0010/ω = 0.0010/(2π×10⁹) ≈ 0.159 pF — a genuinely tiny trim capacitance, which is why practical RF fine-tuning uses sub-picofarad SMD padders rather than trying to hit the ideal value on the first try with the primary matching components alone.
H2: 37.9 Bias Networks: RF Chokes and High-Ohmic Resistors
P: A practical amplifier stage needs DC bias delivered to its active device without disturbing the RF matching networks built around it — the bias network must be effectively invisible to the signal at the operating frequency, while still providing whatever DC path the device actually needs.
P: At an output node (a bipolar transistor's collector, or a FET's drain — the output terminals of the two main transistor families, covered properly later in this series), meaningful DC current must flow, so an RF choke — an inductor sized so its reactance ωL is large compared to the system impedance at the operating frequency — is the standard solution: it looks like a low-resistance DC path (an inductor has zero DC resistance, ideally) while presenting a high impedance, close to an open circuit, to the RF signal, leaving the surrounding matching network's design essentially undisturbed. A common rule of thumb sizes the choke's reactance to at least 5-10 times the system impedance at the design frequency.
P: At an input node (a FET's gate, in particular), the DC current required is essentially zero — an ideal FET gate draws no DC current at all — so a simple high-value resistor works as well as a choke, and is simpler and cheaper to implement. A large resistor barely loads the RF path (since it sits in parallel with a much smaller system impedance) and needs to carry essentially no current, so the power dissipated in it is negligible regardless of its value being “too large” by ordinary circuit-design standards.

WE: Worked Example 37.5
P: An amplifier operating at f = 2.4 GHz uses a 50 Ω matching network at its output. Using a rule-of-thumb factor of 10, find the minimum RF choke inductance for output biasing. Separately, verify that a 10 kΩ gate-bias resistor barely disturbs a 50 Ω input match.
P: In the ideal-inductor model, 33.2 nH gives 500 Ω of reactance at 2.4 GHz. This is a calculation target, not a parts-selection guarantee: a real inductor has parasitic capacitance, loss, tolerance, and a self-resonant frequency. Select a part whose manufacturer data show the required inductive impedance at the operating frequency and bias; a larger nominal inductance alone does not ensure less loading.
P: Bias resistor: a 10 kΩ resistor in parallel with a 50 Ω system impedance combines to (50 × 10,000)/(50 + 10,000) ≈ 49.75 Ω — a deviation of about 0.5% from the unloaded 50 Ω, negligible for essentially any practical purpose, and confirming that a high-ohmic resistor is an entirely adequate choice for a near-zero-current input bias point.
BENCH: Real matching networks never land exactly where the chart says, because pad capacitance, trace inductance and part tolerance all count at RF. Leave footprints on the board for a three-element pi network (one series part and two shunt pads) so the match can be tuned later on a network analyzer; unused positions get a 0 Ω link or stay empty, which costs almost nothing.
P: On the Job
P: RF engineers on consumer products match antennas for a living. Their tools are a vector network analyzer, a box of 0402 inductors and capacitors, a fine-tipped soldering iron, and a Smith chart on the analyzer's screen. A typical task: a Wi-Fi and Bluetooth LE product's 2.4 GHz antenna, matched perfectly on the bare board, detunes once the plastic case and the battery are fitted. The engineer measures it in the finished product, picks a new two-element match from the chart, solders it into the pi-pad footprint the layout left for that purpose, and checks the radiated power in a test chamber before the design is released.
