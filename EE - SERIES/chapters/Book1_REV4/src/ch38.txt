H1: Chapter 38 — Where This Series Goes Next
P: You now have the physics, the mathematics and the circuit theory. The rest of the series is what engineers build with them: amplifiers, chips, radios, power supplies and finished products. This short chapter is the map.
H2: 38.1 What This Book Has Built
P: This book took the physical definitions from Part I — voltage, current, resistance, capacitance, inductance — and built the systematic machinery needed to analyze linear circuit models made from them: Kirchhoff's two laws as the foundation; series/parallel reduction and dividers as the fastest tool when it applies; nodal analysis for resistive networks of any topology and mesh analysis for planar networks; Thevenin and Norton equivalents for reasoning about a network's behavior independent of whatever load is eventually attached to it; superposition as both a practical technique and a conceptual justification for several of the results around it; first-order transients as the first genuinely time-dependent behavior in the book; and finally phasors and impedance, which extend every one of the DC techniques to sinusoidal steady-state AC circuits with only a change from real to complex arithmetic.
H2: 38.2 A Preview: Linearizing a Nonlinear Device
P: It is worth seeing, briefly and concretely, how a central technique of Book 3, Semiconductor Physics and Devices, connects to this one, rather than taking the connection entirely on faith. A diode's actual current-voltage relationship is strongly nonlinear — current grows roughly exponentially with voltage once the diode is forward-biased past a threshold, nothing like a resistor's straight-line V = IR. And yet, once a diode is biased at some fixed operating point (a fixed DC voltage and current, set by the rest of the circuit) and only a small additional signal is applied on top of that bias, the diode's response to that small extra signal is, to good approximation, linear — behaving, for that small signal only, exactly like an ordinary resistor whose value (called the diode's small-signal or dynamic resistance) depends on where along its curve the bias point happens to sit.
P: For a diode biased at current I_D, the exponential model gives a small-signal resistance r_d ≈ nV_T/I_D when I_D is well above reverse saturation current. Here n is the diode ideality factor and V_T = kT/q is about 25.7 mV at 25 °C. Worked Example 38.1 uses n = 1 and neglects the diode’s series resistance, giving r_d ≈ 26 Ω at 1 mA. A real device can differ; use the model and operating point appropriate to that part. The small AC signal then sees this local resistance only while it remains small enough not to move the diode far from its bias point.

WE: Worked Example 38.1 — A Genuine Preview
P: Assume an ideality factor n = 1 and neglect series resistance. A diode biased at I_D = 1.0 mA then has r_d ≈ nV_T/I_D = 0.026/0.001 = 26 Ω. This small-signal model is placed in series with a 1.0 kΩ resistor across a 10 mV AC source. Find the AC voltage across the diode’s small-signal resistance.
SOL: This is nothing more than a voltage divider (Chapter 27), applied to the diode's linearized small-signal model exactly as if it were any other resistor: V_diode = V_signal × r_d/(r_d + R) = 10 mV × 26/(26+1000) ≈ 10 × 0.0253 ≈ 0.25 mV. Every step here — identifying the divider, applying the ratio, computing the numeric answer — is drawn directly and without modification from Chapter 27 of this book. The only genuinely new idea, contributed by Book 3, was finding that 26 Ω small-signal resistance value in the first place, from the diode's underlying (and quite different-looking) nonlinear physics.
H2: 38.3 A Deliberate Limitation, and Why It Is Temporary
P: The systematic circuit-analysis problems in this book use a linear model: resistors, capacitors, inductors, independent sources, and (in Chapter 36) ideal transformers, with no thresholds or saturation. Some Reader Lab and application examples mention nonlinear devices such as LEDs and comparators, but treat them at the interface level rather than analyzing their nonlinear behavior. Real electronic circuits also use diodes and transistors, whose response depends on operating point and signal size. The linear methods in this book apply to those devices only after an appropriate model or local linearization has been chosen.
P: This boundary leads directly to Books 2 and 3. Book 2 puts diodes, transistors, and op-amps to work as building blocks; Book 3 develops semiconductor physics and devices from the ground up. Some devices have a smooth response around a stable operating point, where a sufficiently small signal can be modeled by a linear circuit. For example, a forward-biased diode has a small-signal resistance of approximately V_T/I_D. The approximation is local: it becomes inaccurate when the signal is large enough to move the device far from its bias point, and it does not replace the nonlinear model needed to find that operating point. Once the small-signal model is appropriate, this book's nodal analysis, Thevenin equivalents, and phasor methods can analyze the resulting linear network.
KEY: A small-signal model is valid only near its stated operating point and within the signal range for which the linear approximation holds. Find the operating point first; then use the linear-circuit tools from this book on the model. Outside that range, return to the device's nonlinear behavior.
