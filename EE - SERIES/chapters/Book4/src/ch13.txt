H1: Chapter 13. Small Antennas, Monopoles, Loops, and Horns
P: Why does every small gadget fight its antenna? Because in 1948 Lan Jen Chu put a ceiling on it. An antenna that fits in a 20 mm sphere at 915 MHz can never cover more than about 3.5 % bandwidth at a 2:1 VSWR, whatever its shape, and real antennas fall short of even that.
P: Where you meet this: Chip antennas on IoT boards, key-fob loops, quarter-wave whips over ground planes, and the horns that feed dishes and test ranges.
P: Chapter 7 treated the dipole, the patch, and the large aperture. Most antennas in products are none of these: they are monopoles over a circuit board, small loops in key fobs, chip antennas squeezed into a corner, or horns feeding reflectors and test ranges. This chapter covers those types and the physical limit that governs every small antenna: the smaller it is relative to the wavelength, the narrower its bandwidth and the harder it is to make efficient.
H2: 13.1 The monopole
P: A quarter-wave monopole over a large ground plane behaves like half of a dipole: its image in the plane completes the dipole. All its power goes into the half-space above the plane, so its directivity is twice the dipole's, 5.15 dBi, and its input resistance is half, about 36.5 Ω. On a real product the ground plane is the circuit board, which is often not large compared with a wavelength, so the board itself radiates and the pattern, gain, and tuning depend on its size and on the hand holding it. At 2.45 GHz a quarter wave is 30.6 mm in air, which is why most handheld antennas are folded, meandered, or loaded to fit.
H2: 13.2 The small-antenna limit
P: An antenna that fits inside a sphere of radius a much smaller than a wavelength stores far more reactive energy than it radiates, so it has a high Q and a narrow bandwidth. Chu's limit, refined by McLean, gives the minimum Q of any antenna inside that sphere: Q = 1/(ka)³ + 1/(ka), with k = 2π/λ. The fractional bandwidth for a given VSWR s is at most about (s − 1)/(Q√s). Matching networks cannot beat it, by the same argument as Chapter 11's Bode-Fano limit. Adding loss broadens the band but wastes power, which is how many tiny antennas meet their bandwidth specifications.
EQ: Qmin = 1/(ka)³ + 1/(ka),   FBW ≤ (s − 1)/(Q√s)
FIG: fig13_1.png | Figure 13.1 — The Chu limit. As an antenna shrinks inside its sphere, its minimum Q climbs as 1/(ka)³ and its bandwidth falls. A 915 MHz antenna in a 20 mm sphere can reach at best about 3.5 %.
H2: Worked Example 13.1
P: Problem. A 915 MHz sensor's antenna must fit in a sphere of 20 mm radius. Find ka, the minimum Q, and the maximum bandwidth for a 2:1 VSWR. Repeat for a 2.45 GHz antenna in a 10 mm sphere.
P: Solution. At 915 MHz, k = 2π/0.3277 m = 19.2 rad/m, so ka = 0.384 and Qmin = 1/0.0564 + 1/0.384 = 20.3. The bandwidth for a 2:1 VSWR is at most 1/(20.3 × √2) = 3.5%, or 32 MHz, enough for the 26 MHz ISM band with little margin, and only if the antenna reaches the limit, which practical antennas rarely do. At 2.45 GHz in a 10 mm sphere, ka = 0.513 and Qmin = 9.3, so the bandwidth limit is about 7.6%.
P: Key idea. A small antenna's bandwidth is limited by its size in wavelengths: the minimum Q grows as 1/(ka)³. When a product shrinks its antenna, it pays in bandwidth, efficiency, or both.
H2: 13.3 The small loop
P: A loop much smaller than a wavelength has a radiation resistance of 31,171 (A/λ²)² ohms for one turn of area A, minute for practical sizes. Its conductor loss, set by the surface resistance of Chapter 12 and the wire's circumference, is usually much larger, so a small loop is an inefficient radiator. It is used anyway in key fobs, pagers, and hearing aids, because it is compact, responds to the magnetic field, and is less detuned by the body than a small electric antenna. As a receiving antenna at low frequencies, where atmospheric noise dominates, its inefficiency costs little sensitivity.
EQ: Rrad = 31,171 (A/λ²)² Ω   (one turn)
H2: Worked Example 13.2
P: Problem. A key fob uses a single-turn copper loop 10 mm in diameter, of wire 0.5 mm in radius, at 433 MHz. Find its radiation resistance, its loss resistance, and its efficiency.
P: Solution. A = π × 0.005² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ m² and λ = 0.692 m, so Rrad = 31,171 × (7.85 × 10⁻⁵/0.479)² = 0.84 mΩ. The surface resistance of copper at 433 MHz is 5.43 mΩ per square; the loss resistance is Rs times the circumference over the wire's perimeter, 5.43 mΩ × 31.4 mm / 3.14 mm = 54.3 mΩ. The efficiency is 0.84/(0.84 + 54.3) = 1.5%, or −18.2 dB. Short-range remote controls tolerate that, and regulations limit their radiated power anyway.
H2: 13.4 Horns
P: A horn flares a waveguide into an aperture, transforming the guide's impedance gradually to free space. Its gain follows the aperture formula of Chapter 7, G = εap 4πA/λ², with an aperture efficiency of about 0.5 for an optimum pyramidal horn. Horns are broadband, predictable, and low-loss, which makes them the standard gain references for antenna measurement and the feeds of reflector antennas. Corrugated horns produce symmetrical, low-sidelobe patterns for satellite feeds.
H2: Worked Example 13.3
P: Problem. A pyramidal horn at 10 GHz has an aperture of 100 × 80 mm and an aperture efficiency of 0.51. Find its gain.
P: Solution. λ = 30.0 mm, so G = 0.51 × 4π × 0.1 × 0.08/0.0300² = 57.0, or 17.6 dBi.
BENCH: For a small antenna, the ground plane matters as much as the antenna itself. A monopole on a 30 mm board isn't the monopole in the data sheet, because the board is half the antenna. Leave a pi-match footprint, measure with the device in its final enclosure with the battery installed, and tune last. A small loop's radiation resistance is in milliohms, so copper loss and capacitor ESR set its efficiency; use the highest-Q tuning capacitor you can find.
