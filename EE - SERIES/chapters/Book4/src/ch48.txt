PART: Part IV. Measured Data, Sensing, Light, and the Bench Radio
H1: Chapter 48. Touchstone Files and Noise Parameters: Designing from Measured Data
P: For most RF transistors there is no model you would trust at 2 GHz. What the vendor ships instead is a text file of measured numbers, a few hundred lines that begin with a hash mark and a handful of letters. Read it correctly and it gives you gain, stability, and the best noise figure the device can reach; read it in the wrong order and an amplifier with 17 dB of gain becomes one with a loss of 24 dB.
P: Where you meet this: Vendor download pages for transistors, amplifiers, filters, and connectors; the output of every vector network analyzer; circuit simulators from ngspice to commercial suites; and scripts that check a design against measured data.
P: Chapter 5 defined S-parameters and Chapter 28 the noise parameters NFmin, Γopt, and Rn. This chapter is about the file that carries both. The Touchstone format, named after the simulator that introduced it and now maintained as an open specification, is the common language of measured RF data. A designer who can read one by eye, check it with a few lines of Python, and load it into a simulator can design from any vendor's measurements.
H2: 48.1 The format
P: A Touchstone file's extension gives its number of ports: .s1p, .s2p, .s4p. Lines beginning with an exclamation mark are comments, and vendors put the bias point, the reference planes, and the test fixture there; read them, because the data mean nothing without them. The option line, beginning with #, gives the frequency unit, the parameter type, the number format, and the reference resistance. Its defaults are GHz, S, MA, and 50 Ω, so a file whose option line is a bare # means exactly that.
LI: Frequency unit: Hz, kHz, MHz, or GHz.
LI: Parameter: S, Y, Z, H, or G; S is by far the most common.
LI: Format: MA for magnitude and angle in degrees, DB for decibels and angle, RI for real and imaginary parts.
LI: Reference: R followed by the resistance in ohms.
P: Each data line of a two-port file holds a frequency and eight numbers, four complex parameters in the order S11, S21, S12, S22. That order is the trap: it is not the order of a matrix read row by row, and files with three or more ports use the row order instead, S11, S12, S13 and so on. Version 2.0 of the specification, ratified in 2009, adds keywords in square brackets, [Version] 2.0, [Number of Ports], [Two-Port Data Order] with the values 12_21 or 21_12, [Number of Frequencies], [Reference], [Network Data], [Noise Data], and [End], which make the order explicit and allow different reference impedances per port. Most vendor files are still version 1.1.
EQ: # GHz S MA R 50      f  |S11| ∠S11  |S21| ∠S21  |S12| ∠S12  |S22| ∠S22
P: A two-port file can carry the noise parameters after the S-parameters. The noise block begins at the first line whose frequency is less than or equal to the previous one, and each of its lines holds five numbers: frequency, NFmin in decibels, the magnitude and angle of Γopt, and the noise resistance normalized to the reference, Rn/Z0. In version 2.0 the block follows the [Noise Data] keyword and Rn is given in ohms.
H2: 48.2 Reading a real file
P: The listing below is five frequencies of the Broadcom ATF-54143 enhancement-mode pHEMT at 3 V and 60 mA, transcribed from its data sheet tables, the device of Worked Example 28.3. The full vendor file has more points; this excerpt ships with the book's scripts.
EQ: # GHz S MA R 50
EQ: 2.0  0.62 −168.3  7.31 73.3  0.06 33.3  0.17 −117.0
EQ: ! noise: f  NFmin  |Γopt|  ∠Γopt  Rn/50
EQ: 1.9  0.42  0.27  116.3  0.04
EQ: 2.0  0.45  0.27  120.1  0.04
H2: Worked Example 48.1
P: Problem. From the 2.0 GHz line, find the gain |S21|² in decibels, the input return loss, and the reverse isolation. Then find what a reader who assumed the row order S11, S12, S21, S22 would report as the gain.
P: Solution. |S21| = 7.31, so the gain is 20 log 7.31 = 17.3 dB. The input return loss is −20 log 0.62 = 4.2 dB, a poor match, as is usual for an unmatched transistor. The isolation is −20 log 0.06 = 24.4 dB. A reader using the row order would take the third and fourth numbers, 0.06, as S21 and report a gain of −24.4 dB, a device that loses signal. The check is physical: a transistor biased at 60 mA at 2 GHz has gain, and |S21| larger than |S12| by more than ten times is the normal pattern. If a parsed file says otherwise, the order is wrong.
H2: 48.3 Stability and the gain the device can give
P: With the four S-parameters, Chapter 5's stability factor and the gain bounds follow at every frequency. Where K < 1 the device is potentially unstable and the best stable gain is the maximum stable gain, MSG = |S21|/|S12|; where K > 1 and |Δ| < 1 the maximum available gain exists. The single-test factor μ, the distance from the center of the Smith chart to the nearest unstable source or load, says the same thing in one number: μ > 1 means unconditionally stable.
EQ: K = (1 − |S11|² − |S22|² + |Δ|²)/(2|S12 S21|),   μ = (1 − |S11|²)/(|S22 − Δ S11*| + |S12 S21|),   MSG = |S21|/|S12|
H2: Worked Example 48.2
P: Problem. Find K, μ, |Δ|, and MSG at 2.0 GHz from the ATF-54143 file.
P: Solution. Δ = S11S22 − S12S21 has magnitude 0.354. K = 0.811 and μ = 0.766, both below 1, so the device is potentially unstable at 2 GHz: some source or load impedances would make it oscillate. MSG = 7.31/0.06 = 121.8, 20.9 dB. A design either stays away from the unstable regions of the Smith chart or lowers the gain with a small resistor at the output or inductance in the source leads until K exceeds 1 at every frequency, including those far outside the band, where the file's full frequency range is needed.
H2: 48.4 Noise from the file
P: With Γopt and Rn, the noise figure for any source reflection Γs follows from Chapter 28's formula, written for reflection coefficients. The contours of constant noise figure are circles on the Smith chart, centered on the line from the origin toward Γopt, which shrink to the point Γopt as the noise figure approaches NFmin.
P: F = Fmin + (4 Rn/Z0) |Γs − Γopt|² / ((1 − |Γs|²) |1 + Γopt|²),   N = (F − Fmin)|1 + Γopt|²/(4Rn/Z0),   center Γopt/(1 + N),   radius √(N² + N(1 − |Γopt|²))/(1 + N)
H2: Worked Example 48.3
P: Problem. At 2.0 GHz find the noise figure with a 50 Ω source and at Γopt, the available gain with Γs = Γopt, and the centers and radii of the 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 dB noise circles. Design a two-element network that presents Γopt from 50 Ω.
P: Solution. Rn = 0.04 × 50 = 2.0 Ω. With Γs = 0 the noise figure is 0.51 dB, with Γs = Γopt it is NFmin = 0.45 dB, and the available gain at Γopt is 18.3 dB. The circles are 0.6 dB: center −0.113 + j0.195, radius 0.392; 0.8 dB: center −0.092 + j0.159, radius 0.550; 1.0 dB: center −0.077 + j0.133, radius 0.641. Γopt = 0.27∠120.1° corresponds to Zopt = 50(1 + Γopt)/(1 − Γopt) = 34.5 + j17.4 Ω. A shunt capacitor of 1.07 pF across the 50 Ω source followed by a series inductor of 3.22 nH presents exactly that: the shunt branch with Q = √(50/34.5 − 1) = 0.670 lowers the resistance to 34.5 Ω, and the series inductor supplies the remaining reactance. The 0.06 dB between 50 Ω and Γopt is small for this device; the network is worth building when Rn is larger or the source far from Γopt, and it must be checked against the stability circles before it is accepted.

H2: 48.5 Python, simulators, and the fit-not-truth rule
P: The book's script touchstone_atf.py, about a hundred lines of standard Python with no packages, reads version 1.1 files in MA, DB, or RI format, detects the noise block, and computes K, μ, MSG, noise figure, available gain, and noise circles; it produced every number in this chapter. The open-source scikit-rf package does the same and more: rf.Network('ATF-54143_3V_60mA.s2p') loads the file, its attributes nfmin_db, g_opt, and rn give the noise parameters, and its plotting functions draw Smith charts. Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, teaches the Python behind such scripts in "Python on the Bench".
P: Simulators read the same files. Commercial RF suites load them as S-parameter blocks with noise, ngspice added S-parameter analysis in version 39 and later releases add Touchstone support, and Qucs-S provides an S-parameter file component with its Qucsator engine; LTspice has no native S-parameter element, so a measured device must be approximated there by an equivalent circuit. Check the manual of the version installed before relying on any of them, especially for the noise block.
P: A Touchstone file is a measurement, not a model. It holds at the bias, temperature, and reference planes in its comments and at its frequency points, and nothing between or beyond them is certain: interpolation between points is a guess, extrapolation outside the range is a hope, and a fixture that differs from the vendor's moves every number. The same rule that Book 3 applies to SPICE models applies here: a fit is not the truth. Measure the first prototype, and compare.
P: Key idea. A Touchstone file is the measured device. Read its comments for the bias and reference planes, its option line for the units and format, and its data in the order S11, S21, S12, S22; then gain, stability, and noise circles follow in a few lines of code, valid only where the measurement was made.
BENCH: Before using any vendor file, plot |S21| and |S12| against frequency and confirm that the gain falls with frequency and stays well above the isolation; that one plot catches a wrong data order, a wrong unit on the option line, and a file for a different bias. When you measure your own device on a VNA, save the file with the bias, temperature, fixture, and calibration in the comments, because six months later nobody will remember them.
P: On the Job
P: Device modeling and characterization engineers measure transistors and passives on probe stations and fixtures, extract S-parameter and noise-parameter files and compact models, and publish the data that design engineers download. Their days are calibration, de-embedding, automation, and checking that a model fits the measurements at every bias and temperature it claims. Employers are foundries, device makers, and large design houses; a master's degree with microwave measurement experience is typical, and the work rewards patience and an eye for numbers that look too good.
