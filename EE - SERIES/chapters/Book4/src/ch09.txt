H1: Chapter 9. Couplers, Dividers, and Hybrids
P: Many RF systems need to split a signal into parts, combine parts into one, or sample a wave traveling in one direction without disturbing it. The structures that do these jobs, power dividers, hybrids, and directional couplers, are built from the transmission lines of Chapter 2 and described by the scattering parameters of Chapter 5. They appear in every balanced amplifier, Doherty combiner, phased-array feed, and reflectometer. This chapter explains the main types and the figures that describe them.
H2: 9.1 What a three-port can and cannot do
P: A lossless, reciprocal three-port cannot be matched at all three ports, a consequence of the unitary S-matrix of Chapter 5. A simple T-junction splits power but reflects from its outputs and couples them to each other. The Wilkinson divider avoids that by adding a resistor between the outputs. It is matched at all ports and isolates the outputs, because the resistor absorbs power only when the outputs are unbalanced; when they are driven equally, no current flows through it and the divider is lossless.
H2: 9.2 The Wilkinson divider
P: The equal-split Wilkinson uses two quarter-wave lines of impedance √2 Z0 from the input to the outputs and a resistor of 2Z0 between the outputs. Each output receives half the power, a 3.01 dB split, in phase. As a combiner it adds two equal, in-phase signals without loss and dissipates the difference if they are unequal, which is why Part II's combiners degrade gracefully when one amplifier fails. Its bandwidth is limited by the quarter-wave lines; multisection versions extend it to an octave or more.
EQ: Wilkinson: Zline = √2 Z0,   R = 2Z0,   |S21|² = |S31|² = ½
H2: Worked Example 9.1
WEP: Design an equal-split Wilkinson divider for a 50 Ω system at 2.45 GHz on a microstrip with an effective permittivity of 3.3. Find the line impedance, the resistor, and the physical line length.
WES: The lines are 50√2 = 70.7 Ω and the resistor is 100 Ω. The guided wavelength is 299.8/(2.45 × √3.3) = 67.4 mm, so each quarter-wave line is 16.8 mm long. Splitting four ways with two stages loses 6.02 dB per output, the same as 3.01 dB twice.
H2: 9.3 Quadrature hybrids
P: The branch-line hybrid is a square of quarter-wave lines: two series arms of Z0/√2 and two shunt arms of Z0. A signal at port 1 divides equally between ports 2 and 3, 90° apart, and port 4 is isolated. Balanced amplifiers use a pair of these hybrids: reflections from the two identical amplifiers cancel at the input and go to the isolated port's load, so the pair presents a good match even when each amplifier alone is poorly matched. The rat-race, or 180° hybrid, does the same with in-phase and anti-phase outputs and serves balanced mixers and baluns.
EQ: branch-line: series arms Z0/√2 = 35.4 Ω,  shunt arms Z0 = 50 Ω  (for Z0 = 50 Ω)
KEY: Key idea. Dividers and hybrids are networks of quarter-wave lines arranged so that waves add at the wanted ports and cancel at the isolated one. Their resistors or isolated loads absorb only the imbalance.
H2: 9.4 The directional coupler
P: A directional coupler samples a small part of a wave traveling one way. Two parallel lines close together couple through their mutual capacitance and inductance; a wave on the main line couples into the adjacent line traveling backward, toward the coupled port. The coupling sets the sample's level, and the directivity, how much more strongly the coupler responds to the forward wave than to the reverse one, sets how well it distinguishes the two. The isolation is the coupling plus the directivity. A coupled-line coupler is designed from its even- and odd-mode impedances, which for a voltage coupling factor C are Z0e = Z0√((1 + C)/(1 − C)) and Z0o = Z0√((1 − C)/(1 + C)).
EQ: isolation = coupling + directivity,   Z0e Z0o = Z0²
H2: Worked Example 9.2
WEP: Find the even- and odd-mode impedances of a 10 dB and a 20 dB coupled-line coupler in 50 Ω.
WES: For 10 dB, C = 10^(−10/20) = 0.316, Z0e = 50√(1.316/0.684) = 69.4 Ω and Z0o = 36.0 Ω. For 20 dB, C = 0.1, Z0e = 55.3 Ω and Z0o = 45.2 Ω. Looser coupling needs the two impedances closer together, which means the lines are farther apart.
H2: Worked Example 9.3
WEP: A reflectometer uses a coupler with 30 dB of directivity to measure a load with a true return loss of 20 dB. What range of return loss might it report?
WES: The true |Γ| is 0.1. The leakage of the forward wave into the reverse port, 30 dB down, is 0.0316 in amplitude and adds with an unknown phase, so the measured |Γ| lies between 0.068 and 0.132: a return loss reading anywhere from 17.6 to 23.3 dB. Measuring a good match needs a coupler whose directivity is well beyond the return loss being measured, or vector error correction as in Chapter 5.
