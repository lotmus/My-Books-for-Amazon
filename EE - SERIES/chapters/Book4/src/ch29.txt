H1: Chapter 29. Mixers and Frequency Planning
P: Tune a 2.45 GHz receiver with a 400 MHz IF and it hears 1.65 GHz just as well, unless something stops it. A mixer can't tell the wanted signal from its image, and it responds to a whole list of other frequencies made from LO harmonics. The frequency plan is the part of radio design you get right on paper, before the hardware exists.
P: Where you meet this: Superheterodyne and zero-IF receivers, upconverters in transmitters, spectrum analyzers, and the spur table in every radio design review.
P: Every superheterodyne and zero-IF radio of Book 5 contains a mixer, the circuit that shifts a signal from one frequency to another by multiplying it with a local oscillator. A mixer is a deliberately nonlinear device, and it produces not only the wanted product but a family of unwanted ones. Choosing the intermediate and oscillator frequencies so that those products fall where filters can remove them is frequency planning, and it is one of the decisions that most shape a radio's architecture. This chapter covers mixer types and figures of merit, the image problem, and the spur chart.
H2: 29.1 What a mixer does
P: Multiplying a signal at fRF by an oscillator at fLO produces components at their sum and difference. The receiver keeps the difference, fIF = |fRF − fLO|; the transmitter keeps the sum or difference that lands at the carrier. A double-balanced mixer, a diode ring or a Gilbert cell of transistors, suppresses the oscillator and the input at its output port and cancels many even-order products. Passive mixers have a conversion loss of about 6 to 7 dB and a noise figure roughly equal to it, but very high intercept points; active Gilbert-cell mixers have conversion gain but lower linearity. The oscillator drive must be large enough to switch the mixer cleanly, and the oscillator's phase noise, Chapter 26's subject, is transferred onto every signal.
EQ: fIF = |fRF − fLO|,   spurs at |m fLO ± n fRF| = fIF
H2: 29.2 The image
P: Two radio frequencies produce the same difference with a given oscillator: the wanted one, and the image on the opposite side of the oscillator at the same distance. A mixer cannot tell them apart, so the image must be removed before the mixer by a filter, or cancelled by an image-reject mixer that uses quadrature paths, as in Book 5's I/Q receiver. A high intermediate frequency puts the image far from the wanted band, where a filter can remove it easily, but makes the later IF filtering harder; a low IF does the opposite. Many receivers use two conversions to get both.
EQ: fimage = fRF − 2fIF  (low-side LO),   fRF + 2fIF  (high-side LO)

H2: Worked Example 29.1
P: Problem. A 2.45 GHz receiver uses a 400 MHz IF with low-side injection. Find the oscillator frequency and the image. An interferer at the image is 40 dB stronger than the wanted signal, which needs 20 dB of SNR. How much image rejection is needed?
P: Solution. fLO = 2.45 − 0.40 = 2.05 GHz, and the image is at 2.05 − 0.40 = 1.65 GHz, 800 MHz below the wanted signal. The image must end up at least 20 dB below the wanted signal after conversion, so the filter and mixer together must reject it by 40 + 20 = 60 dB. At an 800 MHz separation a modest ceramic filter can provide that; with a 40 MHz IF the image would be only 80 MHz away and would need a much sharper filter or an image-reject architecture.
P: Key idea. A mixer converts every input that satisfies |m fLO ± n fRF| = fIF, not only the one you want. Frequency planning places the oscillator and IF so that the image and the strong spurs land where a filter can remove them.
H2: 29.3 The spur chart
P: Because a real mixer is not an ideal multiplier, it converts inputs at every frequency where some combination m fLO ± n fRF equals the IF, with m and n small integers. Each such response, a spur, is weaker for higher orders, but a strong interferer at a low-order spur frequency can still block the receiver. The half-IF spur, with m = n = 2, lies halfway between the wanted signal and the oscillator, where it is hard to filter. A frequency plan lists the low-order spur frequencies, checks them against the filter's response and the expected interferers, and moves the IF if a dangerous spur falls in band.
H2: Worked Example 29.2
P: Problem. For the receiver of Example 29.1, find the radio frequencies between 1 and 4 GHz that convert to the 400 MHz IF for m and n up to 3.
P: Solution. Solving m × 2.05 ± n fRF = ±0.40 gives, for (m, n) = (1, 1), the wanted 2.45 GHz and the image 1.65 GHz. For (1, 2), 1.225 GHz. For (2, 1), 3.70 GHz. For (2, 2), 2.25 GHz and 1.85 GHz; 2.25 GHz is the half-IF spur, only 200 MHz below the wanted signal. For (2, 3), 1.50 and 1.233 GHz; for (3, 2), 3.275 and 2.875 GHz; for (3, 3), 2.183 and 1.917 GHz. The front-end filter must reject the half-IF spur at 2.25 GHz and the (3, 3) response at 2.183 GHz, both near the band; the others are far enough away to be easy.
H2: 29.4 The passive mixer as a switch
P: Multiplying a signal by a square wave at the LO frequency is the same as switching its polarity at that rate, which is what a ring of four diodes or four transistors does in a double-balanced mixer. The square wave's fundamental has an amplitude of 4/π times its peak, and the product contains the sum and difference frequencies. For an ideal commutating mixer, the fraction of the RF power that reaches the wanted IF is (2/π)², a conversion loss of 3.9 dB. Real diode mixers show 6 to 7 dB because of diode resistance, the transformers or baluns, and imperfect switching. The switches themselves are the series and shunt elements of Chapter 19.
EQ: L_conv,ideal = −20 log₁₀(2/π) = 3.92 dB
P: The balance of a double-balanced mixer cancels the LO at the RF and IF ports and the RF at the IF port, so port-to-port isolation, often 30 to 40 dB, depends on how well the baluns and diodes are matched. Because the switches are driven hard by the LO, a passive mixer is quite linear; a rule of thumb for diode mixers is an input third-order intercept about 10 dB above the LO drive.
H2: 29.5 Transmit mixers and direct conversion
P: A transmitter that upconverts with a mixer produces the wanted sideband, the image sideband, and oscillator leakage, all of which the emission mask of Book 7 constrains. Quadrature upconverters cancel the image sideband to the extent that their I and Q paths match, the same imbalance arithmetic as in Book 5's receiver, and they null the oscillator leakage by trimming DC offsets. Direct conversion, with the oscillator at the carrier, avoids the image entirely but places the oscillator in band, where its leakage, pulling by the power amplifier, and radiation must be controlled. Many modern transmitters avoid analog mixers altogether by synthesizing the signal digitally at a high rate and converting it with a fast DAC directly at the carrier.
P: Where this continues. Chapter 51 builds receivers that use these frequency plans: a superheterodyne FM receiver with a 10.7 MHz IF and an RTL-SDR, whose tuner and 8-bit converter show every spur and image of this chapter on a waterfall display.
BENCH: Draw the spur chart for the whole tuning range before choosing the IF, and look hardest at the half-IF spur and at LO harmonics landing in band. Image rejection is shared between the preselector and any image-reject mixer, so assign the budget explicitly. In zero-IF designs, DC offset, LO leakage, and IQ imbalance replace the image problem; plan their calibration from the start.
