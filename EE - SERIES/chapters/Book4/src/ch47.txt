H1: Chapter 47. Acoustic and Micro-Mechanical Filters: SAW, BAW, XBAR, and the Smartphone Front End
P: A 5G phone holds dozens of filters, and almost every one is a piece of vibrating crystal. Sound in a solid travels about a hundred thousand times slower than light, so an acoustic resonator at 2.5 GHz is a few micrometers across, has a Q near a thousand or more, and costs a few cents. Without it, a phone that talks on many bands at once could not exist.
P: Where you meet this: Duplexers and multiplexers in every phone, Wi-Fi coexistence filters, GPS and satellite receivers, the 433.92 MHz resonator in a garage-door remote, and the MEMS oscillators that keep time in watches, cars, and servers.
P: Book 3's "Piezoelectric Films and Acoustic Resonators" derives the acoustic resonator from its material: the Butterworth-Van Dyke model, the coupling coefficient kt², the SAW finger period, temperature drift, and the ladder that turns resonators into a band filter. This chapter designs with them, from a single resonator to a band filter, a duplexer, and the front-end module, and then surveys the acoustic and micromechanical devices beyond the filter.
H2: 47.1 The resonator as a design element
P: The model of Book 3 has a motional branch Lm, Cm, Rm in parallel with the plate capacitance C0. A designer sets C0 for the impedance level, the frequency fs by the film thickness or the finger period, and accepts the coupling kt² and the Q the technology gives. The spacing between series and parallel resonance, (fp − fs)/fp ≈ (4/π²) kt², bounds the widest band filter a technology can build.
EQ: (fp − fs)/fp ≈ (4/π²) kt²,   Cm = C0 [(fp/fs)² − 1],   Lm = 1/(ωs² Cm),   Rm = ωs Lm / Q
H2: Worked Example 47.1
P: Problem. A BAW resonator at 3.5 GHz has kt² = 7 %, C0 = 1 pF, and Q = 1000. Find fp, the motional elements, and the fractional spacing, and decide whether a ladder of such resonators can pass the 5G band n78, 3.3 to 3.8 GHz.
P: Solution. (fp − fs)/fp = (4/π²) × 0.07 = 2.84 %, so fp = 3.5/(1 − 0.0284) = 3.602 GHz. Cm = 1 pF × (1.0292² − 1) = 59.2 fF, Lm = 1/((2π × 3.5×10⁹)² × 59.2×10⁻¹⁵) = 34.9 nH, and Rm = 2π × 3.5×10⁹ × 34.9×10⁻⁹/1000 = 0.77 Ω. n78 is 500 MHz wide, 14 % of its center, five times the resonator's spacing, so no ladder of these resonators can pass it. Wide 5G bands use XBAR resonators in thin lithium niobate, whose coupling of roughly 25 % or more gives a spacing near 10 %, or lumped-element filters in glass and ceramic, or a split of the band.

H2: 47.2 From resonators to a band filter
P: A ladder places series resonators in the signal path and shunt resonators to ground, with the shunt resonators lowered in frequency so that their parallel resonance falls on the series resonators' series resonance. The passband sits between the shunt series resonance, which shorts the path below the band, and the series parallel resonance, which opens it above. The steepest skirt can be placed on whichever side matters by choosing the resonator frequencies; a transmit filter puts it on the side toward the receive band.
H2: Worked Example 47.2
P: Problem. Design a Band 7 transmit filter, 2500 to 2570 MHz, which must reject the receive band, 2620 to 2690 MHz, by at least 50 dB, using AlScN BAW resonators with kt² = 9 % and Q = 1500 in a ladder of four series and three shunt resonators. Choose the resonator frequencies and plate capacitances and report the simulated loss, return loss, and rejection.
P: Solution. The spacing is (4/π²) × 0.09 = 3.65 %. To put the upper skirt between the bands, the series resonators' parallel resonance must sit near 2.63 GHz, so their series resonance is about 2.534 GHz; the shunt resonators are lowered by the spacing to about 2.434 GHz, putting their parallel resonance, 2.526 GHz, near the series fs. Plate capacitances of 0.81 pF in series and 1.74 pF in shunt set the impedance level near 50 Ω. A circuit simulation of the BVD ladder, Figure 47.1, gives 0.40 to 0.60 dB of loss across the transmit band, return loss better than 13 dB, and at least 50 dB of rejection across the receive band, 68 dB at its lower edge. The model omits electrode resistance, package parasitics, and coupling between input and output, which in a real part raise the loss toward 1 to 2 dB and limit the far rejection; the design is a starting point that an electromagnetic and acoustic simulation then refines.
H2: 47.3 Duplexers, multiplexers, and their budget
P: A duplexer joins a transmit and a receive filter at the antenna, each designed to look like an open circuit in the other's band. Two numbers set its isolation. The transmitter's own noise in the receive band must fall below the receiver's noise floor, and the transmitted signal must not overload the receiver's low-noise amplifier.
H2: Worked Example 47.3
P: Problem. A handset amplifier produces +27 dBm in the transmit band and −135 dBm/Hz of noise in the receive band. The receiver's noise figure is 3 dB. Find the isolation the duplexer needs in the receive band to keep the transmitter's noise 10 dB below the receiver's floor, and the transmit-band power at the low-noise amplifier with 50 dB of isolation in the transmit band.
P: Solution. The receiver's floor is −174 + 3 = −171 dBm/Hz, so the transmitter noise must reach the receiver at no more than −181 dBm/Hz, and the receive-band isolation must be at least −135 − (−181) = 46 dB. With 50 dB of transmit-band isolation, the transmitted signal reaches the low-noise amplifier at 27 − 50 = −23 dBm, which its compression point must absorb without blocking. A duplexer of about 50 dB in both bands therefore suits this phone; the 2620 MHz edge is the hard one, because the duplex gap of Band 7 is only 50 MHz, 1.9 % of the frequency.
P: Carrier aggregation transmits or receives on several bands at once, so a phone's antenna port joins several filters in a multiplexer, each of which must look open in all the others' bands. Quadplexers and hexaplexers are built from acoustic filters with phase-shifting networks at the common node, and the front-end module packs them with switches, amplifiers, and tuners.
H2: 47.4 Temperature, guard bands, and technology choice
P: A filter's frequency drifts by its temperature coefficient of frequency, TCF, times the temperature range, and a phone works from about −30 °C to +85 °C, 115 K. With Book 3's values, a lithium tantalate SAW at −35 ppm/K moves 4,025 ppm, 10.3 MHz at 2.57 GHz, and an AlN BAW at −25 ppm/K moves 2,875 ppm, 7.4 MHz, each a large fraction of Band 7's 50 MHz gap once manufacturing spread is added. Temperature-compensated SAW, TC-SAW, which covers the fingers with silicon dioxide, cuts the drift to a third or less, and thin-film SAW on bonded substrates, sold under names such as I.H.P. SAW, raises Q and lowers drift together.
P: The result is a working division of the spectrum. SAW and TC-SAW cover most bands below about 2 GHz and many up to 2.7 GHz at the lowest cost. BAW, in its free-standing FBAR and solidly mounted SMR forms, covers 1.5 to about 6 GHz and the hardest duplex gaps. XBAR and other laterally excited lithium niobate resonators cover the wide 5G bands n77, n78, and n79 and the 5 to 7 GHz Wi-Fi bands. Integrated passive devices in glass and LTCC cover very wide bands where acoustic coupling runs out. In 2022 Murata acquired Resonant, the developer of XBAR, for about $290 million, which signals how much these wide bands are worth.

H2: 47.5 Hobby filters and resonators
P: The cheapest acoustic devices are the ones a hobbyist meets first. Garage-door remotes, weather stations, and key fobs at 433.92 MHz use a one-port SAW resonator as the frequency reference of a one-transistor oscillator, and SAW filters clean up the receivers. On ST-cut quartz, with a velocity near 3,158 m/s, a 433.92 MHz resonator has an electrode period of 7.28 µm and fingers 1.82 µm wide, coarse by modern standards, which is why these parts cost cents. A NanoVNA, Chapter 52, measures their series and parallel resonances directly.
H2: 47.6 Beyond the filter
LI: MEMS timing: silicon resonators replace quartz crystals in many oscillators, with electronic temperature compensation and much better shock resistance; they are now in cars, servers, and wearables.
LI: Thin-film lithium niobate, TFLN: the material of XBAR is also the platform of fast electro-optic modulators, Chapter 50, so acoustic, optical, and RF functions may meet on one chip.
LI: Reconfigurable filters: banks of fixed acoustic filters selected by SOI switches dominate today; tunable acoustic resonators, using ferroelectric films whose coupling changes with bias, are in research.
LI: Acoustic delay lines: a SAW delay line stores microseconds of signal in millimeters, once the basis of radar pulse compression and still used in sensors and identification tags.
LI: Acoustic and modulated circulators: modulating a resonator's properties in time and space breaks reciprocity without a magnet, a research path to integrated circulators, Chapter 15.
P: The outlook is more bands, wider bands, higher frequencies, and more filters per phone, with XBAR and other lithium niobate structures growing fastest and BAW holding the hardest gaps.
P: Key idea. An acoustic filter is a ladder of resonators whose coupling sets the widest band it can pass and whose Q and temperature drift set how close it can sit to its neighbor. Choose SAW, TC-SAW, BAW, or XBAR by band, width, and gap, and size the duplexer from the transmitter's noise and the receiver's floor.
BENCH: Measure an acoustic filter on a VNA with the test fixture de-embedded, and then again with the board heated and cooled across the temperature range; a passband that looks comfortable at room temperature can slide into the duplex gap at +85 °C. Keep the drive level low when you measure the resonances, because a few hundred milliwatts can shift a small SAW resonator by heating, and record the level with the data.
P: On the Job
P: Acoustic filter design engineers turn band specifications into resonator layouts, ladders, and multiplexers, using finite-element acoustic simulation, circuit optimization, and wafer-level measurement. They work closely with process engineers on film thickness and trimming, and with module engineers on how the filter behaves in its package. Employers are the few large filter makers and a growing number of startups; the work suits people with a mix of physics and circuits, and many hold a PhD or a master's in electrical engineering or physics.
