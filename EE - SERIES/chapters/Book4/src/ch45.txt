H1: Chapter 45. Gallium Nitride: Broadband, Doherty, and Space Amplifiers
P: A gallium nitride transistor the size of a grain of rice delivers tens of watts at 3.5 GHz from a 48 V rail, and an amplifier built from a few of them drives a 5G sector, an airborne radar module, or a satellite downlink. The material's high breakdown and high electron velocity make that possible; its traps, its negative gate bias, and its heat make it demanding.
P: Where you meet this: 5G and 4G base-station amplifiers, radar transmit-receive modules, satellite solid-state power amplifiers, electronic warfare and test amplifiers spanning octaves, and the fast power switches of Book 6.
P: Book 3 explains why a GaN high-electron-mobility transistor, HEMT, holds a high voltage and a high current at once: a wide bandgap gives a breakdown field near 3.3 MV/cm, and a two-dimensional electron gas at the AlGaN/GaN interface carries a dense, fast channel. On silicon carbide substrates, which conduct heat well, the result is a power density of about 4 to 6 W per millimeter of gate periphery at 28 V and around 10 W/mm at 48 to 50 V in production devices; laboratory devices have exceeded 30 W/mm at much higher voltages. This chapter designs three amplifiers: a broadband class J stage, a Doherty for a 3.5 GHz base station, and a space amplifier.
H2: 45.1 The device and its load line
P: A GaN device's high supply raises its optimum load toward 50 Ω, Section 22.4, which shrinks the matching networks and widens their bandwidth. The gate periphery follows from the power and the power density; the output capacitance, about 0.3 to 0.5 pF per millimeter, grows with it and sets the bandwidth limit of Chapter 11. GaN HEMTs are depletion-mode devices: they conduct at zero gate voltage and pinch off at a negative one, typically −2 to −4 V, so the gate must be biased negative before the drain is powered.
EQ: gate periphery = P / (power density),    Ropt = (VDD − Vk)²/(2P)
H2: 45.2 A broadband class J stage
H2: Worked Example 45.1
P: Problem. Design a 25 W class J stage covering 1.8 to 3.4 GHz from 28 V with a 3 V knee. Find the periphery at 5 W/mm, Ropt, the fundamental and second-harmonic impedances at the class J point, and the continuous-mode range. Check that the second harmonic of the lowest frequency stays out of the band.
P: Solution. Periphery 25/5 = 5 mm. Ropt = 25²/(2 × 25) = 12.5 Ω. At the class J point Z₁ = 12.5 + j12.5 Ω and Z₂ = −j(3π/8) × 12.5 = −j14.7 Ω; across the continuous mode Z₁ = 12.5(1 + jα) Ω and Z₂ = −j14.7α Ω for α between −1 and +1, all at 70.1 % ideal efficiency with the knee. The output network need only keep the fundamental inside this region and the second harmonic on the matching reactive arc over the band, which a stepped low-pass network achieves over close to an octave. The lowest second harmonic, 3.6 GHz, sits above the top of the band at 3.4 GHz, so the network can treat fundamental and harmonic separately; a band wider than an octave would put harmonics inside the band, which is the practical limit of the method.
H2: 45.3 A Doherty for a 3.5 GHz base station
P: A 5G sector at 3.5 GHz transmits OFDM with a peak-to-average ratio of about 8 dB after crest-factor reduction. An asymmetric Doherty, Sections 23.3 and 41.6, puts its second efficiency peak at that back-off when 20 log(1 + r) = 8 dB.
H2: Worked Example 45.2
P: Problem. Design a Doherty for 20 W average with an 8 dB peak-to-average ratio on 48 V GaN with a 4 V knee. Find r, the peak power, the main amplifier's share and its load at peak, the inverter impedance, the common load, and the peaking amplifier's load at peak. Give the ideal efficiency at the average power.
P: Solution. 1 + r = 10^(8/20) = 2.512, so r = 1.512. The peak is 20 × 10^0.8 = 126.2 W. The main amplifier delivers the fraction 1/(1 + r) at peak, 50.2 W, and with a 44 V swing its load at peak is Rm = 44²/(2 × 50.2) = 19.3 Ω, which is also the inverter's characteristic impedance ZT. The common load is Rm/(1 + r) = 7.67 Ω, and the peaking amplifier, carrying r times the main current at peak, sees 7.67 × (1 + r)/r = 12.7 Ω. At the average power the main amplifier saturates into ZT²/RL = 48.4 Ω, 2.51 times its peak load, and the ideal efficiency is 78.5 % × 44/48 = 72.0 %. Measured amplifiers of this kind reach 45 to 55 % with predistortion; the inverter's bandwidth, the peaking device's gradual turn-on, and the 7.67 Ω common load, transformed to 50 Ω by a further network, account for most of the difference.

H2: 45.4 Traps, memory, and bias sequencing
P: Charge trapped in the buffer and at the surface of a GaN device after a high-voltage excursion reduces the channel current for microseconds to seconds afterward. The effect, called current collapse or drain lag, makes the device's gain depend on the recent history of the signal, and it appears as memory in the predistortion of Chapter 38 and as pulse-to-pulse droop in radar. Field plates and surface passivation reduce it; predistortion models with long memory terms and bias held during gaps in a burst handle what remains.
P: Because the device conducts at zero gate voltage, the supply sequence matters. Power up the negative gate supply first, to pinch-off, then the drain, then raise the gate to its quiescent point; power down in the reverse order. A drain applied with the gate at zero passes a current limited only by the supply and the device, which is usually enough to destroy it.
H2: 45.5 Heat
P: GaN's power density concentrates heat. A 25 W class J stage at 65 % efficiency draws 38.5 W and dissipates 13.5 W in a few square millimeters. With 3.5 K/W from channel to case and the case at 85 °C, the channel runs at 85 + 13.5 × 3.5 = 132 °C, comfortably below the 225 °C maximum many data sheets give, and below the lower temperatures their lifetime curves recommend for decades of service. Silicon carbide substrates make that possible; GaN on silicon, cheaper, conducts heat less well and is used where the power density is lower. GaN on diamond, in development, lowers the thermal resistance further, Chapter 46.
H2: 45.6 Space and radar amplifiers
P: Satellites have carried traveling-wave tubes for decades, Chapter 32, and still do at high power and high frequency, where a tube's efficiency of 60 % and more is hard to beat. GaN solid-state power amplifiers, SSPAs, have taken over at moderate power and in phased arrays, where hundreds of small amplifiers replace one tube and fail gracefully. A space amplifier is designed with extra margin: the channel temperature derated well below the terrestrial limit, radiation tolerance verified, and the supply sequencing built into the hardware. Radar transmit-receive modules run GaN in pulsed class AB or C at duty cycles of a few to about 20 %, which lets each device deliver more peak power than it could continuously.
P: Bipolar version. No bipolar technology competes with GaN at these powers and frequencies; LDMOS is the silicon alternative below about 3.5 GHz, with a similar load line at 28 to 50 V but lower power density and lower efficiency at the high end of its range.
P: Key idea. GaN's high supply raises the load toward 50 Ω and makes broadband and Doherty amplifiers practical at tens of watts. Its traps, negative gate bias, and concentrated heat are the price: predistort for memory, sequence the supplies, and design the thermal path as carefully as the match.
BENCH: Build the bias sequencer before the amplifier and test it alone: with the drain supply connected to a resistive dummy load, confirm that the drain cannot rise until the gate is below pinch-off and that it falls before the gate returns to zero on power-down, including when the gate supply fails first. Then measure the quiescent current drift over the first minutes after power-up; a drift of a few percent is trapping and self-heating settling, and the bias loop or the predistortion must allow for it.
P: On the Job
P: Base-station and radar amplifier engineers design GaN stages and Doherty combiners from device models and load-pull data, tune them on the bench, and work with the predistortion team until the transmitter meets its emission mask at the target efficiency. Defense and space work adds pulsed operation, environmental testing, and documentation measured in binders. Employers include infrastructure vendors, radar and satellite builders, and device makers' application groups; a bachelor's degree with strong RF coursework is a common entry, often with a master's later.
