H1: Chapter 51. Receivers and Transmitters You Can Build
P: A coil, a variable capacitor, a germanium diode, and a long wire will pull a radio station out of the air with no battery at all. A few more parts make a Morse transmitter that can reach across a continent on a watt when propagation and the antenna cooperate, and a twenty-dollar USB dongle can receive signals from aircraft and weather satellites.
P: Where you meet this: Amateur radio, school and maker projects, emergency communication, and the first instruments of a home RF lab.
P: This chapter applies earlier chapters to four builds a reader can finish on a kitchen table: a crystal radio, an FM superheterodyne, a low-power Morse transmitter, and a software-defined receiver. Each comes with the numbers that make it work and a measurement that confirms it. A transmitter needs a license; receivers do not.
H2: 51.1 The crystal radio
P: A crystal radio is a resonator, Chapter 6, a detector, and an antenna. A coil of 230 µH tuned by a 30 to 365 pF variable capacitor resonates from 549 kHz to 1.92 MHz, covering the AM broadcast band, 530 to 1700 kHz, once the antenna's capacitance is added. A germanium or Schottky diode rectifies the signal, and high-impedance earphones convert the audio current to sound. The selectivity is the loaded Q of the tank: tap the antenna and the diode onto the coil to load it less and the stations separate. With a long wire and a good ground a strong local station is loud; the radio is also a sensitive test of the antenna theory of Chapter 13.

H2: 51.2 An FM superheterodyne
P: Broadcast FM, 88 to 108 MHz, uses a peak deviation of 75 kHz and audio up to 15 kHz, so Carson's rule gives a bandwidth of 2(75 + 15) = 180 kHz. A superheterodyne converts the station to an intermediate frequency of 10.7 MHz, where ceramic filters set the selectivity. With the local oscillator above the signal, a station at 88.0 MHz needs an oscillator at 98.7 MHz, and the image, 2 × 10.7 = 21.4 MHz above the station, lands at 109.4 MHz, 1.4 MHz outside the band, where the front-end filter must reject it. Single-chip FM receivers do all this, often with a low IF and digital demodulation, but building the classic version with a mixer, a ceramic filter, and a quadrature detector shows each block of Chapter 29.
H2: 51.3 A Morse transmitter for 40 meters
P: A crystal oscillator, a buffer, and a class C or E output stage, Chapter 43, make a one- to five-watt CW transmitter for the 40 m amateur band; 7.030 MHz is a common low-power calling frequency. Keying the oscillator or the buffer on and off sends Morse code. The output must pass a low-pass filter, because the harmonics of a saturated stage are strong and U.S. rules require spurious emissions below 30 MHz to be at least 43 dB below the fundamental. In the United States a Technician-class amateur licensee may transmit CW in part of the 40 m band, 7.025 to 7.125 MHz; other countries have their own rules and licenses, and nobody may transmit without one.
H2: Worked Example 51.1
P: Problem. Design a seven-element Chebyshev low-pass filter with 0.1 dB ripple and a 7.5 MHz cutoff for a 50 Ω, 7.030 MHz transmitter, with capacitors at the ends. Find the element values and the attenuation at the second and third harmonics.
P: Solution. The prototype values are g = 1.1811, 1.4228, 2.0966, 1.5733, 2.0966, 1.4228, 1.1811. With ωc = 2π × 7.5 MHz, capacitors are g/(ωcR) and inductors gR/ωc: C1 = C7 = 501 pF, L2 = L6 = 1.51 µH, C3 = C5 = 890 pF, L4 = 1.67 µH. The attenuation is 10 log(1 + ε² cosh²(7 cosh⁻¹(f/fc))) with ε² = 10^0.01 − 1: at 14.06 MHz it is 53.1 dB, and at 21.09 MHz 80.6 dB. A saturated stage whose second harmonic is 20 dB below the carrier ends near 73 dB below, well inside the 43 dB rule. Standard values of 470 pF plus 33 pF, 820 pF plus 68 pF, and toroidal inductors wound and checked on a NanoVNA make it buildable.
H2: 51.4 The software-defined receiver
P: An RTL-SDR dongle combines a tuner chip, the R820T2 in most versions, covering about 24 to 1766 MHz, with an 8-bit ADC sampling at up to about 2.4 million samples per second without dropped samples. Eight bits give an ideal dynamic range of 6.02 × 8 + 1.76 = 49.9 dB in the full bandwidth, and filtering to a 12.5 kHz voice channel adds a processing gain of 10 log(2.4×10⁶/12.5×10³) = 22.8 dB. With a laptop it receives FM broadcast, aircraft, marine, and amateur signals, ADS-B at 1090 MHz, and weather-satellite images at 137 MHz. A propagation monitor that logs the signal strength of distant beacons through the day turns the receiver into an instrument for Chapter 14. Book 5, Communications, Wireless, and SDR, develops the receiver architecture in "The 2026 Band Plan and the Software-Defined Receiver", and Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, uses SDRs as instruments in "The Budget Bench: NanoVNA, tinySA, SDRs, and Low-Cost Scopes and Supplies".

P: Key idea. Every radio a reader can build is a resonator, an amplifier, a mixer, a filter, and a detector arranged in a familiar order; the numbers of earlier chapters size each one, and a transmitter adds the duty of a clean spectrum and a license.
BENCH: Before a homebuilt transmitter ever meets an antenna, connect it to a 50 Ω dummy load through an attenuator rated for its power, and look at its output on a spectrum analyzer or a tinySA: confirm the carrier frequency, the harmonics below the 43 dB line, and the absence of spurious oscillations when the key is up. A transmitter that looks clean on a scope can still oscillate at 150 MHz.
P: On the Job
P: Amateur radio can provide hands-on experience with antennas, transmitters, interference, and measurement, and that experience can help in RF work. It is one route among several: coursework, technician and test roles, and adjacent engineering experience can also build relevant skills.
