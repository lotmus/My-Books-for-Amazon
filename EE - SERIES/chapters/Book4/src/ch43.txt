H1: Chapter 43. A Design Cookbook, Class by Class
P: An amplifier class on a page is a waveform; an amplifier on a board is a transistor, a supply, a load, and four or five parts around it. This chapter turns each class into a complete recipe with every value computed, from a class A driver to a 10 W class E stage that charges a phone across a desk.
P: Where you meet this: IoT and LoRa transmitters, amateur HF and VHF radios, wireless chargers at 6.78 MHz, induction heaters, broadcast and base-station amplifiers, and every lab bench where an amplifier must be built rather than bought.
P: Chapters 22, 35, and 41 derived the classes; this chapter designs with them. Each recipe follows the same steps: state the specification, choose the class from the signal, compute the optimum load from the supply and the knee, design the match and the harmonic control, set the bias, and check the efficiency and the device stresses. The designs are CMOS or FET first, as in Chapter 42, and each closes with what changes for a bipolar device. The numbers assume ideal waveforms and stated knees; load-pull and simulation refine them.
H2: 43.1 Class A: a driver that needs no match
P: A class A stage conducts all the time and is the most linear, at a theoretical 50 % efficiency with an inductor feed. Its natural home is the driver of a larger stage and the output of a test source. A 10 mW CMOS driver on a 1.2 V supply with a 0.2 V knee has Ropt = (1.2 − 0.2)²/(2 × 0.01) = 50 Ω, so it drives a 50 Ω line directly. Its DC current equals the peak fundamental current, 2P/V1 = 20 mA, so it draws 24 mW and its efficiency is 41.7 %, the 50 % ideal times the knee factor 1.0/1.2. Bipolar version: the bias becomes a mirror setting about 20 mA, the knee drops to about 0.2 to 0.3 V, and the stage's higher transconductance gives more gain for the same current.
H2: 43.2 Class AB: a 915 MHz LoRa amplifier
P: Class AB biases the device slightly on, typically at 5 to 20 % of its peak current, to avoid class B's crossover distortion while keeping most of its efficiency. It is the default for any signal whose envelope varies, and it works saturated for constant-envelope signals such as LoRa chirps.
H2: Worked Example 43.1
P: Problem. Design the output stage of a 915 MHz LoRa transmitter for +22 dBm from 3.3 V with a 0.3 V knee. Find Ropt, a low-pass L-network to 50 Ω, the ideal class B efficiency at saturation, and the frequency the harmonic filter must reject most.
P: Solution. 22 dBm is 158 mW. Ropt = 3.0²/(2 × 0.158) = 28.4 Ω. The L-network has Q = √(50/28.4 − 1) = 0.872: a series inductance on the device side of 0.872 × 28.4 = 24.8 Ω, 4.31 nH at 915 MHz, and a shunt capacitance across the 50 Ω side of 50/0.872 = 57.3 Ω, 3.03 pF. The ideal class B efficiency is 78.5 % × 3.0/3.3 = 71.4 %; a practical AB stage at saturation reaches 50 to 60 %. The strongest harmonic of a single-ended stage is the second, at 1830 MHz, and the low Q of this match filters it only weakly, so a separate low-pass filter follows. Bipolar version: an HBT stage would bias through a ballasted mirror with a diode-connected reference on the same die, its lower knee would raise Ropt slightly, to 30.3 Ω at a 0.2 V knee, and its exponential transfer would compress more abruptly near saturation.
H2: 43.3 Class B push-pull: 100 W on 13.8 V at HF
P: A push-pull pair of class B devices, each conducting half the cycle, combines two half-sine currents into a full sine in a center-tapped transformer and cancels the even harmonics. Amateur HF transceivers have long built their 100 W stage this way from the 13.8 V vehicle and station supply. With a 1 V knee each drain swings 12.8 V about the supply, so the drain-to-drain voltage amplitude is 25.6 V and the drain-to-drain load for 100 W is Rdd = 2 × 12.8²/100 = 3.28 Ω. A transmission-line transformer with a 1:4 turns ratio, 1:16 in impedance, presents 50/16 = 3.13 Ω, close enough to deliver 2 × 12.8²/3.125 = 105 W. The even harmonics cancel to the extent the halves match; the third harmonic does not, so each band has a switched low-pass filter that brings the spurious emissions to the 43 dB below the fundamental that U.S. amateur rules require below 30 MHz. Bipolar version: the classic bipolar push-pull needs a bias diode on the heat sink to track VBE, and emitter resistors of a fraction of an ohm against thermal runaway.
EQ: push-pull: Rdd = 2 (VDD − Vk)² / P,    1:n turns ⇒ Rdd = RL / n²
H2: 43.4 Class C: a narrowband FM transmitter
P: Class C narrows the conduction angle and accepts a lower gain for a higher efficiency, 89.7 % ideal at 120°. It suits constant-envelope FM and CW transmitters and the peaking device of a Doherty. For 25 W at VHF from 13.8 V with a 1 V knee, V1 = 12.8 V and the fundamental current is 2 × 25/12.8 = 3.91 A. At 120° the fundamental is 0.391 of the peak current, so the device must deliver a 9.99 A peak, against 7.81 A in class B for the same power: class C needs a bigger device for the same output. Its DC current is 2.18 A, so it draws 30.1 W and runs at 83.2 %, the ideal 89.7 % times 12.8/13.8. The load is V1/I1 = 3.28 Ω, the same as class B, because the load line depends on the fundamental. Bipolar version: a bipolar class C stage is often biased at zero, with the base returned through an RF choke, and the drive itself sets the conduction angle.
H2: 43.5 Class D at 6.78 MHz
P: A voltage-mode class D half bridge switches its output node between the supply and ground and passes the fundamental to the load through a series resonator. The 6.78 MHz band is used by magnetic-resonance wireless chargers. From 24 V the fundamental amplitude is (2/π) × 24 = 15.3 V, so a 10 W design needs R = 15.3²/(2 × 10) = 11.7 Ω. With 100 pF of output capacitance per switch, the node capacitance of 200 pF is charged and discharged every cycle, dissipating CVDD²f = 200 pF × 24² × 6.78 MHz = 0.78 W, and the efficiency counting only that loss is 10/10.78 = 92.8 %. Zero-voltage switching with a slightly inductive load and a dead time recovers most of it. Bipolar version: bipolar switches are rarely used at 6.78 MHz, because storage time limits how fast they turn off.
H2: 43.6 Class E at 6.78 MHz: a 10 W wireless-power stage
H2: Worked Example 43.2
P: Problem. Design a class E stage for 10 W at 6.78 MHz from 24 V. Find the load resistance, shunt capacitance, excess inductance, DC and peak switch currents, and the peak switch voltage, and choose a voltage rating for the transistor.
P: Solution. R = 0.5768 × 24²/10 = 33.2 Ω. C = 0.1836/(2π × 6.78×10⁶ × 33.2) = 130 pF, which may include the switch's own output capacitance. The excess reactance is 1.1525 × 33.2 = 38.3 Ω, 0.90 µH. The DC current is 10/24 = 0.417 A and the peak switch current 2.862 × 0.417 = 1.19 A. The switch peaks at 3.562 × 24 = 85.5 V, leaving 64.5 V of nominal headroom with a 150 V-rated device. The simulation in Figure 43.1 shows the drain voltage returning to zero with zero slope as the switch closes. A wireless charger's coil coupling changes the load, so practical designs use load-insensitive class E variants or a matching network that is retuned as the receiver moves. Bipolar version: no change in the network; the switch is a GaN or silicon MOSFET, because a bipolar switch's storage time would spoil the zero-voltage timing.

H2: 43.7 Class F at 2.45 GHz
H2: Worked Example 43.3
P: Problem. Design a 10 W class F stage at 2.45 GHz on 28 V GaN with a 3 V knee, controlling the second and third harmonics. Find V1, I1, and the fundamental load, the ideal drain efficiency, and the lengths of a quarter-wave line and a third-harmonic open stub on the book's reference microstrip, εeff = 2.79.
P: Solution. With the third harmonic set for a maximally flat voltage, V1 = 1.155 × (28 − 3) = 28.9 V, and with a half-sine current I1 = 2 × 10/28.9 = 0.693 A, so the fundamental load is R1 = 28.9/0.693 = 41.7 Ω, close to 50 Ω. The efficiency is ½ × (28.9/28) × (π/2) = 81.0 %, against 70.1 % for class B with the same knee. The guided wavelength at 2.45 GHz is 7.33 cm, so a quarter-wave line from the drain to a bypassed supply point is 1.83 cm long and shorts every even harmonic at the drain, and an open stub of λ/12, 0.61 cm, a quarter wave at 7.35 GHz, shorts the third harmonic where it joins and is placed so that the drain sees an open there. Both lengths are referred to the device's internal current source, so its output capacitance and package inductance shorten them in practice. Bipolar version: the HBT's collector-base capacitance and lower breakdown limit the swing; a bipolar class F stage is built at lower voltage, with the same terminations.
P: Inverse class F swaps the terminations: open at the second harmonic, short at the third. Its voltage approaches a half sine with a peak of about π times the swing and its current a square wave, which suits high-breakdown GaN. With infinitely many harmonics the load is R = (π²/4)(VDD − Vk)²/(2P); for the same 10 W design it is 2.47 × 31.25 = 77.1 Ω, and the drain peaks near 3 + π × 25 = 82 V, so the device needs a breakdown well above 100 V. Class J, which needs only a reactive second harmonic, is designed in Chapter 45 as a broadband GaN stage.
EQ: class F (2nd, 3rd): V1 = 1.155 (VDD − Vk),  I1 = 2P/V1;    inverse F: R = (π²/4)(VDD − Vk)² / (2P),  vpk ≈ π (VDD − Vk)
P: Key idea. Every recipe starts from the same load line and the same five steps; the class only decides the waveforms, the harmonic terminations, and the stresses. Choose by the signal and the frequency, then check the peak voltage and current against the device before anything is built.
BENCH: Before a class E or class F prototype is powered, measure the drain-to-ground capacitance of the board with the device fitted but unpowered, and compare it with the design's shunt C or the stub's assumed termination; a few picofarads of layout capacitance at 6.78 MHz and a fraction of a picofarad at 2.45 GHz move the zero-voltage point. Bring the supply up slowly with a current-limited source and watch the drain on a high-voltage probe: a waveform that rings below zero or peaks above 4 VDD means the tuning is off, not that the device is faulty.
P: On the Job
P: RF hardware engineers at radio, IoT-module, and wireless-power companies design discrete amplifiers from data sheets, simulation, and bench measurements. They choose devices, design matching networks, tune boards with network and spectrum analyzers, and complete emissions testing. Amateur radio and hands-on projects can build useful experience. Other preparation includes RF coursework and measurement, or experience in technician, test, or adjacent engineering roles. Employers set their own degree requirements.
