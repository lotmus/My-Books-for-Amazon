H1: Chapter 44. Gallium Arsenide: The Handset Amplifier and the pHEMT
P: Open almost any phone and the cellular power amplifier is not silicon. It is a few square millimeters of gallium arsenide heterojunction bipolar transistors, with a silicon controller beside it, and it has held that place against twenty years of CMOS progress. The reasons are a breakdown voltage that suits a lithium-ion battery, a gain that suits a two-stage module, and ruggedness into a hand-held antenna.
P: Where you meet this: Cellular power-amplifier modules in phones and modems, Wi-Fi front-end modules, low-noise amplifiers in GPS and satellite receivers, and the pHEMT amplifiers and switches in microwave links and test equipment.
P: Book 3's "GaAs, GaN, and InP Are Not One Transistor" explains the devices: the InGaP/GaAs heterojunction bipolar transistor, HBT, and the pseudomorphic high-electron-mobility transistor, pHEMT. This chapter designs with them. The handset amplifier comes first, because it is the largest market for gallium arsenide, and the pHEMT low-noise amplifier second, because it is where the material's low noise matters most.
H2: 44.1 Why the handset amplifier stayed in gallium arsenide
P: An InGaP/GaAs HBT for handset amplifiers has a cutoff frequency near 30 GHz, a collector-emitter breakdown BVCEO of about 15 to 20 V, and a collector-base breakdown BVCBO of 30 V or more. Against a battery that runs from about 3.0 to 4.4 V, that leaves room for the swing to grow several times under mismatch without breakdown, which a fine-line CMOS device lacks. The HBT's current density is high, so the output device is small, and its transconductance is high, so two stages give the 28 to 30 dB a module needs. The semi-insulating substrate keeps the passive losses low, and the process is mature and cheap per die. CMOS has won the Wi-Fi and Bluetooth amplifier, Chapter 42, but the cellular one, with its higher power, higher linearity, and harder mismatch requirements, has mostly stayed in GaAs.
H2: 44.2 Sizing the output stage
P: A cellular handset in power class 3 must deliver 23 dBm at the antenna. The duplexer, switch, and tuner between the amplifier and the antenna cost 2 to 4 dB, and the linear signal of LTE and 5G needs a few decibels of back-off from saturation, so the output stage is sized for about 31 dBm saturated. The load line and the area follow from the supply, the knee, and a current density the emitter can carry for years.
H2: Worked Example 44.1
P: Problem. Size a 1.95 GHz HBT output stage for 31 dBm saturated from 3.4 V with a 0.3 V knee in class B. Find Ropt, the peak collector current, and the emitter area at 0.2 mA/µm². Design a two-section match to 50 Ω and give each section's Q.
P: Solution. 31 dBm is 1.26 W. Ropt = 3.1²/(2 × 1.26) = 3.82 Ω. The fundamental current is 2 × 1.26/3.1 = 0.812 A and the class B peak twice that, 1.62 A, which needs 1.62/(0.2 × 10⁻³) = 8,100 µm² of emitter, for example 100 fingers of about 81 µm². A single L-section from 3.82 Ω to 50 Ω would need Q = √(50/3.82 − 1) = 3.48. Two sections through the geometric mean, √(3.82 × 50) = 13.8 Ω, need Q = √(13.8/3.82 − 1) = 1.62 each, wider in bandwidth and less lossy, which is why modules use two or three sections, often with the first one on the die or the laminate right at the collector.
H2: 44.3 Bias, ballast, and thermal runaway
P: A bipolar transistor's base-emitter voltage falls by about 1.4 to 2 mV for every kelvin at constant current, so a finger that runs hotter than its neighbors draws more current, heats further, and can take the whole current, a failure called current hogging. A ballast resistor in each emitter or base breaks the loop. A finger with thermal resistance Rth that dissipates IcVCE warms by RthIcVCE, which would raise its current as if its VBE had been increased by φRthIcVCE, with φ the VBE temperature coefficient. The emitter resistance and the junction's own small-signal resistance VT/Ic oppose that, so the finger is stable when the total exceeds the thermal term.
EQ: Re + VT/Ic > φ Rth VCE    ⇒    Re > φ Rth VCE − VT/Ic
H2: Worked Example 44.2
P: Problem. The output stage of Worked Example 44.1 has 100 fingers, each with Rth = 2,000 K/W, φ = 1.4 mV/K, and VCE = 3.4 V. Find the emitter ballast needed at the peak current per finger, and the equivalent base ballast for a current gain of 80.
P: Solution. Per finger the peak current is 1.62 A/100 = 16.2 mA, so VT/Ic = 0.0259/0.0162 = 1.59 Ω, and φRthVCE = 1.4×10⁻³ × 2,000 × 3.4 = 9.52 Ω. Re must exceed 9.52 − 1.59 = 7.9 Ω; at the class B DC current per finger, 1.62/π/100 = 5.2 mA, the requirement relaxes to 4.5 Ω. A designer would choose about 9 Ω. In the emitter that costs 16.2 mA × 9 Ω = 0.15 V of swing and some gain, so modules usually put the ballast in the base, where β × Re = 80 × 9 = 720 Ω per finger has the same effect with no loss of collector swing, at the cost of bias drop that the bias circuit must supply.
P: The bias circuit is a current mirror: a diode-connected reference transistor on the same die, at the same temperature, sets the base voltage of the power stage so that the quiescent current tracks temperature and process. A silicon controller beside the GaAs die sets the reference current, switches power modes, and handles the mobile industry processor interface, MIPI RFFE, through which the transceiver commands the module.
H2: 44.4 Linearity, tracking, and harmonic terminations
P: Handset amplifiers run in class AB with the second harmonic shorted near the collector, a step toward class F that raises efficiency a few points. Linearity is specified as adjacent-channel leakage ratio, ACLR, with the error-vector magnitude of the modulated signal. Average-power tracking with a DC-DC converter lowers the collector supply at low power, and envelope tracking, Chapter 36, follows the envelope on high-bandwidth signals; both set the supply below the battery and so use the HBT's breakdown margin for efficiency rather than for headroom.
H2: 44.5 Ruggedness into a hand-held antenna
P: A hand on a phone detunes its antenna, and a module must survive a 10:1 VSWR at every phase at the highest battery voltage. The collector swing can grow by 1 + |Γ|, here 1 + 9/11 = 1.82 times, so with a 4.35 V battery and a 0.3 V knee the collector peaks near 4.35 + 1.82 × 4.05 = 11.7 V, well below a BVCEO of 15 to 20 V. Breakdown at high current is lower than the DC rating suggests, because avalanche and heating interact, so modules add voltage-clamping diode stacks at the collector and limit the drive when a mismatch is detected, Chapter 31.
H2: 44.6 The pHEMT: low-noise amplifiers and switches
P: The pHEMT carries its current in a two-dimensional electron gas with no base current and therefore no shot-noise source at the input, so its minimum noise figure is tenths of a decibel at a few gigahertz and its noise resistance is small. Enhancement-mode pHEMTs, which turn on at a positive gate voltage, run from a single supply, and they are the input devices of most GPS, satellite, and microwave-link low-noise amplifiers. Worked Example 28.3 designs with one at 2 GHz, and Chapter 48 reads its Touchstone file and presents its optimum noise source. pHEMTs once made every handset switch; RF SOI has taken most of that market, Chapter 46, but pHEMT switches and amplifiers remain in microwave and millimeter-wave modules, and pHEMT power amplifiers serve Wi-Fi 5 and 6 GHz front ends where their gain at high frequency helps.
P: Bipolar version, in reverse. Because this chapter's amplifier is already bipolar, the comparison runs the other way: a CMOS version of Worked Example 44.1 would need a stack, Section 42.2, because no single fine-line device survives an 11.7 V peak, and it would need a separate high-voltage protection scheme where the HBT relies on its breakdown margin.
P: Key idea. The GaAs HBT keeps the handset amplifier because its breakdown suits a battery with margin to spare under mismatch, its current density makes a small output stage, and ballasting tames its thermal runaway. Size it by the load line, ballast every finger, and qualify it at a 10:1 VSWR.
BENCH: On a module evaluation board, measure the quiescent current against temperature from −30 °C to +85 °C before any RF test; a mirror that does not track will show up as a drifting gain and ACLR long before it shows up as a failure. Run the ruggedness test with a mismatch tuner at every phase in steps of 15° at the highest battery voltage and maximum drive, and measure the output power at 50 Ω after each step: a module that survives but loses a few tenths of a decibel has damaged fingers.
P: On the Job
P: Power-amplifier module engineers at front-end companies design and qualify the GaAs dies, laminates, and controllers that go into every phone. Their days alternate between harmonic-balance simulation, layout reviews of the module substrate, and bench work on ACLR, efficiency, and ruggedness across bands and temperatures. A customer's antenna, measured in a real phone, often drives the last month of a project. A master's degree with RF circuit work is common, and the field values people who are equally comfortable with a load-pull bench and a thermal simulation.
