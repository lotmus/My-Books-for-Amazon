H1: Chapter 38. Digital Predistortion
P: Chapter 23 showed that a linear amplifier must back off from compression, and that back-off costs efficiency. Digital predistortion recovers much of that efficiency. A digital block ahead of the transmitter distorts the signal in exactly the opposite way to the amplifier, so that the cascade of the two is linear. Every modern base station and many handsets use it. This chapter explains the model, the observation path that trains it, and the efficiency it buys.
H2: 38.1 The idea
P: If the amplifier compresses its peaks, the predistorter expands them; if the amplifier advances the phase at high amplitude, the predistorter retards it. In the simplest, memoryless case, the predistorter is a lookup table indexed by the input amplitude, holding the inverse of the AM-AM and AM-PM curves measured in Chapter 37. The correction cannot exceed the amplifier's saturated power: no predistorter can make an amplifier deliver more than its maximum, so the peaks of the signal must still fit below saturation, usually with help from crest-factor reduction.
H2: 38.2 Memory and the memory polynomial
P: Wideband amplifiers have memory: their output depends on recent inputs as well as the present one, because of bias-network impedances, thermal changes, and trapping in GaN. A memoryless table then leaves residual distortion that grows with bandwidth. The memory polynomial models the amplifier, or its inverse, as a sum over nonlinear orders k and delays q, with complex coefficients fitted to measured data. With K orders and M delays it has K × M coefficients, a small number that a least-squares solver updates continuously.
EQ: y(n) = Σ_k Σ_q a_kq x(n − q) |x(n − q)|^(k − 1)
H2: Worked Example 38.1
WEP: A memory polynomial uses orders k = 1 to 5 and memory depth M = 4. How many complex coefficients does it have, and how many if only odd orders are kept?
WES: All orders give 5 × 4 = 20 coefficients. Odd orders only, k = 1, 3, 5, give 3 × 4 = 12. Including even orders usually improves the fit for real amplifiers, at a modest cost in computation.
H2: 38.3 The observation receiver
P: To fit the coefficients, the transmitter samples its own output through a coupler and an observation receiver, aligns the samples in time and amplitude with the input, and solves for the coefficients. Indirect learning fits the inverse directly, treating the amplifier's output as the input of a postdistorter whose coefficients are then copied to the predistorter. Because third-order distortion spreads the spectrum to three times the signal bandwidth, and fifth-order to five times, the observation path must capture several times the signal bandwidth, which sets the speed of its converters.
EQ: B_observation ≈ K × B_signal  (K = highest odd order corrected)
H2: Worked Example 38.2
WEP: A 100 MHz NR carrier is linearized up to fifth order. Find the observation bandwidth needed.
WES: Fifth-order products extend over five times the signal bandwidth, so the observation receiver must capture about 500 MHz, and its ADC must sample at least that fast for complex sampling. This is one reason DPD for 400 MHz millimeter-wave carriers is difficult, and why those systems often use simpler schemes.
KEY: Key idea. Digital predistortion applies the inverse of the amplifier's nonlinearity and memory, fitted continuously from an observation receiver that captures several times the signal bandwidth. It linearizes the amplifier so that it can run closer to saturation.
H2: 38.4 What DPD buys
P: Without DPD a base-station amplifier might need 9 dB of back-off from its peak to meet ACLR; with DPD and crest-factor reduction, perhaps 6 dB. For an ideal class B amplifier, efficiency at back-off falls as π/4 times the voltage ratio, so the 3 dB change is substantial, and in a Doherty amplifier the gain is even greater because DPD corrects the Doherty's own nonlinearity, which would otherwise rule it out for linear signals.
EQ: η_classB(BO) = (π/4) × 10^(−BO/20)
H2: Worked Example 38.3
WEP: Compare the ideal class B efficiency at 9 dB and at 6 dB of back-off.
WES: At 9 dB, η = 0.785 × 0.355 = 27.9%. At 6 dB, η = 0.785 × 0.501 = 39.4%. DC power for the same average output falls by a factor of 39.4/27.9 = 1.41, a 29% reduction in the power the station draws for its amplifiers, before any Doherty improvement.
H2: 38.5 Practical limits
P: DPD adds a digital block, an observation receiver, and training software, which pay for themselves in base stations but are marginal in low-power devices. It cannot correct noise or distortion that originates before the amplifier, and it must track changes in temperature, supply, and load, the antenna mismatch of Chapter 31 included. Envelope-tracking transmitters of Chapter 36 depend on it, because the supply modulation itself creates distortion that only DPD removes.
