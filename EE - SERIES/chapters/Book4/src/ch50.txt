H1: Chapter 50. Microwave Photonics: Radio over Light and Optical Phased Arrays
P: A radio signal can ride on a laser beam through kilometers of fiber that weighs grams and loses a fraction of a decibel per kilometer, where coaxial cable would lose everything. The same marriage of optics and microwaves steers radar beams without squint, generates oscillators with record-low phase noise, and points the laser beams of solid-state lidar with no moving parts.
P: Where you meet this: Radio-over-fiber links in cellular networks and stadiums, antenna remoting in radio telescopes and ships, wideband phased-array radar, optoelectronic oscillators, and optical phased arrays for lidar.
P: Book 3's "Lasers, VCSELs, APDs, SPADs, and Image Pixels" covers the devices: lasers, modulators, and photodiodes. This chapter uses them as RF components with gain, noise, and bandwidth, in the language of the rest of this book.
H2: 50.1 The analog optical link
P: The simplest link modulates the intensity of a laser with the RF signal, carries it over fiber, and recovers the RF current in a photodiode. With a continuous laser and a Mach-Zehnder modulator, MZM, biased at quadrature, the small-signal link gain depends on the average photocurrent Idc, the modulator's half-wave voltage Vπ, and the resistances at the two ends. For a modulator with a matched input and a photodiode driving a load R directly, the gain is (πIdcR/Vπ)²; a matched photodiode output gives a quarter of it, 6 dB less. The gain rises with the square of the photocurrent, so a link is a power budget in optical milliwatts as much as an RF one.
EQ: G = (π Idc R / Vπ)²   (quadrature MZM, matched modulator input, photodiode into R)
H2: Worked Example 50.1
P: Problem. A link uses an MZM with Vπ = 3 V and a photodiode current of 10 mA into 50 Ω. Find its gain, the gain with a matched detector, and the photocurrent needed for 0 dB.
P: Solution. G = (π × 0.01 × 50/3)² = 0.524² = 0.274, −5.6 dB; with a matched detector, −11.6 dB. For 0 dB, πIdcR/Vπ = 1 gives Idc = 3/(π × 50) = 19.1 mA. Links reach positive gain with lower-Vπ modulators, thin-film lithium niobate devices reach about a volt, or with more optical power, limited by what the photodiode can absorb without compressing. The noise figure of such a link is typically 20 to 40 dB unless the optical power is high, so an RF amplifier usually precedes it.
H2: 50.2 Radio over fiber in networks
P: Cellular networks carry radio between baseband units and antennas over fiber, mostly as digitized samples, the CPRI and eCPRI interfaces, and in some systems as analog radio over fiber, which keeps the remote unit simple. Distributed antenna systems in stadiums and buildings, and antenna remoting for radio telescopes, use analog links where bandwidth and phase stability matter more than noise figure. Book 5, Communications, Wireless, and SDR, covers the network side in "Massive MIMO and Beamforming".
H2: 50.3 True time delay and beam squint
P: A phased array steered with phase shifters, Chapter 8, sets a phase that is correct at one frequency. At another frequency the same phase points the beam elsewhere, an effect called beam squint, which limits the bandwidth of wideband arrays. A true time delay, TTD, is correct at every frequency. Optical fiber provides delays of picoseconds to microseconds with low loss and no dispersion in the RF sense, so photonic beamformers switch lengths of fiber, or tune dispersion, to set each element's delay.
EQ: phase steering: sin θ(f) = sin θ0 · f0 / f,    TTD: τ = d sin θ0 / c,   fiber length = c τ / ng
H2: Worked Example 50.2
P: Problem. An array with half-wavelength spacing at 10 GHz, 15 mm, is steered to 30° by phase shifters. Find the beam direction at 11 GHz. Find the true time delay per element that holds 30° at all frequencies and its length in fiber with group index 1.468.
P: Solution. The phase shifters hold 90° per element. At 11 GHz, sin θ = 0.5 × 10/11 = 0.455, θ = 27.0°, a squint of 3°, larger than the beamwidth of a large array. The delay per element is τ = 0.015 × 0.5/3×10⁸ = 25.0 ps, 5.1 mm of fiber. A 16-element array needs delays up to 15 × 25 = 375 ps, 7.7 cm of fiber, easily switched in steps; the same delays in coaxial cable would weigh far more and lose more at 10 GHz.

H2: 50.4 Optoelectronic oscillators and photonic signal generation
P: An optoelectronic oscillator, OEO, closes a loop through a modulator, a kilometer or more of fiber, a photodiode, an amplifier, and a filter. The fiber's long delay acts as a resonator with an enormous effective Q, so OEOs reach phase noise at 10 GHz far below quartz-referenced synthesizers at offsets of a few kilohertz and above. Beating two lasers on a fast photodiode generates millimeter and terahertz signals, and optical frequency combs, locked to optical references, give the lowest-noise microwave signals yet made. These are laboratory and specialist instruments; Chapter 26's oscillators remain the working tools.
H2: 50.5 Optical phased arrays
P: An optical phased array, OPA, is the antenna array of Chapter 8 at a wavelength of 1550 nm, built in silicon photonics: a row of waveguide antennas, each with a thermo-optic or electro-optic phase shifter, steers a beam in one direction, and tuning the laser wavelength steers it in the other through grating emitters. The grating-lobe rule is the same as for microwave arrays, d < λ/(1 + sin θmax), so steering to ±30° without grating lobes needs a pitch below 1.033 µm, close to the size of a single waveguide. That is why OPAs with large apertures use non-uniform spacing or accept a limited field of view. OPAs are a candidate for solid-state lidar; most lidars in production still use mechanical or MEMS scanning, Book 3's "Sensing Devices: MEMS, Hall, Gas, and Quantum" covers the detectors they use.
P: Key idea. A microwave photonic link is an RF component with a gain set by optical power and modulator voltage; its strengths are low loss over distance, wide bandwidth, and true time delay, and its weakness is noise figure. Optical phased arrays follow the same array rules as microwave ones at a wavelength two thousand times smaller.
BENCH: Optical power at a photodiode is invisible and can be enough to damage an eye or the detector. Measure it with an optical power meter before connecting the photodiode, keep connectors capped, and clean every fiber end face with the proper tool before mating it; a speck of dust on a connector can cost several decibels of RF gain, because the link gain falls as the square of the optical loss.
P: On the Job
P: Microwave photonics engineers work across optics and RF at test-equipment makers, fiber-network companies, research laboratories, and defense or space contractors. They design links and beamformers, characterize modulators and photodiodes, and model noise across both domains. Useful preparation includes RF, optics, measurement, and noise analysis. Graduate study can help for research-heavy roles; relevant design and laboratory experience can also be valuable.
