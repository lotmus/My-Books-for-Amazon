H1: Chapter 16. Measuring Antennas: Ranges and Gain
P: Chapter 7 defined an antenna's gain and pattern, and every link budget in the series uses those numbers. They come from measurement. Measuring an antenna is harder than measuring a circuit because the quantity of interest exists only at a distance, in the far field, and because the room itself reflects. This chapter explains the far-field condition, the ranges built to satisfy it, and the two standard ways to measure gain.
H2: 16.1 The far-field distance
P: Near an antenna, the field has reactive components and its shape changes with distance. Far away, the wave from every part of the aperture arrives nearly in phase, and the pattern stops changing. The usual criterion is that the phase difference between a ray from the center of the aperture and a ray from its edge be no more than π/8, or 22.5°. That gives the far-field distance in terms of the largest dimension D of the antenna, and it grows with the square of the size.
EQ: R_ff = 2D² / λ
H2: Worked Example 16.1
WEP: Find the far-field distance of a horn with a 0.2 m aperture at 10 GHz and of a 1 m dish at 30 GHz.
WES: At 10 GHz, λ = 0.03 m, so R_ff = 2 × 0.04/0.03 = 2.67 m, which fits indoors. At 30 GHz, λ = 0.01 m, so R_ff = 2 × 1/0.01 = 200 m, which does not. Large, high-frequency antennas are measured outdoors, in a compact range, or with near-field scanning.
H2: 16.2 Ranges
P: An anechoic chamber lined with pyramidal absorber, reflecting −40 dB or better at the frequencies of use, provides a far-field range for small antennas. The region where the incident field is uniform enough for measurement is the quiet zone. A compact range uses a large, precise reflector to convert the spherical wave of a feed into a plane wave within a few meters, so a large antenna can be measured indoors. A near-field scanner samples the field's amplitude and phase on a surface close to the antenna, at spacings of no more than half a wavelength, and computes the far-field pattern by a Fourier transform; it trades room size for precise positioning and computation.
KEY: Key idea. Gain and pattern are far-field quantities. When 2D²/λ is too far for a room, a reflector or a near-field scan with a transform provides the plane wave instead.
H2: 16.3 Gain by comparison
P: The most common gain measurement compares the antenna under test with a reference antenna of known gain, usually a calibrated standard-gain horn. Each is placed in turn at the receiving position under identical conditions, and the received powers are recorded. The difference in received power in decibels is the difference in gain.
EQ: G_AUT (dBi) = G_ref (dBi) + P_AUT (dBm) − P_ref (dBm)
H2: Worked Example 16.2
WEP: A 15.0 dBi standard horn receives −32.4 dBm. With the antenna under test in its place, the received power is −29.8 dBm. Find the gain of the antenna under test.
WES: G_AUT = 15.0 + (−29.8) − (−32.4) = 17.6 dBi. The method relies on both antennas being matched equally well, so the mismatch loss of each, from Chapter 2, is measured and corrected.
H2: 16.4 Gain from the Friis equation
P: Without a reference, the gain follows from the link equation of Chapter 7. With two identical antennas facing each other at a known distance, the measured ratio of received to transmitted power equals the product of the gains times the path factor. With three different antennas, measured in all three pairs, the three equations can be solved for each gain without assuming any two are identical. This three-antenna method is how standard horns are themselves calibrated.
EQ: Pr/Pt = G₁ G₂ (λ / 4πR)²
H2: Worked Example 16.3
WEP: Two identical horns are 5 m apart at 10 GHz. The measured Pr/Pt is −36.4 dB. Find each horn's gain.
WES: The path factor is 20 log(0.03/(4π × 5)) = −66.4 dB. Then G₁G₂ = −36.4 − (−66.4) = 30.0 dB, and since the horns are identical, each is 15.0 dBi. With D = 0.2 m, the far-field distance is 2.67 m, so 5 m satisfies it.
H2: 16.5 Pattern, polarization, and efficiency
P: A pattern is measured by rotating the antenna under test on a positioner while recording the received power, usually in two principal planes, and then over a full sphere when the total radiated power is wanted. Rotating the source antenna's polarization measures the cross-polar level. Integrating the pattern over the sphere gives directivity; comparing it with the measured gain gives the radiation efficiency, which for a small antenna, as Chapter 13 explained, can be far below unity. For phones and IoT devices, over-the-air tests measure total radiated power and total isotropic sensitivity with the device's own radio running, which captures the antenna, the matching, and the body of the product together.
