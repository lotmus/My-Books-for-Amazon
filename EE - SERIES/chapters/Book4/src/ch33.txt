H1: Chapter 33. RF Exposure and Safety
P: Is it safe to stand in front of a cell-site antenna? A first-pass calculation for a sector antenna radiating 36 dBW at 3.5 GHz puts the general-public limit about 9 m away in the main beam when the usual conservative ground-reflection factor is included. That estimate is a screening calculation, not a compliance determination: actual assessment depends on the applicable rules, antenna pattern, averaging, installation, accessible locations, and exposure from other sources.
P: Where you meet this: Cell-site planning and rooftop access, SAR testing for phones and wearables, broadcast and radar sites, and the RF-exposure section of every radio filing.
P: A transmitter that radiates kilowatts of EIRP can expose people nearby to radio-frequency energy. For the frequency range covered here, the established adverse effect addressed by exposure limits is excessive heating; the limits include safety margins below levels associated with harmful temperature rises. Equipment authorization and site assessments must follow the rules that apply in their jurisdiction and use case. This chapter introduces power density and specific absorption rate, then shows how to estimate distance in a simplified far-field model.
H2: 33.1 The limits
P: Two bodies of rules dominate. In the United States the FCC limits of 47 CFR 1.1310, based on the IEEE and NCRP work of the 1980s and 1990s, set a maximum permissible exposure for the general public of 1 mW/cm², which is 10 W/m², from 1.5 to 100 GHz, and f/1500 mW/cm² between 300 and 1500 MHz, with f in megahertz. Occupational limits, for trained workers aware of the exposure, are five times higher. Internationally, the ICNIRP 2020 guidelines set 10 W/m² for the general public from 2 to 300 GHz, averaged over 30 minutes for whole-body exposure. Near the body, for phones and other devices held close, the limits are on the specific absorption rate: 1.6 W/kg averaged over 1 g of tissue under the FCC rules, and 2 W/kg over 10 g under ICNIRP.
EQ: FCC general public: S ≤ f/1500 mW/cm² (300–1500 MHz);  1 mW/cm² = 10 W/m² (1.5–100 GHz)
H2: 33.2 Power density in the far field
P: Far from an antenna the power density is the EIRP spread over a sphere, S = EIRP/(4πR²). Solving for R gives the distance beyond which the exposure is below a limit. Near the ground, the direct and ground-reflected waves can add; FCC guidance in OET Bulletin 65 uses a reflection factor of 2.56 in power density, corresponding to a field 1.6 times the free-space value, for a conservative estimate. The far-field formula overestimates the density in the near field of a large aperture antenna, where the beam has not yet spread; there the density is bounded by roughly four times the power divided by the aperture area.
EQ: S = EIRP/(4πR²),   Rcompliance = √(F × EIRP / (4π Slimit)),  F = 1 or 2.56

H2: Worked Example 33.1
P: Problem. A sector antenna radiates an EIRP of 36 dBW (3981 W) at 3.5 GHz. Find the general-public compliance distance in the main beam, with and without the ground-reflection factor, and the occupational distance.
P: Solution. The limit at 3.5 GHz is 10 W/m². Without reflection, R = √(3981 / (4π × 10)) = 5.63 m. With the 2.56 factor, R = 5.63 × 1.6 = 9.0 m. For the occupational limit of 50 W/m², R = √(3981 / (4π × 50)) = 2.52 m. Because sector antennas are mounted high and the beam is narrow in elevation, people at ground level are usually far outside the main beam and well below the limit; the exclusion zone matters mostly to workers on the roof or tower.
P: Key idea. The power density from a transmitter falls as 1/R², so a compliance distance is the square root of the EIRP over 4π times the limit. Workers near the antenna, not the public on the ground, are the usual concern.
H2: Worked Example 33.2
P: Problem. A 900 MHz transmitter has an EIRP of 100 W. Find the general-public limit and the compliance distance with the 2.56 factor.
P: Solution. The limit is 900/1500 = 0.6 mW/cm², 6 W/m². Without reflection R = √(100 / (4π × 6)) = 1.15 m; with the factor, 1.84 m.
H2: 33.3 Specific absorption rate
P: Close to the body, a device's field couples directly into tissue, and a far-field power-density estimate is not the right local metric. SAR is power absorbed per kilogram; handset test procedures use tissue-simulating liquid and defined phantoms and test positions. The FCC general-population limit for portable-device SAR is 1.6 W/kg averaged over 1 g. That is a regulatory exposure metric, not a prediction that a device raises the whole gram uniformly by a fixed temperature each minute: heat spreads and blood flow matters. Compliance can be achieved through antenna placement, operating-power limits, and, in some designs, proximity sensing with power reduction. Above 6 GHz the metric and procedure depend on the jurisdiction. FCC rules use maximum permissible exposure limits; ICNIRP 2020 uses local absorbed power density averaged over specified tissue areas and times. Do not substitute one regime for the other.
H2: 33.4 Compliance in practice
P: For a site assessment, the engineer accounts for relevant transmitters and sums exposure contributions under the applicable jurisdictional method, then checks accessible areas against the correct limits and averaging rules. If the assessment identifies an exceedance, controls may include signs, barriers, access restrictions, or reduced power. Measurements with suitable broadband or frequency-selective probes can validate an assessment. Massive-MIMO systems need methods that account for beam behavior and time averaging; an assumed maximum beam is not interchangeable with a measured or rule-defined exposure average.
BENCH: Use the far-field power-density model early as a screening estimate, with the assumptions and ground-reflection factor shown on the site drawing. Treat the resulting distance as a prompt for the applicable full assessment, not as a certified boundary. For body-worn products SAR testing is expensive, and antenna position relative to the body can drive the result, so changing it late in a design is costly. Keep the calculation, limits, methods, and edition with the compliance file.
