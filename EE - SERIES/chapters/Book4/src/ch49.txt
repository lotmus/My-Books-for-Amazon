H1: Chapter 49. FMCW Radar Front Ends at 24, 60, and 77 GHz
P: A car's corner radar, a smart speaker that sees a hand wave, and a sensor that counts the breaths of a sleeping patient all use the same trick: transmit a chirp whose frequency climbs linearly, mix the echo with the chirp still being sent, and read the distance from a beat tone a few megahertz wide. The hard part, a millimeter-wave front end, now fits on one chip.
P: Where you meet this: Automotive adaptive cruise control and corner radars at 76 to 81 GHz, industrial level and occupancy sensors at 60 GHz, motion detectors and door openers at 10 and 24 GHz, gesture sensing, and vital-sign monitoring.
P: Chapter 27 developed the radar equation and the FMCW principle. This chapter designs the front end: the chirp generator, the transmit and receive chains, the mixer and its intermediate-frequency, IF, filter, and the antenna arrays, using numbers typical of single-chip radar sensors. The signal processing, range and Doppler FFTs and the angle estimate, is treated here only as far as it sets the front-end specification.
H2: 49.1 The chirp and the beat
P: A chirp of bandwidth B swept in a time Tc has a slope S = B/Tc. A target at range R returns the chirp delayed by τ = 2R/c, and mixing it with the transmitted chirp gives a beat at fb = Sτ = 2SR/c. The range resolution depends only on the bandwidth, ΔR = c/(2B); the maximum range is set by the highest beat the IF chain passes; and the velocity follows from the phase change between successive chirps.
EQ: fb = 2 S R / c,   ΔR = c / (2B),   Rmax = fIF,max c / (2S),   Δv = λ / (2 N Tc),   vmax = λ / (4 Tc)
H2: Worked Example 49.1
P: Problem. A 77 GHz sensor has an IF bandwidth of 10 MHz. Compare a 4 GHz chirp and a 1 GHz chirp, each 40 µs long, for range resolution and maximum range. For the 1 GHz chirp, find the beat of a car at 50 m, and the velocity resolution and maximum unambiguous velocity for 128 chirps.
P: Solution. 4 GHz: S = 100 MHz/µs, ΔR = 3.75 cm, and the 10 MHz IF limits the range to 10⁷ × 3×10⁸/(2 × 10¹⁴) = 15 m, a short-range corner sensor. 1 GHz: S = 25 MHz/µs, ΔR = 15 cm, Rmax = 60 m, and a car at 50 m beats at 2 × 2.5×10¹³ × 50/2.998×10⁸ = 8.34 MHz, inside the IF. With λ = 3.89 mm, Δv = 0.00389/(2 × 128 × 40×10⁻⁶) = 0.38 m/s and vmax = 0.00389/(4 × 40×10⁻⁶) = 24 m/s. The bandwidth trades resolution for range at a fixed IF; a long-range mode uses a shallow slope, a short-range mode a steep one, and real sensors interleave both.
H2: 49.2 The front end on one chip
P: A single-chip sensor such as the TI IWR6843, at 60 to 64 GHz, or the IWR1843, at 76 to 81 GHz, integrates three transmit and four receive chains, a ramp generator built from a fractional-N synthesizer, Chapter 39, mixers, IF amplifiers and filters, ADCs, and a signal processor, in 45 nm RF CMOS. The IWR6843 data sheet lists 12 dBm of transmit power, 4 GHz of chirp bandwidth, ramp slopes up to about 250 MHz/µs, and a 10 MHz maximum IF. SiGe BiCMOS, Chapter 46, builds the same functions with more transmit power and lower noise, and it remains common in long-range automotive sensors.
P: Three transmitters and four receivers form a virtual array of twelve elements when the transmitters take turns, by time or by code, so a small antenna on the package resolves angle to about 2/N radians, 9.5° for twelve elements in a line. The antennas are patch or slot arrays printed on the package or the board, fed by lines whose loss at 77 GHz, several tenths of a decibel per centimeter, keeps them short.
H2: 49.3 The budget and phase noise
P: The radar equation of Chapter 27 sets the received power, and the integration over a chirp and over many chirps sets the signal-to-noise ratio. Phase noise matters less in FMCW than its level suggests, because the transmitted and received chirps come from the same oscillator: for a delay τ, phase noise at offset fm is suppressed by the factor 2 sin(πfmτ), which at short range is tiny. Leakage from transmitter to receiver and strong close reflections, the bumper or a radome, are the cases where phase noise still sets the floor.
H2: Worked Example 49.2
P: Problem. A 77 GHz sensor transmits 12 dBm through antennas of 10 dBi each, transmit and receive. Find the received power from a car of 10 m² radar cross section at 50 m, and the signal-to-noise ratio for one 40 µs chirp and for 128 chirps, assuming a 12 dB receiver noise figure. Find the range at which the 128-chirp SNR falls to 13 dB, and the suppression of the oscillator's phase noise at 1 MHz offset for a reflection at 1 m.
P: Solution. With λ = 3.89 mm, Pr = PtG²λ²σ/((4π)³R⁴) = −107.1 dBm. The noise in the 25 kHz bandwidth of one 40 µs chirp is −174 + 12 + 44.0 = −118.0 dBm, so the SNR is 10.9 dB per chirp and 10.9 + 10 log 128 = 32.0 dB after 128 chirps. The SNR falls as R⁴, 40 dB per decade, so 13 dB is reached at 50 × 10^(19/40) = 149 m. A reflection at 1 m has τ = 6.67 ns and 2 sin(π × 10⁶ × 6.67×10⁻⁹) = 0.042, a suppression of 27.6 dB. The receiver noise figure is an assumption for this example, not a data-sheet value.

H2: 49.4 Sensors a reader can build
P: Hobby modules make the principle tangible. The HB100, a 10.525 GHz Doppler module, and 24 GHz modules such as the CDM324 output the Doppler beat directly: fd = 2v/λ is 702 Hz for a target moving at 10 m/s toward a 10.525 GHz module and 1.61 kHz at 24.125 GHz, in the audio range, so a sound card or a microcontroller's ADC measures speed. Single-chip 60 GHz evaluation boards measure range, angle, and even breathing, since chest motion of a few millimeters is a large phase change at a 5 mm wavelength. Google's Soli gesture radar, described by Lien and colleagues in 2016, used a 60 GHz chip with about 7 GHz of bandwidth, a range resolution of 2.1 cm, and relied on fine phase tracking for gestures smaller than that.
P: Bipolar version. The same front end in SiGe has higher transmit power per chain and lower flicker noise in the mixers, which helps at the low IF frequencies of short-range and vital-sign sensing; the architecture does not change.
P: Where this continues. Measuring chirps, their linearity, and pulsed RF is covered in Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, "Bursts, Chirps, and Pulses: BLE, LoRa, FMCW Radar, and Pulsed RF". The FFTs, tracking filters, and point clouds that turn the beat into objects belong to Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, in "Fixed-Point Math and DSP" and "Cameras, Point Clouds, and Edge AI".
P: Key idea. In FMCW the bandwidth sets the range resolution, the IF bandwidth and the slope set the maximum range, and the shared oscillator cancels most of the phase noise. A one-chip front end with a few transmit and receive chains forms a virtual array that resolves angle as well.
BENCH: Before measuring targets, record the radar's response with nothing in front of it in a room lined with absorber or pointed at open sky: the bumps that remain are leakage, the radome, and the mounting bracket, and they set the floor every real target must clear. Check the chirp's linearity with a delay line or a corner reflector at a known distance; a nonlinear ramp smears the beat into several range bins and looks like a bad antenna.
P: On the Job
P: Radar systems engineers at automotive suppliers and sensor companies define chirp plans, antenna arrays, and link budgets, then verify them in chambers and on test tracks. The role spans RF front ends, signal processing, and safety standards, and much of the work is test: corner reflectors on poles, calibrated targets, and long drives with logged data. A bachelor's or master's degree in electrical engineering with RF and signal-processing courses is the usual path.
