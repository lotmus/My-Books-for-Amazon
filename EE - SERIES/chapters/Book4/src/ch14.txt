H1: Chapter 14. Propagation Beyond Free Space
P: The Friis equation of Chapter 7 assumes empty space between two antennas. Real paths run over the ground, past buildings and hills, and through air and rain. This chapter adds the corrections that matter most to a radio engineer: the Fresnel zone that a line-of-sight path must keep clear, the ground reflection that changes the distance law, diffraction over an obstacle, and absorption by oxygen and rain at millimeter waves. Each correction is a number of decibels added to the free-space loss.
H2: 14.1 The Fresnel zone
P: Energy does not travel along an infinitely thin ray. The first Fresnel zone is the ellipsoid around the direct path within which the excess path length is less than half a wavelength; obstacles inside it reduce the received power even if they do not block the line of sight. Its radius at a point a distance d1 from one end and d2 from the other is r1 = √(λd1d2/(d1 + d2)). Microwave links are designed to keep at least 60% of the first zone clear, allowing for the Earth's bulge, which with the standard effective-Earth-radius factor k = 4/3 rises by d1d2/(2kR) at the midpoint.
EQ: r1 = √(λ d1 d2 / (d1 + d2)),   bulge h = d1 d2 / (2 k R),  k = 4/3,  R = 6371 km
H2: Worked Example 14.1
WEP: A 5.8 GHz link spans 10 km. Find the first Fresnel radius at the midpoint, the 60% clearance, and the Earth bulge there.
WES: λ = 51.7 mm, so r1 = √(0.0517 × 5000 × 5000/10,000) = 11.4 m, and 60% clearance is 6.8 m. The bulge is 5000²/(2 × 4/3 × 6.371 × 10⁶) = 1.47 m. The antennas must therefore be mounted high enough that the line between them clears the terrain at mid-path by at least 6.8 + 1.5 = 8.3 m.
H2: 14.2 The ground reflection
P: Over flat ground, the receiver sees the direct wave and a wave reflected from the ground, nearly 180° out of phase at grazing incidence. Close in, the two alternately add and cancel; beyond a breakpoint distance db = 4hthr/λ, they cancel more and more completely, and the received power falls as 1/d⁴ instead of 1/d². In that region the path loss is approximately 40 log10 d − 20 log10(hthr), independent of frequency. This two-ray model is the simplest explanation of why cellular path loss grows faster than free space, and why antenna height matters so much.
EQ: db = 4 ht hr / λ;   beyond db: L ≈ 40 log10 d − 20 log10(ht hr)  dB
H2: Worked Example 14.2
WEP: A 2.4 GHz base antenna is 10 m high and the handset 1.5 m. Find the breakpoint and the two-ray path loss at 2 km, compared with free space.
WES: λ = 0.125 m, so db = 4 × 10 × 1.5/0.125 = 480 m. At 2 km the two-ray loss is 40 × 3.301 − 20 log10 15 = 132.0 − 23.5 = 108.5 dB, against 106.1 dB in free space. The difference grows by 20 dB per decade of distance beyond the breakpoint: at 20 km it would be 22.5 dB.
KEY: Key idea. Real propagation adds losses to free space: obstacles in the Fresnel zone, ground reflections that steepen the distance law beyond a breakpoint, and absorption by gases and rain that grows with frequency.
H2: 14.3 Diffraction over an obstacle
P: An obstacle that blocks the line of sight does not create a perfect shadow; the wave diffracts over its edge. The knife-edge model expresses the obstruction as a dimensionless parameter ν = h√(2(d1 + d2)/(λd1d2)), with h the height of the edge above the line of sight, and the diffraction loss as approximately 6.9 + 20 log10(√((ν − 0.1)² + 1) + ν − 0.1) dB for ν > −0.7. An edge exactly on the line of sight, ν = 0, costs 6.0 dB. Real terrain with several obstacles and rounded hills is handled by extensions of the model and by terrain databases in planning tools.
EQ: ν = h √(2(d1 + d2)/(λ d1 d2)),   J(ν) ≈ 6.9 + 20 log10[√((ν − 0.1)² + 1) + ν − 0.1]  dB
H2: Worked Example 14.3
WEP: On the link of Example 14.1, a ridge at mid-path rises 5 m above the line of sight. Find the diffraction loss.
WES: ν = 5 × √(2 × 10,000/(0.0517 × 5000 × 5000)) = 0.622, so J = 6.9 + 20 log10(√(0.522² + 1) + 0.522) = 11.3 dB. The link's fade margin, 27.3 dB in Example 30.1, would fall to 16 dB.
H2: 14.4 Gases and rain
P: Above about 10 GHz the atmosphere absorbs. Water vapor has a line near 22 GHz, and oxygen a strong band near 60 GHz, where absorption reaches about 15 dB/km at sea level, which limits 60 GHz links to short ranges but also isolates neighboring links from one another. Rain attenuates more strongly with frequency and rain rate. The ITU-R P.838 model gives the specific attenuation as γ = kR^α dB/km with coefficients tabulated by frequency; at 30 GHz with horizontal polarization, k ≈ 0.240 and α ≈ 0.949, so a heavy 25 mm/h rain costs about 5.1 dB/km. Millimeter-wave link budgets must include these losses with the availability statistics of Chapter 30.
EQ: γrain = k R^α  dB/km
