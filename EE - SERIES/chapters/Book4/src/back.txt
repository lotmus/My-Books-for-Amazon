H1: Appendix A. Constants and Formulas Used in This Book
P: Speed of light c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s. Reference impedance Z0 = 50 Ω unless stated.
P: Reference stripline laminate: εr = 3.55, so λg = λ0/1.884; at 2.45 GHz, λ0 = 12.24 cm and λg = 6.49 cm. Reference microstrip: 0.508 mm thick, εr = 3.55, 50 Ω width 1.14 mm, εeff ≈ 2.79.
P: Decibels: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW); 0 dBm in 50 Ω is 0.224 V rms.
P: Reflection: Γ = (ZL − Z0)/(ZL + Z0); VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|); RL = −20 log₁₀|Γ|; ML = −10 log₁₀(1 − |Γ|²).
P: Line input impedance: Zin = Z0 (ZL + jZ0 tan βl)/(Z0 + jZL tan βl); quarter wave: Zin = Z0²/ZL.
P: L-network: Q = √(Rhigh/Rlow − 1), Xseries = Q Rlow, Xshunt = Rhigh/Q.
P: S-parameters: Zin = Z0(1 + S₁₁)/(1 − S₁₁); stability K = (1 − |S₁₁|² − |S₂₂|² + |Δ|²)/(2|S₁₂S₂₁|), Δ = S₁₁S₂₂ − S₁₂S₂₁.

P: Rectangular waveguide TE₁₀ cutoff fc = c/2a; WR-90 a = 22.86 mm (6.56 GHz); WR-28 a = 7.112 mm (21.1 GHz).

H1: Appendix B. Answers to the Practice Problems
P: Every practice problem is worked in full below: the idea that solves it, the numbers, a check that the result makes sense, and the mistake that most often goes wrong. Work each problem before reading its solution.
H2: Chapter 1
P: 1. Idea: the free-space wavelength is c/f, and in stripline the whole field sits in the dielectric, so the guided wavelength is shorter by √εr. λ0 = 2.998×10⁸/5.8×10⁹ = 5.17 cm. √3.55 = 1.884, so λg = 5.17/1.884 = 2.74 cm. Check: at 2.45 GHz Worked Example 1.1's numbers scale by 2.45/5.8, and 5.8 GHz is about 2.4 times higher, so a wavelength near 2.7 cm is about right. The usual mistake is to divide by εr instead of its square root.
P: 2. Idea: a trace starts to behave as a transmission line when it reaches about a tenth of a guided wavelength, so set λg = 10 × 2 cm = 20 cm and solve for f. f = c/(√εr λg) = 2.998×10⁸/(1.884 × 0.20) = 0.80 GHz. Check: at 0.80 GHz the free-space wavelength is 37.5 cm and the stripline wavelength 37.5/1.884 = 19.9 cm, a tenth of which is 2 cm. The usual mistake is to use the free-space wavelength, which would put the limit at 1.5 GHz, nearly twice too high.
P: 3. Idea: dBm is 10 log of power in milliwatts, and power in a resistor is V²/R. 27 dBm = 10^2.7 mW = 501 mW. −90 dBm = 10^(−9) mW = 10⁻¹² W, so V = √(PR) = √(10⁻¹² × 50) = 7.07 µV rms. Check: 0 dBm in 50 Ω is 224 mV rms, and −90 dB in power is −90 dB in voltage terms too, a factor of 31,600 down: 224 mV/31,600 = 7.08 µV. The usual mistake is to use 20 log for a power ratio.
P: 4. Idea: a real capacitor is a series RLC; it is capacitive below self-resonance and inductive above. f = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π√(0.6×10⁻⁹ × 100×10⁻¹²)) = 650 MHz. Check: at 650 MHz the reactance of 100 pF is 2.45 Ω and that of 0.6 nH is 2.45 Ω, equal and opposite. The usual mistake is to keep treating the part as a capacitor above 650 MHz, where it is an inductor.
P: 5. Idea: in decibels gains add and losses subtract. −30 + 20 − 2 − 6 = −18 dBm. Check: in linear terms 1 µW × 100 × 0.631 × 0.251 = 15.8 µW, which is −18.0 dBm. The usual mistake is to add a loss as a positive gain because it is written as a positive number.
P: 6. Idea: at series resonance the capacitive and inductive reactances cancel, leaving only the small ESR. A coupling or bypass capacitor chosen to resonate at the operating frequency is the lowest impedance any single part can offer there, a fraction of an ohm. Check: Problem 4's part has 2.45 Ω of reactance from each element at 650 MHz but only its ESR in total. The usual mistake is to choose the largest capacitance available, whose lower self-resonance makes it an inductor at the operating frequency.
H2: Chapter 2
P: 1. Idea: Z0 = √(L/C) and vp = 1/√(LC) give C = 1/(Z0 vp) and L = Z0/vp. vp = 0.85 × 2.998×10⁸ = 2.55×10⁸ m/s. C = 1/(75 × 2.55×10⁸) = 52.3 pF/m, L = 75/2.55×10⁸ = 294 nH/m. Check: √(L/C) = √(294×10⁻⁹/52.3×10⁻¹²) = 75.0 Ω and 1/√(LC) = 2.55×10⁸ m/s. The usual mistake is to use the free-space velocity, which gives values 15 % too small.
P: 2. Idea: Γ = (ZL − Z0)/(ZL + Z0); VSWR, return loss and mismatch loss all follow from |Γ|. Γ = 25/125 = 0.20. VSWR = 1.2/0.8 = 1.5. RL = −20 log 0.20 = 14.0 dB. ML = −10 log(1 − 0.04) = 0.18 dB. Check: 4 % of the power is reflected and 96 % delivered, and −10 log 0.96 = 0.18 dB. The usual mistake is to confuse return loss, a large number for a good match, with mismatch loss, a small one.
P: 3. Idea: the same formula, with a load below Z0. Γ = (25 − 50)/(25 + 50) = −0.333, so |Γ| = 0.333 and VSWR = 1.333/0.667 = 2.0. The negative sign means the load is below Z0, a voltage minimum at the load. Check: VSWR for a resistive load is the ratio of the larger resistance to the smaller, 50/25 = 2. The usual mistake is to report a negative VSWR or drop the sign of Γ, which carries the information about where the voltage minimum lies.
P: 4. Idea: a quarter-wave line inverts the load about Z0: Zin = Z0²/ZL. Zin = 50²/200 = 12.5 Ω. Check: the normalized load 4 becomes 1/4, and 0.25 × 50 = 12.5 Ω. The usual mistake is to forget that the inversion holds only at the frequency where the line is a quarter wavelength.
P: 5. Idea: an open stub shorter than a quarter wave is capacitive, Zin = −jZ0 cot βl. At one eighth of a wavelength βl = 45° and cot 45° = 1, so Zin = −j50 Ω. C = 1/(ωX) = 1/(2π × 2.45×10⁹ × 50) = 1.30 pF. Check: a 1.30 pF capacitor has 50 Ω of reactance at 2.45 GHz. The usual mistake is to use tan instead of cot, which describes a shorted stub and would give an inductor.
P: 6. Idea: a line of length l rotates the reflection coefficient by 2βl, so the impedance seen depends on where it is measured. The same load reads differently at every plane along the line, and a number has meaning only at a stated plane, usually the package lead or the device pad. Check: 0.1λ of line rotates Γ by 72°, which can turn a resistive load into a strongly reactive one. The usual mistake is to copy a load-pull impedance from a data sheet without its reference plane and then add the board's line length twice.
H2: Chapter 3
P: 1. Idea: an L-network's Q is set by the impedance ratio, Q = √(Rhigh/Rlow − 1); the series element goes on the low side and the shunt element on the high side. Q = √(50/5 − 1) = 3.0. Series reactance Q × 5 = 15 Ω, L = 15/(2π × 900×10⁶) = 2.65 nH. Shunt reactance 50/3 = 16.7 Ω, C = 1/(2π × 900×10⁶ × 16.7) = 10.6 pF. Check: 5 + j15 Ω in parallel with −j16.7 Ω gives 50 Ω real. The usual mistake is to put the shunt element on the 5 Ω side, where it would have to be smaller than 5 Ω and the network could not reach 50 Ω.
P: 2. Idea: the high-pass L swaps the element types: a shunt inductor and a series capacitor. Q = √(200/50 − 1) = √3 = 1.73. The shunt element goes across the higher resistance, the 200 Ω side, and the series capacitor faces the 50 Ω side. Check: a shunt reactance of 200/1.73 = 115 Ω reduces 200 Ω to a series equivalent of 50 Ω with 86.6 Ω of inductive reactance, which the series capacitor cancels. The usual mistake is to choose the side by element type rather than by resistance.
P: 3. Idea: a pi network is two back-to-back L-networks meeting at a virtual resistance Rv = Rhigh/(Q² + 1), which must be below both terminations. Rv = 50/(8² + 1) = 50/65 = 0.77 Ω. Check: Rv is still below 10 Ω, so the 10 Ω side can be matched to it; raising Q from 5 to 8 lowered Rv from 1.92 Ω and narrowed the bandwidth. The usual mistake is to use Q² instead of Q² + 1.
P: 4. Idea: a finite-Q inductor adds a series resistance X/QL, which appears in series with the low-side load and takes its share of the power. R = 15/30 = 0.5 Ω in series with 5 Ω, so the load receives 5/5.5 = 0.909 of the power, a loss of −10 log 0.909 = 0.41 dB. Check: the network Q is 3, and the loss estimate Q/QL = 3/30 = 10 % agrees. The usual mistake is to compare 0.5 Ω with 50 Ω; on the low side it is compared with 5 Ω, which is why high-ratio matches are lossy.
P: 5. Idea: the network must present the series reactance that Worked Example 3.1 placed next to the load, 20 Ω, and the load already supplies part of it. The network supplies 20 − 8 = 12 Ω of inductive reactance, 0.78 nH at 2.45 GHz. Check: 10 + j8 + j12 = 10 + j20 Ω, the impedance Worked Example 3.1 matched. The usual mistake is to design for the resistive part alone and leave the load reactance to detune the match.
P: 6. Idea: an L-network's Q, and with it the bandwidth and the loss in its parts, is fixed by the impedance ratio. Two sections, each transforming by the square root of the total ratio, each have a lower Q, so the cascade is wider and loses less. Check: for 50 to 5 Ω a single section has Q = 3.0; two sections through 15.8 Ω each have Q = √(3.16 − 1) = 1.47. The usual mistake is to think the second section doubles the loss; at half the Q each loses about half as much.
H2: Chapter 4
P: 1. Idea: a quarter-wave transformer's impedance is the geometric mean of the two it joins. Zq = √(100 × 50) = 70.7 Ω. Check: 70.7²/100 = 50 Ω. The usual mistake is to use the arithmetic mean, 75 Ω, which leaves Γ = 0.03 at the center frequency.
P: 2. Idea: the single-section bandwidth formula depends on the ratio of the two impedances, so 100 to 50 Ω behaves as 25 to 50 Ω. Δf/f0 = 2 − (4/π) arccos[(Γm/√(1 − Γm²)) × 2√(Z0RL)/|RL − Z0|] = 2 − (4/π) arccos(0.1005 × 2.828) = 2 − (4/π)(1.282) = 0.367, or 36.7 %. Check: a ratio of 2 is mild, and a third of an octave of bandwidth at |Γ| < 0.1 is about what a single section gives. The usual mistake is to treat 100 to 50 Ω as a different problem from 25 to 50 Ω.
P: 3. Idea: a shunt stub at the load works only if the load conductance already equals 1/Z0. YL = 1/(25 − j25) = (25 + j25)/1250 = 0.02 + j0.02 S. The conductance is 0.02 S = 1/50 Ω, so only the susceptance +j0.02 S must be cancelled. A shorted stub has Y = −j(1/Z0) cot βl, so cot βl = 1, βl = 45° and l = λ/8. Check: a λ/8 shorted stub has Zin = jZ0 tan 45° = j50 Ω, admittance −j0.02 S. The usual mistake is to work in impedances, where the stub's parallel connection does not add.
P: 4. Idea: moving toward the generator by d rotates Γ by 2βd, clockwise on the chart. 2βd = 2 × 360° × 0.1 = 72°. Check: a full circle is half a wavelength, 0.5λ, so 0.1λ is a fifth of a circle, 72°. The usual mistake is to rotate by βd, 36°, forgetting the round trip.
P: 5. Idea: a binomial transformer steps the logarithm of impedance in binomial proportions, so the section next to each end is closest to that end's impedance. For two sections from 25 to 50 Ω, ln Z1 = ln 25 + ln 2/4 and ln Z2 = ln 25 + 3 ln 2/4, giving Z1 = 29.7 Ω next to the 25 Ω load and Z2 = 42.0 Ω next to the 50 Ω line. Check: Z1 × Z2 = 1250 = 25 × 50, the symmetry every binomial design has. The usual mistake is to reverse the order, which puts the largest step at each end.
P: 6. Idea: a line's reactance changes with frequency in proportion to its electrical length, so a short stub and a short line section drift less from their design values as frequency moves. Check: a 45° stub moves to 49.5° at +10 %, a 135° stub to 148.5°; the second's cot changes several times as much. The usual mistake is to choose the stub solution that is easier to lay out without looking at the bandwidth.
H2: Chapter 5
P: 1. Idea: return loss and insertion loss are −20 log of |S11| and |S21|, and a lossless two-port has |S11|² + |S21|² = 1. RL = −20 log 0.1 = 20 dB. IL = −20 log 0.995 = 0.04 dB. |S11|² + |S21|² = 0.010 + 0.990 = 1.000, so it is lossless within the precision given. Check: the 0.04 dB is the 1 % of power that is reflected, not absorbed. The usual mistake is to call 0.04 dB of insertion loss a dissipation; here it is all mismatch.
P: 2. Idea: Z = Z0(1 + Γ)/(1 − Γ). Γ = 0.3∠−45° = 0.212 − j0.212. Zin = 50 (1.212 − j0.212)/(0.788 + j0.212) = 68.3 − j31.9 Ω. Check: the angle −45° puts the point in the lower half of the chart, capacitive, and |Γ| = 0.3 keeps it near the center, so a resistance above 50 Ω with modest negative reactance is right. The usual mistake is to drop the complex arithmetic and use |Γ| alone.
P: 3. Idea: the error bounds of a mismatched source and load are 20 log(1 ± |ΓS||ΓL|). |ΓS||ΓL| = 0.3 × 0.2 = 0.06. 20 log 1.06 = +0.51 dB and 20 log 0.94 = −0.54 dB. Check: the bounds are not symmetric because the log is not; for small products both approach ±8.7 × 0.06 = ±0.52 dB. The usual mistake is to add the two return losses instead of multiplying the reflection coefficients.
P: 4. Idea: Rollett's K = (1 − |S11|² − |S22|² + |Δ|²)/(2|S12S21|), and K > 1 with |Δ| < 1 means unconditional stability. K = (1 − 0.25 − 0.25 + 0.41²)/(2 × 0.1 × 2.0) = 0.668/0.4 = 1.67. With |Δ| = 0.41 < 1, the device is unconditionally stable. Check: Δ = S11S22 − S12S21 = 0.25∠−100° − 0.2∠130°, whose magnitude computes to 0.408. The usual mistake is to test K alone; K > 1 with |Δ| > 1 is not stable.
P: 5. Idea: a reciprocal network has S12 = S21. S12 = 0.7∠−30°. Check: passive networks built of resistors, capacitors, inductors and lines are reciprocal; ferrites and transistors are not. The usual mistake is to conjugate the phase, which is the lossless condition on a different matrix product.
P: 6. Idea: open and short circuits are hard to make at microwave frequencies, because an open radiates and has fringing capacitance and a short has inductance, and an active device terminated in them may oscillate. Matched loads are realizable and keep the device stable while it is measured, and waves are what the instruments measure. Check: every VNA calibration standard set includes a load. The usual mistake is to think S-parameters are only a convenience; for a transistor at 10 GHz the open-circuit measurement may not exist.
H2: Chapter 6
P: 1. Idea: scale the normalized prototype by impedance and frequency: C = g/(R0 ωc), L = g R0/ωc. For the third-order Butterworth, g1 = g3 = 1 and g2 = 2. ωc = 2π × 500×10⁶ = 3.14×10⁹ rad/s. C1 = C3 = 1/(50 × 3.14×10⁹) = 6.37 pF; L2 = 2 × 50/3.14×10⁹ = 31.8 nH. Check: at 500 MHz the 6.37 pF capacitors have 50 Ω of reactance and the inductor 100 Ω. The usual mistake is to forget the 2π and scale by f.
P: 2. Idea: a Butterworth filter of order n has attenuation 10 log[1 + (f/fc)^(2n)]. At three times the cutoff, 10 log(1 + 3⁶) = 10 log 730 = 28.6 dB. Check: far beyond cutoff the slope is 6n = 18 dB per octave, and 3 is 1.58 octaves, giving 28.5 dB. The usual mistake is to use (f/fc)^n, which halves the decibels.
P: 3. Idea: the midband loss of a coupled-resonator filter is about 4.343 Σg/(FBW Qu). For the 0.1 dB third-order prototype Σg = 1.0316 + 1.1474 + 1.0316 = 3.21. Loss = 4.343 × 3.21/(0.02 × 500) = 1.39 dB. Check: halving the bandwidth from Chapter 18's 4 % doubles the loss for the same Qu. The usual mistake is to use the unloaded Q of one resonator as if it set the loss alone; the narrower the filter, the more each resonator's loss counts.
P: 4. Idea: λg = λ0/√(1 − (fc/f)²), with fc = 6.56 GHz for WR-90's 22.86 mm broad wall. λ0 = 2.998×10⁸/12×10⁹ = 24.98 mm. λg = 24.98/√(1 − (6.56/12)²) = 24.98/0.838 = 29.8 mm. Check: λg is always longer than λ0 in a hollow guide and approaches it far above cutoff. The usual mistake is to forget the square inside the root.
P: 5. Idea: the TE10 cutoff is where the broad wall is half a wavelength, fc = c/(2a). fc = 2.998×10⁸/(2 × 0.0158) = 9.49 GHz. Check: 15.8 mm is the broad wall of WR-62, used from about 12.4 to 18 GHz, well above its 9.49 GHz cutoff. The usual mistake is to use the narrow wall.
P: 6. Idea: the midband loss of a filter is inversely proportional to its resonators' Qu, and after a power amplifier that loss is heat. A cavity's Q is roughly ten times that of microstrip, so it dissipates about a tenth of the power, and its size handles the voltage and the heat. Check: Worked Example 18.2 gave 1.74 dB at Qu = 200 and 0.17 dB at Qu = 2000; at 100 W, 1.74 dB is 33 W of heat. The usual mistake is to size the filter for rejection alone.
H2: Chapter 7
P: 1. Idea: a practical half-wave dipole is cut about 4 % short of λ/2 to cancel its reactance; the problem gives 0.48λ. λ = 2.998×10⁸/5.8×10⁹ = 5.17 cm, so 0.48λ = 2.48 cm tip to tip. Check: half of 5.17 cm is 2.58 cm, and the shortened length sits a little below it. The usual mistake is to cut it to the full λ/2 and find the resonance a few percent low.

H2: Chapter 8
P: 1. Idea: to steer to θ0 the progressive phase must be β = −kd sin θ0. With d = 0.5λ, kd = 180°, so β = −180° × sin 45° = −127.3°. H2: Chapter 9
P: 1. Idea: a Wilkinson divider uses quarter-wave lines of √2 Z0 and a resistor of 2Z0. √2 × 75 = 106.1 Ω lines and a 150 Ω resistor. Check: the two 106.1 Ω arms, each transforming the 75 Ω output to 106.1²/75 = 150 Ω, appear in parallel as 75 Ω at the input. The usual mistake is to keep the 50 Ω values of 70.7 Ω and 100 Ω.
P: 2. Idea: each two-way stage halves the power, so N equal stages give 2^N outputs at 3 dB per stage. An eight-way divider is three stages: 3 × 3.01 = 9.03 dB, or 10 log 8. Check: each output carries 1/8 of the input power, 12.5 %. The usual mistake is to call this a loss; it is the division the network is built for, and dissipation comes on top.
P: 3. Idea: isolation is coupling plus directivity in decibels. 20 + 25 = 45 dB. Check: the isolated port sees the input signal 45 dB down, while the coupled port sees it 20 dB down, and the 25 dB difference is the directivity. The usual mistake is to think directivity and isolation are the same number.
P: 4. Idea: finite directivity adds a leakage term of 10^(−D/20) to the true reflection coefficient with unknown phase. True Γ = 10^(−20/20) = 0.1; leakage 10^(−25/20) = 0.0562. The reading lies between 0.1 + 0.0562 = 0.156, a return loss of 16.1 dB, and 0.1 − 0.0562 = 0.044, a return loss of 27.2 dB. Check: the error is asymmetric, larger toward a falsely good reading. The usual mistake is to trust a return loss measured within 10 dB of the coupler's directivity.
P: 5. Idea: in a balanced amplifier the input hybrid splits the signal with a 90° difference. Each amplifier's reflection makes a second pass through the hybrid, so the two reflections arrive at the input port 180° apart and cancel, and at the isolated port in phase, where its load absorbs them. Check: if each amplifier has Γ = 0.3, the input port sees zero reflection in the ideal case and the isolated load absorbs 9 % of the power. The usual mistake is to think the amplifiers must be matched individually; only their reflections must be equal.
P: 6. Idea: the scattering matrix of a lossless network is unitary, and of a reciprocal one symmetric. For three ports with all diagonal terms zero, the unitarity conditions force at least two of the three transmission terms to be zero, which contradicts the remaining conditions, so no such network exists. A matched three-port must be lossy, like the Wilkinson with its resistor, or nonreciprocal, like the circulator. Check: the Wilkinson is matched at all three ports precisely because its resistor can absorb power. The usual mistake is to look for a cleverer lossless T-junction.

The 3.5 GHz macro cell sends OFDM with a high peak-to-average ratio at tens of watts, so a GaN Doherty with predistortion, Chapter 45. The 6.78 MHz charger delivers a constant sine to a varying coil load, so a class E or class D inverter, Chapter 43. Check: Section 41.12's list. The usual mistake is to pick one class for everything because it has the highest ideal efficiency.
H2: Chapter 42
P: 1. Idea: an N-device stack may swing N·Vmax at its drain, centered on VDD = N·Vmax/2. VDD = 4 × 2.4/2 = 4.8 V. Ropt = (4.8 − 0.8)²/(2 × 2) = 16/4 = 4.0 Ω. Check: Worked Example 42.1's three-device stack gave 4.5 Ω at 1 W; one more device and twice the power land at a similar load. The usual mistake is to set VDD = N·Vmax, which puts the drain peak at twice the limit.
P: 2. Idea: N equal amplifiers combine to 10 log N above one, less the combiner loss. One is 20 dBm, so 20 + 6.02 − 1.2 = 24.8 dBm, 303 mW. Check: 400 mW × 10^(−0.12) = 303 mW. The usual mistake is to count the ideal 6 dB and forget the loss.
P: 3. Idea: at fixed total width the gate resistance scales as the square of the finger width. Halving Wf divides it by 4. Check: each finger's resistance halves and twice as many are in parallel, a factor of 2 × 2 = 4. The usual mistake is to think more fingers at the same total width leave the resistance unchanged.
P: 4. Idea: in series combining each primary sees RL/(N n²). 40 = 50/(2n²), so n² = 0.625 and n = 0.79. Check: 50/(2 × 0.625) = 40 Ω. The usual mistake is to forget that the load is shared between the N secondaries.
P: 5. Idea: the extra loss subtracts directly in decibels from the antenna-port power. 0.5 dB more loss lowers 28 dBm to 27.5 dBm, from 631 mW to 562 mW, 11 % less. Check: 10^(−0.05) = 0.891. The usual mistake is to think half a decibel is negligible; it is the difference between meeting and missing a 28 dBm specification.
P: 6. Idea: a mismatched antenna changes the load the devices see, and at some phases the drain swing grows by up to 1 + |Γ| of its matched value. Hot-carrier damage and oxide breakdown depend steeply on that peak voltage, so the reliability check must cover the whole mismatch circle. Check: Worked Example 42.3's top device rose from 3.3 V to 4.7 V at 3:1. The usual mistake is to qualify the amplifier into 50 Ω and lose it in a user's hand.
H2: Chapter 43
P: 1. Idea: Ropt = (VDD − Vk)²/(2P) and Q = √(Rhigh/Rlow − 1). 20 dBm is 100 mW. Ropt = 3.0²/0.2 = 45.0 Ω. Q = √(50/45 − 1) = 0.33. Check: at this power the stage is almost matched to 50 Ω already, which is why low-power IoT amplifiers often need only a harmonic filter. The usual mistake is to design a high-Q match where almost none is needed.
P: 2. Idea: Rdd = 2(VDD − Vk)²/P and a 1:n transformer gives RL/n². Rdd = 2 × 12.8²/50 = 6.55 Ω. n² = 50/6.55 = 7.63, n = 2.76, so a 1:3 transformer (1:9, 5.56 Ω) or a tapped design. Check: 1:3 would give 2 × 12.8²/5.56 = 58.9 W, slightly above target, so the drive is backed off. The usual mistake is to use the single-ended formula and get a quarter of the right load.
P: 3. Idea: the efficiency is the ideal 120° value times the knee factor, and the load is V1/I1. η = 89.7 % × 11/12 = 82.2 %. I1 = 2 × 10/11 = 1.82 A, so R = 11/1.82 = 6.05 Ω. Check: the load equals the class B load for the same swing and power, (11)²/(2 × 10) = 6.05 Ω. The usual mistake is to think class C needs a different load line; it needs a different drive.
P: 4. Idea: R = 0.5768 VDD²/P, C = 0.1836/(ωR), vpk = 3.562 VDD. R = 0.5768 × 144/5 = 16.6 Ω. C = 0.1836/(2π × 13.56×10⁶ × 16.6) = 130 pF. vpk = 3.562 × 12 = 42.7 V. Check: compared with Chapter 35's Problem 1, half the power at the same supply doubles R and halves C. The usual mistake is to choose a 30 V device for a 12 V supply.
P: 5. Idea: λg = c/(f√εeff), and the stub is λg/12. λg = 2.998×10⁸/(5.8×10⁹ × 1.670) = 3.10 cm, so λg/12 = 0.258 cm, 2.6 mm. Check: at 2.45 GHz it was 6.1 mm, and the length scales as 1/f. The usual mistake is to forget that the device's capacitance shortens the stub further.
P: 6. Idea: class C delivers its fundamental from a narrow current pulse whose fundamental is a smaller fraction of its peak, 0.391 at 120° against 0.5 in class B. The same output needs a higher peak current, and the device must be sized for that peak. Check: Section 43.4 found 9.99 A against 7.81 A for 25 W. The usual mistake is to choose class C to save a device; it saves DC power, not silicon.

H2: Chapter 45

H1: About the Author
P: Lothar J. Musiol is a graduate of Munich University of Applied Sciences who spent more than forty years in the semiconductor industry, first at Germany's largest company in the field, then at a string of American start-ups. He studied physics alongside all of it, seriously enough to know exactly how much he is simplifying. Dual citizenship, put to its most sensible use, lets him split his time between San Clemente, California, and Passau, Bavaria, where Austria begins at the garden fence. The household includes one married daughter, one grumpy Teacup Pomeranian named Gucci, and eleven chickens, listed in order of seniority rather than volume.
P: He writes to make complex ideas clearer and more engaging than most treatments manage, without ever pretending they are simpler than they actually are.
