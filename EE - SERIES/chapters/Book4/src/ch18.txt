H1: Chapter 18. A Bandpass Filter from Specification to Layout
P: Chapter 6 gave the prototype values and the loss formula for filters, and Chapter 12 gave the resonators. This chapter carries one filter all the way through, from a specification to the coupling numbers a layout must realize, so that the steps can be seen in order. The example is a third-order Chebyshev bandpass filter for the 2.45 GHz band on the book's reference microstrip, with a comparison against the same filter built from cavities.
H2: 18.1 The specification
P: The filter must pass 2.40 to 2.50 GHz with at most 0.1 dB of ripple, and it should reject a signal at 2.60 GHz as strongly as possible with three resonators. The center frequency is f₀ = 2.45 GHz, and taking the passband edges as about 2.40 and 2.50 GHz with a little margin gives a fractional bandwidth FBW of 4%, about 98 MHz.
H2: 18.2 From prototype to couplings
P: A coupled-resonator filter is described by two kinds of numbers: the external quality factor Qe, which sets how strongly the first and last resonators are loaded by the source and load, and the coupling coefficients Mᵢ,ᵢ₊₁ between adjacent resonators. Both follow directly from the lowpass prototype values gₖ and the fractional bandwidth. For the 0.1 dB Chebyshev prototype of third order, g₁ = g₃ = 1.0316 and g₂ = 1.1474.
EQ: Qe = g₀g₁ / FBW,   Mᵢ,ᵢ₊₁ = FBW / √(gᵢ gᵢ₊₁)
H2: Worked Example 18.1
WEP: Find Qe and the coupling coefficients for the 4% filter.
WES: Qe = 1.0316/0.04 = 25.8. M₁₂ = M₂₃ = 0.04/√(1.0316 × 1.1474) = 0.04/1.088 = 0.0368. The layout must now produce a tap or gap that loads the end resonators to Qe = 25.8, and gaps between resonators that couple them by 3.68%. Electromagnetic simulation of two resonators at a time finds those dimensions: the coupling is half the fractional separation of the two resonant peaks a pair of coupled resonators shows.
H2: 18.3 The resonators
P: On the reference microstrip, with εeff = 2.79, the guided wavelength at 2.45 GHz is λ₀/√εeff = 12.24/1.670 = 7.33 cm, so a half-wave resonator is about 3.66 cm long before corrections for end effects. Folding the resonators into hairpins or squares shortens the filter and lets the coupling be set by the spacing of parallel arms. The unloaded Q of such microstrip resonators is typically about 200, set by conductor and dielectric loss, and it, not the topology, determines the passband loss.
H2: Worked Example 18.2
WEP: Find the midband loss with Qu = 200 and with Qu = 2000.
WES: The formula of Chapter 6 gives loss ≈ 4.343 Σgₖ/(FBW Qu). Σgₖ = 3.2106, so with Qu = 200 the loss is 4.343 × 3.2106/(0.04 × 200) = 1.74 dB, and with Qu = 2000, as with Chapter 12's cavities or dielectric resonators, it is 0.17 dB. The tenfold Q costs volume and money, and it is needed only when the 1.6 dB matters, as it does ahead of an LNA by Chapter 17's rule.
KEY: Key idea. A coupled-resonator filter is specified by its external Q and coupling coefficients, which follow from the prototype and the bandwidth. The resonators' unloaded Q then sets the loss.
H2: 18.4 Rejection
P: The rejection of the Chebyshev response at a frequency f follows from the lowpass prototype after the bandpass transformation, which maps f to a normalized frequency Ω. For the 0.1 dB ripple, ε² = 10^0.01 − 1 = 0.0233, and the attenuation of an n-th order filter is computed from the Chebyshev polynomial.
EQ: Ω = (1/FBW)(f/f₀ − f₀/f),   A = 10 log₁₀[1 + ε² cosh²(n arccosh |Ω|)]
H2: Worked Example 18.3
WEP: Find the rejection of the third-order filter at 2.60 GHz and at 2.70 GHz.
WES: At 2.60 GHz, f/f₀ − f₀/f = 1.0612 − 0.9423 = 0.1189, so Ω = 2.97 and A = 23.4 dB. At 2.70 GHz, Ω = 4.87 and A = 36.7 dB. If 2.60 GHz needs 40 dB, the designer must add a resonator, narrow the bandwidth, or add a transmission zero by cross-coupling the first and third resonators. Each choice raises the passband loss, so the specification and the loss are negotiated together.
H2: 18.5 Tuning and tolerance
P: A 4% filter is sensitive to its dimensions. The resonant frequency of a half-wave resonator scales with 1/√εeff, so a laminate whose εr varies by ±2% moves the center by about ±1%, a quarter of the passband. Production filters therefore either use laminates with tight εr control, as Book 7 discusses, or provide tuning screws or laser trimming. Simulation should include a tolerance sweep before the layout is released.
