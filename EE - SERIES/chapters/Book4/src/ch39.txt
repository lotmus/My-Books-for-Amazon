H1: Chapter 39. Frequency Synthesis: Fractional-N and DDS
P: A 40 MHz reference with a 24-bit fractional accumulator tunes in steps of 2.38 Hz, finer than any channel plan needs. Modern synthesizers get fine steps and low noise together by dividing by a number that changes from one cycle to the next, and then shaping the resulting error out of the band.
P: Where you meet this: Every phone and Wi-Fi local oscillator, signal generators, radar chirp sources, and test-equipment frequency references.
P: Chapter 26 introduced the phase-locked loop and its phase noise. A real radio needs more than one frequency: it must tune to any channel in its band in fine steps, quickly, with low spurs. This chapter describes the two main ways to make such a frequency: the fractional-N PLL, which multiplies a reference by a number that need not be an integer, and direct digital synthesis, which computes the waveform sample by sample. It then compares their noise and spurs.
H2: 39.1 Integer-N and its limits
P: An integer-N PLL produces fout = N × fref, so the channel step equals the reference frequency at the phase detector. Fine steps need a low reference, and a low reference forces a narrow loop bandwidth, for stability, and a large N. Both hurt: the narrow loop settles slowly and suppresses the VCO's noise over a smaller range, and the in-band phase noise rises as 20 log N.
H2: 39.2 Fractional-N
P: A fractional-N PLL switches its divider between neighboring integers so that the average division ratio is N + F/2^k, set by a k-bit accumulator. The reference can then be high, and the step is the reference divided by 2^k, which can be a few hertz. The switching pattern creates a periodic phase error that would produce spurs, so it is shaped by a delta-sigma modulator, Book 3's technique, that pushes the error to high offsets where the loop filters it out.
EQ: fout = (N + F/2^k) × fref,   step = fref/2^k
H2: Worked Example 39.1
P: Problem. A 40 MHz reference and a 24-bit fractional accumulator must produce 2441.5 MHz. Find the step, N, and F.
P: Solution. The step is 40×10⁶/2²⁴ = 2.38 Hz. The ratio is 2441.5/40 = 61.0375, so N = 61 and F = 0.0375 × 2²⁴ = 629,145.6, rounded to 629,146. The rounding leaves an error of 0.4 steps, under 1 Hz.
H2: 39.3 In-band phase noise and the figure of merit
P: Inside the loop bandwidth, the output phase noise is set by the phase detector and charge pump, referred to the output by the multiplication. Datasheets express this with a normalized figure of merit, the noise of a hypothetical 1 Hz loop at a 1 Hz detector rate, from which the in-band noise follows for any detector frequency and ratio. A good modern synthesizer has a figure of merit near −226 to −230 dBc/Hz.
EQ: L_in-band ≈ FOM + 10 log₁₀ f_PFD + 20 log₁₀ N  (dBc/Hz)
H2: Worked Example 39.2
P: Problem. With FOM = −226 dBc/Hz, find the in-band phase noise at 2441.5 MHz for a 40 MHz detector frequency and for a 10 MHz detector frequency.
P: Solution. At 40 MHz, N = 61.04, so L = −226 + 76.0 + 35.7 = −114.3 dBc/Hz. At 10 MHz, N = 244.15, so L = −226 + 70.0 + 47.8 = −108.2 dBc/Hz. Raising the detector frequency by four improves the in-band noise by 6 dB, the 20 log N term outweighing the 10 log f term, which is the main reason fractional-N synthesizers run their detectors as fast as possible.
P: Key idea. Fractional-N lets the detector run fast while the step stays fine; in-band noise improves 10 log f_PFD for every 20 log N it saves. Delta-sigma control hides the fractional pattern at offsets the loop filters away.
H2: 39.4 Direct digital synthesis
P: A DDS adds a frequency tuning word to a phase accumulator at each clock, converts the top bits of the phase to an amplitude through a lookup table, and drives a DAC. The output frequency is the tuning word times the clock over 2^n, so a 32-bit accumulator with a 1 GHz clock tunes in steps of 0.23 Hz, and the frequency can change at the next clock, with continuous phase. Its limits are spurs: truncating the phase to P bits before the table gives spurs of about −6.02P dBc, and the DAC's own nonlinearity adds more. The output is useful up to about 40% of the clock, beyond which the image near the clock cannot be filtered.
EQ: fout = FTW × fclk / 2^n,   spur ≈ −6.02 P dBc
FIG: fig39_1.png | Figure 39.1 — How a DDS works. A phase accumulator adds a step M every clock and wraps around; a sine table turns each phase into one sample. The output frequency is M·fclk/2^N.
H2: Worked Example 39.3
P: Problem. A DDS uses a 32-bit accumulator, a 1 GHz clock, and 14 bits of phase into the table. Find the step and the phase-truncation spur level, and the highest practical output frequency.
P: Solution. The step is 10⁹/2³² = 0.233 Hz. The truncation spur is about −6.02 × 14 = −84 dBc. The highest practical output is about 400 MHz. A common architecture uses a DDS as the fine-tuning reference of a PLL, combining the DDS's agility and resolution with the PLL's reach to gigahertz.
BENCH: Run the phase-detector frequency as high as the part allows; the 20 log N term outweighs the 10 log f term, so higher is better. Expect fractional spurs when the fractional value sits near a simple ratio such as 1/2 or 1/4, and plan the frequency plan or dithering around them. In a DDS, the clock's phase noise and spurs carry straight to the output, so clean the clock first.
