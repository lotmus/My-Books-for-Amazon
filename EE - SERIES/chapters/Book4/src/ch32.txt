H1: Chapter 32. Vacuum Tubes: Klystrons, TWTs, and Magnetrons
P: In 1940 John Randall and Harry Boot at the University of Birmingham built a cavity magnetron that produced centimeter waves at a power nobody had reached before, and compact airborne radar followed. The magnetron still heats the food in nearly every microwave oven, and tubes still handle much of the highest RF power in the world.
P: Where you meet this: Microwave ovens, satellite TWT amplifiers, weather and air-traffic radars, particle accelerators, and fusion-plasma heating.
P: Solid-state amplifiers have replaced vacuum tubes in most radios, but not everywhere. Where the power is kilowatts to megawatts, the frequency is very high, or the device must survive radiation and heat in orbit, electron beams in vacuum still win. Satellite transponders use traveling-wave tubes, particle accelerators and broadcast transmitters use klystrons and inductive output tubes, every microwave oven contains a magnetron, and fusion experiments heat plasma with gyrotrons. This chapter explains how a beam of electrons in vacuum amplifies a microwave signal and where each type fits.
H2: 32.1 The electron beam
P: A heated cathode emits electrons, and a high voltage accelerates them into a beam. The beam's DC power is its voltage times its current, and the tube's job is to convert part of that kinetic energy into RF. The current a gun can draw at a given voltage follows the Child-Langmuir law, I = K V^(3/2), where the constant K, the perveance, depends only on the gun's geometry; perveances around 0.5 to 2 microperveance (µperv, 10⁻⁶ A/V^1.5) are typical. At tens of kilovolts the electrons move at a sizable fraction of the speed of light, which is what lets the beam interact with a slow electromagnetic wave.

H2: Worked Example 32.1
P: Problem. A tube's gun runs at 10 kV and 0.5 A. Find the beam power, the perveance, the electron velocity, and the RF output at 40% efficiency.
P: Solution. Beam power = 10⁴ × 0.5 = 5 kW. Perveance = 0.5 / (10⁴)^1.5 = 0.5 × 10⁻⁶, or 0.5 µperv. With mc² = 511 keV, γ = 1.0196 and v = 0.195c = 5.85 × 10⁷ m/s. At 40% efficiency the RF output is 2 kW and 3 kW must be removed as heat, mostly in the collector.
H2: 32.2 Klystrons
P: In a klystron the RF input drives a resonant cavity whose gap speeds up some electrons and slows others. Downstream, the fast electrons catch up with the slow ones and the beam bunches; at an output cavity the bunched current induces a large RF voltage, delivering power. Intermediate cavities increase the bunching and the gain, which can exceed 40 dB. Klystrons are narrowband, because the cavities are resonant, but they reach very high power: multi-megawatt pulsed klystrons drive linear accelerators, and continuous-wave tubes of tens of kilowatts drive broadcast and industrial heating. The inductive output tube, a hybrid of a klystron and a triode, is common in UHF television transmitters.

H2: 32.3 Traveling-wave tubes
P: A traveling-wave tube slows an RF wave along a helix or a coupled-cavity structure until its phase velocity nearly matches the beam's. Over the length of the tube the wave and the beam exchange energy continuously, and the wave grows. Because the helix is not resonant, a TWT can amplify over an octave or more, with gains of 40 to 60 dB. Space TWTs add multistage depressed collectors that recover energy from the spent beam by slowing the electrons before they land, raising overall efficiency to 60% to 70%, more than a comparable solid-state amplifier at Ku and Ka band. That efficiency is why most high-power satellite transponders still use TWT amplifiers.
H2: Worked Example 32.2
P: Problem. The 5 kW beam of Example 32.1 delivers 2 kW of RF. A depressed collector recovers 60% of the 3 kW remaining in the spent beam. Find the overall efficiency.
P: Solution. The supply now provides 5000 − 0.6 × 3000 = 3200 W net, because the recovered energy returns to the collector supply. The efficiency rises from 40% to 2000 / 3200 = 62.5%, and the heat to be removed falls from 3 kW to 1.2 kW.
P: Key idea. A vacuum tube converts the kinetic energy of an electron beam into RF, so its power is limited by the beam, not by a semiconductor's breakdown field and junction temperature. Depressed collectors recover much of the energy the RF did not take.
H2: 32.4 Magnetrons and gyrotrons
P: The magnetron is an oscillator rather than an amplifier: electrons spiral in crossed electric and magnetic fields past a ring of resonant cavities and give up energy to the oscillation. It is cheap and efficient, about 65% to 75% at 2.45 GHz, which is why a microwave oven delivers about a kilowatt from a component costing a few dollars. Its frequency and phase are not well controlled, which limits it to heating and older radars. The gyrotron uses electrons gyrating in a strong magnetic field to generate megawatts at frequencies of 100 GHz and above, where conventional structures would be too small, and it heats the plasma in fusion experiments.
H2: Worked Example 32.3
P: Problem. A 250 W Ku-band satellite amplifier is built either as a TWT at 60% efficiency or as a solid-state amplifier at 40%. Find the DC power and the heat in each case.
P: Solution. TWT: 250 / 0.6 = 417 W from the bus, 167 W of heat. Solid state: 250 / 0.4 = 625 W, 375 W of heat. On a spacecraft, where every watt of solar array and radiator costs mass, the tube saves 208 W of DC power and more than halves the heat, outweighing its high-voltage supply and finite cathode life.
H2: 32.5 Tubes against solid state
P: Solid-state amplifiers win on lifetime, low voltage, graceful degradation, and cost at moderate power. Reaching 2 kW from 150 W GaN devices takes 16 of them and a combiner; with 0.5 dB of combining loss that yields about 2.14 kW, a large, complex assembly. Tubes win at the highest powers and frequencies, in high-efficiency space amplifiers, and in harsh radiation environments. The boundary keeps moving as GaN improves, but in 2026 it still lies around the kilowatt level at X band and above.
P: Where this continues. Chapter 45 follows GaN solid-state amplifiers into satellites, where they now replace traveling-wave tubes at the lower powers and frequencies.
BENCH: Tubes are the choice when the power is kilowatts to megawatts at high frequency, or when efficiency per kilogram in orbit decides. Their high-voltage supplies need serious respect: interlocks, discharge paths, and controlled access. Plan for cathode life, warm-up time, and the replacement cycle. Compare a tube with solid state on DC power and heat, not RF power, because the heat decides the size of the system.
