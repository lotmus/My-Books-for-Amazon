H1: Chapter 15. Ferrites, Circulators, and Isolators
P: Disconnect the antenna from a transmitter at full power and the whole forward wave comes back at the output transistor. Put an isolator in the path and the transistor barely notices: with a 3:1 antenna and 20 dB of isolation, the amplifier sees a VSWR of about 1.1. A magnetized piece of ferrite does what no network of resistors, capacitors, and inductors can. It lets power through one way and not the other.
P: Where you meet this: Radar and base-station duplexers, isolators after power amplifiers, test benches that protect expensive sources, and satellite transponders.
P: Every network in this book so far has been reciprocal: a signal travels from port 1 to port 2 exactly as it travels from port 2 to port 1, and S₂₁ equals S₁₂. Magnetized ferrite breaks that symmetry. In a ferrite the electron spins precess about the bias field in one direction only, so a wave that rotates with the precession sees a different material from one that rotates against it. That single fact gives the circulator, which sends power around its ports in one direction, and the isolator, which passes power forward and absorbs it backward. This chapter explains how they work, what their datasheet numbers mean, and how they protect amplifiers and share antennas.
H2: 15.1 Gyromagnetic resonance
P: An electron spin in a magnetic field precesses like a top at a rate proportional to the field: about 28 GHz per tesla. In a ferrite, a ceramic of iron oxide with other metal oxides such as yttrium iron garnet, the spins act together, and a microwave field rotating in the same sense as the precession is absorbed strongly near the resonance, while a field rotating in the opposite sense passes almost untouched. Devices operate a safe distance from resonance, where the two senses still see different permeabilities but the loss is low. The bias field comes from a small permanent magnet built into the device.
EQ: f_res = γ H,   γ ≈ 28 GHz/T
H2: Worked Example 15.1
P: Problem. Find the gyromagnetic resonance for an internal field of 0.1 T.
P: Solution. f_res = 28 GHz/T × 0.1 T = 2.8 GHz. A device for the 2.45 GHz band biased at 0.1 T would be too close to resonance to have low loss, so the designer chooses either a weaker field, operating below resonance, or a stronger field, operating above it. The choice is made by the manufacturer; the user meets it as the device's specified band.
H2: 15.2 The three-port circulator
P: The junction circulator is a ferrite disk between two ground planes, or under a microstrip conductor, with three lines meeting it at 120°. Biased correctly, the disk sets up a field pattern rotated so that power entering port 1 leaves at port 2, power entering port 2 leaves at port 3, and power entering port 3 leaves at port 1. An ideal circulator's S-matrix has zeros on its diagonal and ones in a rotating pattern; a real one has an insertion loss of a few tenths of a decibel and an isolation, the leakage in the wrong direction, of about 20 dB over its band.
EQ: S = [[0, 0, 1], [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0]]  (ideal circulator)
H2: 15.3 The isolator
P: Terminating port 3 of a circulator in a matched resistor makes an isolator. Power from the source passes from port 1 to port 2 and on to the load; any power the load reflects enters port 2, is routed to port 3, and is dissipated in the resistor instead of returning to the source. The source therefore sees a good match regardless of the load. The resistor must be rated for the reflected power, which in a fault, such as a disconnected antenna, is all the forward power.
H2: Worked Example 15.2
P: Problem. A 10 W amplifier drives an antenna whose VSWR is 3:1 through an isolator with 20 dB isolation. Find the power reflected by the antenna, the power dissipated in the isolator's termination, and the effective reflection coefficient the amplifier sees.
P: Solution. VSWR 3:1 gives |Γ| = (3 − 1)/(3 + 1) = 0.5, so 25% of the power, 2.5 W, is reflected. Nearly all of it goes to the termination, which must therefore handle at least 2.5 W and, for the open-antenna fault, the full 10 W. The leakage back toward the amplifier is 0.25 × 0.01 = 0.0025 of the forward power, an effective |Γ| of √0.0025 = 0.05 or a VSWR of 1.11. The amplifier sees nearly a perfect load, which keeps its gain, linearity, and stability as designed in Part II.
P: Key idea. Biased ferrite is non-reciprocal. A circulator routes power around its ports in one direction, and an isolator, a circulator with a terminated port, sends reflected power into a resistor instead of back to the source.
H2: 15.4 Circulators as duplexers
P: A circulator can let a transmitter and receiver share one antenna at the same frequency: transmitter at port 1, antenna at port 2, receiver at port 3. Two leakage paths limit it. The first is the circulator's own isolation from port 1 to port 3. The second is usually larger: the antenna's mismatch reflects part of the transmitted power back into port 2, and the circulator dutifully routes it to the receiver. The antenna's return loss therefore sets the isolation as much as the ferrite does, and radar and full-duplex systems add cancellation or a limiter in front of the receiver.
FIG: fig15_1.png | Figure 15.1 — A circulator as duplexer (Worked Example 15.3). Direct leakage through 20 dB of isolation is 10 dBm, but a 15 dB antenna return loss sends 15 dBm straight to the receiver.
H2: Worked Example 15.3
P: Problem. A 30 dBm transmitter feeds an antenna through a circulator with 20 dB isolation. The antenna's return loss is 15 dB. Estimate the power reaching the receiver port by each path.
P: Solution. Direct leakage is 30 − 20 = 10 dBm. The antenna reflection is 30 − 15 = 15 dBm, routed to the receiver with only the circulator's insertion loss. The reflection dominates, and 15 dBm is enough to compress or damage a low-noise amplifier. Improving the antenna's match to 25 dB return loss lowers that path to 5 dBm, which shows why the antenna is part of the isolation budget.
H2: 15.5 Practical limits
P: Ferrite devices are band-limited, typically to an octave or less, because the bias must stay in the low-loss region across the band. Their properties drift with temperature, since the ferrite's magnetization falls as it warms, and they can be detuned by nearby steel or magnets. High power heats the ferrite and can drive it nonlinear. At millimeter wave, and on chips where a magnet is not practical, designers turn to active circulators built from transistors or to time-modulated networks, which break reciprocity electronically at the cost of noise and linearity.
BENCH: An isolator protects only as well as its termination survives. Rate the load for the full forward power, because an open antenna sends all of it there. Check the temperature range and the magnet's sensitivity to nearby steel; mounting an isolator next to a ferrous bracket can shift its band. In a circulator duplexer the antenna's match sets the transmit-to-receive leakage, so the antenna belongs in the isolation budget.
