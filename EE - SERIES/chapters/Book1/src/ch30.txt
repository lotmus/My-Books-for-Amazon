H1: Chapter 30 — Thevenin and Norton Equivalents
P: Why do a car's headlights dim when the starter cranks? Because the battery isn't an ideal source. It has internal resistance, and the starter's large current drops voltage across it. Thevenin's theorem says every linear source looks like that from the outside: an ideal voltage behind a resistance.
P: Where you meet this: Battery internal resistance, the 50 Ω output of signal generators, sensor interfacing, and maximum power transfer in RF (Chapter 37).
H2: 30.1 Why Equivalent Circuits Matter
P: Plug a phone charger, a lamp, and a speaker into the same wall outlet, one after another, and none of them need to know anything about the power plant, the transformers, or the miles of wiring standing behind that outlet — each device only cares about two things: the voltage available, and how much that voltage sags once real current is actually drawn. A small hobby battery and a car battery can both be rated at the same voltage, yet behave very differently once a real load is attached — the car battery barely sags, the small one drops noticeably — so a source is not fully described by its open-circuit voltage alone; something else about it matters too, once current actually flows.
P: What actually matters, for anything you plan to plug into a complicated network of sources and resistors, is not the full internal circuit at all — it's the minimum information needed to predict exactly how that network will behave, for any load you might attach. A genuinely complicated network could have dozens of resistors and sources tangled together, and re-solving that entire internal system from scratch every time a different load — a different speaker, a different sensor — gets connected would be an enormous, and largely wasted, amount of repeated work, when only the load itself actually changed.
P: Thevenin's theorem makes a remarkable promise: no matter how complicated a linear network behind two terminals actually is, it behaves — as far as anything connected to those two terminals can tell — exactly like a single ideal voltage source in series with a single resistor. Reduce it to those two numbers once, and every future load calculation becomes almost trivial.
H2: 30.2 The Thevenin Equivalent
P: The Thevenin equivalent of a network, as seen from a pair of terminals, consists of a voltage source V_Th in series with a resistance R_Th, found as follows:
LI: V_Th is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals — the voltage you would measure across them with nothing connected (an open circuit), found using any of the analysis techniques from Chapters 26–29.
LI: R_Th is the equivalent resistance seen looking back into the terminals with every independent source deactivated — voltage sources replaced by short circuits (0 V, since an ideal voltage source has zero internal resistance and forces its terminal voltage regardless of current), and current sources replaced by open circuits (since an ideal current source forces its current regardless of voltage, which an open circuit — infinite resistance — trivially satisfies at zero current).
P: Once V_Th and R_Th are known, any load connected to the original terminals sees exactly the same voltage and current it would see connected to this much simpler two-element equivalent — a fact that is not obvious on its own terms, but follows rigorously from the superposition property of linear circuits (developed fully in Chapter 31).

H2: 30.3 The Norton Equivalent
P: The Norton equivalent is the source-transformed twin of the Thevenin equivalent: a current source I_N in parallel with the same resistance R_Th (often written R_N, though it is numerically identical to R_Th for the same network). I_N is the short-circuit current — the current that would flow if the two terminals were directly connected together — and it relates to the Thevenin quantities by Ohm's law applied to the equivalent circuit itself:
EQ: IN = VTh / RTh
P: Thevenin and Norton equivalents are always interchangeable via this relationship, and which one is more convenient to compute or to use downstream depends entirely on the specific problem — a network dominated by voltage sources often yields V_Th more directly; a network dominated by current sources often yields I_N more directly.
KEY: R_Th is always found the same way, regardless of which equivalent you ultimately want: deactivate every independent source (short voltage sources, open current sources) and find the resulting resistance looking into the terminals. This single resistance value serves both the Thevenin and Norton equivalents identically.
H2: 30.4 Maximum Power Transfer
P: A Thevenin equivalent makes a classical and practically important question easy to answer: given a fixed source with Thevenin resistance R_Th, what load resistance R_L draws the maximum possible power from it? The answer, the maximum power transfer theorem, is R_L = R_Th — the load resistance is matched exactly to the source's internal (Thevenin) resistance. At this matched condition, exactly half the total power generated by the source is delivered to the load, and the other half is dissipated internally within R_Th; this 50% efficiency ceiling is the price of maximizing delivered power rather than maximizing efficiency, and real power-delivery systems (where efficiency, not raw power transfer, is usually the goal) deliberately avoid matched-impedance operation for exactly this reason — a distinction worth keeping in mind once this series reaches power systems, where the two goals genuinely diverge.

WE: Worked Example 30.1
PROB: A network, viewed from terminals A-B, has an open-circuit voltage of 9.0 V and a short-circuit current of 3.0 A. Find its Thevenin and Norton equivalents, and the load resistance that draws maximum power.
SOL: V_Th = 9.0 V (given directly as the open-circuit voltage). R_Th = V_Th/I_N = 9.0/3.0 = 3.0 Ω (from the relationship between the open-circuit and short-circuit conditions). Norton equivalent: I_N = 3.0 A in parallel with R_N = 3.0 Ω. Maximum power transfer occurs when R_L = R_Th = 3.0 Ω, at which point the load voltage is exactly half of V_Th (by the voltage-divider relationship between R_Th and a matched R_L), i.e., 4.5 V, delivering P = V²/R_L = (4.5)²/3.0 = 6.75 W to the load.
H2: 30.5 Practical Measurement of Thevenin Parameters
P: The derivation above assumes you already know the network's internal composition well enough to compute V_Th and R_Th directly. In practice — testing an unfamiliar circuit board, or characterizing a physical device such as a battery or a sensor — both quantities can be measured directly, without any knowledge of what is inside the black box. V_Th is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals of interest, read using a voltmeter (whose own internal resistance is, ideally, so large that it draws negligible current and does not disturb the measurement). R_Th can then be found either from a separate short-circuit current measurement, via R_Th = V_Th/I_N as in Section 30.3, or — often more safely, since deliberately short-circuiting a real device can be risky — by connecting a known load resistance, measuring the resulting terminal voltage, and solving the resulting voltage-divider relationship for R_Th. This experimental determination of a network's Thevenin equivalent is exactly how a real battery's internal resistance is characterized in practice, and it is why a battery's terminal voltage sags under heavy load: the load resistance and the battery's own (generally small, but nonzero) internal resistance form exactly the voltage-divider situation this book has already developed the tools to analyze.
WE: Worked Example 30.2
PROB: A battery has an open-circuit terminal voltage of 9.0 V. When a 20 Ω load is connected, the terminal voltage drops to 8.0 V. Find the battery's internal (Thevenin) resistance.
SOL: The load and the battery's internal resistance form a voltage divider: V_load = V_Th × R_load/(R_load + R_Th). Substituting known values: 8.0 = 9.0 × 20/(20 + R_Th). Solving: (20 + R_Th) = 9.0 × 20/8.0 = 22.5, so R_Th = 2.5 Ω — a small but nonzero internal resistance, entirely typical of a real battery, and the direct physical reason its terminal voltage sags noticeably under a heavy enough load.
MIS: It is tempting to think maximum power transfer (R_L = R_Th) is always the design goal, since “maximum power” sounds unambiguously desirable. In most real power-delivery contexts it is the opposite of what you want: at the matched condition, the source itself dissipates exactly as much power internally as the load receives, a 50% efficiency ceiling that would be unacceptable for a power grid (where R_Th is deliberately kept as small as possible relative to the load, so that nearly all delivered power reaches the load rather than being wasted as heat inside the source). Maximum power transfer matters most in signal and communication contexts — antenna matching, for instance — where the absolute power levels are small and efficiency is a secondary concern to getting as strong a signal through as possible.
BENCH: Most function generators assume a 50 Ω load. Set one to 1 V, connect it to a high-impedance input, and you'll see about 2 V, because the generator's own 50 Ω no longer drops half the voltage. Check the generator's load or output-impedance setting before you doubt the circuit.
