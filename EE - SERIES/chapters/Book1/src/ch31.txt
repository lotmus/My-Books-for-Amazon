H1: Chapter 31 — Superposition and Linearity
P: Plug a guitar into an amplifier while the fridge compressor kicks in and you hear the music and a hum together. In a linear system, each cause produces its own effect and the effects simply add. That is superposition, and it is why the signal and the noise can be analyzed separately.
P: Where you meet this: Treating DC bias and AC signal separately, noise analysis, and any circuit with more than one source.
H2: 31.1 Linear Circuits
P: A circuit is linear if it is built entirely from linear elements: resistors (V = IR), capacitors and inductors (whose defining relationships, from Part I, are likewise linear), and independent sources. Diodes, transistors, and other devices met later in this series are generally not linear, and none of the techniques in this chapter apply to them directly without first restricting attention to a small enough operating range that their behavior can be approximated as linear — a technique this series returns to explicitly once those devices are introduced.
H2: 31.2 The Superposition Theorem
P: A resistor sits in a circuit fed by two separate sources at once — say a 10 V voltage source and a 4 A current source, both pushing current through the same resistor. Solving for the actual current through that resistor, with both sources active together, generally means setting up the full circuit and solving it as one system.
P: There is a shortcut, though, and it looks almost too good to be true. Turn off the 4 A source (replace it with an open circuit) and solve for the current from the 10 V source alone — say this comes out to 2.0 A. Turn the 10 V source back off instead (replace it with a short circuit) and solve for the current from the 4 A source alone — say this comes out to 1.5 A. Add those two separately-computed numbers together, 2.0 + 1.5 = 3.5 A, and — remarkably — that is the correct current through the resistor with both sources active simultaneously, exactly as if each source's contribution simply did not care that the other one was also present.
P: This should feel a little suspicious. Two sources acting on the same resistor at the same time could easily interact in complicated ways, one source's presence changing how the circuit responds to the other. Whether adding two separately-computed answers together like this actually gives the right total is not something to take for granted just because it worked once.
P: It works here for a specific reason: every equation used to solve this circuit — Ohm's law, KCL, KVL — is linear, meaning doubling a source doubles its effect, and the response to two sources acting together is exactly the sum of their individual effects. This is exactly the same reason forces and fields from multiple charges simply added back in Chapters 2 and 3 — a mathematical property of linear systems generally, not a coincidence specific to this one resistor.
P: This is the superposition theorem, stated formally: in any linear circuit containing multiple independent sources, the voltage or current at any point can be found by considering each independent source separately — deactivating all other independent sources exactly as in the Thevenin-equivalent method of Chapter 30 (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened) — solving the resulting simpler circuit for the contribution of that one source alone, and then algebraically summing the individual contributions from every source to get the total.
KEY: Superposition applies to voltages and currents, never to power. Power depends on voltage and current multiplied together (P = IV or P = I²R), and squaring or multiplying is not a linear operation — the power delivered by two sources acting together is generally not the sum of the powers each would deliver acting alone. Superposition is a technique for finding voltages and currents; power in a multi-source circuit must always be computed afterward, from the correctly superposed total voltage or current, never by adding individually-computed powers.
H2: 31.3 Why Superposition Works
P: Superposition is not a special trick specific to circuits — it is the same principle already met twice in this series: as the statement that Coulomb forces and electric fields from multiple charges simply add (Chapters 2 and 3), and it holds here for exactly the same underlying reason. Every equation used to solve a linear circuit — Ohm's law, KCL, KVL, the nodal and mesh equations built from them — is linear in the sources appearing in it, meaning if you scale every source by some factor, every resulting voltage and current scales by that same factor, and if you have two separate sets of sources active simultaneously, the resulting voltages and currents are exactly the sum of what each set would produce acting alone. This is a mathematical property of linear systems in general, and circuit theory is simply one particular, especially useful, arena in which it applies.
H2: 31.4 When Superposition Is (and Is Not) Worth Using
P: Superposition is rarely the fastest method for finding a single specific quantity in a circuit with many sources — nodal or mesh analysis, solved once for the whole circuit, is usually less work than solving the circuit repeatedly, once per source. Superposition earns its keep in two other situations: when you specifically want to know each source's individual contribution to some quantity (useful for understanding which source dominates a circuit's behavior, or for noise analysis later in this series, where different sources of unwanted signal need to be tracked separately), and as a conceptual tool for proving other results — the Thevenin equivalent's validity, for instance, rests directly on superposition.
WE: Worked Example 31.1
PROB: A resistor R carries current contributed by two independent sources. Acting alone (with the other source deactivated), a 10 V source produces 2.0 A through R. Acting alone, a 4 A current source produces 1.5 A through R in the same direction. Find the total current through R with both sources active.
SOL: By superposition, the total current is simply the sum of the two individual contributions (since both were found to flow in the same direction): I_total = 2.0 + 1.5 = 3.5 A. Had the two contributions been found to flow in opposite directions, they would instead have been subtracted, with the sign of each contribution (relative to a chosen reference direction) tracked explicitly throughout.

H2: 31.5 Dependent Sources and Superposition
P: This book has, so far, only used independent sources — voltage or current sources whose value is fixed, set from outside the circuit. Many practical circuits, and essentially every transistor small-signal model this series will introduce in its semiconductor-devices volume, also contain dependent (controlled) sources, whose value is instead proportional to some other voltage or current elsewhere in the same circuit. Superposition's deactivation rule applies only to independent sources — a dependent source must never be deactivated when superposing, since doing so would discard the very relationship (to whatever voltage or current controls it) that defines it, and that relationship generally still involves contributions from every independent source active in the problem. In practice, this means a circuit with dependent sources is solved by superposing over the independent sources only, while the dependent source's own defining equation is carried through, unmodified, in every one of the individual sub-problems.
WE: Worked Example 31.2
PROB: Acting alone, a 5.0 V independent source produces a current of 1.0 A through a resistor R, and this same current, through a dependent source's controlling relationship, produces a dependent voltage of 2.0 V elsewhere in the circuit (dependent source value = 2× the controlling current, in volts per ampere). A second independent source, a 2.0 A current source, acting alone (with the dependent source's relationship still active, only the independent 5.0 V source deactivated), produces 0.4 A through the same resistor R. Find the total current through R with both independent sources active, and the resulting dependent source value.
SOL: Superposing over the two independent sources only (the dependent source itself is never deactivated in either sub-problem): total current through R = 1.0 + 0.4 = 1.4 A, giving the dependent source's value as 2 × 1.4 = 2.8 V. Because that source's gain is linear and it stays active throughout, its own contributions would superpose too — 2×1.0 = 2.0 V from the first sub-problem and 2×0.4 = 0.8 V from the second, summing to the same 2.8 V — but summing the controlling current first, as above, is the more direct route.
MIS: Because superposition works cleanly for voltage and current, it is a natural (but incorrect) next guess that it should also work for power — that the total power dissipated in a resistor, with multiple sources active, is the sum of the powers each source would cause individually. It is not: power is proportional to the square of voltage or current, and (a + b)² ≠ a² + b² in general (there is a cross term, 2ab, left over). The correct procedure is always to superpose the voltages or currents first, add them algebraically to get the total, and only then compute power from that final total value — never to compute power source-by-source and add the results.
BENCH: Superposition only holds while the circuit stays linear. An amplifier near clipping, a diode switching on or an op-amp at its supply rail breaks it, and two signals that each look fine alone can intermodulate when applied together. If adding a second signal changes the first, you've left linear territory.
