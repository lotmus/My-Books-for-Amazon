H1: Chapter 6 — Conductors, Capacitance, and Dielectrics
P: A camera flash takes a few seconds to recharge and then empties itself in a thousandth of a second or less. A capacitor is what makes that possible: it collects charge slowly from a small battery and gives it back all at once. This chapter shows how two plates and an insulator store energy, and why the insulator matters as much as the plates.
P: Where you meet this: Decoupling capacitors beside every chip, the sensing layer of a touchscreen, camera flashes and defibrillators, and the memory cells of DRAM.
H2: 6.1 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium
P: Chapter 4 established that a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium has zero field inside it and all excess charge on its outer surface. Two further facts follow directly and are used constantly in circuit reasoning: the entire volume of a conductor in equilibrium is at a single, uniform potential (since E = 0 inside means V cannot change from point to point within it), and its surface is therefore an equipotential surface. This is precisely why we can speak of “the potential of a wire” or “the potential of a plate” as a single number, without needing to specify which point on that conductor we mean.
H2: 6.2 Capacitance Defined
P: Pour water into a narrow glass and the level climbs fast, rising noticeably after just a cupful. Pour the same cupful into a wide bucket instead, and the level barely moves — the same amount of water produces a much smaller rise. Charging an isolated conductor works the same way: add some charge and a voltage develops, resisting whatever charge you try to add next. Not every conductor resists equally, though — a large one accepts a great deal of charge before its voltage climbs noticeably, while a small one's voltage rises almost from the first bit of charge added.
P: Neither the charge on a device nor the voltage it has reached, taken alone, actually characterizes it — either number by itself describes only a single passing moment. The same 100 V could sit across a pair of conductors holding a tiny charge or an enormous one, entirely depending on which conductors they are. What does characterize the device, the way the glass's narrowness or the bucket's width characterizes it, is how much charge it takes to raise its voltage by a given amount.
P: Divide however much charge you put on by whatever voltage results, and that ratio turns out not to depend on how much charge you happened to use. Put 1.0 µC on some particular pair of conductors and a voltage of 1.0 V develops; put on 2.0 µC instead and the voltage is 2.0 V; put on 5.0 µC and the voltage is 5.0 V. Divide each charge by its own voltage and every pair gives back the same number, 1.0 µC/V, however much charge was actually used.
P: That fixed ratio is the device's capacitance, and any two conductors separated by an insulator (or vacuum) behave this way: place equal and opposite charges +Q and −Q on the two conductors, and a potential difference V develops between them, in a fixed ratio that depends only on the conductors themselves. Capacitance is defined as that ratio of charge to voltage:
EQ: C = Q / V
P: Capacitance is a purely geometric property of a given pair of conductors — it depends on their shape, size, separation, and the material between them, but not on Q or V individually, since doubling Q doubles V proportionally, leaving the ratio fixed. Its SI unit is the farad (F), equal to one coulomb per volt; a farad is an enormous unit for practical components, so real capacitors are almost always specified in microfarads (µF, 10⁻⁶ F), nanofarads (nF, 10⁻⁹ F), or picofarads (pF, 10⁻¹² F).
H2: 6.3 The Parallel-Plate Capacitor
P: The parallel-plate capacitor is the simplest and most instructive geometry, and its capacitance can be derived directly from the tools already built in this book. Two plates of area A, separated by distance d, carry charges +Q and −Q with surface charge density σ = Q/A on each. From Chapter 4, each charged sheet produces a field σ/(2ε₀) on either side; between the two plates, these fields add (they point the same direction there), while outside the plates they cancel. The field between the plates is therefore:
FIG: fig6_1.png | Figure 6.1 — A parallel-plate capacitor with a dielectric filling the gap between the plates.
EQ: E = σ / ε0 = Q / (ε0A)
P: Combining this with V = Ed from Chapter 5 and C = Q/V gives:
EQ: C = ε0 A / d
P: Capacitance increases with plate area (more room to store charge at a given field) and decreases with plate separation. Here is why: for a fixed charge, the field E = σ/ε₀ is unchanged by the gap size (check the formula above), so the voltage V = Ed shrinks right along with d. Dividing the same charge by a shrinking voltage, C = Q/V then increases as d shrinks. This trips up many students on a first pass, precisely because the intuitive-sounding “smaller gap, bigger field” story is not what is actually happening here.
H2: 6.4 Dielectrics
P: Filling the gap between the plates with an insulating material — a dielectric — rather than vacuum increases the capacitance by a factor κ, the dielectric constant (also called relative permittivity), a dimensionless number greater than 1 that depends on the material (roughly 1 for air, about 4–7 for common glass, and well over 1000 for some specialized ceramics used in high-value capacitors):
EQ: C = κ ε0 A / d
P: The physical mechanism is polarization: the dielectric's own molecules, whether permanently polar or only polarized in response to the applied field, align themselves so that their own small dipole fields (Chapter 3) partially cancel the applied field inside the material. A weaker net field for the same free charge Q means a smaller voltage V = Ed, and since C = Q/V, a smaller V at fixed Q means a larger C. Dielectrics also raise the voltage at which the capacitor breaks down (arcs across), which is often as important in practical component selection as the capacitance value itself, and this maximum safe voltage is what the finished part's datasheet lists as its rated or working voltage. Strictly, that is not quite the same thing as dielectric strength — dielectric strength is a property of the insulating material itself (measured in volts per unit thickness), while the rated voltage is the manufacturer's conservative, thickness-adjusted limit for the finished component, including a safety margin below where breakdown would actually occur.
H2: 6.5 Capacitors in Series and Parallel
P: Just as resistors combine in series and parallel (Chapter 8), so do capacitors, but — reflecting how capacitance is a fundamentally different kind of quantity from resistance — the combination rules come out reversed relative to resistors. Capacitors in parallel (both connected across the same pair of nodes — the junction points in a circuit diagram where two or more wires meet — so both see the same voltage) simply add:
EQ: Cparallel = C1 + C2 + …
P: which makes sense physically: connecting capacitors in parallel is equivalent to increasing the effective plate area, and capacitance from Section 6.3 scales directly with area. Capacitors in series (connected end to end, so each carries the same charge Q, with the voltages across each adding to the total) combine by the reciprocal-sum rule instead:
EQ: 1/Cseries = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …
P: which is the same functional form parallel resistors use, not series resistors — a fact worth committing to memory precisely because it is so easy to reach for the wrong rule by pattern-matching on the word “series” alone.
KEY: A capacitor stores energy, not just charge. The energy stored is U = ½CV² = ½QV = Q²/(2C). This stored energy is genuinely present in the electric field occupying the space between the plates — not a bookkeeping convenience — a fact that becomes important once you study energy flow in electromagnetic fields more generally.
WE: Worked Example 6.1
PROB: A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area 2.0 cm² separated by 0.10 mm of air. Find its capacitance, and the capacitance if the gap is instead filled with a dielectric of κ = 5.0.
SOL: Area in SI units: A = 2.0×10⁻⁴ m², d = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m. Air-filled: C = ε₀A/d = (8.85×10⁻¹²)(2.0×10⁻⁴)/(1.0×10⁻⁴) ≈ 17.7 pF. With the dielectric: C = κ × 17.7 pF = 88.5 pF — a fivefold increase, exactly matching κ.
WE: Worked Example 6.2
PROB: A 6.0 µF capacitor and a 3.0 µF capacitor are connected (a) in parallel and (b) in series. Find the equivalent capacitance in each case.
SOL: (a) Parallel: C = 6.0 + 3.0 = 9.0 µF — larger than either individual value, as parallel capacitance always is. (b) Series: 1/C = 1/6.0 + 1/3.0 = 1/6.0 + 2/6.0 = 3/6.0, so C = 2.0 µF — smaller than either individual value, as series capacitance always is. Notice this is exactly the opposite pattern from resistors, where series combinations are always larger and parallel combinations are always smaller than any individual value.
MIS: Because capacitors and resistors are both drawn the same way in a circuit diagram — components in a line are “in series,” components bridging the same two nodes are “in parallel” — it is a very natural mistake to apply the resistor combination formulas to capacitors by habit. Resistance and capacitance are related to voltage and charge in inverted ways (R = V/I versus C = Q/V), which is exactly why their series and parallel rules come out swapped relative to each other. When in doubt, rederive the rule from first principles — same charge for series capacitors, same voltage for parallel ones — rather than trusting memorized pattern-matching alone.
BENCH: Read the dielectric, not just the value. A 10 µF, 16 V ceramic capacitor with X5R or X7R dielectric may give only a few microfarads with 12 V across it; the datasheet's capacitance-versus-DC-bias curve is the one that matters. These Class 2 ceramics also drift with temperature and age. C0G (NP0) parts are stable, but only come in small values.
