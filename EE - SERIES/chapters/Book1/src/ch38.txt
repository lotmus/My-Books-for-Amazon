H1: Chapter 38 — Where This Series Goes Next
P: You now have the physics, the mathematics and the circuit theory. The rest of the series is what engineers build with them: amplifiers, chips, radios, power supplies and finished products. This short chapter is the map.
H2: 38.1 What This Book Has Built
P: This book took the physical definitions from Part I — voltage, current, resistance, capacitance, inductance — and built the systematic machinery needed to analyze any linear circuit built from them: Kirchhoff's two laws as the foundation; series/parallel reduction and dividers as the fastest tool when it applies; nodal and mesh analysis as universal methods for any resistive network; Thevenin and Norton equivalents for reasoning about a network's behavior independent of whatever load is eventually attached to it; superposition as both a practical technique and a conceptual justification for several of the results around it; first-order transients as the first genuinely time-dependent behavior in the book; and finally phasors and impedance, which extend every one of the DC techniques to sinusoidal steady-state AC circuits with only a change from real to complex arithmetic.
H2: 38.2 A Preview: Linearizing a Nonlinear Device
P: It is worth seeing, briefly and concretely, exactly how the next book's central technique connects to this one, rather than taking the connection entirely on faith. A diode's actual current-voltage relationship is strongly nonlinear — current grows roughly exponentially with voltage once the diode is forward-biased past a threshold, nothing like a resistor's straight-line V = IR. And yet, once a diode is biased at some fixed operating point (a fixed DC voltage and current, set by the rest of the circuit) and only a small additional signal is applied on top of that bias, the diode's response to that small extra signal is, to good approximation, linear — behaving, for that small signal only, exactly like an ordinary resistor whose value (called the diode's small-signal or dynamic resistance) depends on where along its curve the bias point happens to sit.
P: Concretely: if a diode's bias point sits at a current I_D, its small-signal resistance works out (from the exponential diode equation, derived properly in the next book) to r_d ≈ V_T/I_D, where V_T is a small, roughly fixed voltage (about 26 mV at room temperature). A small AC signal riding on top of the DC bias sees the diode as nothing more than this resistance r_d — and once that substitution is made, the entire nonlinear device has been replaced, for purposes of analyzing the small signal, by an ordinary linear resistor, dropped into an otherwise perfectly ordinary circuit built from the same resistors, capacitors, and sources Part III has spent twelve chapters learning to handle. Nodal analysis, Thevenin equivalents, phasors for an AC signal riding on the bias — every technique in this book applies directly to that small-signal picture, exactly as advertised.

WE: Worked Example 38.1 — A Genuine Preview
PROB: A diode is biased at a DC current of 1.0 mA, giving it a small-signal resistance of r_d = V_T/I_D = 0.026/0.001 = 26 Ω. This diode's small-signal model (a 26 Ω resistor) is placed in series with a 1.0 kΩ resistor across a small 10 mV AC signal source. Find the AC voltage appearing across the diode's small-signal resistance.
SOL: This is nothing more than a voltage divider (Chapter 27), applied to the diode's linearized small-signal model exactly as if it were any other resistor: V_diode = V_signal × r_d/(r_d + R) = 10 mV × 26/(26+1000) ≈ 10 × 0.0253 ≈ 0.25 mV. Every step here — identifying the divider, applying the ratio, computing the numeric answer — is drawn directly and without modification from Chapter 27 of this book. The only genuinely new idea, contributed by the next book in this series, was finding that 26 Ω small-signal resistance value in the first place, from the diode's underlying (and quite different-looking) nonlinear physics.
H2: 38.3 A Deliberate Limitation, and Why It Is Temporary
P: Every circuit in this book has been linear: built entirely from resistors, capacitors, inductors, independent sources, and (in Chapter 36) ideal transformers — elements whose defining relationships are all directly proportional, with no thresholds, no saturation, and no fundamentally different behavior in one direction of current or voltage versus the other. Real electronic circuits are built overwhelmingly from devices that are not linear at all: diodes, which conduct current in essentially only one direction; transistors, whose entire usefulness as amplifiers and switches comes from a deliberately engineered, strongly nonlinear response. None of the techniques in this book apply directly to such devices without modification.
P: This is not a gap left unaddressed — it is exactly where this series goes next. The next book covers semiconductor physics and devices from the ground up: how doping creates the PN junction at the heart of every diode; how bipolar and field-effect transistors amplify and switch; and how the same nonlinear devices, once understood physically, are analyzed using a small-signal linearization technique that — not coincidentally — reduces a nonlinear device, operating near some fixed bias point, back into exactly the kind of linear circuit this book already knows how to solve. Every technique in this book — Thevenin equivalents especially — reappears directly once that linearization is performed, which is precisely why this book's systematic circuit-analysis toolkit was worth building thoroughly and rigorously before moving on: it is not merely useful for the resistor-capacitor-inductor circuits examined explicitly here, but for the local, small-signal behavior of every nonlinear device this series goes on to study.
KEY: Nonlinear devices are not exceptions to this book's methods — they are this book's methods, applied locally. A transistor's small-signal model, introduced in the next volume, is itself a linear circuit (resistors, dependent sources, sometimes capacitors), and every technique from this book — nodal analysis, Thevenin equivalents, superposition — applies to that small-signal model exactly as presented here. The nonlinearity is handled once, in finding the right operating point and the right small-signal model; everything downstream of that is this book, again.
