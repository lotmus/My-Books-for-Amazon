H1: Chapter 9 — Magnetism and the Magnetic Field
P: An old CRT television steered a beam of electrons across its screen with magnetic coils, and holding a strong magnet near the glass smeared the colors (please don't). The same force that bent the beam spins every electric motor. It acts only on moving charge, and always at right angles to the motion.
P: Where you meet this: Electric motors and loudspeakers, Hall-effect current sensors and phone compasses, mass spectrometers, and the position sensors in brushless-motor drives.
H2: 9.1 A Different Kind of Field
P: Magnetism was known and used — in lodestones and compass needles — for well over a thousand years before anyone connected it to electricity at all. The connection, once made in the early nineteenth century, turned out to run so deep that magnetism and electricity are now understood as two faces of a single phenomenon, electromagnetism, rather than two separate forces that happen to interact. This chapter introduces the magnetic field on its own terms; the following two chapters show where magnetic fields come from and how electricity and magnetism interconvert.
P: Unlike the electric field, which exerts a force on any charge simply for being present, the magnetic field exerts a force only on charge that is moving (or, equivalently, on current). A stationary charge sitting in a magnetic field feels nothing at all from that field.
H2: 9.2 The Magnetic Force on a Moving Charge
P: A compass needle sitting on a table, doing nothing but pointing north, is already responding to a magnetic field — but the needle itself is not a moving charge, it is a tiny magnet aligning with the field the way a compass always does. Bring an ordinary charged object near a magnet instead, and hold it perfectly still: nothing happens to it at all. The electric field from Chapter 3 pushes on any charge just for sitting there; the magnetic field does not.
P: Set that same charge moving, though, and everything changes. A charge drifting past a magnet feels a real, measurable push — the same effect that channels the charged particles of the solar wind toward Earth's poles, lighting up the sky as the aurora. Whatever this force is, it exists only for charge that is actually moving.
P: So the question is not “what force does this field exert” in general, but “what force does it exert on a charge moving at a particular speed, in a particular direction”? The honest, slightly unsettling answer is that this force does not point along any of the obvious directions. It does not point along the charge's own direction of travel, and it does not point along the field direction either — unlike gravity, which pulls straight along the line to whatever is doing the pulling, or the electric force, which pushes straight along the line between two charges.
P: The force instead points off at a right angle to both the charge's velocity and the field, and its strength depends on how those two directions relate: full strength when velocity and field are perpendicular to each other, shrinking as the two line up, and vanishing entirely when velocity and field point along the same line.
P: A proton moving at 1.0×10⁶ m/s through a 0.10 T field, with its velocity at 90° to the field, feels a force of about 1.6×10⁻¹⁴ N. Angle the proton’s velocity down to 30° from the field instead, everything else unchanged, and the force drops to about 0.80×10⁻¹⁴ N — exactly half, since sin 30° is exactly half of sin 90°. Send the proton moving parallel to the field (0°), and the force drops to zero, no matter how fast the proton is moving or how strong the field is.
P: That force — perpendicular to both velocity and field, scaled by the sine of the angle between them — is the magnetic force, captured by a vector operation called the cross product. The force on a charge q moving with velocity v through a magnetic field B is given by:
EQ: F = q v × B
P: The × denotes a vector cross product, which is the reason the magnetic force behaves so differently from anything encountered so far in this book: its magnitude is F = qvB sinθ, where θ is the angle between v and B (maximal when velocity and field are perpendicular, zero when they are parallel), and — critically — its direction is always perpendicular to both v and B simultaneously, found using the right-hand rule: point the fingers of your right hand along v, curl them toward B, and your thumb points along v × B (adjust the sign for a negative charge, which experiences a force in the opposite direction).
KEY: A magnetic force can never do work on a charge. Because the magnetic force is always perpendicular to the charge's velocity, it can never have a component along the direction of motion, and force perpendicular to displacement does zero work. A magnetic field can change a charge's direction, but never its speed or its kinetic energy. Any speeding up or slowing down of a charged particle in a real device is always due to an electric field, even in devices — like motors — that are popularly described as “magnetic.”
H2: 9.3 Circular Motion in a Uniform Field
P: Because the magnetic force is always perpendicular to velocity and constant in magnitude for a fixed speed, a charged particle moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field travels in a perfect circle, with the magnetic force supplying exactly the centripetal force needed — the inward pull toward the center that any object moving along a curved path requires, whatever provides it:
EQ: qvB = mv2/r  ⟹  r = mv / (qB)
FIG: fig9_1.png | Figure 9.1 — A positive charge in a field pointing out of the page goes round clockwise. The force always points at the center, so it bends the path but never changes the speed.
P: This relationship is the operating principle behind mass spectrometers, cyclotrons, and — in a more elaborate form — the particle accelerators used in high-energy physics. It also explains why charged particles from the solar wind spiral around Earth's magnetic field lines rather than striking the atmosphere directly, redirected toward the poles where the field lines converge — the mechanism behind the aurora.
P: If the particle's velocity has a component along the field direction as well as perpendicular to it, the perpendicular component still produces circular motion exactly as described, while the parallel component is entirely unaffected by the field (since v × B vanishes when v is parallel to B). The combined motion is therefore a helix — a circle that also drifts steadily along the field direction — rather than a closed circle. This helical motion is exactly what channels charged particles along Earth's field lines toward the poles, and it is worth being able to picture clearly.
H2: 9.4 The Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Wire
P: A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field experiences a force, because the current is nothing more than a great many moving charges, each individually subject to F = qv × B. Summed over a straight wire segment of length L carrying current I in a uniform field B, the total force is:
EQ: F = I L × B
P: with magnitude F = BIL sinθ, where θ is again the angle between the wire (taken in the direction of conventional current flow) and the field. This is the force that turns the rotor of every electric motor: current-carrying wire loops placed in a magnetic field experience a torque that spins the motor's shaft, a device treated in full once this series reaches its power-and-machines material.
H2: 9.5 Units
P: The SI unit of magnetic field is the tesla (T), defined so that a charge of one coulomb moving at one meter per second, perpendicular to a one-tesla field, experiences a force of one newton. A tesla is a substantial field — Earth's own magnetic field at its surface is roughly 0.00005 T (50 µT), a small fridge magnet might reach 0.01 T, and MRI machines operate in the range of 1–3 T. An older, non-SI unit, the gauss (1 T = 10,000 G), still appears frequently in older texts and in some manufacturer datasheets, and is worth recognizing even though this book uses tesla throughout.
WE: Worked Example 9.1
PROB: A proton moves at 2.0 × 10⁶ m/s perpendicular to a 0.50 T magnetic field. Find the magnitude of the magnetic force on it, and the radius of its circular path.
SOL: F = qvB = (1.60×10⁻¹⁹)(2.0×10⁶)(0.50) ≈ 1.6×10⁻¹³ N. Radius: r = mv/(qB) = (1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg)(2.0×10⁶)/[(1.60×10⁻¹⁹)(0.50)] ≈ 0.042 m, about 4.2 cm.
WE: Worked Example 9.2
PROB: A straight horizontal wire 0.80 m long carries a current of 6.0 A due east. Find the magnitude and direction of the magnetic force on the wire if it sits in a uniform 0.40 T field pointing straight down (toward the ground).
SOL: Magnitude: F = BIL sinθ. The wire (east) and the field (down) are perpendicular, so θ = 90° and sinθ = 1: F = (0.40)(6.0)(0.80) = 1.92 N. Direction, by the right-hand rule for I L × B: point your fingers east (current direction), curl them downward (field direction) — your thumb points north. The force on the wire is therefore 1.92 N directed due north.
MIS: Because the magnetic force involves a cross product, its direction is one of the more error-prone parts of introductory electromagnetism, and a common shortcut error is to assume the force simply points “along” one of the two vectors involved, or splits the difference between them. It does neither: F = qv×B is always exactly perpendicular to both v and B, full stop, regardless of the angle between v and B (as long as that angle is not 0° or 180°, where the cross product vanishes entirely). There is no partial or intermediate direction to reason about — only perpendicular to both, with the right-hand rule fixing which of the two possible perpendicular directions is correct.
BENCH: Hall-effect sensors are the easy way to measure current without breaking the circuit, and they are what most clamp meters use for DC. They also read any stray field, so a sensor placed beside an inductor or a high-current trace measures that too. Datasheet accuracy figures usually assume no external field.
