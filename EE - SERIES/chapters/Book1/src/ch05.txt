H1: Chapter 5 — Electric Potential Energy and Voltage
P: A bird can perch on a 10,000-volt power line and stay perfectly calm. What matters is not how many volts something sits at but the difference in volts between two points. The bird's feet are at almost the same potential, so almost no current flows through it.
P: Where you meet this: Every multimeter reading (always between two probes), battery voltages, logic levels (a 3.3 V signal is 3.3 V relative to ground), and the electron-volt in Book 3.
H2: 5.1 From Force to Energy
P: Every conservative force has an associated potential energy, and the electric force is conservative — the work done moving a charge between two points does not depend on the path taken, only on the endpoints. This mirrors gravitational potential energy exactly, and the analogy is worth leaning on: as an object gains gravitational potential energy when lifted against gravity, a positive charge gains electric potential energy when pushed toward another positive charge, against the repulsive force between them.
P: For two point charges q and Q separated by distance r, the electric potential energy of the pair is:
EQ: U = kqQ / r
P: Notice the missing square on r, compared to Coulomb's law's force expression — potential energy for a 1/r² force always goes as 1/r, the same relationship gravitational potential energy (U = −GMm/r) has to gravitational force. U is positive for like charges (energy is stored by pushing them together, against their mutual repulsion) and negative for unlike charges (they attract, so energy is released as they come together, and you must supply energy to pull them apart).
H2: 5.2 Electric Potential (Voltage)
P: Every battery has a number printed on it — 1.5 V for an AA cell, 9 V for the boxy rectangular kind, 12 V for a car battery. That number gets used constantly without most people ever asking what it actually measures. It cannot be a raw measure of how much energy the battery can deliver in total: a 12 V car battery and a toy's pack of eight AA cells are both labeled 12 V, yet the car battery can do far more total work before it runs flat.
P: Total capacity, then, is not what that number on the label measures. What it captures instead is something else: for each individual bit of charge the battery pushes around a circuit, how hard is that particular push? A raw quantity like total stored energy tangles together two separate things — how much charge got moved, and how hard each unit of it was pushed — and there is no way to pull those two apart merely by looking at one lump energy figure.
P: The way to separate them is the same move Chapter 3 made for force: stop asking for the total, and divide by however much charge is actually involved. Take the energy a battery delivers while moving some amount of charge around a circuit, and divide that energy by the charge that moved.
P: A 9 V battery moving 1.0 C of charge around a circuit does 9.0 J of work — checkable straight from the definition, since 9 V literally means 9 joules per coulomb. Move only 0.10 C instead, and the work drops to 0.90 J: ten times less charge, ten times less energy. Divide the work by the charge moved in each case, though, and both give back 9.0 J/C. Move 0.010 C, and the work drops again, to 0.090 J — but divide, and the quotient is still 9.0 J/C.
P: That fixed quotient — energy delivered per unit charge moved — is the electric potential, more commonly called voltage, and it is a property of the battery (or, more precisely, of the two points it connects), not of however much charge happens to pass through it on a given occasion. Written as a general definition, electric potential is the potential energy per unit charge:
EQ: V = U / q0
P: Potential is a scalar, not a vector — a considerable practical simplification over working with fields directly, since scalars add algebraically with no direction bookkeeping. Its SI unit is the volt (V), equal to one joule per coulomb, and it is this quantity — not the field, and not the potential energy — that a voltmeter measures and that appears on every component datasheet and circuit diagram you will encounter for the rest of this series.
P: The potential due to a single point charge q, at distance r, taking the zero of potential to be at infinity (the conventional choice), is:
EQ: V = kq / r
KEY: Only differences in potential are ever physically meaningful. The choice of where V = 0 is a convention, not a physical fact — exactly like choosing sea level as the zero of height. What every circuit actually cares about is potential difference, the voltage between two points, which is why circuit diagrams always show voltage across a component, never a component's voltage in some absolute sense.
H2: 5.3 Relating Potential and Field
P: Potential and field are two descriptions of the same underlying physics, related by the fact that the field points in the direction potential decreases most steeply, and its magnitude is the rate of that decrease. In one dimension:
EQ: E = −dV/dx
P: For the simplest and most important practical case — a uniform field between two parallel plates separated by distance d, with potential difference ΔV between them — this reduces to:
EQ: E = ΔV / d
P: This equation is the workhorse relation for parallel-plate capacitors, taken up in the next chapter, and it is also the reason the field's units, newtons per coulomb, are numerically identical to volts per meter — both are legitimate units for E, and V/m is the one you will see far more often in practice.
H2: 5.4 Equipotential Surfaces
P: An equipotential surface is a surface on which every point has the same potential. Because moving a charge along an equipotential requires no work (there is no change in potential energy), electric field lines must always cross equipotential surfaces at right angles — if a field had any component along an equipotential surface, moving a charge along that direction would change its potential energy at constant potential, a contradiction. For a point charge, the equipotentials are concentric spheres; for a uniform field, they are flat planes perpendicular to the field.
FIG: fig5_1.png | Figure 5.1 — Field lines cross equipotential surfaces at right angles. | Concentric equipotential circles around a positive charge cross radial electric-field lines at right angles.
P: This right-angle relationship is a genuinely useful sketching tool: given a diagram of field lines for some charge distribution, you can sketch a reasonable set of equipotential contours by drawing curves everywhere perpendicular to the field lines you already have, and vice versa. Topographic maps use exactly this relationship, incidentally, with contour lines of constant elevation (the gravitational analog of equipotentials) always running perpendicular to the direction water would actually flow downhill.
H2: 5.5 Energy Stored in the Electric Field
P: It is natural, and correct, to think of the potential energy U = kqQ/r as belonging jointly to the pair of charges, rather than to either charge individually — after all, U depends on both charges and vanishes if either one is removed. A more modern and, for this series' purposes, more useful way to think about that same energy is that it is stored in the electric field occupying the space around the charges, at an energy density (energy per unit volume) proportional to E². This field-centered accounting becomes indispensable once fields exist without any nearby charge to “belong” to — as in an electromagnetic wave, where energy propagates through empty space with no charges present at all. Chapter 6 develops the field-energy picture concretely for the case of a capacitor, where it becomes possible to compute a definite, measurable quantity: the total energy stored between two charged plates.
WE: Worked Example 5.1
PROB: Two parallel plates 2.0 mm apart are held at a potential difference of 100 V. Find the electric field between them, and the potential energy change of an electron moved from the negative plate to the positive plate.
SOL: The field magnitude is E = ΔV/d = 100 V / 0.0020 m = 5.0 × 10⁴ V/m. Moving an electron (charge −e) from the negative plate to the positive plate means moving it against the field's push on a positive charge — but the electron is negative, so the field actually assists this motion, and the electron's potential energy decreases. Numerically, ΔU = qΔV = (−1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C)(100 V) = −1.60×10⁻¹⁷ J: the electron loses 1.60×10⁻¹⁷ J of potential energy, which reappears as kinetic energy — exactly the mechanism that accelerates electrons in a cathode-ray tube or a particle accelerator.
WE: Worked Example 5.2
PROB: Find the electric potential at a point midway between a +6.0 nC charge and a −6.0 nC charge, separated by 8.0 cm.
SOL: Potential is a scalar, so it adds algebraically (no vectors needed) — a considerable simplification compared to the equivalent field calculation. Each charge is 4.0 cm from the midpoint. V₁ = kq/r = (8.99×10⁹)(6.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.040) ≈ 1348 V (from the positive charge). V₂ = kq/r = (8.99×10⁹)(−6.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.040) ≈ −1348 V (from the negative charge). Net potential: V₁ + V₂ = 0 V. Contrast this with Worked Example 3.2, which found a nonzero field at the same midpoint of an equal-and-opposite dipole — potential and field do not have to vanish (or fail to vanish) at the same locations, since one is a scalar sum and the other a vector sum, and this particular symmetric case happens to make the potential exactly zero everywhere on the entire perpendicular bisector plane, not only at the midpoint.
BENCH: Voltage is always between two points, so always know where your black lead is. An ordinary oscilloscope's ground clip is tied to mains earth through its power cord. Clip it onto a node that isn't at earth potential, such as the high side of a half-bridge or anywhere in a non-isolated supply, and you short that node to earth, sometimes spectacularly. Use a differential probe or an isolated measurement instead.
