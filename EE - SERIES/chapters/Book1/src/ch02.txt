H1: Chapter 2 — Coulomb's Law and the Electric Force
P: Why doesn't your hand sink into the table? Atoms are mostly empty space, after all. Largely because the electrons in your skin and in the table push each other away with the force in this chapter (quantum mechanics helps), and at atomic distances that force is enormous.
P: Where you meet this: Electrostatic spray painting and dust precipitators, MEMS electrostatic actuators, and the forces that hold every molecule, and therefore every solder joint, together.
H2: 2.1 Stating the Law
P: The charged balloon from Chapter 1 does more than stick to the ceiling. Bring a second charged balloon near it and, depending on how each one was charged, they either swing apart or pull together — the same electric force that stuck the first balloon to the ceiling, now acting between two balloons instead of one. Move the two balloons further apart and the push, or pull, visibly weakens; bring them closer and it visibly strengthens.
P: By how much, though? Twice the distance — is the push half as strong, a quarter, an eighth? Nothing about rubbing two balloons and feeling them push apart tells you the answer. A feel for “weaker further away” is not the same as a number that lets you predict a force you have not yet measured, and predicting exact forces, not just their direction, is what makes a chapter like this one worth having.
P: The honest obstacle is that answering this needs an actual measurement, and the force between two everyday charged objects is far too small to feel by hand or register on any ordinary scale. You cannot rest two charged balloons against a bathroom scale and read off the push in newtons; whatever is pushing them apart is real, but it sits far below what your hands or an ordinary instrument can detect directly.
P: The way round it is to trade a force too small to feel for a twist too small to miss. Hang a light rod from one end of a long, thin fiber, so that even a very gentle sideways push on the far end of the rod visibly twists the fiber — the thinner and longer the fiber, the more even a tiny force winds it. Fix a charged object to that end of the rod, bring a second charged object near it without touching, and the amount of twist, converted back into a force through how stiff the fiber is separately known to be, gives a real number for a push far too small to feel by hand.
P: Do this carefully at several separations, with the same two charges each time, and a clean pattern falls out. Two charges of +1.0 µC, 10 cm apart, push each other with a force of about 0.90 N. Move them to 20 cm apart — twice the separation — and the force drops to about 0.22 N, roughly a quarter of what it was. Move them to 30 cm — three times the original separation — and the force drops further, to about 0.10 N, roughly a ninth of the original.
P: Notice what the numbers never do: they never fall in proportion to the distance itself. Doubling the separation quarters the force; tripling it cuts the force to a ninth — the force is falling as the square of the distance, not the distance itself. Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured exactly this relationship in 1785, using precisely the twisting-fiber trick above, sensitive enough to catch forces far smaller than the weight of the charged spheres producing them. The result is Coulomb's law: the electric force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them:
EQ: F = k q1q2 / r2
P: where q₁ and q₂ are the magnitudes of the two charges, r is the distance between them, and k is the Coulomb constant:
EQ: k = 8.99 × 109 N·m2/C2
P: The force is directed along the line connecting the two charges. If the charges have the same sign, the force is repulsive; if opposite, attractive. This is a vector equation in disguise — vector, meaning a quantity carrying both a size and a direction, unlike the plain numbers used so far — writing it as a magnitude is convenient, but you must always track direction separately, especially once more than two charges are involved.

P: The Coulomb constant is often written in terms of a more fundamental quantity, the permittivity of free space, ε₀:
EQ: k = 1 / (4πε0) ,   ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 C2/(N·m2)
P: This looks like an unnecessary complication when you are only dealing with two point charges, but the 4π factor is there so that later results — Gauss's law in particular — come out clean. You will see the payoff in Chapter 4.
H2: 2.2 The Inverse-Square Behavior
P: The 1/r² dependence is the same functional form that governs Newton's law of gravitation, and for the same underlying geometric reason: both forces are carried by something spreading outward from a point source over the surface of an ever-expanding sphere, and the surface area of a sphere grows as r², so the intensity of anything spread evenly over that surface falls as 1/r². This geometric argument is made precise in Chapter 4 and is one of the most useful pieces of physical intuition you will carry into the rest of this series — the same 1/r² falloff will reappear when you study radiation from antennas.
KEY: Doubling the distance between two charges cuts the force to one quarter. Tripling the distance cuts it to one ninth. This scaling, not the specific value of k, is the fact worth internalizing.
H2: 2.3 Superposition
P: When more than two charges are present, the net force on any one of them is simply the vector sum of the Coulomb forces exerted by every other charge individually, as if each pair existed alone. This is the principle of superposition, and it holds because Coulomb's law is linear in each charge — nothing about the presence of a third charge alters the force law between the first two.
P: Superposition sounds almost too obvious to state, until you notice how much it is quietly assumed. It is not, in fact, a logical necessity — one could imagine a universe where three charges interact through some genuinely three-body force that cannot be decomposed into pairwise contributions, and such three-body effects do arise in more exotic physical settings. That electromagnetism happens not to work that way is an experimental fact, not a mathematical inevitability, and it is one of the reasons circuit analysis is tractable at all: without superposition, adding a second component to a circuit could, in principle, change how every existing component behaved with every other, rather than simply adding its own well-defined contribution.
WE: Worked Example 2.1
PROB: Charges of +3.0 µC and +5.0 µC sit on the x-axis at x = 0 and x = 4.0 cm. Find the net force on a +2.0 µC charge placed at x = 2.0 cm.
SOL: The test charge is 2.0 cm from each source charge. The force from the +3.0 µC charge is repulsive, pushing the test charge in the +x direction: F₁ = k(3.0×10⁻⁶)(2.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.020)² ≈ 135 N. The force from the +5.0 µC charge is repulsive as well, pushing in the −x direction: F₂ = k(5.0×10⁻⁶)(2.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.020)² ≈ 225 N. These act along the same line in opposite directions, so the net force is 225 − 135 = 90 N, directed toward x = 0 (the test charge is pushed harder by the larger, 5.0 µC charge, so the net push is toward the smaller charge's side, i.e., toward x = 0).
WE: Worked Example 2.2
PROB: Three identical charges of +4.0 µC sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle with sides of 10 cm. Find the magnitude of the net force on any one of the charges.
SOL: By symmetry, the force from each of the other two charges has the same magnitude: F = kq²/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁶)²/(0.10)² ≈ 14.4 N. These two forces act along the two sides of the triangle meeting at the corner in question, separated by the triangle's interior angle of 60°. Adding them as vectors (using the law of cosines, or equivalently resolving into components), the net force magnitude works out to 2F cos(30°) ≈ 2(14.4)(0.866) ≈ 24.9 N, directed straight out along the triangle's line of symmetry through that corner, away from the triangle's center.
H2: 2.4 When Coulomb's Law Stops Being Enough
P: Coulomb's law as stated applies exactly to point charges (or equivalently, spherically symmetric charge distributions, measured from outside the sphere) that are not accelerating. Real charge distributions — a charged rod, a charged disk, the electron cloud of an atom — must be handled by dividing the distribution into infinitesimal point charges and integrating. That machinery is exactly what Gauss's law, introduced in Chapter 4, is built to avoid having to do by brute force whenever the charge distribution has enough symmetry.
MIS: Students frequently write Coulomb's law with the separation r substituted as if it were always measured from a fixed origin, rather than the actual distance between the two charges in question. Once you move beyond a single pair of charges arranged conveniently on an axis, r for any given pair is always the straight-line distance between that specific pair, recomputed freshly for each pair under consideration — not a coordinate borrowed from somewhere else in the problem.
BENCH: Sanity-check the exponent before you question the physics. With k ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C², two 1 nC charges a millimeter apart push with about 9 mN. If your answer for small charges comes out in kilonewtons, a nano or micro went missing somewhere, which is by far the most common mistake in this chapter.

Set up (you need not fully solve) the equation you would use to find q, identifying every force acting on one sphere.
