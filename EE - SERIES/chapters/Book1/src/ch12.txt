H1: Chapter 12 — Toward Maxwell's Equations and the Rest of This Series
P: Your phone talks to a tower kilometers away without a wire. Maxwell worked out in the 1860s why that is possible: a changing electric field makes a magnetic field, a changing magnetic field makes an electric field, and the pair can leave together at the speed of light.
P: Where you meet this: Every radio, Wi-Fi router and phone antenna, optical fiber, microwave oven, and the signal-integrity effects of Books 4 and 7.
H2: 12.1 Gathering the Pieces
P: This book has built up electricity and magnetism largely as two separate stories that occasionally touch — first electrostatics (Chapters 1–6), then steady currents and resistance (Chapters 7–8), then magnetostatics (Chapters 9–10), then the one genuine crossover result, electromagnetic induction (Chapter 11). James Clerk Maxwell's achievement, completed in 1865, was to recognize that four equations — two of which you have already met by name — form a complete, self-consistent description of every electric and magnetic phenomenon covered in this book, and a great many that are not.
H2: 12.2 Maxwell's Four Equations, Informally Stated
LI: Gauss's law for electricity (Chapter 4): electric field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge, or extend to infinity when no opposite charge is present; the total flux through any closed surface measures the net enclosed charge.
LI: Gauss's law for magnetism (not previously stated by this name, but implicit in every field-line diagram drawn in Chapters 9–10): magnetic field lines never begin or end anywhere — there is no such thing as an isolated magnetic “charge,” or magnetic monopole. Every magnetic field line that leaves a region must loop back into it somewhere.
LI: Faraday's law (Chapter 11): a changing magnetic flux induces an electric field (which in a conducting loop drives a current, but the induced electric field exists even without a wire present to carry current).
LI: Ampere's law, with Maxwell's own correction (a term beyond Chapter 10's scope, called the displacement current): a changing electric flux, in addition to actual current, also produces a magnetic field.
P: It is that final correction — the displacement current, Maxwell's own contribution to a law that already bore Ampere's name — that completes the symmetry between the third and fourth equations and, in doing so, predicts something neither Faraday nor Ampere had any reason to expect: a changing electric field creates a magnetic field, which if itself changing creates an electric field, which if changing creates another magnetic field, and so on indefinitely, each one regenerating the next without needing any charge or current physically present to sustain it.
H2: 12.3 Electromagnetic Waves and Light
P: Maxwell worked out that this self-sustaining oscillation propagates through empty space at a speed determined entirely by ε₀ and μ₀ — the same two constants that appeared, respectively, in Coulomb's law (Chapter 2) and in the field of a straight wire (Chapter 10), each originally measured in completely unrelated laboratory experiments involving static charges and steady currents:
EQ: c = 1 / √(ε0μ0)

The electric field, in red, oscillates vertically; the magnetic field, in blue, oscillates horizontally, in phase with the electric field. P: Plugging in the values from Chapters 2 and 10 gives c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s — the measured speed of light, to within experimental precision, computed from two constants that had nothing to do with optics. Maxwell concluded, correctly, that light itself is an electromagnetic wave: a self-propagating oscillation of electric and magnetic fields, and that visible light is merely the narrow band of electromagnetic radiation that happens to be detectable by the human eye. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all the same physical phenomenon at different frequencies — a fact this series returns to in full when it reaches antennas, RF engineering, and wave propagation.
P: It is worth appreciating how strange this result must have seemed at the time. Nothing about Coulomb's careful measurements of the force between charged pith balls, and nothing about Ampere's measurements of the force between current-carrying wires, had anything ostensibly to do with light. Maxwell's equations were assembled entirely from static- and steady-current experiments, plus Faraday's induction results, and yet the mathematics of that assembled system — quite without being asked to — produced a wave equation whose predicted propagation speed matched light exactly. This is among the most celebrated instances in the history of physics of a purely mathematical consequence of an existing theory turning out to describe something no one had built the theory to explain in the first place, and it is difficult to overstate how strong a piece of evidence this was, historically, that Maxwell's four equations were not merely a convenient summary of known results but a genuinely complete and correct description of electromagnetism.
KEY: Every subsequent book in this series is, in a real sense, a set of special cases of Maxwell's four equations. DC circuit theory is Maxwell's equations in the limit where nothing changes in time. AC circuit theory is Maxwell's equations for fields that oscillate slowly enough that a circuit's physical dimensions are much smaller than the wavelength involved. RF and antenna theory is what happens once that assumption stops holding. Semiconductor device physics governs how real materials respond to the fields Maxwell's equations describe. There is, in an important sense, only one subject in this entire series — this book has built it from the ground up.
H2: 12.4 A Historical Aside: Waves Need Something to Wave
P: Nineteenth-century physicists, having only ever encountered waves that traveled through a physical medium — sound through air, ripples through water — found it almost unthinkable that an electromagnetic wave could travel through genuinely empty space with nothing there to do the waving. This led to decades of searching for the “luminiferous aether,” a hypothesized medium presumed to fill all of space specifically so that light would have something to be a wave in. The most famous search for it, the Michelson-Morley experiment of 1887, attempted to detect Earth's motion through this aether by comparing the speed of light in different directions, and found — to the considerable surprise of everyone involved — no difference whatsoever. Einstein's special theory of relativity of 1905 resolved the puzzle by taking the speed of light to be the same for every observer, in every direction; historians still debate how much the Michelson-Morley result itself shaped his thinking. Light needs no medium: it is a self-sustaining disturbance in the electric and magnetic fields themselves, exactly as Maxwell's equations describe. The aether was never found because there was never anything there to find — but the search for it, and its eventual resolution, is a useful reminder that even a spectacularly successful theory (and Maxwell's equations remain, in their domain, exactly correct today) can still coexist for decades with a widely-held, entirely wrong assumption about what that theory actually implies.
H2: 12.5 Where This Book Goes Next
P: Part III of this book, starting at Chapter 26, takes the definitions built here — charge, current, voltage, resistance, capacitance, and (in the closing sections of Part I) inductance and induced EMF — and develops the systematic tools (Kirchhoff's laws, Thevenin and Norton equivalents, phasor analysis for AC) needed to analyze real circuits of arbitrary complexity. From there the series runs through circuits, components, and control (Book 2, including digital logic), semiconductor physics and devices (Book 3), RF, microwave, and transceivers (Book 4), communications, wireless, and SDR (Book 5), power and energy (Book 6), and packaging, layout, EMC, and test (Book 7). Books 8 and 9 then cover firmware and software for electronics, and test and measurement instruments. Together, the nine books map the working vocabulary of a practicing electrical engineer. Every one of those later books assumes the physical vocabulary built in Part I of this one: charge, field, potential, current, resistance, capacitance, magnetic field, and induction. If any of it still feels unfamiliar, this is the section to return to.
