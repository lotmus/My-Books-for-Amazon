PART: Part I — The Physics of Electricity and Magnetism
H1: Chapter 1 — Electric Charge
P: Shuffle across a carpet on a dry winter day, reach for a door handle, and you get a small lesson in this chapter: around a microcoulomb of charge, several thousand volts, and a spark you can feel. The charge is tiny. The voltage is not, which is why the same spark can quietly kill a chip.
P: Where you meet this: ESD wrist straps and anti-static bags, touchscreens that sense the charge on your finger, laser printers, and every battery capacity figure, since milliampere-hours are a unit of charge.
H2: 1.1 What Charge Is
P: Electric charge is one of a small number of properties, alongside mass, that a particle can carry without that property being made of anything more fundamental. You cannot explain charge in terms of something simpler; you can only describe what it does. What it does is this: particles with charge exert forces on other particles with charge, and those forces are enormously stronger than gravity. Two protons separated by a meter repel each other with a force about 10³⁶ times stronger than they attract each other gravitationally. Electromagnetism, not gravity, is the force that dominates chemistry, materials, and — for our purposes — every circuit ever built.
P: It is worth pausing on just how strange this is. Gravity is the force most people build their physical intuition around, because it is the only fundamental force whose effects are obvious in daily life without any special equipment. But gravity is, by a spectacular margin, the weakest of the four fundamental forces. The only reason gravity dominates the large-scale structure of the universe — planets, stars, galaxies — is that ordinary matter is, to very high precision, electrically neutral: the positive and negative charges in any large object nearly perfectly cancel, leaving gravity, weak as it is, as the only force with nothing to cancel it out.
P: Get that cancellation even slightly wrong — charge a balloon by rubbing it on your hair — and the electric force wins over gravity by such a wide margin that the balloon sticks to the ceiling, plainly overpowering the gravitational pull of an entire planet.
P: Charge comes in two kinds, arbitrarily labeled positive and negative by Benjamin Franklin in the 1740s, before anyone knew what was actually moving inside a wire. Franklin guessed wrong about which kind of charge does the moving in metals — conventional current, the direction we still use in circuit diagrams today, points the opposite way electrons actually travel — but the labels stuck, and the sign convention is now so deeply embedded in circuit theory that no one has seriously proposed fixing it. You will get used to the fact that current “flows” one way while the electrons carrying it move the other.
H2: 1.2 The Elementary Charge and Quantization
P: Charge is quantized: it comes only in integer multiples of a smallest unit, the elementary charge, denoted e. Every free particle you will ever measure carries a charge that is a whole-number multiple of e (quarks are the exception, but they never appear alone outside of a nucleus, so this does not affect circuit-level physics).
EQ: e = 1.602 × 10−19 C
P: The proton carries charge +e; the electron carries charge −e. A neutral atom has equal numbers of each and so carries no net charge. When we talk about a material becoming “charged,” we mean it has acquired a surplus or deficit of electrons relative to protons — the protons themselves are locked in the nucleus and essentially never move in ordinary electrical processes.
KEY: Charge is conserved. The total charge in an isolated system never changes. Charge can move from one object to another, and positive and negative charge can be created or destroyed together in equal amounts (as happens in particle physics), but the net total is always constant. This single fact underlies Kirchhoff's current law, which you will meet in Part III of this book (Chapter 26).
H2: 1.3 The Coulomb
P: The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C), defined operationally through electric current: one coulomb is the charge transported past a point by a steady current of one ampere — informally, roughly the current a bright flashlight bulb draws; the formal definition waits until Chapter 7 — flowing for one second. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the elementary charge is fixed at exactly e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and the ampere is defined from it, so one coulomb is the charge of about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ elementary charges. Before 2019 the ampere was defined through the force between two current-carrying wires, which is why older books describe the coulomb as a unit derived from the ampere rather than from counting charges.
P: A coulomb is a very large amount of charge at the scale of individual particles: it corresponds to about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ elementary charges. In practical circuits you will almost never move a full coulomb through a wire in a hurry — a typical LED might draw twenty milliamps, meaning it moves about 0.02 coulombs of charge every second. Getting a feel for this scale early pays off: when a later chapter states that a certain wire carries a current of a few amperes, you should be able to picture, at least roughly, the staggering number of individual electrons that represents moving past any given cross-section every second, even though — as Chapter 7 will make clear — none of them individually moves especially fast.
WE: Worked Example 1.1
PROB: A balloon rubbed on hair acquires a charge of − 8.0 nC. How many excess electrons does it carry, and what fraction is this of the roughly 10²³ atoms in a typical balloon's rubber?
SOL: n = Q/e = (8.0×10⁻⁹)/(1.602×10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 5.0×10¹⁰ excess electrons. Comparing to the ~10²³ atoms making up the balloon's rubber, this is a fraction of roughly 5×10¹⁰/10²³ ≈ 5×10⁻¹³ — about one atom in two trillion ends up carrying an extra electron. A charge easily strong enough to stick a balloon to the ceiling involves rearranging only a vanishingly small fraction of the material's total electrons — the same “spectacular margin” theme from Section 1.1: it takes only the tiniest imaginable imbalance for the electric force to overwhelm gravity entirely.
H2: 1.4 Charging by Friction, Contact, and Induction
P: Objects acquire net charge through several everyday mechanisms, all of which amount to moving electrons from one material to another.
LI: Friction (triboelectric charging): rubbing two different insulating materials together strips electrons from one surface and deposits them on the other. A balloon rubbed on hair becomes negatively charged because it pulls electrons off the hair more readily than the hair pulls them back.
LI: Contact charging: touching a charged conductor to a neutral one allows charge to redistribute across both until they reach a common electric potential (a concept formalized in Chapter 5).
LI: Charging by induction: bringing a charged object near a neutral conductor, without touching it, causes the conductor's own free electrons to redistribute — piling up on the near side if the inducing charge is positive, leaving a net positive region on the far side. Grounding the far side — connecting it by a wire to the earth, or to any large neutral body, giving surplus electrons somewhere to drain to or be drawn from — while the inducing charge is still present, then removing the ground and finally the inducing charge, leaves the conductor with a net charge opposite in sign to the one that induced it.
P: These three mechanisms look different in practice but share a single underlying idea: nothing is ever created or destroyed, only moved. It is worth checking, for each of the three, that you could point to exactly where the electrons involved came from and where they ended up — an exercise that pays dividends once Kirchhoff's current law asks you to do the same bookkeeping, far more precisely, for entire circuit networks.
H2: 1.5 Conductors and Insulators, Revisited at the Charge Level
P: The difference between a conductor and an insulator is entirely a difference in how tightly a material's electrons are bound. In a conductor — copper, aluminum, silver, most metals — the outermost electrons of each atom are shared collectively across the whole material rather than staying attached to individual atoms, forming what is often called a “sea” of free electrons. These electrons move readily in response to even a small electric field, which is exactly what makes a conductor useful for carrying current. In an insulator — rubber, glass, most plastics — every electron stays bound to its parent atom or molecule, and only a catastrophic field (well beyond anything found in ordinary electronics) will tear enough of them loose to allow appreciable current.

P: Semiconductors sit between these extremes, and the entire discipline of solid-state electronics exists because that middle ground can be controlled precisely through doping — a subject taken up in full in the semiconductor-devices volume of this series.
MIS: It is tempting to think of a “fully charged” insulator as somehow saturated, unable to hold any more charge. In reality, an insulator's inability to conduct has nothing to do with how much charge it is holding — it is about whether the charge it already has is free to redistribute. A heavily charged insulating rod holds its charge in whatever pattern it was deposited in (often quite unevenly), precisely because the charge cannot move to spread out, unlike on a conductor, where excess charge always redistributes itself until it reaches electrostatic equilibrium (Chapter 6).
BENCH: Treat every bare circuit board as something you can destroy by touching it. The human-body model used in ESD testing is 100 pF behind 1.5 kΩ, and a sensitive MOSFET gate can be damaged by a discharge well below the roughly 3 kV it takes for you to feel one. Wear a grounded wrist strap, keep boards in shielding bags, and remember that a part which worked yesterday and is dead today may have been zapped rather than born defective.
