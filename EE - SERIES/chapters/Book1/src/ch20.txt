H1: Chapter 20 — Trigonometry and the Sinusoid
P: The wall socket delivers a sine wave because a generator turns at constant speed, and a coil rotating in a magnetic field produces a sine, not a square or a triangle. Nearly every signal you will meet can be built from sines, so this chapter is less about triangles than about waves.
P: Where you meet this: Mains power (60 Hz in North America, 50 Hz in most of the world), audio tones, clock jitter, and every phase measurement on an oscilloscope.
P: Alternating current is sinusoidal, and so is every signal that can be built from alternating currents — which, as a later book on Fourier analysis shows, is every signal whatsoever. The sinusoid is therefore not one waveform among many; it is the waveform, and the trigonometry describing it is worth genuine fluency.
H2: 20.1 Angles, Degrees, and Radians
P: An angle in radians is the arc length subtended divided by the radius. A full circle is 2π radians, so 180° = π rad, and the conversion is a simple proportion. Radians are used throughout analysis because the calculus of trigonometric functions is clean only in radians — the derivative of sin x is cos x only when x is in radians, picking up an awkward factor of π/180 otherwise.
P: Degrees survive in practice because they are convenient for phase, where quarter and half cycles matter and 90° reads more naturally than π/2. Both appear constantly, sometimes in the same equation, and the single most common cause of an inexplicably wrong answer in phase calculations is a calculator left in the wrong angular mode.
H2: 20.2 The Sinusoid and Its Parameters
P: A general sinusoid is written
EQ: v(t) = V_m·sin(ωt + φ)
P: with three independent parameters. V_m is the amplitude, the peak departure from zero. ω is the angular frequency in radians per second, related to ordinary frequency by ω = 2πf and to the period by ω = 2π/T. φ is the phase, which shifts the waveform along the time axis; a positive φ advances the waveform, moving it earlier.
FIG: fig20_1.png | Figure 20.1 — A sinusoid's amplitude, period, and phase, shown on a single cycle.
P: Only these three numbers are needed. Two sinusoids of the same frequency can differ only in amplitude and phase, and that observation is the seed of the entire phasor method developed in Chapter 23.
H2: 20.3 Sine, Cosine, and the Relationship Between Them
P: Cosine is sine shifted by a quarter cycle: cos θ = sin(θ + 90°). Which one you use as the reference is arbitrary, and different textbooks choose differently, but you must be consistent within a single problem. This series follows the common convention of taking cosine as the reference for phasor work, for reasons that Chapter 22 makes clear.
P: The identities that earn their keep in circuit analysis are these:
LI: Pythagorean: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. This is what turns a resistance and a reactance into a magnitude, and it is why impedance magnitudes involve a square root of a sum of squares.
LI: Sum of angles: sin(A + B) = sin A cos B + cos A sin B. This is the identity behind mixing and modulation — multiply two sinusoids and you get sum and difference frequencies, which is the foundation of every radio receiver.
LI: Double angle: sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ, and cos 2θ = 1 − 2sin²θ. The second form explains why the instantaneous power in an AC circuit oscillates at twice the signal frequency.
WE: Worked Example 20.1
P: A voltage is described by v(t) = 15·sin(377t + 30°). Find the amplitude, angular frequency, ordinary frequency, period, phase, and the instantaneous value at t = 5 ms.
P: Amplitude: V_m = 15 V. Angular frequency: ω = 377 rad/s.
P: Frequency: f = ω/2π = 377/6.283 = 60.0 Hz. This is the North American mains frequency, and 377 is a number you will meet often enough to recognize on sight.
P: Period: T = 1/f = 16.67 ms. Phase: φ = 30°, meaning the waveform leads a pure sine by one-twelfth of a cycle.
P: At t = 5 ms, the argument is ωt + φ = (377)(0.005) + 30° = 1.885 rad + 30°.
P: The mixed units must be reconciled. Converting 1.885 rad to degrees: 1.885 × 180/π = 108.0°. So the total argument is 108.0° + 30° = 138.0°.
P: Therefore v = 15·sin(138.0°) = 15 × 0.669 = 10.0 V.
P: That reconciliation step is where errors happen. The ωt term is inherently in radians because ω is in radians per second; the phase was given in degrees. One must be converted before they can be added, and no calculator will warn you if you skip it.
H2: 20.4 Average and RMS Values
P: The average value of a sinusoid over a whole number of cycles is zero, since it spends as much time positive as negative. That makes the plain average useless for describing AC magnitude, and something better is needed.
P: The root-mean-square value is defined as the square root of the mean of the square. Squaring makes everything positive, so nothing cancels, and the definition is chosen so that the RMS value of an AC waveform is exactly the DC value that would dissipate the same power in a resistor. For a sinusoid the result is
EQ: V_rms = V_m/√2 ≈ 0.707·V_m
P: When a supply is described as 230 V AC, that is an RMS figure; its peak is 325 V, and the peak-to-peak swing is 650 V. Insulation must survive the peak while power calculations use the RMS, and confusing the two has consequences at both ends — under-specified insulation on one side, power estimates wrong by a factor of two on the other.
KEY: RMS is defined by equal heating, not by averaging. V_rms = V_m/√2 holds for sinusoids only. A square wave has V_rms = V_m; a triangle wave has V_m/√3. Applying the 0.707 factor to a non-sinusoidal waveform is simply wrong, and the error grows with how far the waveform departs from a sinusoid.
BENCH: To measure phase on a scope, trigger on one channel, use matched probes and settings on both, and time the gap between zero crossings: phase = 360° × Δt / T. Probe compensation and unequal cable lengths add phase shifts of their own, which matter more as frequency rises.
