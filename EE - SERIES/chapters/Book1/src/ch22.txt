H1: Chapter 22 — Euler's Formula and the Complex Exponential
P: Some engineers like one equation enough to put it on a mug: e^(jθ) = cos θ + j sin θ. It turns the messy trigonometry of AC circuits into simple multiplication, and it is the whole reason phasors work.
P: Where you meet this: Phasor analysis, Fourier transforms, digital signal processing, and every I/Q modulator.
P: This chapter contains one equation. It is arguably the most useful single equation in electrical engineering, and it is the hinge on which the whole of AC analysis turns.
H2: 22.1 The Formula
P: Euler's formula states that
EQ: e^(jθ) = cos θ + j·sin θ
P: The left-hand side is an exponential — the function from Chapter 19 whose rate of change equals its own value. The right-hand side is a pair of sinusoids — the functions from Chapter 20. The formula asserts they are the same thing, provided the exponent is imaginary.
P: Geometrically the statement is transparent. The right-hand side is a complex number with real part cos θ and imaginary part sin θ, so by the Pythagorean identity its magnitude is √(cos²θ + sin²θ) = 1, and its angle is θ. So e^(jθ) is simply the point on the unit circle at angle θ. As θ increases steadily, the point travels around the circle at a constant rate.
FIG: fig22_1.png | Figure 22.1 — Euler's formula: steady rotation around the unit circle traces out a sinusoid.
P: That last observation is the whole reason the formula matters to engineers. Steady rotation around a circle, projected onto an axis, is a sinusoid. Euler's formula is the bridge between rotation and oscillation, and AC circuit theory lives on that bridge.
KEY: e^(jθ) is a unit-length arrow at angle θ. Nothing more mysterious than that. Multiplying any complex number by e^(jθ) rotates it by θ without changing its length, which is the generalization of "multiplying by j rotates by 90°" from the previous chapter — since j itself is e^(j90°).
H2: 22.2 Polar Form, Properly Written
P: With Euler's formula in hand, the polar form of Chapter 21 can be written as a genuine product rather than as the shorthand angle notation:
EQ: z = |z|·e^(jθ)
P: The engineering shorthand |z|∠θ means exactly this and nothing more. Writing it as an exponential is not mere notation, though — it means the ordinary rules of exponents now apply, and those rules are why multiplication in polar form works the way it does:
EQ: |z₁|e^(jθ₁) · |z₂|e^(jθ₂) = |z₁||z₂| · e^(j(θ₁+θ₂))
P: Magnitudes multiply and angles add, not as a special rule for complex numbers but as the ordinary law that exponents add when you multiply powers of the same base. The polar multiplication rule of Chapter 21 was a consequence of Euler's formula all along.
H2: 22.3 Extracting Sine and Cosine
P: Applying the formula at −θ and using cos(−θ) = cos θ, sin(−θ) = −sin θ gives a companion equation, and adding or subtracting the two isolates the trigonometric functions:
EQ: cos θ = (e^(jθ) + e^(−jθ))/2,     sin θ = (e^(jθ) − e^(−jθ))/2j
P: These are worth knowing because they let you replace trigonometric functions with exponentials, and exponentials are far easier to differentiate, integrate, and multiply. A trigonometric identity that takes half a page to prove geometrically often takes two lines in exponential form. The sum-of-angles identity from Chapter 20, for instance, is just the statement that exponents add.
H2: 22.4 The Rotating Vector
P: Now let the angle grow with time at a steady rate, θ = ωt, and attach an amplitude:
EQ: v(t) = V_m·e^(j(ωt + φ))
P: This describes an arrow of length V_m rotating counterclockwise at ω radians per second, starting at angle φ. Its projection onto the real axis is V_m·cos(ωt + φ), which is exactly the sinusoid of Chapter 20.
P: So a real sinusoidal voltage can be regarded as the real part of a rotating complex quantity. Nothing has been lost — the real signal is recoverable at any moment by taking the real part — but a great deal has been gained, because the rotating exponential is far easier to do algebra with than the cosine is.
MIS: Students often ask what the imaginary part of the rotating vector physically is, expecting it to correspond to some measurable quantity. It does not. The imaginary part is mathematical scaffolding: we deliberately added it because the resulting object is easier to compute with, and we discard it at the end by taking the real part. The physical voltage is, and always was, the real part alone. This is not a defect of the method; it is the method.
H2: 22.5 Why This Solves AC Circuits
P: Here is the payoff, stated now and demonstrated in Chapter 23. Differentiating the rotating exponential with respect to time gives
EQ: d/dt [V_m·e^(j(ωt+φ))] = jω · V_m·e^(j(ωt+φ))
P: The function reproduces itself, multiplied by jω. Differentiation — a calculus operation — has become multiplication by a constant. Integration likewise becomes division by jω.
P: The consequence is enormous. The equations governing capacitors and inductors are differential equations, and differential equations are hard. Written in terms of rotating exponentials, those same equations become algebraic equations in which the derivative has been replaced by the factor jω. An entire class of calculus problems collapses into complex arithmetic of the kind you practiced in Chapter 21.
WE: Worked Example 22.1
P: Use Euler's formula to prove the identity cos(A + B) = cos A cos B − sin A sin B.
P: Start from the exponential of the sum, and use the fact that exponents add:
P: e^(j(A+B)) = e^(jA) · e^(jB)
P: Expand each factor by Euler's formula:
P: e^(jA)·e^(jB) = (cos A + j sin A)(cos B + j sin B)
P: Multiply out, remembering j² = −1:
P: = cos A cos B + j cos A sin B + j sin A cos B − sin A sin B
P: = (cos A cos B − sin A sin B) + j(cos A sin B + sin A cos B)
P: Now apply Euler's formula to the left-hand side: e^(j(A+B)) = cos(A + B) + j sin(A + B).
P: Two complex numbers are equal only if their real parts match and their imaginary parts match. Equating real parts gives the identity we wanted; equating imaginary parts gives the sine sum identity for free.
P: Compare this with the geometric proof, which requires a carefully constructed diagram and several similar triangles. The exponential proof is four lines and produces two identities at once. This is representative of what the complex exponential does throughout the subject.
BENCH: In software, j is usually written 1j (Python) or i (MATLAB and many calculators), and signal-processing functions differ on whether they want frequency in hertz or radians per second. Mixing the two silently gives results that are wrong by a factor of 2π, which is large enough to notice and small enough to explain away if you're tired.
