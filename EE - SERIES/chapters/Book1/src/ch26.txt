PART: Part III — DC and AC Circuit Theory
H1: Chapter 26 — Circuit Elements and Kirchhoff's Laws
P: Every circuit, from a flashlight to a server motherboard, obeys two rules. Current flowing into a junction must flow out, and the voltages around any closed loop add to zero. That's it. Everything else in this part is clever bookkeeping built on those two rules.
P: Where you meet this: Every debugging session, current-sense measurements, power budgets, and the equations inside every circuit simulator.
H2: 26.1 From Physics to Circuit Theory
P: Part I defined voltage, current, resistance, capacitance, and inductance from first physical principles. Circuit theory takes those definitions as given and asks a more restricted, more tractable question: if you know how each individual element in a network behaves — a resistor obeying V = IR, a capacitor obeying C = Q/V, an inductor obeying ε = −L dI/dt, and a source supplying a fixed voltage or current — what can you say about the voltages and currents everywhere in an arbitrary network built by wiring many such elements together?
P: This restriction is not a retreat from physics; it is what makes circuit analysis tractable at all. A real circuit board can have hundreds or thousands of components. Nothing about Coulomb's law or Gauss's law, applied directly, scales to a problem of that size — but the two laws introduced in this chapter, which are themselves direct consequences of charge conservation and energy conservation, scale perfectly well, because they let you reason about a circuit's topology (how things are connected) independently of the messy details of the fields inside any one component.
H2: 26.2 Nodes, Branches, and Loops
P: A node is any point in a circuit where two or more elements connect — physically, an entire stretch of ideal (zero-resistance) wire between components counts as a single node, since every point along it is at the same potential. A branch is a single path between two nodes, generally containing one circuit element. A loop is any closed path through the circuit that starts and ends at the same node without repeating any other node along the way.
P: These three words — node, branch, loop — are the vocabulary the rest of this chapter, and much of this book, is built from, and it is worth being able to identify all three correctly on a circuit diagram before proceeding, since misidentifying a node (for instance, treating two separate points joined by a plain wire as two different nodes) is one of the most common sources of error in an otherwise correct circuit analysis.
H2: 26.3 Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)
P: Picture a T-junction in a water pipe: one pipe flows in, two flow out. Whatever volume of water enters that junction every second has to leave it too, split between the two outgoing pipes in whatever proportion — water does not pile up and vanish inside a junction. A node in a circuit, any point where two or more wires meet, behaves exactly the same way for charge: whatever current flows in during any interval flows back out during that same interval, however many branches it is split across, because an ordinary wire junction has nowhere to store charge and no way to manufacture it from nothing. A node with 4.0 A flowing in from one branch and 7.0 A flowing in from a second must have exactly 11.0 A flowing out overall, however that 11.0 A is then divided among whatever branches leave the node.
P: This is Kirchhoff's current law: the sum of currents entering any node equals the sum of currents leaving it — equivalently, the algebraic sum of all currents at a node, with currents entering counted as positive and currents leaving as negative (or any other single consistent convention), is always zero:
EQ: Σ Iin = Σ Iout   (equivalently: Σ Inode = 0)
P: KCL is nothing more than charge conservation (Chapter 1), applied at a single point: charge cannot accumulate at an ordinary node (which has no capacity to store it), so whatever charge flows in during any interval must flow back out during that same interval. This makes KCL exact for any node, in any circuit, at any instant — it is not an approximation, and it holds regardless of what kind of elements are attached to the node or how they behave individually.
H2: 26.4 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)
P: Kirchhoff's voltage law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and drops around any closed loop is zero:
FIG: fig26_1.png | Figure 26.1 — KCL at a node and KVL around a loop, the two laws every circuit-analysis method rests on.
EQ: Σ Vloop = 0
P: KVL follows from the fact that electric potential (Chapter 5) is a well-defined function of position — travel around any closed loop, back to your starting point, and the net change in potential must be zero, since potential does not depend on the path taken between two points, only on the two points themselves. Practically, this means: pick a direction to traverse a loop, add up every voltage drop you encounter in that direction (a drop across a resistor in the direction of assumed current flow, for instance) and subtract every voltage rise (crossing a source from − to +, for instance), and the total must equal zero. This reasoning holds for the lumped-circuit approximation used throughout this book, where any changing magnetic flux linking a loop is represented explicitly as its own circuit source — an induced EMF, as in Chapter 11 — rather than left as a distributed effect. In full electromagnetic theory, a changing magnetic flux produces a genuinely non-conservative electric field (Faraday's law, Chapter 11), and outside the lumped approximation KVL as stated here no longer holds exactly.
KEY: KCL is about nodes; KVL is about loops. KCL enforces charge conservation at every point where branches meet. KVL enforces that potential is a well-defined, single-valued quantity around any closed path. Together with the individual V-I relationship for each element (Ohm's law, for a resistor), applied systematically, they are sufficient to solve any resistive circuit — nothing more is needed beyond those element laws and careful bookkeeping of these two.
H2: 26.5 Sign Conventions and the Passive Sign Convention
P: Before applying either law, every branch needs an assumed current direction and every element needs a labeled voltage polarity. The passive sign convention, used throughout this book and throughout the rest of this series, assigns the voltage polarity across a passive element (a resistor, capacitor, or inductor) so that the assumed current enters at the + terminal — under this convention, a positive value of P = VI means the element is absorbing power, and a negative value means it is delivering power, which is otherwise unusual for a passive element and typically signals either a sign error or a source hiding somewhere in what was assumed to be a passive branch.
P: It does not matter, in principle, which direction you assume for a branch current before solving a circuit — if you guess the wrong direction, the algebra will simply return a negative value for that current, correctly telling you the actual current flows opposite to your assumed direction. What matters is consistency: once a direction and polarity are assigned to a branch, every equation involving that branch must use them consistently throughout the entire solution.
WE: Worked Example 26.1
PROB: A single loop contains a 12 V battery in series with a 4.0 Ω resistor and an 8.0 Ω resistor. Using KVL, find the current in the loop.
SOL: Assume current I flows clockwise, out of the battery's + terminal. Traversing the loop clockwise starting at the battery's − terminal: rise of 12 V crossing the battery, then a drop of I(4.0) across the first resistor, then a drop of I(8.0) across the second. KVL: 12 − 4.0I − 8.0I = 0, so 12 = 12I, giving I = 1.0 A.
WE: Worked Example 26.2
PROB: At a node, three branches meet. Branch A carries 3.0 A into the node. Branch B carries 5.0 A into the node. Branch C leaves the node. Find the current in branch C.
SOL: By KCL, total current in equals total current out: 3.0 + 5.0 = I_C, so I_C = 8.0 A, leaving the node.
MIS: A very common early error is applying KVL correctly in form but inconsistently in sign — for instance, treating a resistor's voltage as a drop when traversing it in one direction during one part of a solution, and quietly treating the same resistor's voltage as a rise later in the same solution, usually because the assumed current direction was forgotten partway through. Before writing down a KVL equation, it helps to mark every assumed current direction and every resulting + / − polarity directly on the circuit diagram first, and then read the diagram rather than trying to hold the whole sign convention in your head while writing the equation.
BENCH: KVL is your best debugging tool. Measure the voltages around a loop with a meter; if the drops don't add up to the supply, there is a resistance you didn't plan for, usually a poor solder joint, a tired connector or an undersized trace. Measuring the voltage along a few centimeters of supply trace under load is a cheap way to find one.
