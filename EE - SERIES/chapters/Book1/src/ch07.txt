H1: Chapter 7 — Electric Current and Resistance
P: Flip a light switch and the lamp comes on almost instantly, yet the electrons in the wire crawl along at a fraction of a millimeter per second. This chapter sorts out that apparent paradox, and explains why the wire gets warm while it's at it.
P: Where you meet this: Wire-gauge tables, fuse ratings, PCB trace-width calculators, heating elements, and temperature sensors (RTDs and thermistors) that rely on resistance changing with temperature.
H2: 7.1 Current Defined
P: Count how many cars pass a tollbooth every minute, or how many liters of water flow past a mark on a pipe every second, and you already have the shape of the idea this chapter needs: not how much charge is sitting somewhere, but how much of it goes past a given point, per unit time. Everything so far in this book has concerned static charge — charge sitting still. Electric current asks that same question of charge instead of cars or water: it is defined as the rate at which charge passes a given cross-section of a conductor:
EQ: I = dQ/dt
P: with SI unit the ampere (A), equal to one coulomb per second. By long-standing convention (traceable to Franklin's original, backwards guess mentioned in Chapter 1), current is defined as flowing in the direction positive charge would move — which in an ordinary metal wire is opposite to the actual direction the (negatively charged) conduction electrons travel. This convention is used universally in circuit diagrams and will be used throughout the rest of this series without further comment.
H2: 7.2 Current Density and Drift Velocity
P: It is worth being honest about how slowly electrons in a wire actually move, because the result is famously counterintuitive. Current density J is current per unit cross-sectional area, J = I/A, and it is related to the drift velocity vᴅ of the charge carriers (the average velocity they acquire in the direction opposing the field, superimposed on their much larger random thermal motion) by:
EQ: J = n q vd
P: where n is the number density of charge carriers and q is the carrier's charge. For a typical copper wire carrying a modest current, the drift velocity works out to a small fraction of a millimeter per second — an electron takes well over an hour to physically travel just one meter of household wire — considerably longer over any real wire run, as Worked Example 7.0 makes precise. What makes a lamp turn on essentially instantly when you flip a switch is not any individual electron racing to the bulb; it is that the electric field itself, and the resulting nudge to every electron already present throughout the wire, propagates at a substantial fraction of the speed of light. The signal is fast; the carriers themselves are almost leisurely.
P: This distinction between the speed of the signal and the speed of the individual carriers is worth sitting with, because it resolves what otherwise looks like a genuine paradox: a wire a few kilometers long, carrying a current whose individual electrons drift at a fraction of a millimeter per second, will still light a bulb at its far end within a small fraction of a second of the switch closing. Nothing in that observation requires any electron to travel the wire's length quickly — only the field's influence, already present all along the wire, needs to propagate, and that is a wave phenomenon governed by the electromagnetic-wave result of Chapter 12, not by how fast any individual charge carrier happens to be moving.
WE: Worked Example 7.0 — How Slow Is Drift Velocity, Really?
PROB: A copper wire of cross-sectional area 2.0 mm² carries a steady current of 5.0 A. Copper has roughly 8.5 × 10²⁸ free electrons per cubic meter. Estimate the drift velocity of the conduction electrons.
SOL: From J = nqv_d, solve for v_d: v_d = J/(nq) = I/(nqA). Plugging in: v_d = (5.0) / [(8.5×10²⁸)(1.60×10⁻¹⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁶)] ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ m/s — about 0.18 mm per second. At this rate, an individual electron would take roughly 93 minutes to travel just one meter of wire. Every appliance in your home nonetheless responds within a fraction of a second of being switched on, which is only possible because — as this section emphasizes — it is the field's influence propagating through the wire, not the individual electrons' own leisurely progress, that actually carries the “signal.”
KEY: The drift velocity of charge carriers and the speed at which an electrical signal propagates are two entirely different things. A typical drift velocity is a fraction of a millimeter per second, yet a lamp switches on essentially instantly — because what reaches the far end of the wire in that instant is not any particular electron, but the electric field's influence on every electron already present throughout the wire, propagating at a substantial fraction of the speed of light. Never confuse how fast the carriers move with how fast the current's effect is felt.

H2: 7.3 Resistance and Resistivity
P: Push a shopping cart across smooth tile and a small, steady push keeps it gliding along. Push the same cart, same load, across thick carpet instead, and the same steady push barely gets it moving — the floor is fighting back. Drive the same voltage across two different wires, one copper and one some poorer conductor, and something very similar happens: very different currents result, even though the push is identical. Something about the conductor itself, not just the voltage driving the charge, is deciding how much current actually flows.
FIG: fig7_2.png | Figure 7.2 — Reading a resistor’s color bands: yellow, violet, red, gold means 4, 7, two zeros and ±5%, so 4.7 kΩ. Start from the end where the bands bunch together; the tolerance band comes last.
P: Voltage alone can't be the answer, then, since the same voltage can drive wildly different currents through different conductors — the conductor itself is what's deciding the outcome. Ask instead: for this particular conductor, how much voltage does it take to drive a given current through it? Voltage and current on their own do not settle this — a lot of current at a small voltage, or a little current at a large voltage, could each describe the very same wire under different conditions, or entirely different wires under matched conditions. Neither number by itself identifies the conductor.
P: The way to characterize the conductor itself, independent of whichever voltage happens to be applied at the moment, is once again a ratio: divide the voltage across the conductor by the current actually flowing through it. Try this on one particular conductor at several voltages, and a fixed number falls out. Apply 2.0 V and a current of 0.10 A results; apply 4.0 V and the current is 0.20 A; apply 10 V and the current is 0.50 A. Divide each voltage by its own current, and every pair gives back the same ratio, 20 Ω, regardless of which voltage was actually used.
P: Most materials resist the flow of current to some degree, converting electrical energy into heat as charge carriers collide with the material's atomic lattice, and that fixed ratio — voltage divided by the current it drives — is the conductor's resistance. For a given conductor, resistance R is defined by how much voltage is required to drive a given current through it, and for many materials over a wide range of conditions, R is very nearly constant — this is Ohm's law, the subject of Chapter 8.
P: Resistance depends on the conductor's geometry and on an intrinsic material property, resistivity ρ:
EQ: R = ρ L / A
P: where L is the conductor's length and A is its cross-sectional area. Resistance increases with length (more material for carriers to push through) and decreases with cross-sectional area (more parallel paths available). Resistivity is a property of the material alone, independent of shape — copper's resistivity is famously low, which along with its low cost and easy workability is exactly why it dominates wiring; the same reasoning drives the choice of exotic low-resistivity materials like silver in specialized high-frequency applications discussed later in this series.
EQ: Conductivity: σ = 1/ρ  (units: siemens per meter, S/m)
H2: 7.4 Temperature Dependence
P: Resistivity is temperature dependent, and for most metals it increases with temperature: hotter atoms vibrate more vigorously, scattering conduction electrons more frequently and impeding their drift. Over a modest temperature range, this is well approximated as linear:
EQ: ρ(T) ≈ ρ0 [1 + α(T − T0)]
P: where α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity, positive for metals. Semiconductors behave oppositely — their resistivity generally decreases with rising temperature, because additional thermal energy frees more charge carriers to conduct, an effect that dominates over increased scattering. This opposite-sign behavior is one of the first clues, historically, that semiconductors work by a fundamentally different mechanism than metals, a subject developed fully in the semiconductor-physics volume of this series.
H2: 7.5 Superconductivity, Briefly
P: A small number of materials, cooled below a characteristic critical temperature (for many common superconductors, only a few degrees above absolute zero, though so-called high-temperature superconductors reach on the order of a hundred degrees above it), undergo a genuinely abrupt transition to a state of exactly zero resistivity — not merely very low resistivity, but immeasurably, and as far as any experiment has ever detected, exactly zero. A current started in a closed superconducting loop has been observed to persist, undiminished, for years without any external source maintaining it. Superconductivity is a quantum-mechanical phenomenon well beyond this book's scope to explain properly, but it is worth knowing it exists: it demonstrates that the ordinary, temperature-dependent resistivity described above is not some unavoidable law of nature, but a specific consequence of how electrons scatter off a lattice's vibrations and impurities in ordinary conducting materials — a mechanism that a superconductor, below its critical temperature, simply does not have.
WE: Worked Example 7.1
PROB: A copper wire (ρ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m) is 2.0 m long with a cross-sectional area of 1.0 mm². Find its resistance.
SOL: R = ρL/A = (1.68×10⁻⁸ Ω·m)(2.0 m) / (1.0×10⁻⁶ m²) = 0.0336 Ω — a small resistance, typical of why short lengths of ordinary hookup wire are treated as essentially resistance-free in introductory circuit problems.
WE: Worked Example 7.2
PROB: A nichrome heating element (used, for example, in a toaster) has a resistivity of 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m. Find the length of nichrome wire, with a cross-sectional area of 0.20 mm², needed to make a resistance of 20 Ω, and compare this to the copper wire in the previous example.
SOL: Rearranging R = ρL/A for length: L = RA/ρ = (20)(0.20×10⁻⁶)/(1.10×10⁻⁶) ≈ 3.6 m. Nichrome's resistivity is roughly 65 times larger than copper's, which is exactly why it is chosen for heating elements rather than copper: a practical, compact length of wire can be made to have substantial resistance (and therefore, by the power relations of Chapter 8, to dissipate substantial heat) without needing an impractically long or thin wire the way copper would require for the same resistance.
MIS: It is easy to conflate resistance and resistivity, since both describe a material “resisting” current in some loose sense, and to therefore treat resistivity as if it changed when a wire is cut shorter or drawn thinner. Resistivity is a fixed property of the material itself, at a given temperature — cutting a copper wire in half does not change copper's resistivity at all, though it does change that particular piece of wire's resistance, because resistance depends on geometry (R = ρL/A) while resistivity does not.
BENCH: Copper's resistance rises about 0.4% per °C, so a winding that measures 1.00 Ω on a cool bench is about 1.24 Ω at 85 °C, with more voltage drop and more heat to match. Size PCB traces with a calculator based on IPC-2152 rather than by eye: a 0.25 mm trace in 1 oz (35 µm) outer-layer copper carries roughly an amp for a modest temperature rise, and nowhere near three.
