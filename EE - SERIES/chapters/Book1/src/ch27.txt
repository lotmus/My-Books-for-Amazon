H1: Chapter 27 — Equivalent Resistance and Dividers
P: A thermostat, a potentiometer volume knob and the feedback network of a phone charger's regulator all use the same trick: two resistances in series, with the output taken from the middle. This chapter makes series, parallel and dividers second nature.
P: Where you meet this: Sensor interfaces (thermistors, potentiometers), the feedback networks that set a regulator's output voltage, attenuators, and scaling signals for ADC inputs.
H2: 27.1 Series and Parallel, Revisited
P: Part I introduced the series and parallel combination rules for resistors: resistances add directly in series, and combine by reciprocal sum in parallel. This chapter treats those rules as tools for simplifying entire networks, not only pairs of resistors, and develops the two divider circuits — voltage and current — that follow directly from them and that appear, in some form, inside an enormous fraction of practical circuits.

EQ: Rseries = R1 + R2 + … ,     1/Rparallel = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …
H2: 27.2 Reducing a Network by Combination
P: Many practical circuits, though they may look complicated at first glance, can be fully solved by repeatedly identifying resistors that are purely in series or purely in parallel, combining them into a single equivalent resistor, and redrawing the (now simpler) circuit — repeating until only a single equivalent resistance remains between the source terminals. This is not a universal method (Chapters 28 and 29 introduce the systematic techniques needed once a network cannot be reduced this way, typically because of a “bridge” configuration with no purely series or parallel path), but it is the fastest method whenever it applies, and recognizing when it applies is itself a skill worth developing before reaching for heavier machinery.
H2: 27.3 The Voltage Divider
P: Two resistors in series across a voltage source V split that voltage in direct proportion to their resistances, since the same current flows through both:
EQ: V1 = V · R1 / (R1 + R2) ,   V2 = V · R2 / (R1 + R2)
P: The voltage divider generalizes immediately to any number of series resistors: the voltage across any one of them is the source voltage times that resistor's share of the total series resistance. It is worth noting explicitly what the voltage divider assumes: that whatever draws off the divided voltage (a downstream load) draws negligible current compared to the current already flowing through the divider itself. If that assumption fails — if the load draws a comparable or larger current — the simple divider formula no longer gives the correct output voltage, and the load's own resistance must be folded into the calculation as a resistor in parallel with whichever divider resistor it is connected across.
FIG: fig27_2.png | Figure 27.2 — Voltage and current dividers, with their governing formulas.
H2: 27.4 The Current Divider
P: Two resistors in parallel across a current source (or a branch carrying a known total current) split that current inversely in proportion to their resistances — the larger resistor carries the smaller share, since current preferentially takes the path of least resistance:
EQ: I1 = I · R2 / (R1 + R2) ,   I2 = I · R1 / (R1 + R2)
P: Notice that each resistor's current formula uses the other resistor's value in the numerator — the reverse of the voltage divider's pattern, where each resistor's voltage formula uses its own value in the numerator. This inversion is easy to state and easy to forget under exam pressure; rederiving it once from Ohm's law and KCL (each resistor sees the same voltage V = IR_parallel, so I₁ = V/R₁ and I₂ = V/R₂, and substituting R_parallel confirms the pattern above) is a more reliable way to keep it straight than memorizing it as an isolated fact.
KEY: Voltage dividers use each resistor's own value; current dividers use the other resistor's value. This asymmetry falls directly out of Ohm's law applied to series versus parallel configurations, and rederiving it once is more durable than memorizing the two formulas as unrelated facts.
WE: Worked Example 27.1
PROB: A 15 V source drives a series combination of a 5.0 kΩ and a 10 kΩ resistor. Find the voltage across the 10 kΩ resistor.
SOL: V₁₀ₖ = 15 × 10/(5.0+10) = 15 × 10/15 = 10 V.
WE: Worked Example 27.2
PROB: A current source supplies 12 mA to a parallel combination of a 2.0 kΩ and a 6.0 kΩ resistor. Find the current through the 2.0 kΩ resistor.
SOL: Using the current-divider formula (note the other resistor's value appears in the numerator): I₂ₖ = I × R₆ₖ/(R₂ₖ+R₆ₖ) = 12 × 6.0/(2.0+6.0) = 12 × 0.75 = 9.0 mA. Check: the smaller resistor should carry the larger share of the current, and 9.0 mA out of 12 mA total is indeed the larger share, confirming the arithmetic direction is right.
MIS: It is a common shortcut error, once a network contains more than two resistors, to attempt to identify series and parallel groupings by eye without carefully tracing which nodes each resistor actually connects — two resistors that merely look adjacent on a drawn diagram are not necessarily in series or parallel with each other in the sense these rules require. A resistor is in series with another only if they share exactly one node with nothing else connected there (so the same current is forced through both), and two resistors are in parallel only if both of their endpoints connect to the same pair of nodes. When a network's diagram is drawn with resistors at odd angles or overlapping, redrawing it schematically, node by node, before attempting any combination is far more reliable than trying to judge series/parallel relationships from the picture's layout alone.
H2: 27.5 Sensors Are Mostly Dividers
P: Look back at the reader lab in Figure L.1. Every sense in the kit, and in most products, works the same way: a physical quantity changes a resistance, a capacitance, or an inductance, and a circuit turns that change into a voltage a converter can read. Resistive sensors are the simplest and the most common: thermistors for temperature, LDRs for light (Section 8.5), strain gauges for force and weight, potentiometers for position. Capacitive sensors read touch, humidity (most electronic hygrometers use a capacitive polymer film), and liquid level; inductive sensors find metal and measure position without contact. The later books return to capacitive and inductive sensing; this section handles the resistive ones, which need nothing more than the divider of Section 27.3.
P: Your own senses fall into the same families, which is a useful way to remember them. Sight is a light sensor: the LDR of Section 8.5, a photodiode, or a camera. Hearing is a microphone, a capacitor with one plate that moves. Touch is a capacitive pad or a strain gauge. Warmth is a thermistor. Balance is an accelerometer, a tiny capacitive structure etched into silicon (Book 7 opens one up). Smell and taste are gas and pH sensors, whose resistance or voltage changes with chemistry. Every one of them ends as a voltage on a converter's input, and most pass through a divider or a bridge on the way.
P: A negative-temperature-coefficient (NTC) thermistor is a ceramic bead whose resistance falls steeply as it warms. Its data sheet gives two numbers: R₂₅, the resistance at 25 °C, and B, a constant in kelvin. Between those, the B-equation R(T) = R₂₅ e^(B(1/T − 1/T₂₅)), with T in kelvin, describes the curve well over a few tens of degrees. The common 10 kΩ, B = 3950 K part (MF52-type beads) changes by about 4.4 % per degree near room temperature, ten times more than a copper wire.
WE: Worked Example 27.3 — The Thermistor Divider, Analog First
PROB: A 10 kΩ NTC (B = 3950 K) forms the bottom of a divider with a fixed 10 kΩ resistor on top, powered from 3.3 V. Find the output at 0, 25, and 50 °C, the sensitivity near 25 °C in mV per degree, what one count of the ESP32-S3's ADC means in degrees, and how much the thermistor heats itself.
SOL: The B-equation gives 33.6 kΩ at 0 °C (273.15 K), 10.0 kΩ at 25 °C, and 3.59 kΩ at 50 °C. (The maker's table says 32.1 kΩ at 0 °C: the B-equation is a model, good near its two reference temperatures and a few percent off far from them; the three-constant Steinhart-Hart equation does better.) The divider gives V_out = 3.3 × R/(10 k + R): 2.54 V at 0 °C, 1.65 V at 25 °C, and 0.87 V at 50 °C. Sensitivity near 25 °C: dR/dT = −BR/T² = −3950 × 10 kΩ/298.15² ≈ −444 Ω/K, and the divider's slope there is 3.3 × 10 k/(20 k)² = 82.5 µV/Ω, so the output moves about −36.7 mV per degree. One ADC count (0.71 mV) is then about 0.02 °C, far finer than the ±50 mV converter error, which alone is worth about ±1.4 °C until calibrated. Self-heating: at 25 °C the thermistor dissipates (1.65 V)²/10 kΩ ≈ 0.27 mW, and with a dissipation constant of about 2 mW/°C it runs about 0.14 °C warm. Choosing the fixed resistor equal to the thermistor's resistance at the temperature you care about most puts the steepest part of the curve there.
FIG: fig27_3.png | Figure 27.3 — A 10 kΩ NTC thermistor under a 10 kΩ resistor. The output falls from 2.54 V at 0 °C to 0.87 V at 50 °C and is steepest, about −37 mV per degree, around 25 °C, where the thermistor matches the fixed resistor.
H2: 27.6 The Wheatstone Bridge
P: A divider's output sits on top of a large fixed voltage: 1.65 V, changing by tens of millivolts. To see a tiny change, build two dividers side by side and measure between their outputs. That is the Wheatstone bridge. If both dividers have the same ratio, the bridge is balanced and its output is zero; a small change in one arm gives a small output that is now all signal, ready to be amplified or compared. With a supply V_s, one divider of R₁ over R₂ and the other of R₃ over R₄ (each measured to the bottom), V_out = V_s[R₂/(R₁ + R₂) − R₄/(R₃ + R₄)], and balance means R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄.
WE: Worked Example 27.4 — A Thermostat With No Code
PROB: A bridge from 3.3 V has a fixed 10 kΩ over the NTC of Worked Example 27.3 on one side, and a fixed 10 kΩ over a set-point potentiometer on the other. The potentiometer is set to the thermistor's resistance at 20 °C. A comparator (an LM393, say) switches the heater on when the thermistor side is the higher of the two. Find the potentiometer setting and the bridge output at 18 °C and 22 °C.
SOL: At 20 °C (293.15 K), the B-equation gives R = 10 kΩ × e^(3950(1/293.15 − 1/298.15)) ≈ 12.54 kΩ, so the potentiometer is set to 12.54 kΩ, and its side sits at 3.3 × 12.54/22.54 ≈ 1.836 V. At 18 °C the thermistor is 13.75 kΩ and its side sits at 3.3 × 13.75/23.75 ≈ 1.911 V: the output is +75 mV and the comparator turns the heater on. At 22 °C the output is about −75 mV and the heater turns off. The whole thermostat is two resistors, a thermistor, a potentiometer, and a comparator, with no software to crash. A real one adds a little positive feedback around the comparator (hysteresis, Book 2) so that it does not chatter on and off at exactly 20 °C.
FIG: fig27_4.png | Figure 27.4 — The analog-first thermostat: a Wheatstone bridge compares the thermistor with a set-point potentiometer, and a comparator turns the bridge's few tens of millivolts into a clean on or off for the heater.
P: Now the same job in about twenty lines on the ESP32-S3: read the divider of Worked Example 27.3 with the ADC, convert the voltage to resistance and the resistance to temperature with the B-equation, compare with a set point that has a degree of hysteresis, and drive the relay from a pin. The code version can also show the temperature on the OLED, follow a weekly schedule, and report over Wi-Fi. The analog version keeps working if the firmware hangs. That is why real thermostats and appliances often keep a simple hardware cut-out, a thermal fuse or a comparator, beside the microcontroller: the code handles comfort and the hardware handles safety. The same pattern appears in every later book.
P: Bridges are also how scales weigh. A load cell is a metal bar with four strain gauges glued to it, wired as a full bridge, so that bending the bar unbalances all four arms at once. Its data sheet gives the output at full load per volt of supply. Every scale you have stood on contains one, and so does the reader lab's Smart Refrigerator Monitor.
WE: Worked Example 27.5 — Weighing the Milk
PROB: The Smart Refrigerator Monitor puts a 5 kg load cell (rated output 1.0 mV/V, such as a TAL220) under the milk shelf, excited from 5 V and read by an HX711, a 24-bit converter made for bridges, whose gain-128 input spans ±20 mV. Find the full-scale output, the output for a 1 L carton of milk (1.03 kg), and the fraction of the HX711's range in use. A thermistor divider like Worked Example 27.3 watches the fridge temperature beside it.
SOL: Full-scale output = 1.0 mV/V × 5 V = 5.0 mV at 5 kg, so the cell gives 1.0 mV per kilogram, and the carton produces about 1.03 mV. The HX711's ±20 mV input is four times larger than the cell's 5 mV full scale, so a quarter of its range is in use, and its 24 bits leave ample resolution. The bridge's zero is not perfect (the data sheet allows ±0.1 % of full scale, ±5 µV here), so the firmware stores the empty-shelf reading and subtracts it, the tare. When the reading drops below about 0.2 mV, less than a fifth of a carton, the monitor sends a shopping reminder. Learn it here, buy the IC at work: the HX711 is the bridge-plus-amplifier-plus-converter of this section in one small package.
H2: 27.7 Between 5 V and 3.3 V: A Divider as a Level Shifter
P: Older parts and many hobby modules use 5 V logic; the ESP32-S3 uses 3.3 V, and its pins are not designed to accept 5 V. A 5 V output connected straight to a 3.3 V input pushes current through the input's protection diodes into the supply and can damage the chip. For a slow signal going down from 5 V to 3.3 V, a divider is enough. Going up from 3.3 V to 5 V often needs nothing: a 5 V TTL-style input sees anything above about 2.0 V as high. Fast or two-way buses such as I²C use a small transistor level-shifter module instead.
WE: Worked Example 27.6 — The Ultrasonic Echo Pin
PROB: The HC-SR04's echo output swings to 5.0 V. The ESP32-S3 input needs at least 0.75 × 3.3 = 2.475 V to read a 1 and must not see more than 3.6 V. Choose a divider.
SOL: Try 2.2 kΩ on top and 3.3 kΩ below: V = 5.0 × 3.3/(2.2 + 3.3) = 3.0 V. That clears the 2.475 V threshold by about 0.5 V and stays 0.6 V below the limit. The divider draws 5.0/5.5 kΩ ≈ 0.91 mA while the echo is high, which the module drives easily. Its Thevenin resistance, 2.2 ∥ 3.3 ≈ 1.3 kΩ, with a few picofarads of pin capacitance gives a time constant of a few nanoseconds, nothing next to an echo pulse hundreds of microseconds long. The trigger needs no divider: the ESP32-S3's high output, close to 3.3 V, is above the module's TTL-level threshold (2.0 to 2.4 V, depending on the data sheet).
BENCH: A divider only divides properly when its load draws much less current than the divider does. A 100 kΩ/100 kΩ divider feeding a 100 kΩ input gives one-third, not one-half. Rule of thumb: make the load at least ten times the divider's output resistance (the two resistors in parallel), or buffer the output with an op-amp.
P: On the Job
P: Smart-thermostat teams show where this chapter goes. A commercial smart thermostat's sensing layer is a temperature sensor, a humidity sensor, a motion or proximity sensor to know whether anyone is home, and an ambient-light sensor for the display, all feeding a microcontroller that switches the heating and cooling through relays or solid-state switches on the 24 V AC control wires of a North American heating system. Hardware engineers there use thermal chambers, reference thermometers, SPICE, and long logging runs. A typical task: the thermostat reads 1.5 °C too warm because the display, the processor, and the Wi-Fi radio heat the case. The engineer moves the sensor to the bottom edge, cuts slots in the board around it so heat cannot flow in through the copper, and adds a firmware correction based on how hard the electronics are working, then proves it in the chamber over a week of simulated days.
