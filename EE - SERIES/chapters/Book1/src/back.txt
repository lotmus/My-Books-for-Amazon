H2: Chapter 1 Solutions
P: 1. The charge is a whole number of electron charges, so divide the total by e. |q| = 8.0 nC = 8.0×10⁻⁹ C, and n = |q|/e = (8.0×10⁻⁹ C)/(1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C) ≈ 5.0×10¹⁰ electrons. Check the sign: the rod is negative, so these are excess electrons, not missing ones. Fifty billion sounds like a lot, yet it is only about one extra electron for every 10¹³ atoms in a small rod.
P: 2. Charge is conserved and identical spheres share it equally. Total before contact: (+6.0 nC) + (−2.0 nC) = +4.0 nC. After touching, each sphere carries half: +2.0 nC. Check: 2.0 + 2.0 = 4.0 nC, the same total as before. The usual mistake is to split 6.0 and 2.0 separately or to forget the sign of the negative charge.
P: 3. Current is charge per second, so Q = IΔt = (2.0 A)(60 s) = 120 C. The number of elementary charges is n = Q/e = 120/(1.602×10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 7.5×10²⁰. Check the order of magnitude: one coulomb is about 6.24×10¹⁸ charges, and 120 × 6.24×10¹⁸ ≈ 7.5×10²⁰.
P: 4. Work one contact at a time, conserving charge and splitting equally each time. First contact: the neutral sphere (0) touches +9.0 nC; the total +9.0 nC splits into +4.5 nC each. Second contact: the same sphere (+4.5 nC) touches −3.0 nC; the total +1.5 nC splits into +0.75 nC each. Final charges: the first sphere +4.5 nC, the travelling sphere +0.75 nC, the third sphere +0.75 nC. Check: 4.5 + 0.75 + 0.75 = 6.0 nC, equal to the starting total 9.0 − 3.0 = 6.0 nC.
H2: Chapter 2 Solutions
P: 1. Use Coulomb's law with magnitudes, then decide the direction from the signs. F = k|q₁q₂|/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁶)(6.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² = 0.2158/0.090 ≈ 2.4 N. The charges have opposite signs, so the force is attractive: each charge is pulled toward the other along the line joining them. Newton's third law gives the same 2.4 N on both.
P: 2. Rearrange Coulomb's law for the distance: r = √(kq²/F) = √[(8.99×10⁹)(1.0×10⁻⁶)²/1.0] = √(8.99×10⁻³) ≈ 0.095 m, about 9.5 cm. Check: at 9.5 cm, F = (8.99×10⁹)(10⁻¹²)/(0.095)² ≈ 1.0 N. A microcoulomb is a large charge for everyday objects; two of them push hard even at ten centimeters.
P: 3. Find each force on the middle charge separately, give it a direction, then add as vectors along the line. From +2.0 µC at x = 0 (10 cm away): attractive, so it pulls the −3.0 µC charge toward x = 0, with F₁ = (8.99×10⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁶)(3.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.10)² ≈ 5.39 N. From +1.0 µC at x = 20 cm (10 cm away): also attractive, pulling toward x = 20 cm, with F₂ = (8.99×10⁹)(1.0×10⁻⁶)(3.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.10)² ≈ 2.70 N. They point in opposite directions, so the net force is 5.39 − 2.70 ≈ 2.7 N toward x = 0. The usual mistake is to add the magnitudes (8.1 N) without drawing the arrows.
P: 4. Draw one sphere: gravity mg acts down, the string tension T acts along the string, 10° from vertical, and the Coulomb repulsion F acts horizontally, away from the other sphere. In equilibrium, vertical: T cos 10° = mg; horizontal: T sin 10° = F. Dividing eliminates T: F = mg tan 10° = (1.0×10⁻³ kg)(9.81 m/s²)(0.1763) ≈ 1.73×10⁻³ N. The geometry gives the separation: each sphere hangs L sin 10° = (0.50 m)(0.1736) = 0.0868 m from the vertical, so r = 2L sin 10° ≈ 0.174 m. Now Coulomb's law: kq²/r² = F, so q = r√(F/k) = (0.174)√(1.73×10⁻³/8.99×10⁹) ≈ 7.6×10⁻⁸ C, about 76 nC on each sphere. Check: the tension is mg/cos 10° ≈ 9.96×10⁻³ N, a little more than the weight, as it must be when the string also supplies the sideways force.
H2: Chapter 3 Solutions
P: 1. The field of a point charge is E = k|q|/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(12×10⁻⁹)/(0.10)² ≈ 1.08×10⁴ N/C. Direction: field lines end on negative charge, so at the point the field points radially inward, toward the charge. Check the units: N·m²/C² × C / m² = N/C.
P: 2. Each charge is 2.0 cm from the midpoint, so each contributes E = kq/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(5.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.020)² ≈ 1.12×10⁵ N/C. At the midpoint, the field of +q points away from +q, toward −q, and the field of −q points toward −q as well, so the two add instead of cancelling: E ≈ 2.25×10⁵ N/C, directed from the positive charge toward the negative one. The usual mistake is to assume that equal and opposite charges cancel at the midpoint; that happens with two equal charges of the same sign.
P: 3. F = qE, with q = −e for the electron. The magnitude is (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)(500 N/C) ≈ 8.0×10⁻¹⁷ N. Because the charge is negative, the force points opposite to the field: in the −x direction. Check: field arrows show the push on a positive test charge, so an electron always goes against them.
P: 4. τ = pE sin θ = (2.0×10⁻²⁹ C·m)(3.0×10⁵ N/C) sin 90° = 6.0×10⁻²⁴ N·m. Perpendicular is the orientation of maximum torque; at 0° or 180° the torque would be zero. The torque turns the dipole until p lines up with E.
H2: Chapter 4 Solutions
P: 1. Gauss's law: Φ = Q_enc/ε₀ = (2.0×10⁻⁹ C)/(8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m²) ≈ 226 N·m²/C. The 3.0 cm radius never enters: the flux through a closed surface depends only on the charge inside, not on the size or shape of the surface. Check with a direct calculation: E = kQ/r² at r = 0.030 m is 2.0×10⁴ N/C, and E × 4πr² = 2.0×10⁴ × 0.01131 ≈ 226 N·m²/C.
P: 2. For an infinite sheet, E = σ/(2ε₀) = (4.0×10⁻⁶ C/m²)/[2(8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m²)] ≈ 2.26×10⁵ N/C, pointing away from the positively charged sheet on both sides. The 2.0 cm is a distractor: an ideal infinite sheet gives the same field at every distance, because its field lines are parallel and never spread out. A real sheet behaves this way only at distances small compared with its size.
P: 3. Suppose the field immediately outside the surface had a component along the surface. That component would push the free charges sideways along the surface, and they would move. In electrostatic equilibrium nothing moves, so the charges keep rearranging until the sideways component is zero. What remains is a field perpendicular to the surface. Gauss's law then fixes its size: a small pillbox straddling the surface gives E = σ/ε₀ immediately outside, and zero inside the metal.
P: 4. For a long line of charge, E = λ/(2πε₀r) = (5.0×10⁻⁹ C/m)/[2π(8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m²)(0.040 m)] ≈ 2.25×10³ N/C, directed radially away from the wire. Note the 1/r fall-off, slower than a point charge's 1/r², because the field lines spread in two dimensions instead of three.
H2: Chapter 5 Solutions
P: 1. V = kq/r = (8.99×10⁹)(3.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.15) ≈ 180 V. Potential is a scalar, so there is no direction to state; it is positive because the charge is positive and the reference is zero at infinity.
P: 2. The work needed equals the potential energy at the end, since U = 0 at infinity: W = kqQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁹)(5.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.10) ≈ 9.0×10⁻⁷ J. It is positive because like charges repel and you must push. Check: the same answer comes from W = qV, with V = kQ/r = 449.5 V from the fixed charge: (2.0×10⁻⁹)(449.5) ≈ 9.0×10⁻⁷ J.
P: 3. In a uniform field, ΔV = Ed = (2.0×10⁴ V/m)(5.0×10⁻³ m) = 100 V. Check the units: V/m × m = V.
P: 4. Energy first: the electron gains KE = |q|ΔV = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)(500 V) ≈ 8.0×10⁻¹⁷ J, which is 500 eV. Then speed from KE = ½mv²: v = √(2KE/m) = √[2(8.0×10⁻¹⁷)/(9.11×10⁻³¹)] ≈ 1.33×10⁷ m/s, about 4.4 % of the speed of light. Is the non-relativistic formula good enough? The relativistic correction scales with KE/(mc²) = 500 eV/511 keV ≈ 0.001, so the classical answer is off by only about 0.1 %. Above about 10 keV, as in an old CRT or an X-ray tube, you would need the relativistic formula.
H2: Chapter 6 Solutions
P: 1. Q = CV = (4.0×10⁻⁶ F)(12 V) = 4.8×10⁻⁵ C = 48 µC. Energy: U = ½CV² = ½(4.0×10⁻⁶)(12)² ≈ 2.9×10⁻⁴ J, which is 0.29 mJ. Check with the other form: U = ½QV = ½(48 µC)(12 V) = 288 µJ.
P: 2. With C = ε₀A/d, doubling d halves C. The charge is fixed (the capacitor is isolated), so V = Q/C doubles. The energy U = Q²/(2C) also doubles. Where did the extra energy come from? The plates attract each other, and you did work pulling them apart; that work is now stored in the field, which fills twice the volume at the same field strength (E = σ/ε₀ does not change with Q fixed).
P: 3. A dielectric multiplies the capacitance by κ: C = κC₀ = 3.5 × 10 pF = 35 pF. The dielectric's molecules polarize and partly cancel the field, so the same charge produces a smaller voltage, and C = Q/V rises.
P: 4. Capacitors in parallel add: C = 2.0 + 4.0 + 4.0 = 10.0 µF. The source charges this total: Q = CV = (10.0×10⁻⁶ F)(9.0 V) = 9.0×10⁻⁵ C = 90 µC. Check by parts: 18 µC + 36 µC + 36 µC = 90 µC, since every capacitor sees the full 9.0 V.
H2: Chapter 7 Solutions
P: 1. Q = IΔt = (3.0 A)(5.0 s) = 15 C. Check the units: an ampere is a coulomb per second, so A × s = C.
P: 2. R = ρL/A. Tripling L multiplies R by 3; halving A multiplies R by 2, because R ∝ 1/A. Together R rises by 3 × 2 = 6. This is what happens when a wire is stretched: it gets longer and thinner at the same time.
P: 3. Use the linear model R(T) = R₀[1 + α(T − T₀)]. Here ΔT = 80 − 20 = 60 °C, so R = 100[1 + (0.004)(60)] = 100 × 1.24 = 124 Ω. A 24 % change from a 60 °C rise is why copper windings and PCB traces run noticeably more resistive when hot.
P: 4. Equal length and equal resistance means ρ_Cu L/A_Cu = ρ_Al L/A_Al, so A_Cu/A_Al = ρ_Cu/ρ_Al = (1.68×10⁻⁸)/(2.65×10⁻⁸) ≈ 0.634. The copper wire needs only about 63 % of the aluminum wire's cross-section. Aluminum still wins on overhead power lines because it is about a third as dense, so the thicker aluminum wire is roughly half the weight of the copper one.
H2: Chapter 8 Solutions
P: 1. Ohm's law: R = V/I = 9.0 V/0.30 A = 30 Ω. Power: P = VI = (9.0)(0.30) = 2.7 W. Check: I²R = (0.30)²(30) = 2.7 W. A 2.7 W resistor is a large part; a common 0.25 W resistor would burn.
P: 2. Product over sum: R = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂) = (4.0)(12)/(4.0 + 12) = 48/16 = 3.0 Ω. Check: the parallel result must be smaller than the smaller resistor, and 3.0 Ω < 4.0 Ω.
P: 3. From P = V²/R, R = V²/P = (5.0)²/0.25 = 100 Ω. This is a minimum: a smaller resistor would draw more current and dissipate more than 0.25 W. Following this chapter's bench note, a designer would derate to half power and choose about 200 Ω, or a 0.5 W part.
P: 4. The divider rule: V₈ₖ = V × R₈ₖ/(R₂ₖ + R₈ₖ) = 10 × 8.0/(2.0 + 8.0) = 8.0 V. Check: the current is 10 V/10 kΩ = 1.0 mA, and (1.0 mA)(8.0 kΩ) = 8.0 V; the other 2.0 V falls across the 2.0 kΩ.
P: 5. This is the divider rule with the LDR on top: V_out = 3.3 × 10/(10 + R_LDR), with resistances in kilohms. Dim hallway: V_out = 3.3 × 10/40 ≈ 0.83 V. Desk lamp: V_out = 3.3 × 10/13 ≈ 2.54 V. More light gives a higher voltage, which is what a night light or a display-brightness control wants. Power in the LDR under the lamp: I = 3.3/13 kΩ ≈ 0.254 mA, and P = I²R = (0.254×10⁻³)²(3000) ≈ 0.19 mW, far below a GL5528-type cell's 100 mW rating.
H2: Chapter 9 Solutions
P: 1. F = |q|vB sin 90° = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)(3.0×10⁵ m/s)(0.20 T) ≈ 9.6×10⁻¹⁵ N. The direction follows from F = qv × B with the electron's negative sign, so it is opposite to what the right-hand rule gives for a positive charge.
P: 2. F = BIL = (0.30 T)(4.0 A)(0.50 m) = 0.60 N, perpendicular to both the wire and the field. This is the force that turns every electric motor.
P: 3. The magnetic force F = qv × B is always perpendicular to the velocity. A force at right angles to the motion does no work (W = F·d = 0), so the kinetic energy and the speed cannot change; only the direction can. That is why a charge in a uniform field moves in a circle at constant speed.
P: 4. From r = mv/(qB): v = rqB/m = (0.10 m)(1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)(0.80 T)/(1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg) ≈ 7.7×10⁶ m/s. Check: that is 2.6 % of the speed of light, so the non-relativistic formula is fine.
H2: Chapter 10 Solutions
P: 1. Parallel wires with currents in the same direction attract; opposite currents repel. Here they attract, with a force per meter of F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd) = (2×10⁻⁷)(3.0)(3.0)/(0.050) ≈ 3.6×10⁻⁵ N/m, about the weight of a few milligrams per meter. The usual mistake is to borrow the charge rule (like repels like); for currents it is the other way round.

H2: Chapter 12 Solutions
P: 1. The field E at a point is the force per unit charge a test charge would feel there: a vector, with a size and a direction, in N/C or V/m. The potential V is the potential energy per unit charge a test charge would have there: a scalar, in volts, defined relative to a reference point. Field tells you which way a charge is pushed; potential tells you how much energy each coulomb gains or loses moving between two points. They are linked: the field points from high potential to low, and its size is the rate at which the potential falls with distance.
P: 2. They describe the same fact, that a static charge produces a field whose strength falls as 1/r² and whose lines start and end on charges. Coulomb's law states it as a force between two point charges; Gauss's law states it as a total flux through a closed surface. Section 4.3 shows that applying Gauss's law to a sphere around a point charge gives Coulomb's law back. The surface form is more convenient for symmetric charge distributions, and it is the form that survives into Maxwell's equations.
P: 3. A resistor turns the energy delivered to it into heat, which leaves the circuit for good. An ideal capacitor stores the energy in its electric field, ½CV², and can return all of it to the circuit later. Both limit current in some sense, but only the resistor dissipates; the capacitor only borrows.
P: 4. Turning the coil through the magnetic field induces a current when a load is connected. That current, by Lenz's law, produces a force that opposes the motion causing it, a braking torque. More load means more current and a stronger braking torque, so whoever turns the crank must push harder. This is energy conservation: every watt delivered to the load is a watt of mechanical work at the shaft.
P: 5. ε₀ comes from electrostatics, from the force between charges (Coulomb's law). μ₀ comes from magnetism, from the force between current-carrying wires. Maxwell's equations predict waves of linked electric and magnetic fields travelling at c = 1/√(ε₀μ₀). Putting in the two measured constants gives about 3.00×10⁸ m/s, which matched the measured speed of light. No optical measurement went into the calculation, so the match told Maxwell that light is an electromagnetic wave.
P: 6. A capacitor is defined by Q = CV, so its current is i = C dv/dt: a fast change of voltage needs a large current, and the capacitor resists sudden voltage changes. An inductor is defined by Φ = LI, so its voltage is v = L di/dt: a fast change of current needs a large voltage, and the inductor resists sudden current changes. Swap voltage and current, and capacitance and inductance, and one statement turns into the other. That duality returns in Chapter 32 and in every AC circuit in Part III.

H2: Chapter 38 Solutions
P: 1. KCL says the currents into a node sum to zero. A node stores no charge, and charge is conserved, so whatever flows in must flow out at the same instant. KVL says the voltages around a closed loop sum to zero. Potential is a property of position, like height on a map: walk any loop back to where you started and you are at the same potential, so the rises and drops must cancel.
P: 2. Count the equations. Nodal analysis needs one per node minus the reference; mesh analysis needs one per mesh. Choose the method with fewer. Current sources favor nodal analysis, because they drop straight into a KCL equation; voltage sources favor mesh analysis, because they drop straight into a KVL equation. A non-planar circuit rules out mesh analysis.
P: 3. At R_L = 0 the load voltage is zero and the current is at its maximum, V_Th/R_Th. As R_L rises, the voltage rises toward V_Th and the current falls toward zero. Power, the product of the two, is zero at both ends and peaks between them, at R_L = R_Th, where the load gets V_Th²/(4R_Th).
P: 4. Superposition works because voltages and currents depend linearly on the sources: double a source and its contribution doubles. Power depends on the square of current (or the product of voltage and current), and (a + b)² ≠ a² + b²; the cross term 2ab is missing from the sum of the separate powers. So superpose voltages and currents, then compute power from the totals.
P: 5. Below resonance, ωL is smaller than 1/(ωC): the inductive reactance has fallen with frequency and the capacitive reactance has risen. The net reactance ωL − 1/(ωC) is negative, so the circuit is capacitive and the current leads the voltage.
P: 6. An ideal transformer neither stores nor dissipates energy, so the power into the primary equals the power out of the secondary: V₁I₁ = V₂I₂. The voltages follow the turns ratio, V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁. For the products to stay equal, the currents must follow the inverse ratio, I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂. Step the voltage up tenfold and the current steps down tenfold.
H1: Appendix C — Glossary
P: Accuracy — closeness of a measurement to the true value. Distinct from precision and from resolution.
P: ADC (analog-to-digital converter) — a circuit that turns a voltage into a number; an N-bit ADC splits its range into 2ᴺ steps.
P: Admittance — the reciprocal of impedance, Y = 1/Z, in siemens. Adds for parallel branches.
P: Ampere (A) — SI unit of electric current; one coulomb per second.
P: Ampere's law — Relates the circulation of the magnetic field around a closed loop to the current enclosed by that loop.
P: Amplitude — the peak departure of a sinusoid from zero, written V_m. Not the same as the RMS value.
P: Apparent power (S) — The product of RMS voltage and RMS current, measured in volt-amperes (VA), combining real and reactive power.
P: Argand diagram — the complex plane, with real part plotted horizontally and imaginary part vertically.
P: Branch — A single path between two nodes in a circuit, generally containing one circuit element.
P: Capacitance (C) — The ratio of charge stored to voltage applied across a pair of conductors; measured in farads.
P: Conductor — A material in which charge (typically electrons) moves freely in response to an electric field.
P: Conjugate — the complex number with the sign of its imaginary part reversed, written z*. Used to make denominators real.
P: Coulomb (C) — SI unit of electric charge.
P: Coulomb's law — The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
P: Decibel — a logarithmic ratio. 10·log₁₀ for power ratios, 20·log₁₀ for voltage ratios. Dimensionless unless a reference is stated, as in dBm.
P: Derivative — the instantaneous rate of change of a quantity, written dv/dt.
P: Determinant — a scalar computed from a square matrix. A zero determinant signals that a system has no unique solution.
P: Dielectric — An insulating material placed between a capacitor's plates to increase its capacitance.
P: Dimensional analysis — checking that both sides of an equation reduce to the same combination of base units. Blind to dimensionless constants.
P: Drift velocity — The average velocity of charge carriers in the direction of current flow, superimposed on much larger random thermal motion.
P: Electric field (E) — Force per unit charge at a point in space; the physical condition set up by charge that a second charge responds to.
P: Electric flux — A measure of the electric field passing through a given surface.
P: Electric potential (V) — Potential energy per unit charge; commonly called voltage.
P: Electromotive force (EMF) — The energy per unit charge supplied by a source (battery, generator, or induction) that drives current around a circuit.
P: Elementary charge (e) — The smallest unit of free charge, carried by the proton (+e) and the electron (−e).
P: Engineering notation — scientific notation restricted to exponents that are multiples of three, so each maps onto an SI prefix.
P: Equipotential surface — A surface on which every point is at the same electric potential; always perpendicular to the local electric field.
P: Euler's formula — e^(jθ) = cos θ + j sin θ. The link between exponentials and sinusoids, and the foundation of phasor analysis.
P: Faraday's law — A changing magnetic flux through a loop induces an electromotive force around that loop.
P: Ferromagnetic material — A material (such as iron) whose internal atomic-scale current loops align strongly with an applied magnetic field, greatly amplifying it.
P: Gauss's law — The total electric flux through a closed surface is proportional to the net charge enclosed.
P: Hysteresis — two switching thresholds instead of one, so that a slowly changing or noisy input causes one clean change of state instead of chatter.
P: Impedance (Z) — The complex-number generalization of resistance, relating voltage and current phasors: V = IZ.
P: Inductance (L) — A coil's characteristic constant relating the induced EMF opposing a change in its own current to the rate of that current's change.
P: Insulator — A material in which electrons remain bound to individual atoms and do not conduct appreciable current under ordinary fields.
P: Integral — the accumulated total of a quantity, equivalently the area under its curve.
P: I²C — a two-wire bus (clock and data) on which chips only pull the lines low and pull-up resistors return them high.
P: j — the imaginary unit, defined by j² = −1. Written i by mathematicians; electrical engineers use j because i denotes current.
P: Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) — The sum of currents entering any node equals the sum of currents leaving it.
P: Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) — The sum of voltage rises and drops around any closed loop is zero.
P: LDR (light-dependent resistor) — a photoresistor whose resistance falls as the light on it increases.
P: Lenz's law — The direction of an induced current always opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it.
P: Level shifting — converting a logic signal between two supply voltages, such as 5 V and 3.3 V.
P: Load cell — a metal element carrying strain gauges wired as a bridge, whose output is proportional to the force or weight on it.
P: LSB (least significant bit) — the smallest step an ADC can resolve: its full-scale range divided by 2ᴺ.
P: Magnetic field (B) — The field that exerts a force on moving charge; measured in tesla.
P: Magnetic flux — A measure of the magnetic field passing through a given surface; measured in webers.
P: Mesh — A loop in a planar circuit that contains no other loop within it.
P: Mesh analysis — A systematic circuit-analysis method using loop (mesh) currents as unknowns, governed by KVL.
P: Mutual inductance (M) — A measure of the coupling between two coils, by which a changing current in one induces a voltage in the other.
P: Nodal analysis — A systematic circuit-analysis method using node voltages as unknowns, governed by KCL.
P: Node — A point in a circuit where two or more elements connect, all at the same potential.
P: Norton equivalent — A simplified representation of a linear network as a current source in parallel with a resistance.
P: Ohm's law — For an ohmic material, voltage across it is proportional to the current through it: V = IR.
P: Passive sign convention — The convention assigning a passive element's voltage polarity so assumed current enters at its + terminal.
P: Permeability of free space (μ₀) — The constant relating magnetic field to the current that produces it in vacuum.
P: Permittivity of free space (ε₀) — The constant relating electric field to the charge that produces it in vacuum.
P: Phase — the angular offset of a sinusoid from a reference, in degrees or radians.
P: Phasor — a complex number encoding the RMS magnitude and phase of a sinusoid, with the time dependence factored out.
P: Power factor — The cosine of the phase angle between voltage and current; the fraction of apparent power that is real power.
P: Precision — the repeatability of a measurement. An instrument can be precise and inaccurate simultaneously.
P: Pull-up resistor — a resistor from a signal line to the supply that holds the line high unless something pulls it low.
P: Quality factor (Q) — A measure of how sharply peaked a resonant circuit's response is.
P: Reactance — the imaginary part of impedance. Positive for inductors, negative for capacitors. Stores energy rather than dissipating it.
P: Reactive power (Q) — Power exchanged between source and load each cycle with zero net average transfer, measured in VAR.
P: Real power (P) — The average power actually delivered to and consumed by a load, measured in watts.
P: Resistance (R) — The opposition a conductor presents to current flow; measured in ohms.
P: Resistivity (ρ) — An intrinsic material property describing how strongly a material resists current flow, independent of its shape.
P: Resolution — the smallest change an instrument can display. Independent of accuracy.
P: Resonant frequency — The frequency at which a circuit's inductive and capacitive reactance exactly cancel.
P: RMS (root-mean-square) — The value of a sinusoid that delivers the same average power to a resistor as a DC value of the same number.
P: Significant figures — the digits of a number that carry real information about its precision.
P: Superconductivity — A state, reached below a critical temperature in certain materials, of exactly zero electrical resistivity.
P: Supermesh — A combined mesh technique used when a current source is shared between two meshes.
P: Supernode — A combined node technique used when a voltage source connects two non-reference nodes.
P: Superposition — The principle that the net force, field, voltage, or current from multiple linear sources equals the sum of the contributions from each source acting alone, with all other independent sources deactivated in turn.
P: Tesla (T) — SI unit of magnetic field.
P: Thermistor — a resistor made to change strongly with temperature; an NTC type's resistance falls as it warms.
P: Thevenin equivalent — A simplified representation of a linear network as a voltage source in series with a resistance.
P: Time constant (τ) — The characteristic timescale of a first-order transient, equal to RC or L/R.
P: Tolerance — the guaranteed range within which a component value lies, usually a percentage of the marked value.
P: Transformer — A device using mutual inductance between two windings to change voltage and current by a fixed turns ratio.
P: Transient — The temporary response of a circuit as it moves from one steady condition to another.
P: Volt (V) — SI unit of electric potential; one joule per coulomb.
P: Voltage divider — A pair of series resistors that splits an applied voltage between them in proportion to their resistances.
P: Wheatstone bridge — two voltage dividers side by side with the output taken between their midpoints, zero when balanced.
P: Worst-case analysis — evaluating a design with every component at its least favorable tolerance extreme simultaneously.
H1: Appendix D — Chapter Summaries
P: The following summaries are meant for review after finishing the book, not as a substitute for reading each chapter in full — they compress each chapter to its central thread, deliberately leaving out the worked examples, misconceptions, and much of the connective reasoning that make the ideas stick on a first read. Use them the way you would use a set of flashcards: to check, a week or a month after finishing a chapter, whether its core idea has actually stayed with you, and to see at a glance how the thirty-eight chapters connect into a single, continuous argument rather than a list of unrelated topics.
H2: Chapter 1 — Electric Charge
P: Charge is a fundamental property of matter, quantized in units of the elementary charge e, and always conserved. Objects become charged not by creating charge but by moving electrons between materials — by friction, by contact, or by induction. Conductors allow their electrons to move freely; insulators do not. This distinction, purely a matter of how tightly electrons are bound, is the seed from which the entire rest of electrical engineering grows: every subsequent chapter is, in one way or another, a study of what charge does once it is free to move, or of what happens when a field acts on charge that cannot move.
H2: Chapter 2 — Coulomb's Law and the Electric Force
P: Coulomb's law quantifies the force between two point charges: proportional to the product of the charges, inversely proportional to the square of their separation. The inverse-square form reflects the geometry of a spreading sphere and reappears throughout physics. Superposition — that forces from multiple sources add as vectors — is what makes any charge configuration, however complex, tractable in principle, even though the bookkeeping quickly becomes the practical bottleneck rather than the physics itself.
H2: Chapter 3 — The Electric Field
P: Rather than charges acting on each other directly across empty space, each charge sets up a field that fills the surrounding space, and a second charge responds only to the local field at its own position. This reframing — force as a two-step process, source to field to second charge — resolves the “action at a distance” puzzle and sets up field lines as a way of visualizing both direction (the tangent to the line) and strength (how closely lines are spaced) at a glance.
H2: Chapter 4 — Electric Flux and Gauss's Law
P: Gauss's law relates the total electric flux through any closed surface to the net charge enclosed, and is exactly equivalent to Coulomb's law — but for symmetric charge distributions (spheres, infinite lines, infinite sheets), it turns what would be a difficult integral into simple algebra by letting you choose a Gaussian surface that matches the distribution's own symmetry. Applied to a conductor, it explains why excess charge sits only on the outer surface and why the field inside a conductor in equilibrium is always exactly zero.
H2: Chapter 5 — Electric Potential Energy and Voltage
P: Electric potential — voltage — is potential energy per unit charge, a scalar quantity that is almost always more convenient to work with than the field directly, since it adds algebraically with no direction to track. Field and potential are two views of the same physics: the field points in the direction potential falls off fastest, and for a uniform field this reduces to the simple, practically important relation E = ΔV/d. Only potential differences, never an absolute potential, are ever physically meaningful.
H2: Chapter 6 — Conductors, Capacitance, and Dielectrics
P: A capacitor is any pair of conductors that stores equal and opposite charge at some voltage between them, with capacitance C = Q/V depending only on geometry. The parallel-plate formula C = ε₀A/d (or κε₀A/d with a dielectric) is derived directly from the field of a charged sheet and the field-to-voltage relation of the previous chapter. Capacitors store real energy in their electric field, and — in a rule that inverts the resistor pattern from the next chapter — combine in parallel by simple addition and in series by reciprocal sum.
H2: Chapter 7 — Electric Current and Resistance
P: Current is the rate at which charge moves past a point, conventionally taken in the direction positive charge would flow, even though in metals it is electrons moving the opposite way. Individual charge carriers drift remarkably slowly; what makes a circuit respond quickly is the field's own rapid propagation, not the carriers' speed. Resistance, R = ρL/A, depends on both geometry and an intrinsic material property, resistivity, which itself depends on temperature — increasing for metals, decreasing for semiconductors, a contrast that hints at the very different physics underlying each.
H2: Chapter 8 — Ohm's Law and a First Look at Circuits
P: Ohm's law, V = IR, is an empirical rule (not a fundamental law) that holds well for metals and manufactured resistors, though it fails deliberately for diodes and transistors later in this series. Power dissipated in a resistor, P = IV = I²R = V²/R, is converted irreversibly to heat. Resistors combine by direct addition in series and reciprocal sum in parallel — the opposite pattern from capacitors — and a series pair forms a voltage divider, one of the most common sub-circuits in all of electronics. The LED's series resistor works from a 3.3 V microcontroller pin too, and a light-dependent resistor in a divider turns light into a voltage.
H2: Chapter 9 — Magnetism and the Magnetic Field
P: Unlike the electric field, the magnetic field exerts a force only on moving charge, given by F = qv×B: always perpendicular to both velocity and field, and therefore never capable of doing work or changing a particle's speed — only its direction. A charge moving perpendicular to a uniform field travels in a circle; a current-carrying wire, being a great many moving charges together, feels a corresponding force F = IL×B, the mechanism behind every electric motor.
H2: Chapter 10 — Sources of the Magnetic Field
P: Moving charge does not only respond to magnetic fields — it produces them, unifying electricity and magnetism into a single phenomenon. A long straight wire produces a field falling off as 1/r; two such wires attract if their currents run the same direction and repel if opposite, the reverse of the static-charge rule. Ampere's law provides the same kind of symmetry-exploiting shortcut for magnetic fields that Gauss's law provides for electric fields, most usefully in deriving the uniform field inside a solenoid, whose strength can be amplified enormously by a ferromagnetic core.
H2: Chapter 11 — Electromagnetic Induction
P: A changing magnetic flux through a loop induces an electromotive force, by Faraday's law, ε = −N dΦʙ/dt — the single fact underlying every electric generator and transformer. Lenz's law, encoded in that minus sign, guarantees the induced current always opposes the change that produced it, a direct consequence of energy conservation. A coil's own self-inductance, quantifying its resistance to changes in its own current, is the first hint of the inductor as a third fundamental circuit element, alongside the resistor and capacitor.
H2: Chapter 12 — Toward Maxwell's Equations
P: Maxwell's four equations — two versions of Gauss's law, Faraday's law, and a corrected Ampere's law — unify everything in this book into one self-consistent system. Maxwell's own addition, the displacement current, completes a symmetry that predicts self-sustaining electromagnetic waves, propagating at a speed computable from ε₀ and μ₀ alone, that turns out to match the measured speed of light exactly — revealing light itself as an electromagnetic wave, and setting up every later book in this series as, in one way or another, a special case of these same four equations.
H2: Chapter 13 — Quantities, Units, and the SI System
P: A quantity is a number times a unit, and the multiplication is real, so units obey algebra. Seven SI base units; the electrical derived units are built from four of them. Dimensional analysis catches structural errors for free but is blind to dimensionless constants, including the 2π that separates hertz from radians per second.
H2: Chapter 14 — Prefixes, Scientific Notation, and Engineering Notation
P: Prefixes multiply by powers of ten and case is significant. Engineering notation restricts exponents to multiples of three so they map onto prefixes. Reciprocal prefix pairs cancel: micro times mega is one, milli times kilo is one. Exponents must match before mantissas can be added.
H2: Chapter 15 — Precision, Significant Figures, and Uncertainty
P: A measurement includes its uncertainty. Significant figures describe how to report a result, not how to compute one, so do not round intermediate steps. Relative uncertainties add for products; absolute uncertainties add for sums. Subtracting nearly equal numbers destroys precision. Accuracy, precision, and resolution are three different things. An ADC's resolution (0.71 mV per count on the ESP32-S3) is not its accuracy (about ±50 mV), and a sensor reading is only as good as the constants behind it, such as the temperature-dependent speed of sound.
H2: Chapter 16 — Algebra for Circuit Analysis
P: The reciprocal of a sum is not the sum of the reciprocals. Product-over-sum handles exactly two parallel elements. Voltage divides in proportion to resistance, current in inverse proportion. The discriminant of a quadratic tells you whether a second-order circuit is overdamped, critically damped, or oscillatory.
H2: Chapter 17 — Systems of Linear Equations
P: Circuit equations from Kirchhoff's laws are linear, so systematic solution methods apply. Elimination scales better than substitution. A zero determinant indicates the system has no unique solution — either redundant equations or, less often, a physically inconsistent circuit — not an arithmetic mistake. The matrix form G·V = I is what simulators actually solve.
H2: Chapter 18 — Functions, Graphs, and Limiting Behavior
P: Read a transfer function at its limits before plotting it: low-frequency asymptote, high-frequency asymptote, and behavior at the corner give the shape. Log axes turn power laws into straight lines whose slope reveals the order of the system. A logarithmic axis has no zero. A trend is a slope: the weather station's pressure change per hour, where a constant offset cancels and only the relative accuracy matters.
H2: Chapter 19 — Exponentials, Logarithms, and the Decibel
P: The exponential is the function whose rate of change equals itself, which is why it governs every first-order circuit. Logarithms convert multiplication to addition, which is the entire reason decibels exist. Use 10·log for power and 20·log for voltage. dB values add along a chain; dBm values do not.
H2: Chapter 20 — Trigonometry and the Sinusoid
P: A sinusoid is fixed by three numbers: amplitude, angular frequency, and phase. Radians are required for calculus; degrees are convenient for phase. The Pythagorean identity produces impedance magnitudes and the sum-of-angles identity produces mixing. RMS is defined by equal heating, and the 0.707 factor applies only to sinusoids.
H2: Chapter 21 — Complex Numbers
P: A complex number carries two quantities at once, which for engineers means magnitude and phase. Rectangular form suits addition; polar form suits multiplication. The conjugate makes denominators real. Multiplying by j is a 90° rotation, and j² = −1 is that fact applied twice.
H2: Chapter 22 — Euler's Formula and the Complex Exponential
P: Euler's formula identifies e^(jθ) with a unit arrow at angle θ, bridging rotation and oscillation. A real sinusoid is the real part of a rotating complex exponential. Differentiating that exponential multiplies it by jω, which converts calculus into arithmetic.
H2: Chapter 23 — Phasors and Impedance
P: In a linear circuit at one frequency, only amplitude and phase vary, so the frequency can be factored out into a phasor. Impedance is the resulting complex voltage-to-current ratio: R, jωL, and 1/(jωC). Every DC circuit technique carries over with R replaced by Z. Component voltages add as phasors, so they may exceed the supply voltage.
H2: Chapter 24 — Calculus for Electrical Engineering
P: A derivative is a rate and an integral is an accumulation. Capacitors and inductors are defined by derivatives, which is why a capacitor blocks DC and an inductor passes it. Differentiating a sinusoid brings down a factor of ω and advances the phase by 90°, which is exactly the jω of phasor analysis.
H2: Chapter 25 — Where This Book Leads Next
P: Part III uses Chapters 16, 17, 19, and 24 for transients and Chapters 20 to 23 continuously for AC, with the Smith chart of Chapter 37 as the RF extension. Filters use Chapters 18 and 19. RF work leans on complex arithmetic and decibels throughout. The Fourier and Laplace transforms generalize the phasor idea, and nothing later in the series depends on a fundamentally unfamiliar mathematical foundation, even though later material builds on these tools in more demanding ways.
H2: Chapter 26 — Circuit Elements and Kirchhoff's Laws
P: KCL (charge conservation at a node) and KVL (potential as a well-defined function of position, applied around a loop) are the two laws sufficient, together with each element's own v-i relationship, to solve any circuit. The passive sign convention fixes voltage polarity relative to assumed current direction so that power calculations come out with a consistent, interpretable sign.
H2: Chapter 27 — Equivalent Resistance and Dividers
P: Series/parallel reduction, whenever it applies, is the fastest path to a solution. The voltage divider splits voltage in proportion to each resistor's own value; the current divider splits current in proportion to the other resistor's value — an inversion worth rederiving rather than memorizing. Most sensors are dividers: a thermistor or LDR changes a resistance and a divider turns it into a voltage. A Wheatstone bridge removes the large common voltage so a small change can be compared or amplified, which is how an analog thermostat and a load cell work, and a divider also brings a 5 V signal safely down to 3.3 V.
H2: Chapter 28 — Nodal Analysis
P: Treating node voltages as unknowns and applying KCL at each non-reference node produces a system solvable for any resistive network, planar or not. Voltage sources between two non-reference nodes require the supernode technique.
H2: Chapter 29 — Mesh Analysis
P: Treating mesh currents as unknowns and applying KVL around each mesh is nodal analysis's dual, applicable only to planar circuits. Current sources shared between meshes require the supermesh technique. Choosing between nodal and mesh analysis is a practical efficiency decision, not a question of correctness — both, done right, agree.
H2: Chapter 30 — Thevenin and Norton Equivalents
P: Any linear network, viewed from two terminals, reduces to a single source and a single resistance — a voltage source in series (Thevenin) or a current source in parallel (Norton), related by I_N = V_Th/R_Th. Maximum power transfer to a load occurs when the load resistance matches R_Th, at a cost of only 50% efficiency.
H2: Chapter 31 — Superposition and Linearity
P: In a linear circuit with multiple sources, each source's individual contribution to a voltage or current can be found separately and summed — a direct consequence of the linearity of Ohm's law, KCL, and KVL. Superposition does not extend to power, since power depends nonlinearly (via squaring or multiplication) on voltage and current.
H2: Chapter 32 — Capacitors and Inductors as Circuit Elements
P: A capacitor's current and an inductor's voltage depend on the rate of change of the other quantity, making both elements dynamic rather than instantaneous like a resistor. Capacitor voltage and inductor current cannot change instantaneously — the starting point for transient analysis. Inductors combine like resistors (series adds, parallel reciprocal-sums); capacitors combine the opposite way.
H2: Chapter 33 — First-Order Transients
P: RC and RL circuits, following a switching event, relax exponentially toward a new steady state, governed by the time constant τ = RC or τ = L/R. Every such transient follows the same general form, x(t) = x_final + (x_initial − x_final)e^(−t/τ), regardless of which specific quantity or circuit is involved. The same exponential sets how long a debounced button takes to settle and how fast an I²C bus can run: t_r = 0.8473RC from 30 % to 70 %.
H2: Chapter 34 — Sinusoidal Steady-State Analysis
P: With impedance defined, every DC technique from Chapters 26–31 — KVL/KCL, series/parallel combination, dividers, nodal and mesh analysis, Thevenin/Norton, superposition — applies unchanged to AC circuits, using complex impedances in place of real resistances. RMS values, not peak amplitudes, are the practical standard for quoting AC voltages and currents.
H2: Chapter 35 — AC Power and Power Factor
P: Average AC power is P = V_rms I_rms cosθ, where cosθ, the power factor, measures what fraction of the apparent power (S = V_rms I_rms) is real, useful power (P) rather than reactive power (Q) that is exchanged but not net-delivered. Low power factor means larger current for the same real power, and correspondingly larger transmission losses — the practical reason power factor correction matters. An energy dashboard multiplies real power by time; cycling appliances need their average power, and multiplying RMS volts by RMS amps overstates the cost of motor loads.
H2: Chapter 36 — Resonance, Frequency Response, and Transformers
P: A series RLC circuit's inductive and capacitive reactances cancel exactly at the resonant frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC), leaving a purely resistive, current-maximizing impedance; the quality factor Q measures how sharply peaked this resonance is. An ideal transformer trades voltage for current (or vice versa) in exact proportion to its turns ratio, keeping apparent power unchanged — the basis of high-voltage long-distance power transmission.
H2: Chapter 37 — The Smith Chart, Impedance Matching, and Bias Networks
P: Matching a complex load to a real system impedance requires a conjugate match, not merely equal resistances. The Smith chart plots the reflection coefficient Γ over a grid of constant-resistance circles and constant-reactance arcs, letting an L-network or a microstrip stub be designed graphically: series elements move a point along a circle of constant r, shunt elements along a circle of constant g. RF chokes and high-value resistors bias a stage without disturbing its matching networks, because one presents a high reactance and the other draws negligible current, at the frequency that actually matters.
H2: Chapter 38 — Where This Series Goes Next
P: Every technique in this book applies only to linear circuits. Books 2 and 3 introduce the nonlinear semiconductor devices — diodes, transistors — that make up the bulk of real electronics, and Book 3 shows how their small-signal, linearized behavior around a fixed operating point reduces, once again, to exactly the linear circuit analysis this book has built.
H1: Appendix E — A Practical Circuit-Analysis Checklist
P: This appendix collects, in one place, the habits this book has recommended chapter by chapter — not new material, but a single reference to run through when a solution comes out wrong, or before trusting one that came out right.
H2: Before Writing Any Equations
LI: Identify every node, and confirm you have not accidentally split a single node (joined by plain wire) into two, or merged two genuinely distinct nodes into one.
LI: Assign a current direction and voltage polarity to every branch, using the passive sign convention, and mark them directly on the diagram rather than trying to hold them in your head.
LI: For nodal analysis, choose a reference node — usually the one with the most connections — and set it to 0 V.
LI: For mesh analysis, confirm the circuit is planar before proceeding; if it is not, nodal analysis is the only systematic option available.
LI: Check for supernodes (a voltage source directly between two non-reference nodes) or supermeshes (a current source shared between two meshes) before writing ordinary node or mesh equations.
H2: While Solving
LI: Count your equations against your unknowns before solving — one KCL equation per non-reference node (or one combined equation per supernode), or one KVL equation per mesh (or one per supermesh, plus its constraint equation).
LI: For AC (phasor) problems, confirm every impedance is written correctly — R for a resistor, jωL for an inductor, 1/(jωC) or −j/(ωC) for a capacitor — and that ω has been computed correctly from the source frequency before any impedance is evaluated numerically.
LI: Add and subtract complex numbers in rectangular form; multiply and divide them in polar form. Converting to the wrong form for a given operation is one of the most common sources of arithmetic error in phasor problems.
H2: After Solving
LI: If a branch current came out negative, that is not necessarily an error — it means the actual current flows opposite to your originally assumed direction, and the magnitude is still correct.
LI: Check KCL and KVL directly against your final answer: plug the solved voltages and currents back into the original node and loop equations and confirm they balance, rather than trusting the algebra that produced them.
LI: Where two independent methods are available for the same quantity (for instance, nodal analysis and a direct Thevenin-equivalent reduction), solving the same problem both ways is one of the most reliable checks available — genuine agreement between two structurally different methods is strong evidence against a hidden error in either one.
LI: For power calculations in multi-source circuits, confirm you superposed voltages/currents first and computed power only once, from the final total — never by summing individually-computed powers from each source.
LI: For transient problems, confirm the initial and final conditions separately before writing the general exponential solution — an error in either endpoint propagates through the entire time-domain answer even if the time constant itself is correct.
H2: A Final Habit Worth Keeping
P: Every method in this book — nodal analysis, mesh analysis, Thevenin equivalents, superposition, phasor analysis — is built on the same two laws from Chapter 26. When a solution feels uncertain, the most reliable recovery is rarely to search for a more advanced technique; it is to return to KCL and KVL directly, applied carefully and patiently to the specific circuit in front of you, and to let the rest of the book's machinery earn back your trust one correctly-checked step at a time.
