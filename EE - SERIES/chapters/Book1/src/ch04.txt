H1: Chapter 4 — Electric Flux and Gauss's Law
P: Why does a car protect you from lightning? Largely because of a consequence of Gauss's law: excess charge on a conductor sits on its outer surface, and the static field inside a closed conducting shell is zero. Your phone losing signal in a steel elevator is the radio-frequency cousin of the same idea.
P: Where you meet this: Metal enclosures and shielded cables, coaxial cable (whose field Gauss's law gives in one line), and the fact that a capacitor's charge sits on the facing surfaces of its plates.
H2: 4.1 Electric Flux
P: Hold a window screen out in falling rain, facing the rain squarely, and count how many drops pass through it in a few seconds. Now tilt the same screen, in the same rain, so it faces more sideways: fewer drops get through, even though the rain itself has not changed at all — only how squarely the screen meets it has. Tilt the screen far enough, edge-on to the rain, and essentially no drops pass through it, however heavy the actual rainfall.
P: So a useful measure of “how much rain gets through” cannot be just the rain’s intensity, or just the screen’s size — it has to combine how heavy the rain is, how large the screen is, and how squarely the screen faces it. Electric field lines behave exactly the same way: a surface sitting in a field has no way around dealing with its own tilt, once the field is actually there to pass through it.
P: Electric flux is exactly that combined measure: how much field passes through a given surface, once the surface’s own orientation is accounted for. A flat surface facing squarely into a field gets the full effect; the same surface tilted gets less, scaled down by the same cosine factor a tilted rain-screen would use.
P: A uniform field of 100 N/C passing through a flat surface of area 0.50 m², facing the field squarely, carries a flux of 50 N·m²/C. Tilt that same surface to 60° from facing the field, and the flux drops to 25 N·m²/C — exactly half. Tilt it all the way to 90°, edge-on to the field, and the flux drops to zero, however strong the field actually is.
P: That combined quantity — field strength times area times the cosine of the tilt angle — is the electric flux through the surface, the field-line-counting intuition from Chapter 3 made precise. For a flat surface of area A in a uniform field E, with the surface oriented so its normal (the perpendicular direction) makes angle θ with the field, the flux is:
EQ: ΦE = E A cosθ
P: Flux is maximized when the field is perpendicular to the surface (θ = 0) and zero when the field runs parallel to the surface (θ = 90°), since in that case no field lines pierce through it at all. For a curved surface or a nonuniform field, this generalizes to a surface integral, E·dA integrated over the surface — but for this book's purposes, the cases that matter are the ones with enough symmetry that the integral becomes trivial, which is exactly the point of Gauss's law.
H2: 4.2 Gauss's Law
P: Here is the genuinely surprising payoff of building flux this carefully: wrap any closed surface, any shape at all, entirely around a charge, and add up the flux through every part of it — bulges, dents, and all — and the total depends on nothing except how much charge is trapped inside. Move the charge around inside the surface, and the total is unchanged. Change the surface's shape while keeping the same charge inside, and the total is unchanged again. Gauss's law states this precisely: the total electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the net charge enclosed by that surface:
EQ: ΦE = Qenc / ε0
P: This is one of the four Maxwell equations (met formally in Chapter 12) and it is exactly equivalent to Coulomb's law — nothing new physically is being added — but it is often dramatically easier to use. The closed surface in question, called a Gaussian surface, is entirely a mathematical construction you get to choose; it does not need to correspond to any physical boundary, and it does not need to contain any actual material at all.
FIG: fig4_1.png | Figure 4.1 — A Gaussian surface enclosing a point charge: the flux through it depends only on the enclosed charge.
KEY: Choose a Gaussian surface that matches the symmetry of the charge distribution. For a point charge or any spherically symmetric distribution, use a sphere. For an infinite line or cylinder of charge, use a coaxial cylinder. For an infinite sheet of charge, use a “pillbox” straddling the sheet. Get the shape right, and the field comes out of the surface integral as a constant that can be pulled outside it, turning the integral into simple algebra.
H2: 4.3 Deriving Coulomb's Law from Gauss's Law
P: As a consistency check, and as the cleanest illustration of the method, take a single point charge q and surround it with an imaginary sphere of radius r centered on the charge. By symmetry, the field must point radially and have the same magnitude E everywhere on the sphere's surface — there is no direction along the sphere that is special, so the field cannot depend on where on the sphere you look. The flux integral therefore reduces to E multiplied by the sphere's total surface area, 4πr²:
EQ: E (4πr2) = q / ε0
EQ: E = q / (4πε0r2) = kq / r2
P: which is exactly the point-charge field from Chapter 3 — and this is precisely why the 4π was folded into the definition of k back in Chapter 2. Choosing ε₀ that way makes Gauss's law come out with no stray factors of 4π in it, at the cost of Coulomb's law carrying that factor instead. Physicists made that trade deliberately because Gauss's law, not Coulomb's law, is the one that generalizes to Maxwell's equations.
H2: 4.4 The Field of an Infinite Charged Sheet, a Line Charge, and a Charged Conductor
P: Three results from this method are used constantly in circuit-adjacent physics and are worth having on hand. First, an infinite sheet of charge with uniform surface charge density σ (charge per unit area) produces a field of constant magnitude on either side, independent of distance from the sheet:
EQ: E = σ / (2ε0)
P: Second, an infinite line of charge with uniform linear charge density λ (charge per unit length), found using a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius r, produces a field:
EQ: E = λ / (2πε0r)
P: falling off as 1/r rather than the 1/r² of a point source — a direct consequence of the source being an infinite line rather than a point, so that the “surface” over which the field spreads out grows only as the cylinder's circumference (proportional to r), not as a sphere's full surface area (proportional to r²).
P: Third — and this result underlies why charge sits on the outer surface of a conductor and why the interior of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium has exactly zero field — a Gaussian surface drawn entirely inside a conducting material, where charge is free to move, must enclose zero net charge, because if there were any field inside the conductor, its free electrons would still be accelerating, contradicting the assumption of equilibrium. All excess charge on a charged conductor therefore resides entirely on its outer surface, a fact this book will use again in Chapter 6 when introducing capacitors.
WE: Worked Example 4.1
PROB: A solid conducting sphere carries a total charge of +6.0 µC. Find the electric field at a point 20 cm from the center, given the sphere's radius is 5.0 cm.
SOL: Since 20 cm is outside the sphere, and the sphere is spherically symmetric, Gauss's law gives exactly the point-charge result with all the charge treated as concentrated at the center: E = kq/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(6.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² ≈ 1.35 × 10⁶ N/C. Note that the field outside a uniformly charged sphere is identical to that of a point charge of the same total charge — the sphere's actual radius plays no role once you are outside it.
WE: Worked Example 4.2
PROB: An infinite sheet carries a uniform surface charge density of +2.0 µC/m². Find the field 1.0 cm and 10 cm from the sheet, and comment on what you find.
SOL: E = σ/(2ε₀) = (2.0×10⁻⁶)/[2(8.85×10⁻¹²)] ≈ 1.13 × 10⁵ N/C — at both distances, since the field of an ideal infinite sheet does not depend on distance from the sheet at all. This is a genuinely surprising result on first encounter (compare it against the point-charge and line-charge results, which both fall off with distance), and it follows directly from the field-line picture in Chapter 3: for a truly infinite sheet, the field lines run straight out perpendicular to the sheet and never spread apart, so their density — and hence the field strength — never decreases with distance. Real, finite sheets only approximate this behavior close to the sheet, well away from its edges.
MIS: The infinite-sheet result E = σ/(2ε₀) is sometimes misapplied to a conductor's surface, where the correct result is instead E = σ/ε₀ — twice as large, with no factor of 2 in the denominator. The difference is not an error in either formula; it reflects a genuinely different physical situation. An isolated charged sheet (with vacuum or a symmetric environment on both sides) splits its flux evenly, half going each way, which is where the factor of 2 in the sheet result comes from. A conductor's outer surface, by contrast, has zero field on its interior side (Chapter 6) by definition of electrostatic equilibrium, so all of the flux is forced out through the exterior side alone, doubling the field there relative to the two-sided sheet case for the same σ.
BENCH: A shield only shields if it is closed and connected. Seams and slots leak once their length is a sizable fraction of a wavelength, and a cable shield whose drain wire was never actually bonded to the chassis does little at all. Both are common reasons a 'shielded' design fails its emissions test; Book 7 returns to the subject.
