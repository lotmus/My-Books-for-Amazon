H1: Chapter 15 — Precision, Significant Figures, and Uncertainty
P: Your meter says 4.732 V. How many of those digits are true? On an ordinary handheld meter, perhaps three. This chapter is about knowing which digits to believe, and why a resistor marked ±5% can make a four-digit calculation pointless.
P: Where you meet this: Resistor and capacitor tolerances, meter accuracy specifications, ADC resolution versus accuracy, and production-test limits.
P: A measurement is not a number; it is a number together with a statement of how well it is known. An oscilloscope that reads 5.02 V is not telling you the voltage is 5.02 V. It is telling you the voltage is near 5.02 V, with a spread that depends on the instrument, the probe, the temperature, and the day. This chapter is about carrying that spread through a calculation honestly.

H2: 15.1 Significant Figures
P: The significant figures of a number are the digits that carry real information. The rules are mechanical: all non-zero digits are significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant; leading zeros are never significant; trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.
P: So 0.00470 has three significant figures — the leading zeros are placeholders, the trailing zero is a claim of precision. The genuinely ambiguous case is a whole number ending in zeros: 4700 might have two, three, or four significant figures, and the plain notation cannot tell you which. Scientific notation resolves this completely, which is one of its main practical virtues: 4.7 × 10³ has two, 4.70 × 10³ has three, 4.700 × 10³ has four.
H2: 15.2 Propagating Precision Through a Calculation
P: Two rules cover most cases. For multiplication and division, the result carries as many significant figures as the least precise input. For addition and subtraction, the result carries as many decimal places as the input with the fewest decimal places.
P: The rules differ because the operations degrade precision differently, and it is worth seeing why rather than memorizing both. Multiplication scales relative error; addition scales absolute error. If you multiply a quantity known to 1% by one known to 10%, the answer is known to roughly 11%, regardless of the units involved. If you add a quantity known to ±0.5 V to one known to ±0.001 V, the sum is known to ±0.5 V — the precise measurement is swamped, and no amount of decimal places in the second number rescues it.
MIS: The most common error with significant figures is rounding at every intermediate step. Do not. Carry full precision through the calculation and round only the final answer. Rounding intermediate values injects error at each step, and those errors accumulate — in a long chain of calculations, aggressive intermediate rounding can easily shift a final answer by more than the measurement uncertainty you were trying to represent. The significant-figure rules describe how to report a result, not how to compute one.
H2: 15.3 Absolute and Relative Uncertainty
P: Absolute uncertainty is expressed in the units of the quantity: 5.02 V ± 0.05 V. Relative uncertainty is the same thing as a fraction or percentage of the value: 5.02 V ± 1%. Converting between them is straightforward, and which one you want depends on the operation you are about to perform.
P: For products and quotients, relative uncertainties add. If R is known to ±2% and I is known to ±3%, then V = IR is known to roughly ±5%. For sums and differences, absolute uncertainties add. These are conservative, worst-case rules, useful for a quick hand estimate; when independent random uncertainties are treated statistically, standard uncertainties are more commonly combined in quadrature (the square root of the sum of squares), which gives a smaller, more realistic combined uncertainty than simple addition. This is the formal version of the observation in the previous section, and it has a sharp practical consequence.
WE: Worked Example 15.1
P: Two voltages are measured as V₁ = 10.0 V ± 0.1 V and V₂ = 9.8 V ± 0.1 V. Find the difference and its uncertainty, as an absolute figure and as a percentage.
P: The difference: V₁ − V₂ = 10.0 − 9.8 = 0.2 V.
P: The absolute uncertainties add: ±0.1 + ±0.1 = ±0.2 V.
P: So the difference is 0.2 V ± 0.2 V — a relative uncertainty of 100%.
P: Each individual measurement was good to 1%. Their difference is good to 100%. Nothing went wrong in the measurement; this is what subtracting two nearly equal numbers does. The signal is the small residue between two large quantities, and the uncertainty of each large quantity survives into that small residue undiminished.
P: This phenomenon has a name — catastrophic cancellation — and it is a permanent hazard. It is why four-wire (Kelvin) resistance measurement exists, why instrumentation amplifiers are built the way they are, and why a bridge circuit that measures a small imbalance directly will always beat a scheme that measures two large quantities and subtracts them.
KEY: Subtracting nearly equal numbers destroys precision. The absolute uncertainty is preserved while the value shrinks, so the relative uncertainty explodes. Whenever a design calls for the small difference between two large measured quantities, look for a way to measure the difference directly instead.
H2: 15.4 Accuracy, Precision, and Resolution
P: These three words are not synonyms, and instrument datasheets depend on the distinction.
LI: Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value. It is limited by calibration, and it drifts with time and temperature.
LI: Precision (or repeatability) is how close repeated measurements are to each other. An instrument can be precise and inaccurate: reading 4.512 V every time when the true value is 5.000 V.
LI: Resolution is the smallest change the instrument can display. A six-digit meter has fine resolution, which says nothing whatever about whether those six digits are accurate. Resolution is free; accuracy is expensive.
P: A typical bench multimeter specification reads something like "±(0.5% of reading + 3 counts)". The first term is a relative error that scales with the measurement; the second is an absolute error, in units of the last displayed digit, that dominates at the low end of a range. This is why measuring 0.1 V on a 1000 V range is a poor idea even though the meter will happily display a number.
H2: 15.5 Component Tolerance
P: Real components differ from their marked values. A resistor marked 4.7 kΩ with a 5% tolerance is guaranteed only to lie between 4.465 kΩ and 4.935 kΩ. This is not a defect; it is the specification, and designs must work across the whole range.
P: The standard E-series values — E12, E24, E96 — are chosen as geometric progressions so that consecutive values are spaced by roughly the tolerance. E24 steps by about 10%, matching 5% parts, so that the tolerance bands of adjacent values just meet without large gaps or heavy overlap. This is why the odd-looking sequence 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24, 27, 30, 33, 36, 39, 43, 47, 51, 56, 62, 68, 75, 82, 91 exists: it is a logarithmic ladder, not an arbitrary list, and once you see that, the values stop looking random.
P: Worst-case analysis assumes every component sits at the tolerance extreme that hurts most. It is pessimistic — the probability of every part being simultaneously worst-case is negligible — but it is the honest way to establish that a design cannot fail, as opposed to establishing that it usually works.
BENCH: Resolution is not accuracy. A meter specified as ±(0.5% of reading + 2 digits), reading 4.732 V on a range whose last digit is 1 mV, could be off by about ±26 mV, so only the first decimal place is fully trustworthy. In worst-case design, tolerances stack: check every part at whichever end of its range hurts most, not just at its nominal value.
