H1: Chapter 35 — AC Power and Power Factor
P: Your electricity meter charges for watts, but the utility has to build wires and transformers for volt-amperes. When a load's current is out of step with its voltage, the two differ, and somebody pays for the difference. That difference is what power factor measures.
P: Where you meet this: Motors and lighting, the power-factor-correction stage in laptop and server supplies, UPS ratings in VA and W, and utility tariffs for large customers.
H2: 35.1 Instantaneous and Average Power
P: Push down hard on a bicycle pedal at the exact top of its stroke, when it is momentarily motionless, and you can push as hard as you like — none of that force turns into useful pedaling, because at that instant your push and the pedal's own motion are at right angles to each other. Push the same pedal, the same force, once it has swept around to the side and is actually moving beneath your foot, and nearly all of that same push converts into forward motion. Your effort was identical in both cases; only the alignment between the push and the motion decided how much of it did anything useful.
FIG: fig35_1.png | Figure 35.1 — Power as it actually flows. With a resistor (left) the product v·i never goes negative. With the current lagging by 60° (right) part of every cycle sends energy back to the source, and the average power halves.
P: So how hard you push, multiplied by how fast the pedal is moving, does not by itself say how much of that effort actually goes into forward motion — alignment matters enormously, and a badly aligned push can involve just as much raw effort while accomplishing almost nothing. AC circuits split the same way. A load draws 8.0 A (RMS) from a 100 V (RMS) source, with the current arriving 53.1° out of step with the voltage. Multiply voltage by current directly, ignoring the misalignment, and the result is 100 × 8.0 = 800 — the total back-and-forth exchange between source and load, every cycle. Only part of that 800 is properly aligned and actually does useful work — 480, it turns out — and because the aligned and misaligned parts combine like two sides of a right triangle rather than by simple subtraction, recovering that 480 from the 800 takes the Pythagorean relationship developed below, not ordinary arithmetic.
P: That total back-and-forth exchange is called apparent power; the portion actually delivered is real power; and the portion that sloshes back and forth without net transfer is reactive power. For a sinusoidal voltage v(t) = V_m cos(ωt) across an element carrying current i(t) = I_m cos(ωt − θ) (where θ is the phase angle by which current lags voltage, positive for an inductive load), the instantaneous power p(t) = v(t)i(t) oscillates in a generally complicated way, but its time-average — the quantity that actually matters for billing, heating, and most practical purposes — reduces to a clean, simple result:
EQ: Pavg = Vrms Irms cosθ
P: The factor cosθ is called the power factor, and it is the single most important new concept this chapter introduces: it measures what fraction of the product of RMS voltage and RMS current is actually converted to real, usable average power, as opposed to being exchanged back and forth between source and load without net transfer.
H2: 35.2 Real, Reactive, and Apparent Power
P: Three related power quantities are used throughout AC power engineering, and this series returns to all three, in far greater depth, in its dedicated power-systems volume:
LI: Real power (P), measured in watts (W): the average power actually delivered to and consumed by the load — the only one of the three that corresponds to real, billable energy consumption or genuine heating/mechanical work.
LI: Reactive power (Q), measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR): power that is exchanged back and forth between source and load each cycle (stored temporarily in an inductor's magnetic field or a capacitor's electric field, then returned), with zero net transfer averaged over a full cycle.
LI: Apparent power (S), measured in volt-amperes (VA): simply the product V_rms I_rms, with no cosθ factor at all — the total power a source must be capable of supplying, real and reactive combined, even though only the real portion does useful work.
P: These three quantities relate through a right triangle (the “power triangle”), with apparent power as the hypotenuse:

H2: 35.3 Why Power Factor Matters
P: A load with a low power factor (cosθ well below 1, typical of large motors and other strongly inductive loads) draws a large current for a given amount of real power delivered, since P = V_rms I_rms cosθ means I_rms must be correspondingly larger to compensate for a small cosθ at fixed P and V_rms. That larger current causes larger resistive (I²R) losses in the wiring and generating equipment supplying the load, even though those losses correspond to no additional useful work being done — which is exactly why electric utilities charge industrial customers a power-factor penalty, and why power-factor correction (typically, adding capacitors in parallel with an inductive load to cancel some of its reactive power) is standard practice in industrial electrical systems, a topic developed fully in this series' power-systems volume.
KEY: Power factor is the cosine of the phase angle between voltage and current, and it directly measures how much of a load's apparent power is actually useful. A purely resistive load has power factor 1 (voltage and current in phase, all apparent power is real power); a purely reactive load (an ideal inductor or capacitor alone) has power factor 0 (voltage and current 90° out of phase, all apparent power is reactive, zero real power delivered on average).
WE: Worked Example 35.1
PROB: A load draws an RMS current of 10 A from a 120 V RMS source, with the current lagging the voltage by 36.9°. Find the real power, reactive power, and apparent power delivered to the load.
SOL: Apparent power: S = V_rms I_rms = (120)(10) = 1200 VA. Real power: P = S cosθ = 1200 cos(36.9°) ≈ 1200(0.80) = 960 W. Reactive power: Q = S sinθ = 1200 sin(36.9°) ≈ 1200(0.60) = 720 VAR. Check: √(P² + Q²) = √(960² + 720²) = √(921,600 + 518,400) = √1,440,000 = 1200 VA, matching S exactly, as the power-triangle relationship requires.
H2: 35.4 Power Factor Correction
P: Since a low power factor arises from a load's net reactance — the imaginary part of its impedance, made precise in Chapter 36 — most commonly inductive, as in motors and transformers, which draw lagging current — the standard remedy is to add capacitors in parallel with the load, specifically sized so that the capacitor's own reactive power partially or fully cancels the load's inductive reactive power. Because reactive power, unlike real power, is not consumed but merely exchanged back and forth each cycle, a capacitor's leading reactive contribution and an inductor's lagging reactive contribution genuinely cancel in the net reactive-power total seen by the source, reducing S (and hence the current the source and wiring must supply) for the same real power P actually being delivered to the load — without needing to modify the load itself in any way, since the correcting capacitors are added entirely separately, typically right at the point where power enters an industrial facility.
WE: Worked Example 35.2
PROB: A factory load draws 100 kW of real power at a power factor of 0.70 lagging, from a 480 V RMS supply. Find the apparent power and the reactive power drawn, and state qualitatively what adding power-factor-correcting capacitors would do to the current the supply must provide for the same 100 kW of real power delivered.
SOL: Apparent power: S = P/cosθ = 100/0.70 ≈ 142.9 kVA. Reactive power: since cosθ = 0.70 corresponds to sinθ ≈ 0.714 (from cos²θ + sin²θ = 1), Q = S sinθ ≈ 142.9 × 0.714 ≈ 102 kVAR. Adding capacitors sized to supply some or all of this 102 kVAR locally means the source itself no longer needs to supply that reactive power — S decreases toward P (the power factor rises toward 1.0), and since S = V_rms I_rms at fixed V_rms, the current I_rms the supply must provide decreases correspondingly, even though the same 100 kW of real power is still being delivered to the factory's actual equipment.
MIS: It is easy to read “apparent power” as simply a less-accurate or approximate version of real power, since the everyday word “apparent” often means “seeming, but not actually true.” In this context the two quantities are not competing estimates of the same thing — apparent power (S = V_rms I_rms) is a genuinely different physical quantity from real power (P = S cosθ), representing the full electrical burden a source and its wiring must be sized to handle, of which only the real-power portion does useful work. A generator, transformer, or set of wires must be rated for apparent power, in VA, not for real power alone in W, precisely because the reactive component still demands current-carrying capacity even though it delivers no net energy.
BENCH: Read both ratings on a UPS or generator: a 1500 VA / 900 W unit is limited by whichever you reach first. Most modern switching supplies above a few tens of watts use active power-factor correction and reach 0.9 or better, but older and cheaper ones draw peaky current with a poor power factor and plenty of harmonics.
