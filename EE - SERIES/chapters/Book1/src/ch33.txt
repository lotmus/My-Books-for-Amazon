H1: Chapter 33 — First-Order Transients: RC and RL Circuits
P: Why does the indicator LED on a power strip fade slowly after you switch it off? The supply's capacitor is discharging through the LED and its resistor, along an exponential set by a single number, the time constant τ = RC.
P: Where you meet this: Power-on reset circuits, switch debouncing, RC timers, the rise time of signals on cables, and inrush current at power-up.
H2: 33.1 What a Transient Is
P: A transient is the temporary response of a circuit as it moves from one steady condition to another, following a sudden change such as a switch closing or opening. The preceding chapters established the tools for steady-state resistive circuit analysis; this chapter is the first to ask what happens during the interval of change itself, and it is where the dynamic elements introduced in Chapter 32 — capacitors and inductors — finally do something a purely resistive circuit cannot: respond gradually, over time, rather than instantaneously.
H2: 33.2 The RC Circuit — Discharging
P: Consider a capacitor, initially charged to voltage V₀, connected at time t = 0 across a resistor R (with no source), allowing it to discharge. Applying KVL and the capacitor's defining relationship i = C dv/dt leads to a first-order differential equation whose solution is an exponential decay:
EQ: v(t) = V0 e−t/RC
P: The quantity τ = RC, called the time constant, has units of time (seconds, when R is in ohms and C in farads) and sets the natural timescale of the decay: after one time constant, the voltage has fallen to about 37% (1/e) of its initial value; after five time constants, it has fallen below 1% and is conventionally treated as having reached its final (here, zero) value for most practical purposes.
H2: 33.3 The RC Circuit — Charging
P: If instead an initially uncharged capacitor is connected, at t = 0, in series with a resistor R across a DC source of voltage V_s, the capacitor voltage rises according to:
EQ: v(t) = Vs (1 − e−t/RC)
P: approaching V_s asymptotically, with the same time constant τ = RC governing how quickly it gets there: after one time constant, the capacitor has charged to about 63% of V_s; after five time constants, it is within about 1% of fully charged.

H2: 33.4 The RL Circuit
P: An RL circuit behaves as the direct dual of the RC circuit, with the inductor's current — not a capacitor's voltage — being the quantity that cannot change instantaneously and that follows the exponential time course. For a series RL circuit switched onto a DC source V_s at t = 0, with the inductor initially carrying no current:
EQ: i(t) = (Vs/R) (1 − e−tR/L)
P: with time constant τ = L/R (units of seconds, when L is in henries and R in ohms) playing exactly the same role here that RC played for the capacitor circuit.
KEY: Every first-order transient follows the same universal template. Whatever the quantity of interest — capacitor voltage, inductor current, or any other voltage or current in a single-time-constant circuit — it can always be written as x(t) = x_final + (x_initial − x_final) e^(−t/τ), where x_initial and x_final are the quantity's values immediately after the switching event and infinitely long afterward, respectively, and τ is RC for a capacitive circuit or L/R for an inductive one. Memorizing this one general template is more useful than memorizing the charging and discharging formulas separately, since every specific case in this chapter is just this template with the appropriate initial value, final value, and time constant substituted in.
WE: Worked Example 33.1
PROB: A 100 µF capacitor, charged to 20 V, is discharged through a 5.0 kΩ resistor starting at t = 0. Find the capacitor voltage at t = 0.50 s.
SOL: τ = RC = (5.0×10³)(100×10⁻⁶) = 0.50 s. v(t) = V₀e^(−t/τ) = 20e^(−0.50/0.50) = 20e⁻¹ ≈ 7.36 V — exactly the “37% of initial value” benchmark, since the elapsed time here happens to equal exactly one time constant.
WE: Worked Example 33.2
PROB: A series RL circuit with R = 10 Ω and L = 2.0 H is connected to a 20 V source at t = 0, with the inductor initially carrying no current. Find the current at t = 0.10 s, and the final steady-state current.
SOL: τ = L/R = 2.0/10 = 0.20 s. Final current (steady state, once di/dt = 0 so the inductor's voltage drop vanishes and it behaves like a plain wire): I_final = V_s/R = 20/10 = 2.0 A. At t = 0.10 s: i(t) = I_final(1 − e^(−t/τ)) = 2.0(1 − e^(−0.10/0.20)) = 2.0(1 − e^(−0.5)) ≈ 2.0(1 − 0.607) ≈ 0.79 A.
H2: 33.5 A Note on Second-Order Circuits
P: This chapter has deliberately restricted itself to first-order circuits — those containing exactly one energy-storage element (one capacitor or one inductor, possibly combined with any number of resistors that can all be reduced, by the equivalent-resistance methods of Chapter 27, to a single effective R). A circuit containing both a capacitor and an inductor together (an RLC circuit) is second-order, governed by a second-order differential equation whose solution can take three qualitatively different forms — overdamped (a sluggish, non-oscillating return to steady state, similar in character to the first-order case but slower), underdamped (an oscillating, gradually decaying return, ringing back and forth around the final value before settling), or critically damped (the fastest possible return without any oscillation, sitting exactly at the boundary between the other two behaviors). Working through the general second-order case properly requires machinery — characteristic equations, damping ratios, natural frequencies — that this book has not yet built, and it is deferred deliberately rather than treated in an abbreviated, unsatisfying way here. What this book has built, however, is not wasted: Chapter 36's resonant frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC) is exactly the undamped natural frequency of the very same second-order RLC system being described here, found by an entirely different (sinusoidal-steady-state) route — a second confirmation, alongside the small-signal preview in Chapter 38, that the specific tools built in this book keep resurfacing, largely unchanged, in contexts this book itself does not fully develop.
MIS: It is tempting to assume an inductor “blocks” current indefinitely, by loose analogy with how a capacitor blocks DC current once fully charged. In steady state (once enough time constants have elapsed that transients have died out), an inductor behaves like a plain wire — zero voltage drop, since v = L di/dt vanishes once current is no longer changing — allowing DC current through it completely unimpeded. It is only during the transient itself, while current is actively changing, that the inductor's back-EMF has any effect; a capacitor, by contrast, does genuinely block steady DC current in its final, fully-charged steady state, the opposite long-term behavior from an inductor's.
BENCH: Rise time and bandwidth are linked. For a single RC pole, the 10–90% rise time is about 2.2τ, or roughly 0.35 divided by the −3 dB bandwidth. The same rule applies to your oscilloscope: a 100 MHz scope can't show a rise much faster than about 3.5 ns, so a 'beautiful 2 ns edge' on it is mostly the scope's own response.
