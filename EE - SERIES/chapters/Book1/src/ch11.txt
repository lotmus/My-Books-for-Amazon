H1: Chapter 11 — Electromagnetic Induction
P: Wave a magnet through a coil and a voltage appears from nowhere. Faraday found the trick in 1831. It generates nearly all the world's electricity, charges an electric toothbrush through its sealed plastic case, and explains why a relay coil can kick back hard enough to kill the transistor driving it.
P: Where you meet this: Generators and alternators, transformers and wireless chargers, guitar pickups, induction cooktops, and the inductive kick of every relay and motor.
H2: 11.1 Faraday's Discovery
P: A hand-crank flashlight needs no battery at all. Turn the crank and a magnet inside spins past a coil of wire, and light appears — current flowing through the bulb from nothing but the turning of your own wrist. Every result so far in this book's magnetism chapters has concerned magnets and currents that sit still, producing steady, unchanging fields; nothing in that picture explains where a hand-crank flashlight's current is actually coming from.
P: Hold that same magnet perfectly still next to the coil, though, and the bulb goes dark immediately, no matter how strong the magnet is. Whatever generates current in the spinning case, a magnet's mere presence is clearly not it.
P: Nothing encountered so far in this book gives any reason to expect that change itself should matter. A steady magnetic field, however strong, does nothing to a stationary wire loop sitting in it — no current source anywhere in sight, no battery, nothing visibly pushing charge around the loop. Yet the moment that same field starts changing, current appears, as if from nowhere. The field's strength turns out not to be the relevant question at all — what matters is whether the field near this loop is changing, and how quickly.
P: The one thing that changes is exactly the field-through-a-surface bookkeeping already built in Chapter 4 for electric fields: how much magnetic field is threading through the loop's own area. Track that quantity as a magnet spins nearby, and it rises, falls, and reverses, over and over, even though the wire loop itself never moves. Whatever produces the current, it must be responding to how fast that quantity changes, not to its size at any one instant.
P: Take a circular loop of radius 10 cm sitting in a field that grows steadily from 0.10 T to 0.40 T over 0.25 s. The loop's area is about 3.14×10⁻² m², so that field-through-the-loop quantity starts at about 3.14×10⁻³, rises to about 7.85×10⁻³ at the midpoint, and reaches about 1.26×10⁻² by the end — a total change of about 9.42×10⁻³ over the 0.25 s it took. Divide that change by the time it took, and the result is about 3.77×10⁻² — a genuine voltage, appearing across the loop with no battery anywhere near it.
P: That rate of change is exactly what a changing magnetic field, or a changing magnetic flux through a loop of wire more generally, produces: not a new force, but an electromotive force — EMF for short, despite the old-fashioned name nothing more exotic than a voltage the changing flux creates on its own. Michael Faraday discovered this in 1831, and it is, without exaggeration, the single physical fact that makes electric generators, transformers, and power grids possible; without it, essentially all large-scale electricity generation as it currently exists would not work.
H2: 11.2 Magnetic Flux
P: Magnetic flux is defined exactly analogously to electric flux from Chapter 4 — for a flat surface of area A in a uniform field B, oriented so its normal makes angle θ with the field:
EQ: ΦB = B A cosθ
P: with SI unit the weber (Wb), equal to one tesla-meter-squared. It is a change in this quantity — not the field itself, and not even the presence of current — that induction responds to.
H2: 11.3 Faraday's Law
P: Faraday's law states that the induced electromotive force (EMF) around a closed loop equals the negative rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop:
EQ: ε = −dΦB/dt
P: For a coil of N turns, each threaded by the same changing flux, the induced EMFs from every turn add:
EQ: ε = −N dΦB/dt
P: Flux can change for several distinct reasons — the field itself changing in time, the loop's area changing, the loop rotating relative to the field, or the loop moving into or out of a field-filled region — and Faraday's law responds identically to all of them, because all of them change the magnetic flux. This is precisely how an electric generator works: a coil is mechanically rotated within a magnetic field, continuously changing the flux as the coil's orientation relative to the field changes, producing an alternating EMF — the direct physical origin of the AC waveform Part II of this book (Chapters 20–23) takes as its starting point.
H2: 11.4 Lenz's Law and the Meaning of the Minus Sign
P: The minus sign in Faraday's law is not a bookkeeping detail — it encodes Lenz's law, one of the more important qualitative facts in this book. Lenz's law states that the induced current always flows in whatever direction opposes the change in flux that produced it. If flux through a loop is increasing, the induced current flows in the direction that would, by the results of Chapter 10, produce a magnetic field opposing that increase; if flux is decreasing, the induced current flows to reinforce it.
FIG: fig11_1.png | Figure 11.1 — Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change in flux that produced it.
KEY: Lenz's law is a direct consequence of energy conservation. If induced current instead reinforced a changing flux, the effect would build on its own cause without limit, generating energy from nothing. Lenz's law guarantees that any electromagnetic induction always opposes the mechanical or electrical change that created it — which is precisely why turning a generator's shaft takes real mechanical effort: you are always pushing against an induced magnetic braking force, and that mechanical work is exactly what becomes the electrical energy delivered by the generator.
H2: 11.5 Motional EMF
P: A useful special case: a conducting rod of length L, moving with velocity v perpendicular to a magnetic field B, develops an EMF along its length:
EQ: ε = BLv
P: This can be derived directly from the magnetic force on the rod's own free charges (F = qv×B from Chapter 9) without invoking flux at all, and it is reassuring — and a useful check on your understanding — that both approaches, flux-based and force-based, agree exactly. This is the operating principle behind simple linear generators and behind induced voltages in moving conductors more generally, including eddy currents induced in metal moving through a field, which is exploited deliberately in magnetic braking systems.
H2: 11.6 Energy Stored in a Magnetic Field: A First Look at Inductance
P: Just as a capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates (Chapter 6), a current-carrying coil stores energy in its own magnetic field. A coil's ability to do this — to build up a magnetic field, store energy in it, and (by Faraday's law, applied to itself) resist any change in the current that produces it — is quantified by its self-inductance L, defined so that the induced EMF opposing a change in the coil's own current is:
EQ: ε = −L dI/dt
P: with SI unit the henry (H). Notice the structural parallel to Ohm's law and to the capacitor relation C = Q/V: three of this book's core circuit elements — resistor, capacitor, inductor — each relate a pair of circuit quantities (voltage and current, charge and voltage, and now voltage and the rate of change of current) through a single characteristic constant of the component. A full treatment of inductors, RL circuits, and the energy U = ½LI² stored in an inductor's magnetic field belongs properly to Part III of this book, which develops all three of these elements — resistor, capacitor, and inductor — together, once the tools for handling time-varying circuits are in place. What matters here is recognizing that Chapter 10's solenoid, once wired into a circuit and thought of as opposing its own changing current via Faraday's law, is exactly what an inductor is.
WE: Worked Example 11.1
PROB: A circular loop of wire, radius 5.0 cm, sits in a magnetic field that increases uniformly from 0.20 T to 0.80 T over 0.30 s, with the field perpendicular to the loop throughout. Find the average induced EMF.
SOL: Loop area: A = πr² = π(0.050)² ≈ 7.85×10⁻³ m². Change in flux: ΔΦ = AΔB = (7.85×10⁻³)(0.80−0.20) = 4.71×10⁻³ Wb. Average EMF: ε = ΔΦ/Δt = 4.71×10⁻³/0.30 ≈ 1.57 × 10⁻² V.
WE: Worked Example 11.2
PROB: A metal rod 0.40 m long slides along a pair of conducting rails, moving at a constant 3.0 m/s, perpendicular to a uniform 0.60 T magnetic field pointing straight into the plane of the rails. Find the motional EMF developed, and state, using Lenz's law, which way current flows in the circuit formed by the rod and rails as the rod moves.
SOL: Motional EMF: ε = BLv = (0.60)(0.40)(3.0) = 0.72 V. To find the current direction with Lenz's law: as the rod slides (say, to the right, into the region enclosed by the rails and rod), the enclosed loop area increases, and since B points into the page, the enclosed flux (into the page) is increasing. The induced current must oppose that increase, meaning it must create its own field pointing out of the page inside the loop — by the right-hand rule for a current loop (Chapter 10), this means the induced current flows counterclockwise as viewed by someone looking at the page, with B pointing away from them into it.
MIS: Faraday's law is sometimes misremembered as responding to the magnetic field itself, so that a strong but constant field is expected to induce a large EMF. It is the rate of change of flux that matters, not the field's absolute size — a room permeated by an enormous, perfectly steady magnetic field induces no EMF in any wire loop sitting motionless within it, no matter how strong that field is, while a comparatively weak field changing quickly can induce a substantial EMF. The relevant question for any induction problem is always “how fast, and in what way, is the flux through this loop changing?” — never “how strong is the field?” by itself.
BENCH: Put a flyback diode across any relay coil or small DC motor driven by a transistor, cathode to the positive supply. When the transistor turns off, the coil's current has to go somewhere; without the diode it raises the voltage until something breaks down, often the transistor. The diode slows the relay's release a little; a diode plus a Zener speeds it up again if release time matters.
