H1: Appendix B — Practice Problem Solutions
P: Every practice problem in the book is worked here in full, chapter by chapter: the idea, the formula, the numbers, and a check. Attempt each problem before you read its solution.
H2: Chapter 1 Solutions
P: 1. The largest symmetric swing is set by the smaller of the two headrooms. Headroom to the lower rail: 6 − 1 = 5 V. Headroom to the upper rail: 11 − 6 = 5 V. The two are equal, so the stage passes a 5 V amplitude, and neither rail limits before the other: the bias point is centered. Check: 6 ± 5 V spans exactly 1 V to 11 V.
P: 2. Find the output headroom, then divide by the gain to refer it to the input. Output headroom = min(2 − 0, 5 − 2) = min(2, 3) = 2 V, set by the lower rail. The input amplitude that produces 2 V at the output is 2 V/20 = 0.1 V, or 100 mV. Check: 20 × 100 mV = 2 V, so the output swings from 0 V to 4 V and touches the lower rail.
P: 3. A small-signal model replaces a curved transfer characteristic by its tangent line at the bias point, and the gain is that line's slope. Close to the bias point the curve and the tangent agree; far from it the curve bends away, so the slope, and every gain or impedance derived from it, no longer describes the circuit. The analysis is trustworthy only while the signal stays small compared with the curvature.
P: 4. A centered bias sits at 9/2 = 4.5 V, so both headrooms are 4.5 V. The maximum input amplitude is the output headroom divided by the gain: 4.5 V/8 = 0.5625 V, about 563 mV. Check: 8 × 0.5625 = 4.5 V, so the output swings from 0 V to 9 V.
P: 5. Headroom to the lower rail: 1 − 0 = 1 V. Headroom to the upper rail: 5 − 1 = 4 V. The lower rail limits, with only 1 V of headroom; the stage clips on negative peaks first. Moving the bias toward 2.5 V would raise the symmetric swing from 1 V to 2.5 V.
P: 6. The output headroom is fixed by the bias point and the rails, not by the gain. The output amplitude is gain × input amplitude, so doubling the gain means the same output headroom is reached with half the input. The maximum undistorted input amplitude is therefore halved. Example: with 2 V of headroom, a gain of 10 allows 200 mV at the input and a gain of 20 allows 100 mV.
H2: Chapter 2 Solutions
P: 1. Each noise margin is the gap between what a driver guarantees and what a receiver needs. NM_H = V_OH(min) − V_IH(min) = 3.5 − 2.0 = 1.5 V. NM_L = V_IL(max) − V_OL(max) = 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.3 V. The low margin is the weak one: only 0.3 V of noise on a low signal can cause a misread.
P: 2. Weight each bit by its power of two, from the right: 1011₂ = 1×8 + 0×4 + 1×2 + 1×1 = 11₁₀. Check: 11 = 8 + 2 + 1.
P: 3. Every digital gate decides whether its input is high or low and then drives a fresh, full-strength output level, so noise that did not cross a threshold is discarded at each stage. An analog stage amplifies whatever it receives, signal and noise together, so each stage adds its own noise to everything before it and the degradation accumulates instead of resetting.
P: 4. NM_H = V_OH(min) − V_IH(min) = 4.4 − 3.15 = 1.25 V. NM_L = V_IL(max) − V_OL(max) = 1.35 − 0.1 = 1.25 V. The margins are equal, which is typical of CMOS logic with thresholds near 30% and 70% of a 4.5 V supply.
P: 5. 11010₂ = 1×16 + 1×8 + 0×4 + 1×2 + 0×1 = 26₁₀. Check: 16 + 8 + 2 = 26.
P: 6. A noise margin is a static figure: it says how much steady offset a level can tolerate. Real industrial noise arrives as fast spikes, ground bounce, and coupled bursts whose peak can exceed the margin for a few nanoseconds, which is all a fast gate needs to switch falsely. Equal margins on paper therefore guarantee equal static tolerance only; layout, grounding, shielding, filtering, and slower or Schmitt inputs decide reliability in the noisy environment.
H2: Chapter 3 Solutions
P: 1. Distribute, then absorb. F = A(A + B) = AA + AB = A + AB, because AA = A. Then A + AB = A(1 + B) = A·1 = A. This is the absorption law. Check with the truth table: F equals A in all four rows.
P: 2. Evaluate F = A′B + AB′ row by row. A = 0, B = 0: 0 + 0 = 0. A = 0, B = 1: 1 + 0 = 1. A = 1, B = 0: 0 + 1 = 1. A = 1, B = 1: 0 + 0 = 0. The output is 1 when the inputs differ: this is exclusive-OR, F = A ⊕ B.
P: 3. De Morgan's law turns the complement of an OR into the AND of the complements: (A + B)′ = A′B′. Check: the left side is 1 only when A + B = 0, that is, when both A and B are 0, which is exactly when A′B′ = 1.
P: 4. Factor A out of both terms: H = A + AB′C = A(1 + B′C) = A·1 = A. This is absorption again: A OR (A AND anything) equals A. Check: if A = 1, H = 1; if A = 0, both terms are 0.
P: 5. Factor out A′: F = A′(B′C′ + B′C + BC′ + BC). Group in pairs: B′(C′ + C) + B(C′ + C) = B′ + B = 1. So F = A′·1 = A′. The four terms list every combination of B and C, so together they are always 1. Check: F is 1 exactly in the four rows where A = 0.
P: 6. Apply De Morgan's law to the outer AND first: ((A + B)·C)′ = (A + B)′ + C′. Apply it again to the OR: (A + B)′ = A′B′. So the result is A′B′ + C′. Check: with C = 0 the expression is 1, as it must be, since the original AND is then 0.
H2: Chapter 4 Solutions
P: 1. For an active-high NOR latch the forbidden input is S = 1 and R = 1. Both NOR outputs are then forced to 0, so Q and Q′ are no longer complements, and when both inputs return to 0 together the latch falls into whichever state the faster gate wins: the final state is not determined by the circuit.
P: 2. A D flip-flop copies D to Q at the active clock edge, so Q = 1 immediately after the edge (after its clock-to-output delay). It then holds 1 until a later edge samples a different D.
P: 3. Each flip-flop stores one bit, so a 4-bit register needs four D flip-flops, holding any of 2⁴ = 16 values from 0000 to 1111.
P: 4. A half adder computes the sum S = A ⊕ B and the carry C = AB. With A = 1, B = 0: S = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1 and C = 1·0 = 0. Check: 1 + 0 = 1 in binary, with no carry.
P: 5. S = 1, R = 0 sets the latch, forcing Q = 1. When both inputs return to 0, neither gate is forced, and the cross-coupled feedback holds the last state, so Q stays at 1. That memory is the point of a latch.
P: 6. In a shift register each flip-flop must capture its neighbor's old value at the same instant. With a common clock every stage samples before any stage's output changes, so the data moves exactly one place per edge. If one stage were clocked late, it could capture its neighbor's new value instead, skipping or duplicating a bit; Chapter 28 treats this as clock skew and hold time.
P: 7. The 555 frequency is f = 1.44/((R₁ + 2R₂)C) = 1.44/((1 kΩ + 94 kΩ) × 2.2 µF) = 1.44/(95 kΩ × 2.2 µF) = 6.89 Hz. Each LED is lit for one period, 1/6.89 = 145 ms, so the light passes all ten LEDs in 1.45 s. Check: 10 × 145 ms = 1.45 s.
P: 8. The ratio is 32,768/8 = 4096 = 2¹², so the ÷4096 output (the twelfth stage, Q12) gives 8 Hz. The last output divides by 16,384 = 2¹⁴, giving 32,768/16,384 = 2 Hz. Check: 2¹⁵ = 32,768, so a fifteenth stage, the extra flip-flop of Figure 4.3, gives 1 Hz.
P: 9. A k-input LUT stores one bit per truth-table row, 2ᵏ rows. For k = 5: 2⁵ = 32 bits. Each bit is free to be 0 or 1, so the number of functions is 2³² = 4,294,967,296, about 4.3 × 10⁹. Check against a 2-input LUT: 4 bits and 2⁴ = 16 functions.
P: 10. Reach for an FPGA. Forty-eight independent counters, each able to change on every 10 ns clock edge, is parallel logic: an FPGA builds 48 copies of a small PWM counter that all run at once. A microcontroller has a limited number of hardware PWM channels and cannot update 48 of them every 10 ns in software. An ASIC would do it at lower power and cost per unit, but only a high-volume product justifies its mask cost and its lack of changes.
H2: Chapter 5 Solutions
P: 1. For an inverting amplifier, gain = −R_f/R_in = −47 kΩ/4.7 kΩ = −10. V_out = −10 × 0.2 V = −2.0 V. Check: the input current 0.2 V/4.7 kΩ = 42.6 µA flows on through R_f, dropping 42.6 µA × 47 kΩ = 2.0 V, with the output 2.0 V below the virtual ground.
P: 2. A non-inverting gain is 1 + R_f/R_in. Worked Example 5.2 used R_f = 10 kΩ and R_in = 2 kΩ for a gain of 6. With R_f doubled to 20 kΩ: gain = 1 + 20/2 = 11. Doubling R_f does not double the gain, because of the fixed 1 in the formula.
P: 3. The two inputs draw no current (Golden Rule 2), so no current can pass between them. Their voltages are equal only because the feedback network keeps adjusting the output until the difference is negligible; the virtual short is a result of the loop, not a physical connection.
P: 4. Gain = −R_f/R_in = −220 kΩ/22 kΩ = −10. V_out = −10 × 0.15 V = −1.5 V. Check: the ratio, not the absolute values, sets the gain, so this is the same −10 as Problem 1.
P: 5. Set 1 + R_f/R_in = 4, so R_f/R_in = 3 and R_in = R_f/3 = 30 kΩ/3 = 10 kΩ. Check: 1 + 30/10 = 4.
P: 6. The output stage is powered from the rails and cannot produce a voltage outside them; most op-amps cannot even reach the rails. When the golden-rule formula asks for more, the output saturates, the loop loses control, and the virtual short no longer holds. This is the headroom limit of Chapter 1 applied to the op-amp.
H2: Chapter 6 Solutions
P: 1. f_c = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π × 2200 Ω × 47 × 10⁻⁹ F) = 1/(6.497 × 10⁻⁴ s) ≈ 1539 Hz, about 1.54 kHz. Check: RC = 103.4 µs, and 1/(2π × 103.4 µs) ≈ 1.54 kHz.
P: 2. Each first-order stage contributes −20 dB/decade above its corner, and cascaded stages multiply their responses, so their decibels add: 4 × (−20) = −80 dB/decade, the slope of a fourth-order filter.
P: 3. An active filter's output comes from the op-amp, whose closed-loop output impedance is a small fraction of an ohm, so a load draws its current without disturbing the RC network that sets the response. A passive filter's output is the network itself, so the load forms a divider with it and shifts both the gain and the corner frequency.
P: 4. f_c = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π × 3300 Ω × 15 × 10⁻⁹ F) = 1/(3.110 × 10⁻⁴ s) ≈ 3215 Hz, about 3.22 kHz. The formula is the same for a high-pass as for a low-pass section; only which side passes changes.
P: 5. A decade is log₂ 10 = 3.32 octaves, so −40 dB/decade ÷ 3.32 ≈ −12 dB/octave. Check with the rule of thumb: 6 dB per octave per order, times two orders, is 12 dB/octave.
P: 6. 60 Hz is only log₁₀(1000/60) = 1.22 decades below 1 kHz. A single pole placed at, say, 500 Hz to leave the signal mostly untouched attenuates 60 Hz by only about 20 log₁₀(500/60) ≈ 18 dB, while already costing the 1 kHz signal about 1 dB. Moving the corner up removes more hum but eats into the signal. A higher-order high-pass or a 60 Hz notch gives far more rejection without touching 1 kHz.
P: 7. At f₀ a second-order low-pass section has gain Q × H₀ = 0.7071 × 1 = 0.7071, and 20 log₁₀(0.7071) = −3.0 dB. This is why Q = 0.7071 defines the Butterworth response: its corner is the −3 dB point.
P: 8. For the equal-resistor unity-gain Sallen-Key, Q = ½√(C₁/C₂), so C₁/C₂ = 4Q² = 4 × 0.5773² = 4 × 0.3333 = 1.33. Check: ½√1.333 = 0.577.
P: 9. f₀ = 1/(2πR√(C₁C₂)) = 1/(2π × 22 kΩ × √(6.8 nF × 3.3 nF)). √(22.44 × 10⁻¹⁸) = 4.737 nF, so f₀ = 1/(2π × 22 × 10³ × 4.737 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 1527 Hz. Q = ½√(6.8/3.3) = ½ × 1.435 ≈ 0.718, close to Butterworth.
P: 10. For the equal-resistor MFB section, Q = ⅓√(C₁/C₂) = ⅓√(47/5.6) = ⅓ × 2.897 ≈ 0.966. At f₀ the gain is Q = 0.966, or −0.3 dB, but because Q is above 0.707 the response peaks slightly below f₀, at Q/√(1 − 1/(4Q²)) ≈ 1.13, about +1.0 dB: a little peaked compared with Butterworth.
P: 11. The rule of Section 6.10 asks for GBW ≥ 100 × H₀ × f₀, with a further factor of Q for high-Q sections. The higher-Q section of a fourth-order Butterworth has Q = 1.3066, and H₀ = 1, so GBW ≥ 100 × 1 × 10 kHz × 1.3066 ≈ 1.3 MHz. Choose an op-amp with at least that, and preferably more.
P: 12. Each second-order Butterworth section is already 3 dB down at f₀, so two identical sections are 6 dB down there and the knee is rounded. A true fourth-order Butterworth uses two sections with different Q values, 0.5412 and 1.3066, whose peaking and droop cancel to give a maximally flat response that is 3 dB down at f₀.
H2: Chapter 7 Solutions
P: 1. From P = I²R, I = √(P/R) = √(0.25 W/100 Ω) = √0.0025 = 0.050 A = 50 mA. Check: (0.05)² × 100 = 0.25 W. In practice derate further for temperature (Section 7.5).
P: 2. A wirewound resistor is a coil of wire, so it carries series inductance, often microhenries. At RF that inductance's reactance 2πfL can exceed the resistance itself, and the part behaves more like an inductor than a resistor. Film resistors have far smaller parasitics.
P: 3. ±10% of 1 kΩ is ±100 Ω, so individual values may lie anywhere from 900 Ω to 1100 Ω.
P: 4. Each resistor lies within 2200 ± 22 Ω, from 2178 Ω to 2222 Ω. In the worst case all three err the same way: 3 × 2178 = 6534 Ω to 3 × 2222 = 6666 Ω, which is still ±1% of 6600 Ω. Random errors would usually partly cancel, but the worst case is what a design must survive.
P: 5. 50% margin means a rating of at least 1.5 × 0.3 W = 0.45 W. 1/8 W and 1/4 W are below even the actual 0.3 W. 1/2 W = 0.5 W clears 0.45 W, so it is the smallest acceptable rating.
P: 6. In a precision measurement, every part per million of resistance drift becomes a part per million of error in the reading, so the temperature coefficient sits directly in the error budget. An LED limiter only needs a current that is roughly right; a 1% current change shifts brightness invisibly, so the same drift has no consequence.
P: 7. Above the 70 °C knee the rating falls linearly to zero at 155 °C: P = 0.25 W × (155 − 125)/(155 − 70) = 0.25 × 30/85 ≈ 0.088 W. At 125 °C the part may dissipate barely a third of its headline rating.
P: 8. Each resistor can drift by ±50 ppm/°C × 40 °C = ±2000 ppm. If they drift in opposite directions, the ratio changes by up to 2000 + 2000 = 4000 ppm, or 0.4%. Resistors that track each other, from one network on one substrate, cut this to a few ppm per °C.
P: 9. Temperature rise ΔT = Pθ = 0.020 W × 400 °C/W = 8 °C. Resistance change ΔR/R = TCR × ΔT = 100 ppm/°C × 8 °C = 800 ppm, or 0.08%.
P: 10. 600 V/150 V = 4 resistors, each then sitting exactly at its limit, with no allowance for tolerance in how the voltage divides or for transients. Use 5 or 6 so that each sees 100 to 120 V, comfortably below its rating, with creepage between pads also checked.
P: 11. V = IR = 20 A × 0.005 Ω = 0.100 V = 100 mV. P = I²R = 400 × 0.005 = 2.0 W. Check: P = VI = 0.1 × 20 = 2 W. A 2 W shunt needs a rating of 3 W or more with margin and good copper to carry its heat away.
P: 12. With four terminals, the load current flows through one pair and the sense voltage is taken from the other pair, right at the resistive element. The sense leads carry almost no current, so the drop in solder joints and copper, which at milliohm values can rival the shunt itself and drifts strongly with temperature, stays out of the measurement.
H2: Chapter 8 Solutions
P: 1. SRF = 1/(2π√(L_ESL C)) = 1/(2π√(2 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.2 × 10⁻⁷)) = 1/(2π × 2.098 × 10⁻⁸) ≈ 7.59 MHz. Above that frequency the capacitor behaves as an inductor.
P: 2. No. The 16.5 V peaks exceed the 16 V rating outright, whatever the 12 V average. An electrolytic's rating applies to the peak voltage, ripple included. Use a 25 V part, which also improves lifetime and leakage.
P: 3. A Class 2 dielectric such as X7R gets its high permittivity from a ferroelectric ceramic, and the DC field partly locks its polarization domains in place, so they contribute less to the response to further changes. The permittivity, and with it the capacitance, falls as the DC voltage rises, often by half or more at rated voltage, which is why manufacturers publish DC-bias curves.
P: 4. SRF = 1/(2π√(8 × 10⁻⁹ × 1 × 10⁻⁸)) = 1/(2π × 8.944 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 17.8 MHz.
P: 5. No. A 50% derating of a 10 V part allows 5 V, and the 6 V rail exceeds that, even though it is below the absolute 10 V rating. Choose a 16 V tantalum (8 V derated) or a different capacitor type.
P: 6. A film capacitor's value barely changes with applied voltage, temperature, or time, and it has low dielectric absorption, so an RC timing circuit built with it stays at its designed frequency. A Class 2 ceramic of the same nominal value can lose a large fraction of its capacitance with DC bias and drift with temperature, moving the timing by tens of percent.
P: 7. SRF = 1/(2π√(0.8 × 10⁻⁹ × 1 × 10⁻⁶)) = 1/(2π × 2.828 × 10⁻⁸) ≈ 5.63 MHz. At resonance the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel, so |Z| = ESR = 10 mΩ, the minimum of the impedance curve.

H1: Appendix C — Glossary
P: Absolute maximum rating — A datasheet limit the part survives without damage; it is not an operating condition, and the specifications do not hold there.
P: Absorption law — A + AB = A, or its dual A(A + B) = A; a term adding no new information can be removed from a Boolean expression.
P: Acquisition time — The time a sample-and-hold needs, after a step, to track the input to within a stated error before it can hold.
P: Active filter — A filter built around an op-amp, whose low output impedance largely isolates its frequency response from the load it drives, and whose gain can also make up for any signal loss the filtering network introduces.
P: Aliasing — The folding of signal content above half the sample rate into the band below it.
P: Angle condition — The requirement that ∠G(s) be an odd multiple of 180° for s to lie on the root locus.
P: Antiresonance — A parallel-resonant impedance peak formed between two paralleled capacitors when one has become inductive while the other is still capacitive.
P: Aperture jitter — Random variation in the sampling instant, which converts signal slope into voltage error.
P: ASIC — Application-specific integrated circuit: a chip whose logic is fixed at the factory for one job; fastest and cheapest per unit at volume, but unchangeable.
P: Asymptote centroid — The point on the real axis, (Σ poles − Σ zeros)/(n − m), from which the root-locus asymptotes radiate.
P: Backward Euler — A discretization that approximates an integral by adding the present sample times the period to a running sum.
P: Bandgap reference — A voltage reference that adds a PTAT voltage to a base-emitter voltage so that their temperature slopes cancel, producing about 1.2 V.
P: Bang-bang control — On-off control: the actuator is fully on or fully off, usually with a hysteresis band between the switching thresholds.
P: Barkhausen condition — Loop gain of exactly one with a phase shift of zero (or 360°), the condition for a sustained oscillation.
P: Bessel response — A response with nearly constant group delay and minimal step overshoot; Q = 0.5773 for a second-order section.
P: Bias point (Q-point) — The fixed DC operating condition a circuit is set to before a smaller signal is applied on top of it.
P: Bipolar PWM — H-bridge modulation that alternates between +V_s and −V_s each period, with average voltage (2D − 1)V_s.
P: Boolean algebra — The algebra of AND, OR, and NOT, used to describe and simplify digital logic.
P: Box method — Specifying a reference's temperature coefficient as total output variation divided by nominal voltage and temperature span.
P: Breakaway point — A point on the real axis where two branches of the root locus meet and leave the axis.
P: Buffer — A stage that provides current gain without voltage gain, isolating a weak source from a demanding load.
P: Bus pumping — The rise of a drive's DC-bus voltage when regenerated energy cannot flow back into the supply.
P: Butterworth response — The maximally flat passband response; Q = 0.7071 for a second-order section.
P: Charge amplifier — An op-amp with a capacitor as its feedback element, which turns the charge from a piezoelectric sensor into a voltage −q/C_f independent of cable capacitance.
P: Class 2 dielectric — A ceramic capacitor dielectric family (X7R, Y5V, and similar) that packs more capacitance into a given size than Class 1 types, at the cost of capacitance that decreases as DC bias voltage rises.
P: Clipping — Distortion caused by a signal's peaks being cut flat at a circuit's output rail.
P: Clock skew — The difference in arrival time of the same clock edge at two flip-flops.
P: Combinational circuit — A circuit whose output depends only on its present inputs, with no memory of the past.
P: Common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) — The ratio of an op-amp's differential gain to its common-mode gain; it converts common-mode input swing into an input-referred error.
P: Common-mode voltage — The average of the two input voltages of a differential amplifier, (V₊ + V₋)/2, which carries no information about the measurement.
P: Comparator — A high-gain differential stage without internal compensation, used open-loop to produce a logic output from an analog comparison.
P: Controllability — The property that the input can steer every state; tested by the rank of [B AB A²B …].
P: CTAT — Complementary to absolute temperature; describes a quantity that falls as temperature rises, such as a base-emitter voltage at fixed current.
P: Current transfer ratio (CTR) — In an optocoupler, the output transistor's collector current divided by the LED's forward current.
P: De Morgan's laws — (A + B)′ = A′B′, and (AB)′ = A′ + B′.
P: Dead time — A deliberate delay between turning off one switch of a leg and turning on its partner, during which body diodes carry the load current.
P: Debouncing — Removing the burst of edges a mechanical switch produces as its contacts bounce, by an RC filter with a Schmitt input or by software.
P: Delay margin — The largest added time delay a loop can tolerate before instability, PM in radians divided by the crossover frequency.
P: Derating curve — A plot of a component's allowed power (or other stress) versus ambient temperature.
P: Desensitivity factor — The quantity 1 + T, by which feedback divides changes in forward gain, distortion, and output impedance.
P: Dielectric absorption — The partial return of voltage on a capacitor after a brief discharge, caused by slowly relaxing charge in the dielectric.
P: Difference amplifier — A single op-amp with four resistors whose output is proportional to the difference between two inputs; its common-mode rejection is set by resistor matching.
P: Disturbance — An external input that pushes the plant's output away from the reference.
P: Droop — The steady change in a held voltage caused by leakage current, at a rate I_leak/C.
P: Equivalent noise bandwidth (ENBW) — The bandwidth of an ideal brick-wall filter that passes the same noise power as a real response; 1.571 times the −3 dB frequency for a single pole.
P: ESR / ESL — Equivalent series resistance / equivalent series inductance — the real, non-ideal resistive and (for a capacitor) inductive components no ideal model includes; this book calls a real inductor's own series resistance its DCR instead, since an inductor's self-resonance comes from parasitic winding capacitance, not a parasitic inductance of its own.
P: Excess noise — Current-dependent noise in a resistor, beyond its Johnson noise, larger in thick-film and carbon types.
P: Ferrite bead — A lossy series component whose impedance becomes mostly resistive at high frequency, used to absorb noise.
P: Flicker (1/f) noise — Low-frequency noise whose power density rises inversely with frequency below a corner frequency.
P: Flip-flop — A clocked, edge-triggered bistable circuit that stores one bit, updating only at a clock transition.
P: Flyback diode — A diode across an inductive load that gives the coil current a path when the switch turns off, clamping the voltage spike.
P: FPGA — Field-programmable gate array: a chip of lookup tables, flip-flops, and programmable wiring that is configured into a digital circuit at power-up.
P: Full-power bandwidth — The highest frequency at which an op-amp can deliver a sine of a given peak amplitude without slew-rate distortion, SR/(2πV_p).
P: Gain — The ratio of a circuit's output signal to its input signal.
P: Gain margin — The factor by which loop gain could increase, at the frequency where its phase is −180 degrees, before the loop oscillates.
P: Golden rules (op-amp) — V₊ = V₋ and I₊ = I₋ = 0; the two assumptions that make an ideal op-amp circuit with negative feedback solvable by algebra alone.
P: H-bridge — Four switches in two legs that connect a load between two midpoints, allowing either polarity of applied voltage.
P: Headroom — The distance from a circuit's bias point to the nearer of its two output rails, setting the largest signal it can pass undistorted.
P: Heating current (I_rms) — The current that raises an inductor's temperature by a stated amount through winding loss.
P: Hold time — The interval after a clock edge during which a flip-flop's input must remain stable.
P: Hysteresis — The gap between the rising and falling thresholds of a Schmitt trigger.
P: Input bias current (I_b) — The DC current that flows into each op-amp input.
P: Input offset current (I_os) — The difference between the bias currents of the two inputs.
P: Input offset voltage (V_os) — The DC voltage that must be applied between an op-amp's inputs to make its output zero; modeled as a source in series with one input.
P: Instrumentation amplifier — A differential amplifier with buffered inputs and a single gain-setting resistor, usually built from three op-amps, whose rejection rises with gain.
P: Integrator windup — Accumulation of error in an integrator while the actuator is saturated, causing overshoot when the saturation ends.
P: Isolation resistor — A small resistor placed between an op-amp output and a capacitive load, outside the feedback loop, to preserve phase margin.
P: Johnson noise — Thermal noise of a resistance, with density √(4kTR), flat with frequency.
P: Karnaugh map — A grid arrangement of a truth table's outputs in which every adjacent cell differs in exactly one input bit, letting groups of adjacent 1s be read off directly as a simplified Boolean expression.
P: Kelvin (four-terminal) connection — Separate current-carrying and voltage-sensing connections to a component, so that lead and joint resistance are excluded from the measurement.
P: kT/C noise — The rms noise voltage √(kT/C) frozen onto a capacitor when it is disconnected from a resistive source.
P: Lag compensator — A pole-zero pair, pole first, that adds low-frequency gain while leaving crossover nearly unchanged.
P: Latch — An unclocked bistable circuit whose output can change at any time its inputs allow, unlike a flip-flop.
P: Lead compensator — A zero-pole pair, zero first, that adds phase near the crossover frequency.
P: Limit cycle — A small sustained oscillation in a digital loop caused by quantization or nonlinearity.
P: Load capacitance — The capacitance a crystal must see across its terminals to oscillate at its marked frequency.
P: Lookup table (LUT) — A small memory holding a truth table; a k-input LUT can implement any logic function of k inputs.
P: Loop gain (T) — The product Aβ of forward gain and feedback fraction; the gain seen once around a feedback loop.
P: Macromodel — A simulation model that reproduces a circuit's behavior at its pins with a few ideal sources and passives fitted to the datasheet, instead of modeling its transistors.
P: Metastability — A flip-flop state between 0 and 1, caused by an input change inside the setup-hold window, that resolves after a random time.
P: Microphonics — Conversion between mechanical vibration and voltage in a piezoelectric component, such as a Class 2 ceramic capacitor.
P: Microstepping — Driving a stepper's windings with intermediate currents so the rotor moves in fractions of a full step.
P: Multiple-feedback (MFB) filter — An inverting active filter in which two feedback paths, one resistive and one capacitive, shape the response.
P: Noise gain — The reciprocal of the feedback fraction, 1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ; the gain applied to the op-amp's own input noise and offset, and the divisor of GBW in the bandwidth calculation.
P: Noise margin — The gap between a gate's guaranteed output level and the next gate's guaranteed input threshold, for a given logic level.
P: Nyquist plot — The curve traced by L(jω) in the complex plane as ω runs over all frequencies.
P: Observer — A software model of the plant corrected by the measured output, used to estimate states that are not measured.
P: Open-collector (open-drain) output — An output stage that can only pull low; a pull-up resistor supplies the high level.
P: Optocoupler — An LED and a photodetector in one package, passing a signal by light between two circuits with no electrical connection.
P: Overdrive — The amount by which a comparator's input passes its threshold; the response time depends on it.
P: Pedestal — The step in held voltage caused by charge injected from the switch as it turns off, ΔV = Q/C.
P: Phase margin — 180 degrees plus the phase of the loop gain at the frequency where its magnitude equals 1.
P: PID controller — A controller whose command is a weighted sum of the error, its integral, and its derivative.
P: Plant — The physical system a controller acts on, such as a motor, heater, or joint.
P: Power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR) — The ratio of a supply-voltage change to the resulting change in input-referred offset.
P: PTAT — Proportional to absolute temperature; describes ΔV_BE between two junctions at different current densities.
P: Quality factor (Q) — A measure of how efficiently a resonant circuit or reactive component stores energy versus dissipating it as heat; for an inductor, Q = ωL/R.
P: Quality factor of a filter section (Q) — The parameter that sets the shape of a second-order response near its natural frequency; the gain at f₀ is Q times the passband gain.
P: Reference pin (REF) — The terminal of an instrumentation or difference amplifier that sets the output level for zero differential input; it must be driven from a low impedance.
P: Regeneration — A digital gate reading a noisy input and producing a fresh, full-strength output level, which is why noise does not accumulate through a long chain of gates.
P: Relaxation oscillator — An oscillator that charges and discharges a capacitor between two thresholds rather than using a resonant network.
P: Response time (comparator) — The delay from an input step to the output crossing a stated level, at a stated overdrive and load.
P: Root locus — The paths of the closed-loop poles in the s-plane as a loop gain varies from zero to infinity.
P: Sallen-Key filter — An active filter built around a non-inverting (often unity-gain) op-amp with two resistors, two capacitors, and positive feedback through one capacitor.
P: Saturation current (I_sat) — The DC current at which an inductor's inductance has fallen by a stated percentage, commonly 20% or 30%.
P: Schmitt trigger — A comparator with positive feedback that switches at two different thresholds depending on its present output state.
P: Self-resonant frequency (SRF) — The frequency at which a real capacitor's or inductor's parasitic reactance cancels its intended reactance.
P: Sensitivity function — S = 1/(1 + L), the factor by which feedback changes the effect of disturbances and plant variations at each frequency.
P: Separation principle — For linear systems, the poles of a state-feedback controller with an observer are the union of the control poles and the observer poles.
P: Sequential circuit — A circuit whose output depends on both its present inputs and its stored history.
P: Servo (hobby) — A motor, gearbox, position sensor, and controller in one package that turns to an angle set by the width of a repeating pulse.
P: Setup time — The interval before a clock edge during which a flip-flop's input must be stable.
P: Shoot-through — Simultaneous conduction of both switches in one bridge leg, which short-circuits the supply.
P: Shunt reference — A two-terminal reference fed through a resistor that regulates by sinking the current the load does not take.
P: Sign-magnitude PWM — H-bridge modulation that holds one leg to set direction and switches the other, with average voltage D·V_s.
P: Slew rate — The maximum rate at which an op-amp output can change, in volts per microsecond.
P: Small-signal analysis — Treating a circuit's response to a sufficiently small signal as linear, even when the underlying device is not linear over its full range.
P: State — One of the minimum set of internal variables whose present values, with future inputs, determine a system's future behavior.
P: State feedback — A control law that feeds every state back with its own gain, u = −Kz + k_r·r, placing the closed-loop poles at the eigenvalues of A − BK.
P: Steady-state error — The difference between reference and output that remains after transients have died out.
P: Stepper motor — A motor whose rotor advances a fixed angle for each change in the winding current pattern, so position is known by counting steps.
P: Synchronizer — A chain of flip-flops, usually two, that gives a metastable state time to resolve before the signal is used.
P: Target impedance — The maximum supply-network impedance that keeps a rail within its voltage tolerance for a given load-current step, ΔV/ΔI.
P: Tolerance — The manufacturer-specified range within which a real component's actual value may fall relative to its nominal, labeled value.
P: Tracking coefficient — The temperature coefficient of the ratio between two resistors, typically in ppm/°C.
P: Transimpedance amplifier (TIA) — An op-amp with a feedback resistor that converts an input current, such as a photodiode's, into an output voltage I R_f.
P: Type-1 system — A control loop containing one integrator, which tracks a constant reference with zero steady-state error.
P: Typical value — The center of a datasheet parameter's distribution; unlike a minimum or maximum, it is not guaranteed.
P: Waterbed effect — The consequence of Bode's sensitivity integral that lowering |S| in one band raises it in another.
P: Wien bridge — An RC network with zero phase shift and a gain of 1/3 at f = 1/(2πRC), used in low-distortion audio oscillators.
P: Zero-order hold — An output stage that holds each computed value constant until the next update, producing a staircase.
H1: Appendix D — Chapter Summaries
H2: Chapter 1
P: A circuit is biased at an operating point, around which a smaller signal varies. Headroom is the distance from that point to the nearer output rail; it limits the largest undistorted signal. For fixed supply rails, gain and headroom trade off. A signal distorts when it exceeds the stage's linear range; clipping at a rail is one common cause.
H2: Chapter 2
P: Digital signals use two voltage ranges rather than a continuous one, separated by a forbidden gap. Noise margin measures how much of that gap protects against misread bits, and it is regeneration — each gate reading and re-outputting a fresh level — that keeps digital signals clean over long chains; binary place-value notation is how those two levels represent multi-bit numbers.
H2: Chapter 3
P: Boolean algebra's laws, especially De Morgan's, let a logic expression be simplified without a truth table; Karnaugh maps do the same visually for more variables than algebra alone easily handles.
H2: Chapter 4
P: Combinational circuits compute from present inputs alone; sequential circuits add memory via bistable elements — the SR latch and, far more commonly in practice, the clocked D flip-flop — that depend on their own history; chaining flip-flops together builds registers, counters, and shift registers. The 555, 4000-series, and 7400-series parts build counters, chasers, and clock dividers with no code. A microcontroller is a Swiss Army knife run by code, an FPGA a box of Lego of lookup tables and flip-flops rewired at power-up, and an ASIC a custom-built machine fixed at the factory; a k-input LUT holds 2ᵏ bits.
H2: Chapter 5
P: The ideal op-amp's golden rules — equal input voltages, zero input current — hold only under negative feedback, and from them the inverting and non-inverting amplifier gain formulas follow directly by algebra; a real op-amp's output still saturates at its supply rails once the ideal formula predicts more than they can provide.
H2: Chapter 6
P: A filter's order sets its roll-off rate at −20 dB/decade per order; a band-pass or band-stop (notch) filter targets a range of frequencies rather than a single cutoff; an active filter adds an op-amp to isolate its response from the load and to add gain, something no passive network alone can do.
P: Second-order sections are defined by H₀, f₀, and Q, with Butterworth (Q = 0.7071), Bessel (0.5773), and Chebyshev responses as named choices. The unity-gain Sallen-Key sets Q by C₁/C₂ = 4Q² and f₀ by R; the MFB inverts, sets Q by C₁/C₂ = 9Q² with equal resistors, and holds its stopband better. Higher orders cascade sections with tabulated, unequal Q values, low Q first. Use C0G or film capacitors and an op-amp with ample gain-bandwidth.
H2: Chapter 7
P: A real resistor departs from Book 1's ideal model through tolerance, power rating, temperature drift, and — at high frequency — parasitic inductance or capacitance, depending on its construction.
P: A resistor's power rating is a temperature limit and derates above its knee ambient. Precision circuits depend on ratios, so tracking coefficients and shared temperature matter more than absolute temperature coefficients; unequal self-heating can still shift a ratio. Working voltage limits high-value parts independently of power. Pulses are limited by absorbed energy. Current-sense resistors need Kelvin connections and suitable amplifiers. Thick-film parts add excess noise, and all resistors age.
H2: Chapter 8
P: Every capacitor carries a maximum voltage rating, and for a polarized electrolytic that rating applies to the correct polarity only; a real capacitor's equivalent series resistance and inductance also give it a self-resonant frequency, above which it stops behaving like a capacitor, and Class 2 ceramic types lose capacitance as DC bias voltage rises.
P: A real capacitor's impedance falls, bottoms at its ESR at the self-resonant frequency, then rises with its ESL; above resonance only mounting inductance matters. Decoupling is designed to a target impedance ΔV/ΔI over the band of the load's current steps. Paralleling unlike values creates antiresonant peaks near 1/(2π√((L₁+L₂)C_small)), tamed by equal values, close spacing, or damping. Bulk capacitance follows C ≥ IΔt/ΔV, with ESR drop added. Ripple heating shortens electrolytic life. Dielectric absorption, leakage, microphonics, and aging steer timing and sampling designs toward film and C0G parts.
H2: Chapter 9
P: Real inductor construction (air-core, ferrite- or iron-core, toroidal) trades off linearity, size, and stray field; a real inductor's core can also saturate, collapsing its inductance; its quality factor Q = ωL/R measures how efficiently it stores energy versus dissipating it, and it too has a self-resonant frequency above which it stops behaving like an inductor.
P: A power inductor is selected against two different current checks: peak current against the saturation rating and rms current against the heating rating. The circuit sets the ripple, ΔI = (V_in − V_out)D/(L f_sw), and the peak and rms follow from it; checks should include the low-tolerance inductance. DC resistance sets copper loss. Cores saturate hard or soft; shielding limits stray field. Ferrite beads are lossy parts specified by impedance, not inductance, and can resonate with decoupling capacitors.
H2: Chapter 10
P: Every loop measures, compares, corrects, and repeats. A bang-bang thermostat with a hysteresis band cycles rather than settles; the band sets the swing and the cycling rate, and the heater's average power equals the room's heat loss. Proportional control leaves a steady error that an integrator removes. A robot joint runs the same loop hundreds or thousands of times a second; a 200 Hz tracker has a 5 ms period, and sampling plus compute delay costs phase at crossover. The model proposes, and the tracker and an independent safety controller dispose.
H2: Chapter 11
P: A feedback amplifier's gain is A/(1 + Aβ), which approaches 1/β when the loop gain T = Aβ is large; its fractional gain error is about 1/T. Feedback divides gain variations and distortion by 1 + T, trades gain for bandwidth at a constant gain-bandwidth product, and moves input and output impedances in a direction set by how the loop connects. All of it depends on the returned signal being subtracted.
H2: Chapter 12
P: A buffer supplies current gain so a weak source is not loaded down. The follower has unity gain, an input impedance near that of the op-amp itself, and a gain error near 1/A, but it is limited by common-mode range and CMRR and has the least phase margin of any configuration when driving a capacitor. The inverting unity-gain stage loads its source with Rᵢₙ, avoids common-mode issues, and has half the follower's bandwidth. Capacitive loads are tamed with an isolation resistor, split feedback, or a part rated for the load. An LED is set by its current: a series resistor or an op-amp current sink, I = V_set/R_s, dimmed by PWM. An optocoupler's pull-up is sized to the minimum CTR, with margin for aging.
H2: Chapter 13
P: Closed-loop bandwidth equals the gain-bandwidth product divided by the noise gain, 1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ, which equals the signal gain only for a non-inverting stage. Holding gain within 1% requires staying below about 0.14 of that bandwidth. Splitting gain across stages extends bandwidth. Slew rate is a separate large-signal limit; the full-power bandwidth SR/(2πV_p) falls as the output amplitude rises. A photodiode TIA gives V_out = I_ph R_f at a virtual ground; its input capacitance needs C_f ≈ √(C_in/(2πR_f GBW)) for a usable bandwidth near √(GBW/(2πR_fC_in)).
H2: Chapter 14
P: Resistors produce Johnson noise √(4kTR), 40 nV/√Hz for 100 kΩ at 290 K; op-amps add input-referred voltage and current noise. Uncorrelated sources add as root-sum-of-squares, so the largest dominates. Input-referred noise times noise gain gives output density, and density times the square root of the equivalent noise bandwidth (1.571 × f₋₃dB for one pole) gives rms voltage. Below the 1/f corner noise rises, which dominates precision DC work. The optimum source resistance e_n/i_n guides op-amp choice.
H2: Chapter 15
P: Offset voltage is amplified by the noise gain; bias current flowing through the feedback resistor produces an output error that a matched resistor at the non-inverting input reduces to the offset-current term. Drift remains after trimming and must be budgeted worst-case. PSRR and CMRR act as additional offsets. Integrators turn DC errors into ramps and need a reset, a DC feedback path, or an outer loop. A datasheet's minimum and maximum are guaranteed and its typical values are not; a SPICE macromodel is a fit to those lines, valid only near the conditions it was fitted to.
H2: Chapter 16
P: A comparator turns an analog voltage into a logic decision and must never be given linear feedback. Slow, noisy inputs make a single-threshold comparator chatter; positive feedback creates two thresholds whose gap, the hysteresis, rejects noise smaller than it. Open-collector and open-drain outputs need a pull-up that trades static current against rise time (2.2 RC), translate logic levels for free, and can share one line as wired logic. An RC filter and a Schmitt input debounce a switch if t = RC ln(1/(1 − k)) exceeds the longest bounce. A comparator's threshold error is V_IO + I_IB R_th, and its response time holds only at the stated overdrive.
H2: Chapter 17
P: A control loop applies Chapter 11's feedback to a physical plant: the output follows the reference as C P/(1 + C P), and disturbances are divided by 1 + C P. A first-order plant K/(τs + 1) under proportional control becomes faster and more accurate by the factor 1 + K_p K, but keeps a steady-state error; integral action removes it at the cost of phase lag. Actuator saturation, sensor noise, unmodeled dynamics, and the safety layer bound how aggressive a controller may be.
H2: Chapter 18
P: The PID controller combines proportional, integral, and derivative action, each with physical units. For a first-order plant, a PI controller whose zero cancels the plant pole gives a pure-integrator loop with 90 degrees of phase margin and a crossover set by K_p K/τ. Lead networks add phase near crossover (maximum arcsin((α − 1)/(α + 1)) at √(ω_z ω_p)); lag networks add low-frequency gain. Phase margin predicts overshoot. Anti-windup stops the integrator from accumulating while the actuator is saturated.
H2: Chapter 19
P: A digital controller samples, computes, and holds. The zero-order hold adds about half a period of delay and a one-period compute delay adds a full period; a delay costs ω_c × delay radians of phase at crossover without reducing gain. For the 5 ms tracker, a 100 rad/s crossover loses about 43° of margin, and halving the crossover or sampling faster restores it. The backward-Euler PI is a running sum with a clamp for anti-windup; Tustin matches the continuous response more closely. Aliasing, anti-alias filter lag, and quantization complete the picture. On a Pico or a Teensy, a timer interrupt fixes the sample rate, f_clk/f_PWM sets the PWM steps, and the arithmetic must fit inside the period; the code itself is in Book 8.
H2: Chapter 20
P: A differential measurement separates V_d = V₊ − V₋ from V_cm = (V₊ + V₋)/2, and CMRR = A_d/A_cm measures how well the amplifier ignores the second. A one-op-amp difference amplifier's rejection is set by resistor matching, about (1 + G)/(4t) in the worst case, and its finite input resistance loads the source. The three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier buffers both inputs, sets its gain with one resistor through G = (1 + 2R/R_G)(R₂/R₁), and multiplies the difference stage's rejection by its front-end gain. Internal nodes at V_cm ± G₁V_d/2 must stay within their swing, and the REF pin must be driven from a low impedance, because resistance there adds directly to the matched network. Front ends match the sensor: a voltage to an instrumentation amplifier, a current to a TIA, a charge to a charge amplifier with V_out = −q/C_f. A CT burden is sized so the peak secondary current times R_b stays within the mid-supply bias.
H2: Chapter 21
P: A plant's states determine its future; linear plants are written ż = Az + Bu, y = Cz, with open-loop poles at the eigenvalues of A. State feedback u = −Kz + k_r·r places the closed-loop poles at the eigenvalues of A − BK; for a mass, k₁ = mωₙ² and k₂ = 2ζωₙm, which is PD control with a measured derivative. Pole placement requires controllability. An observer dẑ/dt = Aẑ + Bu + L(y − Cẑ) estimates unmeasured states with error poles set by L, typically three to five times faster than the control poles, and the separation principle lets the two be designed independently. An integral state removes steady error from constant disturbances. Actuator limits and noise, not algebra, bound how fast the poles can be placed.
H2: Chapter 22
P: A sample-and-hold is a switch, a capacitor, and a buffer. Tracking settles as an RC network, needing (N + 1) ln 2 time constants to reach ½ LSB. The capacitor freezes kT/C noise independent of resistance. Opening the switch injects a pedestal Q/C, and aperture jitter limits SNR to −20 log₁₀(2πfσ_t). During hold, leakage causes droop I/C and the off-switch passes feedthrough. A larger capacitor improves every error except acquisition time. Switched-capacitor converters draw a charge-sharing kick each sample, so they need a driver amplifier with an isolating resistor and an external charge reservoir.
H2: Chapter 23
P: A base-emitter voltage falls about 2 mV/K (CTAT), while the difference between two junctions at different current densities, (kT/q) ln N, rises in exact proportion to temperature (PTAT). Weighting the PTAT term by K = |dV_BE/dT|/((k/q) ln N) and adding it to V_BE gives a reference near 1.2 V with zero slope at one temperature. An op-amp bandgap sets I = ΔV_BE/R₁ and V_REF = V_BE + (R₂/R₁)ΔV_BE, so only ratios matter, while op-amp offset is amplified by K. Datasheet temperature coefficients use the box method. In an error budget, initial accuracy is calibrated away, but temperature drift, hysteresis, and long-term drift remain. A shunt reference's resistor must supply full load plus minimum current at the lowest supply.
H2: Chapter 24
P: An H-bridge applies +V_s, −V_s, or zero to a motor through four switches, with dead time to prevent shoot-through. Bipolar PWM gives (2D − 1)V_s and ripple 2V_sD(1 − D)/(Lf_sw), which is largest at zero voltage; sign-magnitude PWM gives D·V_s and half the ripple. Dead time adds an error near V_s t_d f_sw per leg opposing the current. The DC motor obeys V = Ri + L di/dt + K_eω and J dω/dt = K_ti − bω − T_L, with τ_e = L/R and τ_m = JR/(K_tK_e). Drives nest a PI current loop, whose zero cancels R/L and whose crossover is limited by the sample delay, inside a speed loop a decade slower whose current command is clamped. Braking returns energy to the bus, which a rectified supply cannot absorb; a brake chopper sized for the peak regenerative power protects it. Every switched coil needs a flyback path. A hobby servo closes its own loop from a 1 to 2 ms pulse every 20 ms; a stepper counts steps open-loop and microsteps for resolution.
H2: Chapter 25
P: The root locus traces the closed-loop poles as one gain varies. Branches start at the open-loop poles and end at the zeros or along asymptotes at (2k + 1)180°/(n − m) from the centroid. A second-order plant 1/(s(s + 4)) cannot settle faster than 2 s by gain alone; the third-order plant 1/(s(s + 2)(s + 10)) breaks away at −0.945, reaches ζ = 0.5 at K = 28.7, and goes unstable at K = 240 and 4.47 rad/s. Lead control bends the locus left; lag control raises low-frequency gain while leaving it nearly unchanged.
H2: Chapter 26
P: The Nyquist criterion counts encirclements of −1 by L(jω): Z = N + P. Gain margin is read where the phase is −180°, phase margin where |L| = 1, and the delay margin is PM/ω_c. For L = K/(s(s + 2)(s + 10)), K = 28.7 gives 18.4 dB, 51.7°, and 0.74 s; K = 60 gives 12.0 dB, 32.7°, and 0.28 s. The sensitivity peak M_s bounds both margins, GM ≥ M_s/(M_s − 1) and PM ≥ 2 arcsin(1/(2M_s)), and a value of 1.2 to 2 is a good target.
H2: Chapter 27
P: An oscillator satisfies the Barkhausen condition, Aβ = 1 at zero phase, at one frequency. The Wien bridge runs at 1/(2πRC) with a gain of 3; the phase-shift oscillator at 1/(2π√6RC) with a gain of 29; the Colpitts at the resonance of L with C₁ and C₂ in series. Relaxation oscillators charge a capacitor between two thresholds: T = 2RC ln((1 + β)/(1 − β)), and the 555 runs at 1.44/((R₁ + 2R₂)C). A crystal in a Pierce circuit needs its specified load capacitance, the series pair plus stray. The 555, Schmitt inverters, op-amp clipping stages, and Nixie tubes make synthesizers, pedals, amplifiers, and clocks with no controller; a 555 at 440 Hz with 10 nF needs R₁ + 2R₂ = 327 kΩ.
H2: Chapter 28
P: A path between flip-flops passes setup if T_clk ≥ t_cq + t_pd + t_su + t_skew and hold if t_cq + t_cd ≥ t_h + t_skew; a 7.0 ns path runs at 142.9 MHz, and a hold violation persists at any clock rate. An asynchronous input can leave a flip-flop metastable; MTBF = e^(t_r/τ)/(T_w f_clk f_data) rises from 40 minutes at 1.0 ns of resolution time to 10²⁶ years at 4.5 ns, which is why two-flip-flop synchronizers, Gray codes, and handshakes guard every clock-domain crossing.
