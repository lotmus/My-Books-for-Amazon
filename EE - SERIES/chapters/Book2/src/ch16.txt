H1: Chapter 16 — Comparators, Hysteresis, and Open-Collector Outputs
P: A thermostat that clicks on and off ten times a second as the room crosses the set point is the comparator problem in miniature. The cure is the same in both: two thresholds instead of one.
P: Where you meet this: Button inputs, zero-crossing detectors, battery-low indicators, overcurrent trips and open-drain interrupt lines.
P: Somewhere in almost every analog system, a continuous voltage has to become a decision: the battery is low, the temperature is too high, the zero crossing has arrived. A comparator makes that decision. Chapter 5 introduced it as an op-amp-shaped part with the feedback deliberately left off. This chapter shows why a bare comparator misbehaves on slow, noisy signals, how a little positive feedback fixes that, and how the open-collector output lets several comparators, or several chips, share one wire.
H2: 16.1 What Makes a Comparator Different
P: A comparator and an op-amp share a symbol and a differential input, but they are optimized for opposite jobs. An op-amp is internally compensated so that it stays stable under negative feedback, and that compensation slows it down. A comparator has no such compensation; its output stage is designed to swing hard from one logic level to the other and to recover quickly when the inputs cross. Its datasheet quotes a propagation delay, the time from the input crossing to the output changing state, rather than a gain-bandwidth product.
P: An op-amp can be used as a comparator in a pinch, and for slow signals it often works. Its weaknesses are real, though: it is slow to come out of saturation, its output levels may not match logic thresholds, and some op-amps have input protection diodes between the two inputs that conduct when a large differential voltage appears, which a comparator's inputs are built to tolerate.
MIS: Never put linear negative feedback around a comparator to "make it more accurate." Its lack of compensation means it will oscillate. The only feedback a comparator should see is the positive feedback of Section 16.3, which pushes it further in the direction it is already going.
H2: 16.2 Chatter on a Slow Edge
P: Feed a comparator a slowly rising voltage that carries even a few millivolts of noise. As the signal approaches the threshold, the noise carries it back and forth across the threshold many times, and the output toggles every time. Instead of one clean transition, the output produces a burst of edges, called chatter. A counter connected to that output counts dozens of events instead of one; a motor driver switches on and off at kilohertz rates and heats up. Faster comparators make chatter worse, because they respond to every crossing.
H2: 16.3 Hysteresis: Two Thresholds Instead of One
P: The cure is to give the comparator two thresholds: a higher one that the input must rise above to switch the output high, and a lower one that it must fall below to switch it back. Once the output has switched, noise smaller than the gap between the thresholds cannot switch it back. A circuit with this behavior is called a Schmitt trigger, and the gap is its hysteresis.
FIG: fig16_1.png | Figure 16.1 — An inverting Schmitt trigger has two thresholds, so noise smaller than the gap between them can’t make the output chatter.
P: A comparator acquires hysteresis through positive feedback. In the inverting form, the input signal drives the inverting pin, and a divider sets the non-inverting pin from both a reference voltage V_ref (through R₁) and the comparator's own output (through R₂). Superposition gives the non-inverting pin's voltage, which is the threshold:
EQ: V_th = V_ref · R₂/(R₁ + R₂) + V_out · R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
P: Because the threshold depends on the output, it has two values, one for each output state. The hysteresis is the output swing multiplied by R₁/(R₁ + R₂).
WE: Worked Example 16.1
PROB: A comparator runs from 5 V with a push-pull output that swings 0 V to 5 V. The input drives the inverting pin. The non-inverting pin connects to V_ref = 2.5 V through R₁ = 10 kΩ and to the output through R₂ = 100 kΩ. Find the two thresholds and the hysteresis.
SOL: The reference contributes 2.5 × 100k/110k ≈ 2.273 V. The output contributes 5 × 10k/110k ≈ 0.455 V when high and 0 V when low. With the output high (input below threshold), the upper threshold is 2.273 + 0.455 ≈ 2.727 V; the input must rise above this to drive the output low. With the output low, the lower threshold is 2.273 V; the input must fall below this to drive the output high again. The hysteresis is 0.455 V, centered on 2.5 V. Noise up to about 0.45 V peak-to-peak can no longer cause chatter.
FIG: fig16_2.png | Figure 16.2 — Top: a slow, noisy input with the two thresholds of Worked Example 16.1. Bottom: the Schmitt trigger output switches once per crossing, where a single-threshold comparator would chatter.
KEY: Hysteresis trades a small, known threshold error for immunity to noise smaller than the gap. Size the gap larger than the peak-to-peak noise on the input and smaller than the error the application can tolerate.
P: Positive feedback is entirely legitimate here. The golden rules do not apply; the two inputs are not at the same voltage except at the instant of switching, and nothing in the circuit is trying to make them so. This is the same sign change Chapter 11 warned about, used deliberately to make the circuit decisive.
H2: 16.4 The Open-Collector Output
P: Many comparators, including the long-lived LM311 and LM339 families, have an open-collector output: a single transistor that can pull the output pin low, and nothing that can pull it high. The high level is supplied by an external pull-up resistor to whatever supply the next circuit needs. That one feature makes the part a level translator for free: a comparator powered from 12 V can drive a 3.3 V logic input by returning its pull-up to 3.3 V. CMOS parts with the same arrangement are called open-drain.
P: The pull-up resistor sets two competing quantities. When the transistor is on, the resistor wastes a current V_pu/R_pu. When it turns off, the resistor alone must charge the capacitance of the wire and the inputs on it, so the rising edge is an RC exponential with time constant R_pu C, and its 10% to 90% rise time is 2.2 R_pu C. Falling edges are fast, because the transistor sinks current actively.
WE: Worked Example 16.2
PROB: An open-drain output pulls up to 3.3 V through 4.7 kΩ into a line with 50 pF of total capacitance. Find the rise time and the low-state current. What largest pull-up gives a 100 ns rise time, and what current does it draw?
SOL: The time constant is 4.7 kΩ × 50 pF = 235 ns, so the rise time is 2.2 × 235 ns ≈ 517 ns. The low-state current is 3.3 V/4.7 kΩ ≈ 0.70 mA, ignoring the small output-low voltage. For a 100 ns rise, R_pu ≤ 100 ns/(2.2 × 50 pF) ≈ 909 Ω; a standard 820 Ω part gives 90 ns and draws about 4.0 mA when low. Check that the output transistor can sink that current while still meeting the receiver's maximum input-low voltage; the datasheet's V_OL at a stated sink current is the number to compare.
H2: 16.5 Wired Logic on One Line
P: Several open-collector or open-drain outputs can share one pull-up resistor and one wire. If any output turns on, the line goes low; the line is high only if every output is off. For active-low signals, such as a "fault" line that any of several monitors may assert, this is a wired-OR: the line is asserted if any monitor asserts it. In positive logic the same connection is a wired-AND. Push-pull outputs cannot be connected this way, because one driving high while another drives low creates a short circuit between the supply and ground through two output stages.
P: The arrangement is common far beyond comparators. Interrupt lines shared by several chips use it, and the I²C bus uses it for both its clock and data lines, so any device can hold the clock low to stretch it. The rise-time arithmetic of Worked Example 16.2 is exactly what limits how fast such a bus can run and how many devices it can carry.
H2: 16.6 The Window Comparator
P: Two comparators with open-collector outputs tied together make a window comparator: one fires when the input exceeds an upper limit, the other when it falls below a lower limit, and the shared line goes low if either fires. A supply monitor that flags both overvoltage and undervoltage on one pin is built exactly this way, with the two thresholds set by a single resistor ladder from a reference.
H2: 16.7 Debouncing a Switch: the Refrigerator Door
P: A mechanical switch does not close once. Its contacts bounce for a few milliseconds, and a logic input or a microcontroller interrupt sees a burst of edges. The Smart Refrigerator Monitor of Book 1 counts door openings, and every bounce would count as one. The analog-first cure is an RC filter feeding a Schmitt-trigger input such as a 74HC14: the capacitor smooths the bounces, and the hysteresis of this chapter turns the slow, smoothed edge into one clean transition.
P: With the switch to ground and a pull-up R to V_DD, closing the switch discharges C almost at once through the contacts, and each bounce open lets it recharge only slowly through R. The output must not change until the capacitor charges from near 0 V to the Schmitt input's upper threshold. With that threshold at a fraction k of V_DD, the time is t = RC ln(1/(1 − k)), and it must exceed the longest bounce. Schmitt inputs typically put the upper threshold between about 0.6 and 0.7 of V_DD; design with the lower value, which gives the shorter time.
WE: Worked Example 16.3
PROB: The refrigerator door switch bounces for up to 5 ms. It pulls a 10 kΩ pull-up low, and the Schmitt input's upper threshold lies between 0.6 and 0.7 of V_DD. Choose C.
SOL: With k = 0.6, t = RC ln(1/0.4) = 0.916 RC, so RC ≥ 5 ms/0.916 = 5.46 ms and C ≥ 5.46 ms/10 kΩ = 546 nF. Choose 680 nF: RC = 6.8 ms and t = 6.2 ms. Check: at k = 0.7, t = 6.8 ms × ln(1/0.3) = 8.2 ms; both exceed 5 ms, and a response delay under 10 ms is invisible for a door counter. A microcontroller can debounce in code instead (Book 8, Chapter 6, GPIO, Buttons, and Interrupts), but the RC and Schmitt version needs no code and spares the interrupt input a burst of edges.
KEY: Debounce with an RC that outlasts the bounce and a Schmitt input that turns the slow edge into one clean transition: t = RC ln(1/(1 − k)) must exceed the longest bounce.
H2: 16.8 Reading a Comparator Datasheet and Its Model
P: The LM393 of the reader lab, read from the TI datasheet (SLCS005AH, LM393 without suffix) at V_CC = 5 V and 25 °C, has a shorter list than an op-amp, and almost every line matters:
LI: Input offset voltage V_IO: 2 mV typical, 5 mV maximum, and 9 mV maximum over 0 °C to 70 °C. It is the threshold error before anything else.
LI: Input bias current I_IB: −25 nA typical, −250 nA maximum, −400 nA over temperature. The minus sign means the current flows out of the pin, from the PNP input stage, into whatever resistance drives it. Input offset current: 5 nA typical, 50 nA maximum.
LI: Common-mode input range: 0 V to V_CC − 1.5 V, and V_CC − 2 V over temperature. Only one input needs to be inside it for a correct output.
LI: Voltage gain A_VD: 50 V/mV minimum, 200 V/mV typical, so a millivolt of input is enough to switch the output.
LI: Output: an open collector. Leakage when off: 0.1 nA typical at 5 V and 1 µA maximum at 30 V over temperature. Low level V_OL: 130 mV typical and 400 mV maximum at 4 mA; at least 6 mA of sink current at V_OL = 1.5 V.
LI: Supply current: 0.45 mA typical, 1 mA maximum, for the package.
LI: Response time: 1.3 µs typical for a 100 mV input step with 5 mV of overdrive, and 0.3 µs for a TTL-level step, with 5.1 kΩ to 5 V and 15 pF, measured to the moment the output crosses 1.4 V. There is no maximum.
P: Overdrive is how far the input goes past the threshold. A small overdrive drives the internal stages with a small current, so they take longer to swing; a large one saturates them, and the delay stops improving. A single response time is one point on that curve, valid at its own overdrive and load.
WE: Worked Example 16.4
PROB: An LM393 compares a sensor voltage with a reference made by a 100 kΩ/100 kΩ divider from 5 V. Find the worst-case threshold error from offset and bias current at 25 °C and over 0 °C to 70 °C, and repeat at 25 °C for a 10 kΩ/10 kΩ divider.
SOL: The divider's Thévenin resistance is 100 kΩ ∥ 100 kΩ = 50 kΩ. Up to 250 nA flows out of the pin into it, raising the reference by 250 nA × 50 kΩ = 12.5 mV. Adding the 5 mV offset gives 17.5 mV of worst-case threshold error at 25 °C. Over 0 °C to 70 °C it is 400 nA × 50 kΩ + 9 mV = 29 mV. With 10 kΩ/10 kΩ the Thévenin resistance is 5 kΩ, the bias term falls to 1.25 mV, and the total is 6.25 mV, set mostly by the offset. Hysteresis (Section 16.3) deals with noise; accuracy comes from these two lines.
P: A comparator macromodel has the same input sources as an op-amp model, a gain stage whose current is limited and whose capacitor is clamped, and an output switch whose on-resistance is fitted to V_OL: 130 mV/4 mA = 32.5 Ω. Its timing has two parameters, the current limit I_max and the capacitor C, and the datasheet gives exactly two timing points. Fitting both, with ngspice, gives the model below. Figure 16.3 shows the result: the model meets 1.30 µs at 5 mV of overdrive and 0.30 µs for the TTL step, and predicts 3.2 µs at 2 mV. That prediction is the model extrapolating; no datasheet line constrains it, and it should be checked against the datasheet's response-time curves or measured (Book 9) before a design depends on it.
P: * CMPFIT: a datasheet-fitted behavioral comparator macromodel (Book 2, Section 16.8).
P: * Fitted to TYPICAL values of the TI LM393 datasheet (SLCS005AH, LM393 without suffix, VCC = 5 V, 25 C):
P: *   VIO 2 mV typ (5 mV max), IIB 25 nA typ flowing out of the inputs, response time 1.3 us
P: *   (100 mV step, 5 mV overdrive) and 0.3 us (TTL step), RL = 5.1k to 5 V, CL = 15 pF,
P: *   VOL 130 mV at 4 mA, output leakage 0.1 nA, ICC 0.45 mA.
P: * Two timing parameters (IMAX, C) are fitted to the two response times; nothing else about timing is known.
P: .subckt CMPFIT inp inn vcc gnd out params: VIO=0 GM=0.470m IMAX=10u C=5.66p
P: Vio inp inp1 DC {VIO}
P: Iop inp1 vcc DC 25n
P: Ion inn vcc DC 25n
P: * integrating stage: limited transconductance into C, clamped at +-0.5 V
P: Bgm 0 n1 I = {IMAX}*tanh({GM}*V(inp1,inn)/{IMAX})
P: Cint n1 0 {C}
P: Rint n1 0 425Meg
P: Dcl1 n1 cp DCL
P: Vcp cp 0 0.5
P: Dcl2 cm n1 DCL
P: Vcm cm 0 -0.5
P: .model DCL D(IS=1e-15 N=0.05)
P: * open-collector output: switch on when n1 < 0 (output low), Ron fitted to VOL = 130 mV at 4 mA
P: Sout out gnd 0 n1 SOC

FIG: fig16_3.png | Figure 16.3 — Response time against overdrive for the fitted LM393 macromodel (ngspice), with the two datasheet points it was fitted to. Between and beyond them the curve is the model's prediction, not a datasheet value.
KEY: Two datasheet points can fit two model parameters, and no more. Everything else the model says is a prediction, as good as the model's equations and no better.
BENCH: Don't use an op-amp as a comparator unless its datasheet allows it. Many op-amps recover slowly from saturation, and some have input clamp diodes that misbehave with large differential voltages. Comparators are cheap and much faster out of saturation. And always add some hysteresis; a few millivolts stops chatter on slow, noisy edges.
