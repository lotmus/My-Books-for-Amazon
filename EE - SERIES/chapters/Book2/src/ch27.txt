H1: Chapter 27 — Oscillators: Feedback That Is Meant to Ring
P: Every engineer's first oscillator is an accident: an amplifier that rings because its feedback came back in phase. This chapter does it on purpose, which is harder than it sounds, because a good oscillator must start reliably and then stop growing.
P: Where you meet this: Clocks in every digital system, crystal oscillators, function generators, and the RC oscillators inside microcontrollers.
P: Chapters 11 to 18 worked to keep loops away from the point where the loop gain is −1. An oscillator is a loop designed to sit exactly there. Every clock in Chapter 2's digital circuits, every carrier in a radio, and every timer that blinks a light comes from a circuit that turns its own noise into a steady waveform. This chapter states the condition for oscillation, builds the two families of oscillator, linear and relaxation, and sizes each with numbers.
H2: 27.1 The Barkhausen Condition
P: Break a feedback loop and send a sine wave around it. If it returns with exactly the same amplitude and phase, the loop can sustain it without any input. That is the Barkhausen condition: the loop gain Aβ equals one, with a total phase shift of 0° or a multiple of 360°. A practical oscillator is designed with a loop gain slightly above one at the wanted frequency, so that noise grows into an oscillation, and some nonlinearity then limits the amplitude, reducing the effective gain to exactly one.
EQ: A(jω₀) β(jω₀) = 1   (|Aβ| = 1,  ∠Aβ = 0° or 360°)
P: The frequency is set by the network β, which has the right phase at only one frequency. The amplifier supplies the gain to make up the network's loss. The quality of the oscillator, its frequency stability and its phase noise as Book 4 measures it, depends on how sharply β's phase changes with frequency near ω₀: an RC network changes slowly, an LC tank faster, and a quartz crystal, with a Q of tens of thousands, fastest of all.
H2: 27.2 The Wien-Bridge Oscillator
P: The Wien network is a series RC in the forward path and a parallel RC to ground. At ω₀ = 1/(RC) its phase shift is zero and its gain is exactly 1/3. A noninverting op-amp stage with a gain of 3, set by R_f = 2R_g, completes the loop. Because the required gain is exact, a fixed resistor ratio either fails to start or grows until the output clips; real Wien oscillators use a lamp, a thermistor, a JFET, or diodes to reduce the gain as the amplitude grows. Hewlett's 1939 audio oscillator, the first product of Hewlett-Packard, used a small incandescent lamp for exactly this purpose.
EQ: f₀ = 1/(2πRC),   β(f₀) = 1/3,   A = 1 + R_f/R_g = 3
WE: Worked Example 27.1
PROB: A Wien-bridge oscillator uses R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF. Find its frequency and the feedback resistor needed with R_g = 4.7 kΩ.
SOL: f₀ = 1/(2π × 10⁴ × 10⁻⁸) = 1,592 Hz. R_f = 2R_g = 9.4 kΩ for a gain of exactly 3. In practice R_f is made slightly larger, perhaps 10 kΩ, so the oscillation starts reliably, and a pair of back-to-back diodes in series with a resistor across part of R_f lowers the gain as the amplitude grows, holding the output at a level set by the diode drops.
H2: 27.3 Phase-Shift and LC Oscillators
P: Three cascaded RC sections with an inverting amplifier form the phase-shift oscillator. Each section adds lag until the total is 180° at f₀ = 1/(2π√6·RC), where the network attenuates by a factor of 29, so the amplifier needs a gain of at least 29. At radio frequencies, an LC tank provides the frequency. In the Colpitts oscillator, the tank's capacitance is split into two capacitors in series, whose midpoint supplies the feedback; the frequency is that of L with the series combination of C₁ and C₂. Book 4 treats these oscillators' phase noise; here the point is that the same Barkhausen condition governs them all.
EQ: Phase shift: f₀ = 1/(2π√6·RC), |A| ≥ 29;   Colpitts: f₀ = 1/(2π√(L·C₁C₂/(C₁ + C₂)))
WE: Worked Example 27.2
PROB: Find the frequency of a phase-shift oscillator with R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF, and of a Colpitts oscillator with L = 1 µH and C₁ = C₂ = 200 pF.
SOL: The phase-shift oscillator runs at 1/(2π × 2.449 × 10⁴ × 10⁻⁸) = 650 Hz, with an amplifier gain of at least 29. For the Colpitts, the series capacitance is 100 pF, so f₀ = 1/(2π√(10⁻⁶ × 10⁻¹⁰)) = 15.9 MHz.
H2: 27.4 Relaxation Oscillators
P: A relaxation oscillator does not use a resonant network. It charges a capacitor until a threshold is reached, switches, and discharges it to a second threshold. The Schmitt trigger of Chapter 16 provides the two thresholds. With an op-amp comparator whose positive feedback divider has ratio β = R₂/(R₁ + R₂), and an RC from output to the inverting input, the capacitor swings between +βV and −βV, and each half cycle takes RC ln((1 + β)/(1 − β)).
EQ: T = 2RC ln((1 + β)/(1 − β));   β = 0.5 gives T = 2.197 RC
FIG: fig27_1.png | Figure 27.1 — The op-amp relaxation oscillator: a Schmitt trigger that keeps flipping because its own output charges a capacitor toward the other threshold. | Schematic. The op-amp output feeds back through resistor R to the inverting input, where capacitor C goes to ground. The output also feeds a divider, R1 to the non-inverting input and R2 from there to ground, which sets the thresholds. The output is a square wave. Formulas: beta equals R2 over R1 plus R2; the period T equals 2 R C times the natural log of (1 plus beta) over (1 minus beta).
WE: Worked Example 27.3
PROB: An op-amp relaxation oscillator uses R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 nF, and a hysteresis divider with β = 0.5. Find the period and frequency.
SOL: T = 2 × 10⁴ × 10⁻⁷ × ln(1.5/0.5) = 2 × 10⁻³ × 1.0986 = 2.197 ms, so f = 455 Hz. The frequency depends on the ratio of thresholds, not on the supply voltage, as long as the output swings symmetrically.
FIG: fig27_2.png | Figure 27.2 — Relaxation oscillator waveforms for R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 nF, β = 0.5: the capacitor voltage ramps exponentially between ±βV while the comparator output switches between ±V, with a period of 2.197 ms. | Relaxation-oscillator traces show the capacitor voltage ramping exponentially between two thresholds while the comparator output switches between its rails.
P: The 555 timer, introduced in 1972 and still in production from several makers, is a packaged relaxation oscillator. Two comparators at one-third and two-thirds of the supply, a flip-flop, and a discharge transistor charge C through R₁ + R₂ and discharge it through R₂. Its frequency is 1.44/((R₁ + 2R₂)C), and its duty cycle (R₁ + R₂)/(R₁ + 2R₂) is always above 50% in the basic circuit. With R₁ = 1 kΩ, R₂ = 10 kΩ, and C = 10 nF it runs at 6,857 Hz with a duty cycle of 52.4%.
KEY: An oscillator is a loop with a gain of exactly one and a phase of exactly zero at one frequency. The network sets the frequency, the amplifier makes up the loss, and a nonlinearity holds the amplitude; the sharper the network's phase versus frequency, the more stable the oscillator.
H2: 27.5 The Crystal Oscillator
P: Most microcontroller clocks that need accuracy start from a quartz crystal in a Pierce oscillator: an inverting gate biased into its linear region, the crystal across it, and two capacitors to ground. The crystal is cut to resonate at its marked frequency when it sees a specified load capacitance C_L, typically 8 to 20 pF. The two capacitors appear in series, plus the stray capacitance of the pins and traces. With 22 pF on each side and 5 pF of stray, C_L = 22 × 22/44 + 5 = 16 pF. Choosing them wrong pulls the frequency by tens of parts per million, enough to break a UART at the edge of its tolerance or a radio's channel accuracy in Book 5.
MIS: An oscillator that works on the bench is not proven. The startup gain margin shrinks at low temperature and low supply voltage, the crystal's drive level can be exceeded, and a scope probe's capacitance can pull or even start an oscillator that would otherwise fail. Verify startup at the corners of temperature and supply, and measure frequency with a buffered output or a low-capacitance probe.
H2: 27.6 Projects Without a Controller: Synthesizers, Pedals, Amplifiers, and Nixies
P: The oscillators of this chapter are the heart of a family of projects that need no microcontroller at all, and they remain some of the most satisfying builds in electronics. A 555 astable with a potentiometer in R₂ is a one-chip synthesizer voice; two 555s, one modulating the other through its control-voltage pin, make the classic noise box known as the Atari Punk Console. A CD40106 hex Schmitt inverter makes six relaxation oscillators from one resistor and one capacitor each. A full analog synthesizer is built from three ideas: an oscillator whose frequency follows a control voltage (a VCO), a filter whose corner follows another (the sections of Chapter 6 with a variable element), and an amplifier whose gain follows a third.
P: Guitar effects pedals use this book's op-amp circuits on purpose outside their linear range. An overdrive or fuzz is a gain stage driving a pair of diodes that clip the peaks, the headroom of Chapter 1 made musical; a tremolo is a low-frequency oscillator modulating a gain; and a delay repeats the sample-and-hold of Chapter 22 thousands of times, in a bucket-brigade chip or a converter and memory. A small audio power amplifier such as the LM386 completes the chain to a speaker. A Nixie-tube clock combines the CD4060 time base of Section 4.5 with a high-voltage supply: its tubes need roughly 170 V to strike and then run at a few milliamps through an anode resistor. That supply can deliver a painful and dangerous shock; Book 6 covers the converter that makes it.
WE: Worked Example 27.4
PROB: Tune a 555 synthesizer voice to concert A, 440 Hz, with C = 10 nF and R₁ = 1 kΩ. Find R₂, choose a standard value, and find the resulting frequency and duty cycle.
SOL: From f = 1.44/((R₁ + 2R₂)C), R₁ + 2R₂ = 1.44/(440 × 10⁻⁸) = 327.3 kΩ, so R₂ = (327.3 − 1)/2 = 163 kΩ. The nearest E24 value, 160 kΩ, gives f = 1.44/(321 kΩ × 10 nF) = 449 Hz, about 34 cents sharp, so a real synthesizer uses 150 kΩ in series with a 20 kΩ trimmer and tunes by ear or with a tuner. The duty cycle is (1 + 160)/(1 + 320) = 50.2%, close to a square wave, which is rich in odd harmonics. Check: 1.44/(327.3 kΩ × 10 nF) = 440 Hz.
KEY: An oscillator, a filter, and a gain stage, each with a knob, are a synthesizer; a gain stage with diodes is a pedal. No code is needed, and the analog-first rule says to build them first.
BENCH: Crystal oscillators fail in quiet ways. Use the load capacitance the crystal is specified for (including about 2 to 5 pF of board stray), keep the traces short and guarded by ground, and check start-up at cold temperature and low supply voltage, where gain margin is smallest.
