H1: Chapter 5 — Operational Amplifiers
P: The ideal op-amp is a lie, and a magnificently useful one. With two rules, no current into the inputs and no voltage between them under negative feedback, you can design most of the amplifiers in this book in your head, and then spend the rest of the book learning where the lie breaks down.
P: Where you meet this: Sensor amplifiers, audio preamps, active filters, current-sense circuits and the analog front end of almost every measurement instrument.
P: The operational amplifier, or op-amp, is the single most common building block in analog design, and it earns that status by being simple to reason about despite the elaborate transistor circuitry inside it. This chapter treats the op-amp the way Book 1 treated the resistor, capacitor, and inductor: as an ideal element, defined by a small number of assumptions, whose exact behavior in a circuit can be derived from first principles — leaving the transistor-level detail of how an op-amp achieves these properties to Book 3.
H2: 5.1 The Ideal Op-Amp and the Golden Rules
P: An ideal op-amp is defined by three properties: infinite open-loop voltage gain, infinite input impedance, and zero output impedance. From these follow two rules that make analyzing an op-amp circuit almost entirely a matter of algebra, provided — and this proviso matters — the circuit uses negative feedback to keep the op-amp out of saturation:
EQ: Golden Rule 1:  V₊ = V₋ (a “virtual short”)         Golden Rule 2:  I₊ = I₋ = 0
P: Rule 1 does not claim the two input terminals are actually connected together; no current flows between them, and nothing forces their voltages to match except the surrounding feedback circuit actively adjusting the output until they do. Rule 2 follows directly from infinite input impedance: no real current can flow into either input terminal, so any current arriving at a node connected to an op-amp input must go somewhere else entirely.
MIS: The golden rules are not a property of the op-amp in isolation — they are a property of a correctly designed negative-feedback circuit built around one. Remove the feedback path, or feed the output back to the wrong input, and the op-amp instead saturates hard against one supply rail, and neither golden rule describes its behavior anymore.
H2: 5.2 The Inverting Amplifier
P: The most common op-amp configuration grounds the non-inverting input, applies the input signal through a resistor Rᵢₙ to the inverting input, and feeds the output back to that same inverting input through a resistor R_f. By Golden Rule 1, the inverting input sits at 0 V (a “virtual ground”), since the non-inverting input is grounded. By Golden Rule 2, whatever current flows in through Rᵢₙ has nowhere to go but through R_f to the output:
EQ: Vₒᵤₜ = −(R_f / Rᵢₙ) · Vᵢₙ
P: The negative sign is the defining feature of this configuration: the output is an inverted, scaled copy of the input.
WE: Worked Example 5.1
PROB: An inverting amplifier has R_f = 100 kΩ and Rᵢₙ = 10 kΩ. Find the output for an input of 0.5 V, and verify the result directly from the golden rules.
SOL: Gain = −R_f/Rᵢₙ = −100k/10k = −10. Vₒᵤₜ = −10 × 0.5 = −5 V. Checking directly: the inverting input sits at 0 V (virtual ground), so the current into Rᵢₙ is I = (0.5 − 0)/10k = 50 µA. That current has nowhere to go but through R_f, so Vₒᵤₜ = 0 − (50 µA)(100k) = −5 V — matching the formula exactly.

H2: 5.3 The Non-Inverting Amplifier
P: Applying the input directly to the non-inverting terminal, and feeding the output back to the inverting terminal through a voltage divider (R_f from output to the inverting input, Rᵢₙ from the inverting input to ground), gives a gain that is always at least 1 and never inverts the signal:
EQ: Vₒᵤₜ = (1 + R_f / Rᵢₙ) · Vᵢₙ
WE: Worked Example 5.2
PROB: A non-inverting amplifier must provide a gain of exactly 6. If Rᵢₙ = 2 kΩ, find R_f.
SOL: 6 = 1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ, so R_f/Rᵢₙ = 5, and R_f = 5 × 2k = 10 kΩ.

H2: 5.4 Real Limitations
P: A real op-amp's open-loop gain is large but finite, and it falls off with frequency — the gain-bandwidth product (GBW) is roughly constant, so a design that needs high gain at high frequency runs out of available gain sooner than one that needs only modest gain. Slew rate limits how fast the output can move at all, regardless of gain, and becomes a hard ceiling on the largest undistorted signal amplitude at high frequency — a close cousin of Chapter 1's headroom limit, but caused by a rate-of-change constraint rather than a voltage constraint. A small input offset voltage, ideally zero, in practice means the output is not exactly zero even when both inputs are nominally at the same voltage.
WE: Worked Example 5.3
PROB: The inverting amplifier from Worked Example 5.1 (R_f = 100 kΩ, Rᵢₙ = 10 kΩ) is powered from ±5 V supply rails. Find the output the ideal gain formula predicts for an input of 1.0 V, and explain what the amplifier actually does instead.
SOL: The ideal formula predicts Vₒᵤₜ = −(R_f/Rᵢₙ)·Vᵢₙ = −10 × 1.0 = −10 V. But no op-amp can output more than its own supply rails allow, and most parts saturate a volt or more short of the rail itself — so instead of reaching −10 V, the output clips at whatever its actual negative saturation voltage is (commonly a volt or two above −5 V for an ordinary, non-rail-to-rail part). This is exactly Chapter 1's headroom limit again, reached here because the demanded gain × input product outran what the supply rails can physically provide, rather than because a small AC signal grew too large around a fixed bias point.
H2: 5.5 The Follower Is a Wire
P: The voltage follower, or unity-gain buffer, has no resistor in the loop at all. The output goes straight to the minus pin, and the signal is on the plus pin. That is the only configuration in which the feedback loop is a pure short. The virtual short forces the output to equal the plus input, so the gain is exactly one. The virtual open means the source sees essentially infinite impedance. The output impedance is near zero, so the stage can drive a heavy load without loading that source. That is the whole point: a high-impedance signal is buffered so the next stage does not drag it down. Because the noise gain is one, the bandwidth is the op-amp's full gain-bandwidth product, the widest bandwidth any closed-loop configuration gets.
P: The inverting buffer is a different circuit, not a follower with a minus sign. The plus pin is grounded. An input resistor runs from the signal to the minus pin, and a feedback resistor runs from the output back to the minus pin. The loop still closes, but it closes through those two resistors. Equal resistors give a gain of minus one. The source sees only the input resistor, not an open circuit. Use the follower when the source cannot stand a load. Use the inverting buffer when you need the sign flip and can afford that resistor as the load.
H2: 5.6 The Bode Limit, and a Declared Picture
P: The virtual short is exact only at DC. Open-loop gain falls at about twenty decibels per decade and crosses unity at the gain-bandwidth product. Closed-loop bandwidth is that product divided by the noise gain. For a non-inverting stage, noise gain and signal gain are the same number. For an inverter, the signal gain is minus Rf/Rin, but the noise gain is one plus Rf/Rin.
P: Declared picture, a general-purpose shape: DC gain 100,000 (100 dB), gain-bandwidth product 1 MHz. The dominant pole then sits at 10 Hz. A noise gain of 100 leaves 10 kHz of closed-loop bandwidth. Phase margin is how far the phase still is from minus 180 degrees where loop gain crosses unity. About 60 degrees is comfortable. About 30 degrees rings. Less than that oscillates. Treat those three numbers as a design rule of thumb, not as a theorem.
H2: 5.7 A Comparator Is Not a Bad Op-Amp
P: A comparator compares its inputs without the negative-feedback loop that keeps an op-amp in its linear region. The virtual-short rule therefore does not apply; the inputs may differ enough to drive the output state. The high-impedance-input approximation still needs the comparator's input-current and common-mode limits. Its output changes state according to which input is higher, after the specified propagation delay. An open-collector output, as on the LM311, can pull low but cannot drive high by itself; an external pull-up sets the high level and may use another supply within the part's ratings.
H2: 5.8 Bias Current, Offset, and the Resistor's Own Noise
P: Bipolar inputs draw nanoampere bias currents. CMOS inputs are essentially open gates. JFET sits between them. The output stage is a separate choice. Offset is what remains when the inputs are tied together. Old parts trim it on null pins. New parts laser-trim or chop.
P: Bias-current compensation puts a resistor in series with the plus input equal to the parallel combination of the two resistors on the minus side, so the DC drops match. It does not cancel that resistor's thermal noise. Johnson density is the square root of 4 k T R. Declared: k = 1.380649×10^−23 J/K, T = 290 K, R = 100 kΩ. Then √(4 k T R) = 40.0 nV/√Hz, which is 40 nV/√Hz for this book. That is why those resistors stay as small as the bias current allows. Bipolar wants them small. CMOS does not.
P: Key idea. Section 5.1 gave the two rules. This section is where they stop being exact, and where a comparator refuses to be an amplifier.
BENCH: Check the input common-mode range and output swing before anything else. Many op-amps, particularly older ones, can't get within a volt or two of their rails; a design that is fine on paper can clip or misbehave on a single 3.3 V supply. 'Rail-to-rail' on the front page is worth confirming in the specification tables, at your load current.
