H1: Chapter 9 — Inductors
P: An inductor in a switching regulator can be perfectly fine at 2 A and nearly useless at 2.5 A. Nothing burns, nothing smells; the core saturates and the inductance quietly collapses. This chapter is about the numbers that tell you where that cliff is.
P: Where you meet this: Buck and boost converters, EMI filters, ferrite beads on supply lines, and RF matching networks.
P: Book 1 already derived inductance from Faraday and Ampere: voltage is L di/dt, and a long solenoid is μ0 N² A / ℓ. This chapter does not derive that again. It sizes a catalog part. Declared air solenoid, 100 turns, 20 mm long, 1 cm² of area: L = 62.8 µH. Wound on a closed ferrite core of relative permeability 1000 that carries the whole flux path, and still linear, it would be up to a thousand times that, 62.8 mH; an open rod core gains far less, because most of the flux path is still air. Saturation current is where the core stops being linear. Self-resonant frequency is where winding capacitance takes over and the part stops acting like an inductor. Line-frequency iron, switch-mode ferrite, pulse transformers, and RF baluns are four shelves in Book 6. They are not this chapter.
P: Book 1's Chapter 10 introduced the solenoid and the effect a ferromagnetic core has on its field, and Chapter 36 introduced the ideal inductor's role in a resonant circuit. This chapter, the last of the three component chapters, treats the inductor the way the previous two chapters treated the resistor and capacitor: as a real component with real, specified limitations.
H2: 9.1 Real Inductor Types
P: An air-core inductor — a coil with no core material at all — is simple, perfectly linear regardless of current, and immune to the core-saturation effects of Section 9.2, but achieves relatively little inductance for its size. A ferrite- or iron-core inductor, exactly as Book 1's Chapter 10 described, multiplies the inductance dramatically for the same coil by concentrating the magnetic field in a high-permeability material, at the cost of the saturation behavior that material introduces. A toroidal (ring-shaped) core goes further still, closing the magnetic path on itself so that essentially all of the field stays confined within the core — both increasing inductance per turn beyond an equivalent open rod core and sharply reducing the stray field that would otherwise radiate outward as electromagnetic interference.
H2: 9.2 Core Saturation and Losses
P: A ferromagnetic core's enormous permeability comes from its internal magnetic domains aligning with the applied field; once current is high enough that essentially all of those domains have aligned, the core is saturated, and further increases in current no longer produce a proportional increase in flux. An inductor driven into saturation loses most of its inductance abruptly, right when a circuit's design may most depend on it — a large surge current, for instance, is exactly the situation this failure mode tends to appear in. Beyond saturation, a real core also dissipates a small amount of energy on every cycle as heat, through hysteresis losses (the core's magnetic domains lagging the applied field) and eddy-current losses (small circulating currents induced within the core material itself).
MIS: Core saturation is not a sudden, single threshold so much as it looks like one in practice: inductance falls off increasingly sharply as current approaches the saturation region, so a design with even a small margin below the manufacturer's rated saturation current can behave very differently from one pushed right up against it.
WE: Worked Example 9.1
PROB: An inductor is rated for a saturation current of 3.0 A. In a circuit, it carries a DC bias current of 2.0 A plus an AC ripple with a peak amplitude of 0.8 A. Find the peak instantaneous current and determine whether the inductor risks saturating.
SOL: Peak current = DC bias + AC peak = 2.0 + 0.8 = 2.8 A. This is below the 3.0 A rated saturation current, so the inductor does not saturate — but the margin is only 0.2 A, about 7% of the rating, which is tight enough that a designer would normally choose a higher-Isat part or reduce the ripple rather than rely on so little headroom.
H2: 9.3 DCR, Quality Factor, and Self-Resonance
P: A real inductor's winding has some DC resistance (DCR) — the wire itself is not a perfect conductor — which Book 1's ideal inductor did not include. The quality factor, already introduced in Book 1's Chapter 36 for a resonant circuit as a whole, applies equally to a single real inductor considered on its own:
EQ: Q = ωL / R
P: where R here is the inductor's own DCR (or, more precisely, its total effective series resistance at the frequency of interest, which rises somewhat with frequency due to skin effect). A high-Q inductor stores and returns energy efficiently; a low-Q inductor dissipates a significant fraction of it as heat on every cycle. Exactly as with the capacitor in Chapter 8, a real inductor also has a self-resonant frequency, set this time by its own winding capacitance resonating with its intended inductance, above which it stops behaving like an inductor at all.
WE: Worked Example 9.2
PROB: An inductor has L = 10 µH and a DC resistance of 0.5 Ω, operating at 1 MHz. Find its quality factor.
SOL: ω = 2πf = 2π × 1×10⁶ ≈ 6.283×10⁶ rad/s. ωL = 6.283×10⁶ × 10×10⁻⁶ ≈ 62.83. Q = ωL/R = 62.83/0.5 ≈ 125.7 — a high-Q inductor, storing energy far more efficiently than it dissipates it at this frequency.

H2: 9.4 Inductors in Practice: Reading the Datasheet
P: Sections 9.1 to 9.3 introduced the real inductor: its core can saturate, its winding has resistance, and its own capacitance gives it a self-resonant frequency. The rest of this chapter takes the next step a designer actually faces, which is choosing one particular part from a catalog of thousands. The physics of inductance stays in Book 1, and the design of the converters that use power inductors belongs to Book 6. What lives here is the datasheet: what each number means, which current waveform each one must be checked against, and which numbers are easiest to misread.
H2: 9.5 The Columns of a Power-Inductor Table
P: A power-inductor datasheet usually gives, for each part number, an inductance value with its tolerance, a DC resistance, two current ratings, and often a self-resonant frequency. The inductance is measured at a small test signal, commonly 100 kHz and 0.1 V or 1 V, with no DC current flowing. Tolerances of ±20% are normal for power parts, and ±30% is not unusual; precision is not what these parts are for.
P: The two current ratings are the ones most often confused. The saturation current I_sat is the DC current at which the inductance has fallen by a stated amount, commonly 20% or 30% below its zero-current value. It is a magnetic limit, set by the core, and it is checked against the peak current the part will ever carry. The heating current, often labeled I_rms or I_temp, is the current that raises the part's temperature by a stated amount, commonly 40 °C, through losses in its winding. It is a thermal limit, set mostly by the DC resistance, and it is checked against the rms current.
MIS: An inductor's two current ratings do not describe the same thing, and neither is a "maximum current." A part can run comfortably below its heating current while its peak current drives it into saturation on every cycle, or it can sit well below saturation while overheating. Both checks are required, each against its own measure of the current.
H2: 9.6 The Current Waveform the Part Must Survive
P: An inductor in a switching circuit carries a DC current with a triangular ripple on top of it. Section 9.2 checked a peak against I_sat; here the ripple comes from the circuit. In a buck regulator, whose full design is the subject of Book 6, the ripple's peak-to-peak value is set by the voltage across the inductor while the switch is on and the time it is on:
EQ: ΔI = (V_in − V_out) · D / (L · f_sw),   D = V_out / V_in (ideal)
P: The peak current is the DC current plus half the ripple. The rms current of a triangle riding on a DC level is only slightly above the DC value:
EQ: I_pk = I_DC + ΔI/2,    I_rms = √(I_DC² + ΔI²/12)
WE: Worked Example 9.3
PROB: A buck regulator converts 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A, switching at 500 kHz, with a 10 µH inductor. Find the ripple, the peak current, and the rms current, and state the minimum I_sat and heating-current ratings, allowing 20% margin on I_sat.
SOL: The ideal duty cycle is D = 3.3/12 = 0.275. The ripple is ΔI = (12 − 3.3) × 0.275/(10×10⁻⁶ × 500×10³) = 2.39/5 ≈ 0.479 A peak-to-peak. The peak current is 2 + 0.24 ≈ 2.24 A. The rms current is √(2² + 0.479²/12) ≈ 2.005 A, essentially the DC value. With 20% margin, I_sat should be at least 1.2 × 2.24 ≈ 2.7 A. The heating current should be at least 2 A, and more if the board around the inductor is already warm. Note also that the 10 µH could be as low as 8 µH at its −20% tolerance, which would raise the ripple to about 0.60 A and the peak to about 2.30 A; a careful selection checks the low-tolerance corner as well.
H2: 9.7 DC Resistance and Copper Loss
P: The winding loss is the rms current squared times the resistance. At the frequencies of a typical regulator, skin and proximity effects raise the winding's effective resistance for the ripple component, but because the ripple is small compared with the DC current, the DC resistance gives a good first estimate. Core loss, from the ripple's alternating flux in the ferrite, adds to it and is usually given by the manufacturer as a curve or an online calculator rather than as a single number.
WE: Worked Example 9.4
PROB: The inductor chosen for Worked Example 9.3 has a DC resistance of 30 mΩ. Estimate its copper loss and its share of a 6.6 W output.
SOL: P = I_rms² × DCR = 2.005² × 0.030 ≈ 0.121 W. That is about 1.8% of the 6.6 W output, a significant part of a regulator's total losses. A part with half the DCR is usually physically larger or has lower inductance; trading among size, loss, and ripple is the real substance of choosing a power inductor.
H2: 9.8 Soft and Hard Saturation
P: Not all cores saturate the same way. A ferrite core with a small air gap holds its inductance nearly flat up to I_sat and then falls quickly; it is said to saturate hard. A powdered-iron or metal-composite core, in which the gap is distributed through the material itself, loses inductance gradually from low currents onward; it saturates soft. A hard-saturating part must never see its peak exceed I_sat, because slightly beyond it the ripple grows rapidly, which raises the peak further. A soft-saturating part tolerates brief overloads gracefully but delivers less than its nominal inductance at its rated current.

H2: 9.9 Shielded or Unshielded
P: An unshielded drum-core inductor leaves part of its magnetic field outside the part, where it can couple into nearby traces, other inductors, or a sensitive feedback node. A semi-shielded part adds a magnetic coating, and a fully shielded part encloses the winding in a closed magnetic path. Shielded parts cost a little more and, for the same size, have somewhat lower inductance or current ratings, but in a dense board the reduction in coupled interference usually decides the question. Section 9.1's toroid is the same idea in its oldest form.
H2: 9.10 Ferrite Beads Are Not Small Inductors
P: A ferrite bead looks like a chip inductor and is placed in series with a supply or signal line, but it is designed to be lossy. Its datasheet gives its impedance at 100 MHz, perhaps 600 Ω, and a plot of impedance versus frequency in which a resistive part dominates above some tens of megahertz. A bead therefore absorbs high-frequency noise as heat, rather than reflecting it as an inductor would. Two cautions apply. A bead's impedance falls sharply with DC current, often to a fraction of its rated value at half its current rating. And a bead in series with a supply, followed by a decoupling capacitor, forms an LC circuit at low frequency where the bead is mostly inductive; that circuit can resonate and amplify noise near its resonance instead of removing it.
H2: 9.11 Inductors in Signal Filters
P: When an inductor is used in a filter or resonant circuit rather than a regulator, the important columns change. The quality factor Q at the operating frequency, introduced in Section 9.3, sets the loss and the sharpness of the response. The self-resonant frequency should sit well above the highest frequency the filter must handle, commonly by a factor of ten, because the inductance effectively rises as the SRF is approached. Tolerance matters again: a 5% or 2% part is chosen when the filter's corner frequency must be accurate.
KEY: Check peak current against I_sat and rms current against the heating rating, at the low-tolerance corner of the inductance. Then decide among DCR, size, shielding, and saturation style; those trade-offs, not the inductance value, are what distinguish one catalog part from another.
BENCH: Size a power inductor to the peak current, not the average: the DC load current plus half the peak-to-peak ripple, plus margin for start-up and load steps. Compare that with the saturation current and the heating (RMS) current separately, since datasheets often quote them under different definitions (for example a 20% or 30% inductance drop). And remember that a ferrite bead is mostly a resistor at high frequency, not an inductor.
