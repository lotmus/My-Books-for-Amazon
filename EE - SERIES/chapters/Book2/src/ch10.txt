H1: Chapter 10 — The Loop Under the Model
P: A thermostat clicking a furnace on and off and a robot arm reaching for a cup look like different worlds. Underneath, both run the same small, unglamorous loop: measure, compare, correct, repeat.
P: Where you meet this: Thermostats, refrigerators, motor speed control, voltage regulators, drone stabilization, and robot joints.
P: Book 1 built the sensing layer of a smart thermostat in its Chapter 27: a thermistor in a Wheatstone bridge, a set-point potentiometer, and a comparator that turns a few tens of millivolts into a clean on or off. That circuit measures and compares. This chapter closes the loop: it adds the correction, follows the temperature around the loop over time, and then moves the same loop onto a robot joint. Chapters 17 to 19 give the loop its full theory; this chapter gives it a shape and a sense of speed.

H2: 10.1 The Four Parts of Every Loop
P: Every loop in this book has the same four parts. The reference is what you want: 21 °C, or a joint angle of 30°. The sensor reports what you have. The controller compares the two and decides on a command. The plant is the physical thing the command acts on: a room and its heater, or a motor and its arm. The difference between reference and measurement is the error, and every controller in this book is a rule for turning error into command.
H2: 10.2 The Thermostat Loop: On, Off, and Hysteresis
P: The simplest controller is a switch. When the room is too cold, the heater turns fully on; when it is warm enough, fully off. This is bang-bang control, and with a single threshold it fails at once: noise on the sensor flicks the comparator across the threshold many times a second, and the relay chatters. The cure is the hysteresis of Chapter 16. Two thresholds, say 20.5 °C to turn the heater on and 21.5 °C to turn it off, give a band in which nothing changes, and the room temperature rides up and down a sawtooth between them (Figure 10.2).
P: The band sets the trade. A wide band means slow cycling, long relay and burner life, and a room that swings by the full band. A narrow band holds the temperature tighter and cycles the heater more often. Furnaces, boilers, and refrigerator compressors all enforce a minimum on time and off time for this reason: starting is where they wear.
FIG: fig10_2.png | Figure 10.2 — A bang-bang thermostat with a 1 °C band. The room warms at 0.5 °C/min with the heater on and cools at 0.25 °C/min with it off, so the heater runs 2 minutes in every 6 (Worked Example 10.1).
WE: Worked Example 10.1
PROB: A room warms at 0.5 °C per minute with its 1.5 kW heater on and cools at 0.25 °C per minute with it off. The thermostat turns the heater on at 20.5 °C and off at 21.5 °C. Find the on time, the off time, the cycle period, the duty cycle, and the average heater power.
SOL: The band is 1.0 °C. Rising through it at 0.5 °C/min takes 1.0/0.5 = 2 min; falling through it at 0.25 °C/min takes 1.0/0.25 = 4 min. The cycle is 2 + 4 = 6 min, ten cycles an hour, and the duty cycle is 2/6 = 33%. The average power is 0.333 × 1.5 kW = 0.5 kW, which is also the heat the room loses at this temperature: in the steady state the heater's average power matches the loss. Check: halving the band to 0.5 °C halves both times, giving 20 cycles an hour with the same 33% duty and the same 0.5 kW.
P: The analog-first rule of Book 1's reader lab applies directly. The bridge, a comparator with a resistor of positive feedback, and the relay driver of Section 24.6 make a complete thermostat with no software at all. A smart thermostat adds what code does well: schedules, a display, Wi-Fi, a log, and learning when the house is empty. The loop underneath is the same, and Book 8 is where its firmware lives.
KEY: A bang-bang loop with hysteresis never settles; it cycles. The band sets the swing and the cycling rate, and the heater's average power, not its rating, equals the heat the plant loses.
H2: 10.3 When On and Off Is Not Enough
P: On-off control is right for a room, which is slow and forgiving. It is wrong for a motor speed, a power supply, or a joint, where the sawtooth would be a vibration. Those need a command that varies smoothly with the error. The simplest is proportional control: command = K_p × error. Proportional control has a catch, which Chapter 17 derives. If the plant needs a steady command to hold its output, a steady command needs a steady error, so the output settles short of the reference. A thermostat with proportional control and a heat loss to feed holds the room a little cold forever. An integrator removes that error, and Chapter 18 shows how to tune the result; many smart thermostats run this proportional-integral (PI) law, often as a slow PWM of the heater relay or as a modulation command to a boiler.
WE: Worked Example 10.2
PROB: A room loses 100 W for every °C it sits above the 5 °C outdoor temperature. A proportional controller commands heater power P = K_p(21 °C − T) with K_p = 1000 W/°C, and the heater can deliver up to 2 kW. Find the steady room temperature, the error, and the heater power.
SOL: In the steady state the heater supplies exactly the loss: K_p(21 − T) = 100(T − 5). Writing e = 21 − T, the right side is 100(16 − e), so 1000e = 1600 − 100e, 1100e = 1600, and e = 1.45 °C. The room settles at 21 − 1.45 = 19.55 °C, and the heater delivers 1000 × 1.45 = 1455 W, inside its 2 kW limit. Check: the loss at 19.55 °C is 100 × 14.55 = 1455 W. Raising K_p tenfold cuts the error to about 0.16 °C but makes the loop far more sensitive to sensor noise and delay; an integrator removes the error at any K_p.
H2: 10.4 The Robot Joint: The Same Loop, Hundreds of Times a Second
P: A robot arm reaching for a cup looks like intelligence. Underneath each joint is the thermostat's loop, running far faster: an encoder measures the angle, a controller compares it with the commanded angle, and a motor driver (Chapter 24) applies the correction. A joint tracker at 200 Hz has a period of 1/200 s, which is 5 ms. Five milliseconds is slow for a switching regulator and fast for a language model. It is the right speed for a joint that must not wait on a data center.
P: Book 7 describes the layers of a modern robot: a general model that interprets the scene and the request, a task planner, a motion tracker, and the mechanism. This book owns the tracker, the loop at the bottom. The model proposes where the arm should go; the tracker and an independent safety controller decide how it gets there, and when the two disagree, the safety controller is the one allowed to move the joint. Hobby robots and RC models have the same layers in miniature: a radio receiver or a small computer proposes, and a servo's own internal loop (Section 24.7) disposes.
WE: Worked Example 10.3
PROB: A joint loop samples at 1 kHz on a microcontroller and applies each new command one sample after it reads the encoder. Using the delay rule of Chapter 19 (half a period for the hold plus one period of compute), find the total delay and the phase it costs at a 20 Hz crossover.
SOL: T = 1 ms, so the delay is 0.5 + 1.0 = 1.5 ms. At 20 Hz, ω_c = 2π × 20 = 125.7 rad/s, and the phase cost is ω_c × delay = 125.7 × 0.0015 = 0.189 rad, or 10.8°. That is a noticeable bite out of a typical 45° to 60° phase margin, and it is why joint loops run at 1 kHz or faster even though the arm itself moves at a few hertz. Check: at 200 Hz sampling the same rule gives 7.5 ms and 54°, which would leave almost no margin at a 20 Hz crossover.
KEY: Measure, compare, correct, repeat. A thermostat and a robot joint differ in speed by a factor of hundreds of thousands, not in kind, and the sample rate must sit well above the speed the loop is asked to control.
BENCH: Build the analog thermostat first: the Book 1 bridge, an LM393 comparator with a 1 MΩ resistor from its output back to its non-inverting input for hysteresis, a pull-up on the open-collector output, and an LED in place of the heater. Warm the thermistor between your fingers and watch the LED switch once, cleanly, at each threshold. Then remove the feedback resistor and watch the LED flicker as the temperature crawls through the single threshold. That flicker is the reason Chapter 16 exists.
