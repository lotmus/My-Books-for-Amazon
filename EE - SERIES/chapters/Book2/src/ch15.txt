H1: Chapter 15 — DC Errors: Offset, Bias Current, and Drift
P: A thermocouple produces about 41 µV per degree. Feed it to an op-amp with 2 mV of input offset and you have a thermometer that is wrong by fifty degrees before you have even switched it on. DC errors are small numbers that matter enormously at the bottom of the scale.
P: Where you meet this: Thermocouple and strain-gauge amplifiers, precision current sensing, integrators, and any measurement near zero.
P: Ground the input of a high-gain amplifier and measure its output with a good voltmeter. It will not read zero. It may read a few millivolts, or a few hundred, and the reading will creep as the board warms up. None of this is noise in the sense of Chapter 14; it is a steady error that would be there on every measurement, and it can be budgeted, predicted, and in many cases removed. This chapter builds that budget from three datasheet numbers: input offset voltage, input bias current, and their temperature drift.
H2: 15.1 Input Offset Voltage
P: The two input transistors of an op-amp are never perfectly matched. The result behaves exactly as if a small DC voltage source, the input offset voltage V_os, were connected in series with one input of an otherwise perfect op-amp. Because it sits at the input, it is amplified by the noise gain from Chapter 13, not by the signal gain:
EQ: V_out,os = V_os × NG = V_os × (1 + R_f / Rᵢₙ)
P: General-purpose bipolar op-amps have V_os in the range of 1 to 5 mV. Precision bipolar parts reach tens of microvolts. Zero-drift parts, which continually measure and cancel their own offset, reach a few microvolts.
WE: Worked Example 15.1
PROB: An inverting amplifier with gain −100 uses R_f = 1 MΩ and Rᵢₙ = 10 kΩ. The op-amp has V_os = 2 mV. Find the output error with the input grounded.
SOL: The noise gain is 1 + 1M/10k = 101. The output error is 2 mV × 101 = 202 mV. Against a full-scale output of perhaps 10 V, that is 2% of full scale before a signal has been applied, far larger than any gain error from Chapter 11.
H2: 15.2 Input Bias Current and Offset Current
P: Bipolar input transistors need base current to operate, and even FET inputs leak a little through their protection structures. This input bias current I_b flows into (or out of) both inputs. At the inverting input it has only one path, through the resistors connected there, and because the inverting node is held near ground, the current flows through R_f and appears at the output as a voltage I_b R_f.
P: The standard compensation adds a resistor R_c in series with the non-inverting input, equal to the parallel combination R_f ‖ Rᵢₙ. The bias current at that pin then produces a matching drop, and the two errors cancel to the extent that the two bias currents are equal. What remains is set by their difference, the input offset current I_os, which is typically a fraction of I_b:
EQ: Uncompensated: V_out,b ≈ I_b R_f    Compensated: V_out,b ≈ I_os R_f
WE: Worked Example 15.2
PROB: The amplifier of Worked Example 15.1 uses an op-amp with I_b = 80 nA and I_os = 20 nA. Find the output error from bias current without compensation, the value of R_c, and the error with compensation.
SOL: Without compensation, 80 nA × 1 MΩ = 80 mV. The compensation resistor is R_c = 1M ‖ 10k ≈ 9.90 kΩ, and a standard 9.76 kΩ or 10 kΩ part is close enough. With it, the error falls to I_os R_f = 20 nA × 1 MΩ = 20 mV. Chapter 5 pointed out the price: R_c adds its own Johnson noise, about 12.6 nV/√Hz at 290 K, which a low-noise design may not be able to afford. A bypass capacitor across R_c removes that noise at signal frequencies while keeping the DC correction.
H2: 15.3 Drift and the Worst-Case Budget
P: Both V_os and I_b change with temperature, and an initial offset can be trimmed out at the factory, while drift cannot. Datasheets give the offset drift as a coefficient in µV/°C, and bias current often roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in a FET-input part. A worst-case DC budget adds all contributions with their signs aligned, because nothing guarantees they will cancel:
EQ: V_out,err = NG × (V_os + ΔT · dV_os/dT) + I_os R_f + (other terms)
WE: Worked Example 15.3
PROB: The op-amp of Worked Example 15.1 has an offset drift of 10 µV/°C. The board operates from 25 °C to 75 °C. Assuming the initial 2 mV offset has been trimmed out at 25 °C and bias-current compensation is used, estimate the worst-case output error at 75 °C, ignoring the drift of I_os.
SOL: The offset drifts by 10 µV/°C × 50 °C = 0.5 mV, which appears at the output as 0.5 mV × 101 ≈ 50.5 mV. The compensated bias-current term adds 20 mV. The worst-case total is about 70.5 mV. Trimming removed the larger initial term, but drift and offset current together still produce about 0.7% of a 10 V full scale.

H2: 15.4 Supply and Common-Mode Terms
P: Two more specifications behave like extra offset. The power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR) states how much a change in supply voltage shifts the input offset; 80 dB means 100 mV of supply ripple acts like 10 µV at the input. The common-mode rejection ratio from Chapter 12 does the same for common-mode voltage. Both terms are usually small in an inverting stage and should be checked in a follower or a stage running from a noisy supply.
H2: 15.5 The Integrator Is a DC-Error Magnifier
P: An ideal integrator, a capacitor C in the feedback path and a resistor R at the input, has infinite gain at DC. Its noise gain at DC is therefore infinite too, and any offset or bias current does not produce a fixed error; it produces a ramp.
WE: Worked Example 15.4
PROB: An integrator uses R = 100 kΩ and C = 1 µF with an op-amp having V_os = 1 mV and I_b = 10 nA. With the input grounded, how fast does the output drift, and how long until it reaches a 10 V rail?
SOL: The offset forces a current V_os/R = 1 mV/100 kΩ = 10 nA through R, which must flow through C. The bias current adds another 10 nA in the worst-case direction. The capacitor charges at I/C = 20 nA/1 µF = 0.02 V/s. The output reaches 10 V in 10/0.02 = 500 s, a little over eight minutes. A practical integrator therefore needs one of three things: a reset switch across C, a large resistor across C that gives it a finite DC gain, or an outer feedback loop, such as the control loops of Chapter 17, that keeps its input centered.
KEY: An offset is amplified by the noise gain, and a bias current is converted to voltage by the feedback resistor. Trim the initial value if you must, but budget the drift, because drift is what remains in the field.
MIS: A zero-drift op-amp does not make the whole circuit drift-free. Its own V_os may be a microvolt, but resistor ratios, thermocouple voltages at dissimilar-metal junctions on the board, and leakage across a dirty board surface can each add tens of microvolts. In precision work the op-amp is often no longer the dominant DC error once it has been chosen well.
H2: 15.6 Removing What Cannot Be Budgeted
P: When a signal carries no information at DC, AC coupling removes every DC error at once: a series capacitor and a resistor to ground form a high-pass filter whose corner sits below the lowest frequency of interest. When DC matters, the remaining choices are a better op-amp, smaller resistors where bias current dominates, a trim performed at calibration, or a chopper or auto-zero technique that measures the error periodically and subtracts it. Each choice trades cost, noise, or bandwidth; the budget tells you which term to attack first.
H2: 15.7 Reading an Op-Amp Datasheet
P: A datasheet is a contract with fine print. Its first table, the absolute maximum ratings, says what the part survives, not what it does: a part held at an absolute maximum is outside its specifications. The electrical characteristics tables say what it does, and every line holds only under the test conditions printed above the table and beside the line. The three columns are not equal. A minimum or maximum is guaranteed, by test or by design and characterization. A typical value is the middle of a distribution, and the curves that follow the tables are typical too: Microchip's own note on its curves says they are a statistical summary of a limited number of samples and are not tested or guaranteed.
P: Here is the MCP6002 of the reader lab, read line by line from the Microchip MCP6001/2/4 datasheet (DS20001733L). The common test conditions are T_A = 25 °C, V_DD = 1.8 V to 5.5 V, V_CM = V_DD/2, R_L = 10 kΩ to V_DD/2, and, for the AC lines, C_L = 60 pF. Each line names the part of this book that uses it.
LI: Input offset voltage V_OS: ±4.5 mV maximum at V_CM = V_SS. There is no typical value in the table, only a histogram among the curves. Section 15.1.
LI: Offset drift: ±2.0 µV/°C typical from −40 °C to +125 °C, with no maximum, so a worst-case budget (Section 15.3) needs margin or a measurement.
LI: Input bias current I_B: ±1 pA typical at 25 °C, 19 pA at 85 °C, and 1100 pA at 125 °C. A CMOS input's bias current is the leakage of its protection diodes, and it rises more than a thousandfold over that range. Input offset current: ±1 pA typical. Section 15.2.
LI: Input impedance: 10¹³ Ω in parallel with 6 pF common-mode and 3 pF differential. The capacitances set the noise gain of the photodiode amplifier of Section 13.7.
LI: Common-mode input range: V_SS − 0.3 V to V_DD + 0.3 V. The application section adds the fine print: two input pairs hand over near V_CM = V_DD − 1.1 V, and the offset changes there.
LI: CMRR: 60 dB minimum, 76 dB typical, for V_CM from −0.3 V to 5.3 V at V_DD = 5 V. PSRR: 86 dB typical. Section 15.4.
LI: Open-loop gain A_OL: 88 dB minimum, 112 dB typical, measured with the output between 0.3 V and V_DD − 0.3 V. Chapter 11.
LI: Output swing: within 25 mV of each rail at V_DD = 5.5 V with R_L = 10 kΩ; a curve shows the headroom growing with output current. Short-circuit current: ±6 mA at 1.8 V and ±23 mA at 5.5 V. Chapter 1.
LI: Gain-bandwidth product 1.0 MHz typical; phase margin 90° typical at a gain of +1 with 60 pF, and, on the front page, 45° with 500 pF. Chapter 13 and Section 12.4.
LI: Slew rate 0.6 V/µs typical. Section 13.5.
LI: Noise: 28 nV/√Hz at 1 kHz, 6.1 µV peak to peak from 0.1 Hz to 10 Hz, and 0.6 fA/√Hz of current noise. Chapter 14.
LI: Quiescent current: 100 µA typical, 50 µA to 170 µA, per amplifier.
P: Three habits follow. Design with the minimum and maximum, and treat typical as the likely case, not the promise. Check that the test conditions match your circuit: the CMRR above is specified over a 5.6 V common-mode span, and A_OL with the output away from the rails. And where a line has only a typical value, budget margin or measure it. Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, shows how each of these lines is measured on the bench.
WE: Worked Example 15.5
PROB: An MCP6002 follower buffers a sensor whose output spans 0.3 V to 4.7 V on a 5 V supply, into a 12-bit converter with a 5 V reference. Using the datasheet limits, find the worst-case input error from offset and common-mode rejection, in LSB, and compare with the typical CMRR.
SOL: The offset is at most 4.5 mV. The common-mode voltage moves by 4.7 − 0.3 = 4.4 V, and a CMRR of 60 dB is a ratio of 1000, so the offset can change by 4.4 V/1000 = 4.4 mV across the span. Worst case: 4.5 + 4.4 = 8.9 mV. One LSB is 5 V/4096 = 1.22 mV, so the error is 7.3 LSB. With the typical 76 dB (a ratio of 6310) the CMRR term is only 0.70 mV, six times smaller: the guarantee and the typical part are far apart. The span also crosses V_DD − 1.1 V = 3.9 V, where the input stages hand over and the offset steps. A one-point calibration (Section 15.6) removes the fixed offset; it does not remove the CMRR term or the step.
H2: 15.8 From Datasheet to SPICE: the Macromodel
P: Nearly every op-amp has a vendor SPICE model, and every vendor warns, as Microchip does in this datasheet, that the model is an initial design tool whose results must be checked against the datasheet. The model is not the chip's hundreds of transistors. It is a macromodel: a small circuit of ideal sources, resistors, and capacitors whose values are chosen so that its behavior at the pins reproduces the datasheet. The idea goes back to Boyle, Cohn, Pederson, and Solomon in 1974, who built one from a handful of transistors and controlled sources; modern vendor models are mostly behavioral, but every element value is still computed from a datasheet line (Figure 15.2).
FIG: fig15_2.png | Figure 15.2 — The blocks of an op-amp macromodel. Each element is set from one datasheet line, at that line's test conditions; the values are fitted, not measured inside the chip. | Four blocks: input (offset, bias currents, noise, CMRR and PSRR), gain stage (transconductance with slew limit into R1 parallel C1), output (clamps and output impedance), and supply current.
P: The mapping from datasheet line to model element:
LI: V_OS: a DC voltage source in series with one input. A model of the typical part often sets it to zero, so the simulation shows no offset unless you add the maximum yourself.
LI: I_B and I_OS: current sources at the inputs. Input impedance: resistors and capacitors at the inputs.
LI: CMRR and PSRR: controlled sources that add V_CM/CMRR and the supply change divided by PSRR to the input.
LI: A_OL and GBW: a transconductance g_m driving R₁ in parallel with C₁, so that A₀ = g_mR₁, the dominant pole is f_p = 1/(2πR₁C₁), and GBW = g_m/(2πC₁).
LI: Slew rate: a limit on the transconductor's current, I_max = SR × C₁, because C₁ can charge no faster than I_max/C₁.
LI: Phase margin and capacitive-load behavior: further poles, or an output impedance that forms a pole with the load.
LI: Output swing and short-circuit current: clamps and current limits at the output. Noise: voltage and current noise sources. Quiescent current: a current source between the supply pins.
WE: Worked Example 15.6
PROB: Build the gain stage of a macromodel from the MCP6002's typical A_OL = 112 dB, GBW = 1 MHz, and SR = 0.6 V/µs, using g_m = 1 mS. Then choose the output resistance R_o so that the phase margin with 500 pF is 45°, as the datasheet states.
SOL: A₀ = 10^(112/20) = 398,000, so R₁ = A₀/g_m = 398 MΩ. C₁ = g_m/(2π × GBW) = 1 mS/(2π × 1 MHz) = 159 pF, which puts the dominant pole at f_p = GBW/A₀ = 2.5 Hz. I_max = SR × C₁ = 0.6 V/µs × 159 pF = 95.5 µA. With the dominant pole alone the loop phase is −90° near crossover; R_o and C_L add a pole at f_o = 1/(2πR_oC_L). For 45° of margin that pole must sit at the crossover, and there the loop magnitude is (GBW/f)/√2 = 1, so the crossover falls to GBW/√2 = 707 kHz. R_o = 1/(2π × 707 kHz × 500 pF) = 450 Ω. The choice of g_m was arbitrary; any value gives the same behavior at the pins, which is a reminder that these elements describe the datasheet, not the silicon.
P: The complete model, written for ngspice 44 and run there, is below, followed by the deck that measures its open-loop response. The names follow the blocks of Figure 15.2; the parameters in the first line are the fitted values. The output resistance is shorted at DC by L_o, because 450 Ω is a fit to the 500 pF phase margin, not a real DC output resistance, and a real 450 Ω would contradict the 25 mV output-swing line.

P: * Fitted to the TYPICAL values of the Microchip MCP6001/2/4 datasheet (DS20001733L):

P: Run in ngspice, the model reproduces what it was fitted to (Figure 15.3): A_OL = 112.0 dB, a unity-gain crossover of 947 kHz (the 10 kΩ load pulls it slightly below 1 MHz), 46° of phase margin with 500 pF, an output swing to 25 mV from each rail, and 28.0 nV/√Hz at 1 kHz. Its slew rate comes out at 0.58 V/µs, because the smooth limit rounds the corners of the step. It also predicts 81° of phase margin with 60 pF, where the datasheet says 90°. One output resistance cannot satisfy both load conditions, because the real output stage is not a resistor; the fit is right where it was made and approximate elsewhere.

KEY: A SPICE model is not the physical truth. It is a best fit of a few parameters to the model's equations, and the equations are approximations. The parameters are fitting constants, valid only over the conditions they were fitted to. Inside that range a good model is excellent; outside it, it is confidently wrong, and nothing in the simulation warns you.
P: This model knows nothing it was not fitted to. Outside its range:
LI: Low-frequency noise: it has no 1/f noise, so from 0.1 Hz to 10 Hz it integrates to 88 nV rms, about 0.58 µV peak to peak, ten times less than the datasheet's 6.1 µV.
LI: Temperature: its bias current is 1 pA at every temperature; the datasheet gives 1100 pA at 125 °C.
LI: Common-mode range: it has no input-stage handover near V_DD − 1.1 V, so it shows no offset step there.
LI: Load and overload: its output headroom does not grow with current, it has no short-circuit limit, and it does not model recovery from saturation.
LI: Spread: it is one typical part, with V_OS = 0, A_OL = 112 dB, and GBW = 1 MHz; a real batch spreads around those values within the datasheet limits.
P: Vendor models are more complete than this one, but each has the same kind of boundary, usually stated in a note like Microchip's. Check the model against the datasheet curves your circuit depends on, and measure what the datasheet does not give (Book 9). Book 3, Semiconductor Physics and Devices, takes the same approach to the diode, transistor, and MOSFET models built into SPICE itself.
BENCH: A perfect simulation fails on the bench most often outside the model's fitted range. A photodiode amplifier with R_f = 10 MΩ, simulated with a typical model at 25 °C, shows a 10 µV error from 1 pA of bias current. In a sealed enclosure at 85 °C the datasheet's 19 pA gives 0.19 mV, and at 125 °C its 1100 pA gives 11 mV. A slow sensor buffer simulates at 0.6 µV peak to peak of noise and shows 6 µV on the scope, because the model has no 1/f noise. Before trusting a simulation, list the conditions your circuit will see, temperature, common-mode range, load, frequency band, and the worst part rather than the typical one, and compare each with the conditions the model was fitted to. Where they differ, the bench is the reference, not the simulator.
BENCH: Build the DC error budget before choosing the part: offset, bias current through the source resistance, drift over temperature, and CMRR and PSRR terms, each in microvolts referred to the input. Many designs then reveal one dominant term, which tells you whether you need a precision op-amp, a zero-drift (chopper) part or a calibration step.
P: On the Job
P: Applications engineers at analog chip companies write the datasheets and the SPICE models in this section, and spend much of their time answering one question from customers: why does the circuit not do what the simulation showed? The answer is usually a test condition, a typical value read as a guarantee, or a model used outside its range. Engineers who can read a datasheet line by line, and check a model against it, are trusted with the parts that matter.
