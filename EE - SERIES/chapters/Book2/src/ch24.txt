H1: Chapter 24 — The H-Bridge and Motor as a Plant
P: Four switches and a motor can drive forward, drive backward, brake or coast, and if two of them are on at the wrong moment, short the supply. The H-bridge is simple to draw and unforgiving to get wrong.
P: Where you meet this: Robot and toy motors, electric vehicles, stepper drivers, class-D audio amplifiers and inverters.
P: Chapters 17 to 19 controlled a motor through a block labeled "actuator" that turned a command into a voltage. This chapter builds that block and then writes down the plant it drives. An H-bridge of four switches turns a logic-level duty cycle into a motor voltage of either polarity, and in doing so it adds current ripple, a dead-time error, and a path for energy to flow back into the supply. Each of those is a number, and together with the motor's two time constants they determine how the current and speed loops above them must be designed.
H2: 24.1 Four Switches and Their States
P: The bridge has two legs, each a high-side and a low-side switch in series across the supply V_s, with the motor connected between the two leg midpoints. Turning on the high switch of the left leg and the low switch of the right leg puts +V_s across the motor; the opposite diagonal puts −V_s across it; both low switches (or both high switches) short the motor terminals together, which lets the current circulate at zero applied voltage. The one forbidden state turns on both switches of one leg, a direct short across the supply called shoot-through. Real switches take tens to hundreds of nanoseconds to turn off, so the driver inserts a dead time between turning one switch off and its partner on, during which the inductive motor current flows through the switches' body diodes.

Close both switches on one side and you have built a short circuit. | Schematic. A supply rail at the top and ground at the bottom. Switches Q1 above Q2 form the left leg, Q3 above Q4 the right leg. A motor M connects the midpoints of the two legs. Red arrows show forward current down through Q1, left to right through the motor, and down through Q4. Blue arrows show reverse current down through Q3, right to left through the motor, and down through Q2. Note: never Q1 and Q2, or Q3 and Q4, together; that shorts the supply, called shoot-through.
H2: 24.2 Two Ways to Modulate
P: In bipolar (locked anti-phase) PWM both diagonals alternate every period, so the motor sees +V_s for a fraction D of the period and −V_s for the rest; the average is (2D − 1)V_s, and zero average voltage occurs at D = 0.5. In sign-magnitude PWM one leg is held to set the direction while the other leg switches, so the motor sees V_s for a fraction D and zero for the rest, and the average is D·V_s. The motor's inductance L averages the switched voltage into a current with a triangular ripple, and the two schemes produce very different ripple.
EQ: Bipolar: Δi_pp = 2 V_s D(1 − D)/(L f_sw),    Sign-magnitude: Δi_pp = V_s D(1 − D)/(L f_sw)
WE: Worked Example 24.1
PROB: A 24 V bridge switches at 20 kHz into a motor with L = 1 mH. Find the peak-to-peak current ripple at zero average voltage for bipolar PWM, and the worst-case ripple for sign-magnitude PWM.
SOL: Bipolar PWM reaches zero average voltage at D = 0.5, where the ripple is 2 × 24 × 0.25/(10⁻³ × 2 × 10⁴) = 0.6 A peak to peak. Sign-magnitude PWM reaches its worst ripple at D = 0.5 as well, with half the value, 0.3 A, and at zero speed it applies D = 0 and has no ripple at all. The bipolar bridge therefore heats a stationary motor with 0.6/√12 = 0.17 A rms of ripple current that produces no torque. Its compensating advantage is a duty-to-voltage relation that passes smoothly through zero, with no change of switching pattern when the direction reverses.
FIG: fig24_2.png | Figure 24.2 — Peak-to-peak current ripple versus duty cycle for bipolar and sign-magnitude PWM (V_s = 24 V, L = 1 mH, f_sw = 20 kHz), with each scheme's zero-voltage operating point marked. | Peak-to-peak current ripple is plotted against duty cycle for bipolar and sign-magnitude PWM; each curve reaches zero at its marked operating point.
P: The dead time produces a small voltage error. During each dead interval the leg's output follows the diode that conducts, and that depends on the direction of the current, not on the command. For a switching leg the average error is about V_s·t_d·f_sw, always opposing the current. With 24 V, 500 ns, and 20 kHz it is 0.24 V, 1% of the supply. It barely matters at speed, but near zero speed, where the commanded voltage is itself a few hundred millivolts, it appears as a dead band that the current loop must overcome.
KEY: The bridge is a voltage amplifier with a gain of V_s per unit of duty cycle (2V_s for bipolar). Every controller gain computed in volts must be divided by this gain to become a gain in duty cycle, and the gain changes whenever the supply voltage does, which is why good drives measure V_s and compensate.
H2: 24.3 The Motor's Two Equations
P: A permanent-magnet DC motor has an electrical side and a mechanical side, coupled by one constant. In SI units the torque constant K_t (N·m/A) and the back-EMF constant K_e (V·s/rad) are numerically equal. With winding resistance R, inductance L, rotor inertia J, viscous friction b, and load torque T_L, the motor obeys two equations.
EQ: V = R i + L di/dt + K_e ω,     J dω/dt = K_t i − b ω − T_L
P: When friction is small, the plant from voltage to speed has two time constants: the electrical τ_e = L/R and the mechanical τ_m = JR/(K_tK_e). When τ_m is much longer than τ_e, as it nearly always is, the transfer function is well approximated by two separate poles.
EQ: ω(s)/V(s) ≈ (1/K_e) / ((τ_m s + 1)(τ_e s + 1))
WE: Worked Example 24.2
PROB: A motor has R = 1 Ω, L = 1 mH, K_t = K_e = 0.05, J = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg·m², and negligible friction. Find both time constants, the no-load speed at 24 V, and the stall current.
SOL: τ_e = 1 mH/1 Ω = 1 ms. τ_m = 10⁻⁴ × 1/(0.05 × 0.05) = 40 ms, forty times longer. The no-load speed is V/K_e = 24/0.05 = 480 rad/s, or 4,580 rpm. At stall there is no back-EMF, so the current is 24/1 = 24 A, typically several times the continuous rating. Any drive that can apply full voltage to a stationary motor must limit its current, and the clean way to do that is a current loop.
H2: 24.4 Cascaded Current and Speed Loops
P: Standard drives nest two loops. The inner loop controls current, and therefore torque, through the plant 1/(Ls + R), and its output is the bridge voltage. The outer loop controls speed and produces the current command, which is clamped to the motor's rating. Each loop is designed as in Chapter 18, with the outer loop crossing over well below the inner loop so that, seen from outside, the current loop looks like an ideal current source.
P: A PI current controller can cancel the electrical pole by placing its zero at R/L, that is K_i/K_p = R/L. The loop gain then reduces to K_p/(Ls), which crosses unity at ω_c = K_p/L. The digital delay of Chapter 19, half a period of hold plus one period of computation, costs ω_c × 1.5T of phase at crossover.
WE: Worked Example 24.3
PROB: Design a PI current loop for the motor of Worked Example 24.2, sampled and updated at the 20 kHz switching frequency with one period of compute delay. Compare a 2 kHz and a 1 kHz crossover, then give the gains in duty cycle per ampere for a bipolar 24 V bridge.
SOL: The delay is 1.5 × 50 µs = 75 µs. At 2 kHz the lag is 2π × 2000 × 75 µs = 0.94 rad = 54°, leaving about 36° of phase margin from the 90° of the integrator-like loop, too little for a clean step. At 1 kHz the lag is 27° and the margin about 63°. Choose 1 kHz: K_p = ω_c L = 2π × 1000 × 10⁻³ = 6.28 V/A, and K_i = K_p R/L = 6,283 V/(A·s). Dividing by the bipolar bridge gain 2V_s = 48 V gives K_p = 0.131 per ampere and K_i = 131 per ampere-second in duty-cycle units.
P: The speed loop sees the current loop as a near-ideal current source, and the plant from current to speed is K_t/(Js), an integrator. For a crossover of 100 Hz, a decade below the current loop, K_p,ω = Jω_c/K_t = 10⁻⁴ × 628/0.05 = 1.26 A per rad/s, with the PI zero a factor of five below crossover, K_i,ω = 158 A/rad. The clamp on the current command sets the largest acceleration: at 5 A the torque is 0.25 N·m and the acceleration 2,500 rad/s², so the motor reaches 480 rad/s from rest in 0.19 s while the speed controller is saturated, and its integrator must be held by the anti-windup of Chapter 18 during that time.
MIS: A current loop is not an optional refinement for large drives only. Without it, a speed controller that commands full voltage to a stalled or reversing motor drives the stall current, or twice that when the back-EMF adds to the supply during a reversal, through the bridge and the windings. The current loop is what makes the bridge's switches and the motor's magnets survive the controller's mistakes.
H2: 24.5 Energy That Comes Back
P: When the speed loop decelerates the motor, the current reverses while the back-EMF keeps its sign, and the bridge transfers the rotor's kinetic energy, less the copper loss, into the supply. A battery absorbs it. A rectified supply cannot, so the energy lands on the bus capacitor and raises its voltage. This bus pumping is the most common way that a correctly designed controller destroys a drive.
WE: Worked Example 24.4
PROB: The motor of Worked Example 24.2 runs at 480 rad/s and is braked to rest at the 5 A current limit. The 24 V bus has 470 µF of capacitance and a supply that cannot sink current. Find the energy returned to the bus, the bus voltage it would reach with no protection, and the capacitance needed to keep the bus below 30 V.
SOL: The kinetic energy is E = 1/2 J omega^2 = 1/2 x 1e-4 x 480^2 = 11.52 J. At a 5 A braking current, alpha = Kt I/J = 2500 rad/s^2 and the stop takes 0.192 s. Winding loss is I^2 R t = 4.80 J, so the net energy added to the bus is 6.72 J. With 470 uF, the unprotected voltage is sqrt(24^2 + 2 x 6.72/470 uF) = 171 V. Holding the bus below 30 V by capacitance alone requires C = 2 x 6.72/(30^2 - 24^2) = 41.5 mF. A brake chopper is the usual remedy, but size its resistor at the actual clamp threshold, not at the bus ceiling. Initial regenerative power is I(Ke omega - I R) = 5(24 - 5) = 95 W. At a 28 V clamp threshold, absorbing that peak immediately requires R <= 28^2/95 = 8.25 ohm; a 9.5 ohm resistor dissipates only 82.5 W at 28 V, so the bus would keep rising. A 7.5 ohm +/-5% example dissipates at least 99.6 W at 28 V and as much as 126 W and 4.21 A at 30 V. Under the stated constant-current stop, regenerative power falls to zero at 100 rad/s and about 7.22 J of positive energy is returned before then; 6.72 J is the net bus-energy increase over the full stop. Check the actual resistor pulse-energy and repeated-stop average-power ratings, drive-specific resistance limits, switch voltage/current ratings, and clamp tolerance and overshoot. Verify on the selected drive and load that the bus remains below 30 V; this example is not a brake-resistor design approval.
P: The plant, then, is the bridge's duty-to-voltage gain, the electrical pole at R/L, the mechanical pole at K_tK_e/(JR), a sample delay, a current clamp, and a bus that rises when energy is returned. Chapter 10's tracker, and every robot joint built on it, sits on top of that stack and inherits each of those limits.
H2: 24.6 Relays, Solenoids, and the Flyback Diode
P: A relay is a switch moved by an electromagnet; a solenoid valve or a door lock is the same electromagnet moving a plunger instead of a contact. Both are inductors, and switching an inductor off is where they bite. The coil current cannot stop instantly, so when the driving transistor turns off, v = L di/dt drives the collector far above the supply, often to hundreds of volts, until something breaks down, usually the transistor. A diode across the coil, cathode to the supply (Figure 24.3), gives the current a path: it circulates through the coil and diode and decays with the coil's own time constant L/R, and the collector rises only one diode drop above the supply.
FIG: fig24_3.png | Figure 24.3 — A relay driven from a 3.3 V GPIO. The NPN transistor sinks the coil current; the flyback diode gives that current a path when the transistor turns off, so the collector rises only one diode drop above 5 V. | Schematic: a 5 volt relay coil from the supply to the collector of an NPN transistor, a 1N4148 diode across the coil with its cathode at the supply, and a 680 ohm base resistor from a GPIO pin.
P: The diode slows the release, because the current that holds the armature now decays gradually instead of stopping. Where release speed matters, a Zener diode in series with the flyback diode, or a TVS diode, lets the voltage rise to a chosen clamp and removes the energy faster. The same protection belongs across every inductive load a logic circuit switches: a brushed motor driven by one transistor, a solenoid valve, or the coil of a contactor. The water valve of a smart garden controller is exactly such a load.
WE: Worked Example 24.5
PROB: A 5 V relay (the common SRD-05VDC-SL-C) has a 70 Ω coil and must be driven from a 3.3 V GPIO through an NPN transistor with β ≥ 100. Find the coil current, choose the base resistor for a forced β of 20, and size the flyback diode.
SOL: The coil current is 5 V/70 Ω = 71 mA, ignoring the transistor's 0.1 to 0.2 V of saturation voltage. A forced β of 20, well below the minimum of 100, guarantees saturation: I_B = 71/20 = 3.6 mA, and R_B = (3.3 − 0.7)/3.6 mA = 730 Ω; the standard 680 Ω gives 3.8 mA, within a typical GPIO's drive. The flyback diode carries the full 71 mA at the instant of turn-off, decaying from there, so a small-signal 1N4148 is adequate; a 1N4007 is the usual choice for coils of half an amp or more. Check: without the diode, interrupting 71 mA in a fraction of a microsecond in a coil of tens of millihenries would demand thousands of volts; in practice the transistor breaks down first and degrades a little each time.
H2: 24.7 Servos, Steppers, and Small Robots
P: A hobby servo is a complete position loop in a box: a small DC motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer on the output shaft, and a control chip that compares the potentiometer with the command and drives an H-bridge. The command is a pulse, typically 1.0 to 2.0 ms long, repeated every 20 ms (Figure 24.4); 1.5 ms is center, and the ends map to roughly ±45° to ±90° depending on the servo. The servo's own loop runs inside, so the controller's only job is to produce accurate pulses, which any microcontroller's PWM block does in hardware.
FIG: fig24_4.png | Figure 24.4 — Servo command pulses. The frame repeats every 20 ms; the pulse width, from about 1.0 to 2.0 ms, sets the angle, with 1.5 ms at center. | Three pulse trains with 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 millisecond pulses repeating every 20 milliseconds.
P: A stepper motor needs no position sensor at all. Its rotor moves one step, 1.8° (200 steps per revolution) for the most common type, each time the driver advances the current pattern in its windings, so counting steps counts position, as long as the load never demands more torque than the motor can give; when it does, the motor skips steps without telling anyone. Stepper drivers chop the winding current to a set limit, the current loop of Section 24.4 in miniature, and microstep by setting intermediate currents: at 1/16 microstepping, a 200-step motor takes 3200 microsteps per revolution. A star-tracker telescope mount and a 3D printer depend on this open-loop counting; the inexpensive 28BYJ-48 geared stepper of many starter kits is a slow, low-torque version of the same idea.
P: Robots and RC models combine all three motor types. A rover drives its wheels with DC motors through H-bridges (a dual-bridge chip such as the DRV8833 for small motors), pans a sensor with a servo, and positions an arm with steppers. An RC car's electronic speed controller (ESC) is an H-bridge or a three-phase brushless driver that accepts the same 1 to 2 ms servo pulse as its throttle command. The code that generates the pulses and step trains with hardware timers rather than software delays is in Book 8, Chapter 7, Timers and PWM.
WE: Worked Example 24.6
PROB: A Raspberry Pi Pico runs its RP2040 at 125 MHz. Configure one PWM slice for a servo: choose the clock divider and wrap value for a 50 Hz frame with 1 µs resolution, and find the counter level for a 1.5 ms pulse.
SOL: A divider of 125 makes the counter tick at 125 MHz/125 = 1 MHz, so one count is 1 µs (the RP2040's PWM divider accepts values up to 255). A 20 ms frame is then 20,000 counts; the counter runs from 0 to its wrap value inclusive, so wrap = 19,999. The level for 1.5 ms is 1500 counts, and the full 1.0 to 2.0 ms range spans 1000 counts. Check: 1 MHz/20,000 = 50 Hz. If the 1.0 to 2.0 ms range maps to 90° of travel, each count is 0.09°, finer than the servo's own gearbox can resolve.
WE: Worked Example 24.7
PROB: A stepper with 200 steps per revolution drives an 8 mm-per-revolution lead screw through a driver set to 1/16 microstepping. Find the distance per microstep and the microstep rate for 25 mm/s.
SOL: There are 200 × 16 = 3200 microsteps per revolution, so each moves 8 mm/3200 = 2.5 µm. 25 mm/s is 25/8 = 3.125 rev/s, or 3.125 × 3200 = 10,000 microsteps per second. Check: 10,000 × 2.5 µm = 25 mm/s. Whether the motor can follow at 3.1 rev/s depends on its torque-speed curve and supply voltage: the winding inductance limits how fast the current can reverse, the same L di/dt that the flyback diode handles.
KEY: Every coil a transistor switches needs a path for its current at turn-off. Servos close their own loop from a pulse width; steppers count steps with no sensor and lose position silently if overloaded.
BENCH: Insert dead time between turning one switch off and its partner on, so the two in a leg are never on together, even briefly. The required time depends on the switching speed of the actual transistors and gate drivers, and is typically hundreds of nanoseconds for MOSFETs. Then check with a current probe on the supply that no shoot-through spikes remain.
P: On the Job
P: Motor-drive engineering is a field of its own, spanning robots, e-bikes, pumps, and electric vehicles. At work the H-bridge is usually an integrated motor-driver or gate-driver IC (a DRV8833-class part for small motors, a dedicated stepper driver with current chopping), and the engineering is choosing it, protecting it with flyback paths, current limits, and thermal margin, and proving it on the bench with a current probe. Learn the bridge here, buy the IC at work.
H2: 24.8 Audio Power Amplifiers: AB, D, G, and H
P: An audio power amplifier must deliver a low-distortion voltage and current to a speaker. Class AB uses complementary output devices biased slightly on, reducing the crossover notch of ideal class B while wasting more power at idle. Its output devices dissipate the difference between the supply voltage and the speaker voltage, so heat sinking and safe operating area matter.
P: Class D switches its output devices between supply rails. A modulator, often pulse-width modulation, makes the switching pattern represent the audio waveform; the output network and speaker recover the audio band. The required filter depends on the amplifier topology, switching frequency, load, and emissions limits. Some products are specified for filterless use, but that is a device-level design condition, not a general rule. Switching edges, dead time, layout, speaker cable, and load impedance affect distortion and electromagnetic interference.
P: Classes G and H describe ways to reduce the loss of a linear output stage by changing its supply rails. A common class-G design selects a higher rail when the signal needs more headroom; a common class-H design tracks or modulates the rail more continuously. Naming and implementation vary across products. These supply strategies can be combined with a linear output stage; they do not describe the same switching operation as class D. Class T was Tripath's trade name for a proprietary class-D implementation, not a separate basic output conduction class.
WE: Worked Example 24.8
P: A bridge-tied-load (BTL) amplifier produces a 10 V peak differential sine wave across an 8 ohm speaker. Find ideal output power and peak and rms speaker current.
P: The load sees the difference between two active outputs, so use differential voltage: P = Vpk^2/(2R) = 10^2/(2 x 8) = 6.25 W. The peak current is Ipk = Vpk/R = 1.25 A, and the sine-wave rms current is Ipk/sqrt(2) = 0.884 A. This is ideal load power; real output is limited by supply headroom, device current and thermal limits, distortion, and protection. Neither BTL speaker terminal is ground; connecting a grounded instrument or load there can short an output.
P: Treat this as an output-stage and load calculation, not a complete amplifier design. Choose an actual IC or discrete topology from its datasheet and reference design, including its load, output network, supply, layout, thermal, and protection conditions. Book 6 covers the energy conversion that supplies amplifier rails; this chapter covers how the output stage drives the speaker.
