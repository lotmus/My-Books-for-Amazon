H1: Chapter 8 — Capacitors
P: Pick up any modern circuit board and count the capacitors. Usually they outnumber everything else. Most of them aren't there to store energy at all; they are there to hold a supply rail steady for the nanoseconds when a chip suddenly wants current.
P: Where you meet this: Decoupling around every IC, bulk capacitors at power inputs, timing and filter circuits, and the ripple ratings of power supplies.
P: The ideal capacitor of Book 1 stores charge in exact proportion to voltage, with no further complication. A real capacitor adds two practical concerns almost every design eventually runs into: what type of capacitor actually suits a given job, and how far its behavior departs from ideal once frequency or voltage push it outside its comfortable range.
H2: 8.1 Real Capacitor Types
P: Ceramic capacitors split into two practically different families sharing one name: Class 1 types (commonly labeled C0G or NP0) hold their rated capacitance almost exactly regardless of applied voltage or temperature, while Class 2 types (X7R, Y5V, and similar) pack far more capacitance into the same size at the cost of a capacitance that measurably decreases as DC voltage across it increases — a real, specified effect called DC bias derating, not a manufacturing defect. Electrolytic capacitors (aluminum and tantalum) reach far higher capacitance values than any ceramic in a comparable size, but are polarized — connecting one backwards is not merely ineffective but can destroy the part, sometimes violently — a failure mode both aluminum and tantalum electrolytics are known for. Film capacitors sit between the two extremes: unpolarized, stable, and typically used where ceramics' voltage sensitivity or electrolytics' polarity requirement is undesirable.
MIS: A Class 2 ceramic capacitor's marked capacitance is a nominal value under specific, standardized test conditions, not a promise the part will supply that capacitance at its actual operating voltage. A 10 µF X7R part biased near its rated voltage can easily deliver less than half its marked capacitance in practice — always check a manufacturer's DC bias curve for a Class 2 part in a voltage-sensitive design.
WE: Worked Example 8.1
PROB: A 10 µF X7R ceramic capacitor's datasheet specifies 50% capacitance loss at its rated 25 V DC bias. Find its actual effective capacitance when operated at that voltage.
SOL: Effective capacitance = 10 µF × (1 − 0.50) = 5 µF — only half of the value printed on the part, exactly the kind of DC bias derating the misconception above warns about. A designer who needed a genuine 10 µF at this bias would have to select a physically larger part, a lower-voltage-rated one operated well under its DC bias curve's knee, or a different dielectric class entirely.
H2: 8.2 Voltage Rating and Polarity
P: Every capacitor carries a maximum voltage rating, and exceeding it risks dielectric breakdown — permanent, often catastrophic failure. For a polarized electrolytic, the rating applies specifically to the correct polarity; reverse voltage, even briefly, is a separate and typically much lower limit, if it is tolerated at all. Good design practice, exactly as with a resistor's power rating in Chapter 7, leaves real margin below the voltage rating rather than designing right up against it.

H2: 8.3 ESR, ESL, and Self-Resonant Frequency
P: A real capacitor is not a pure capacitance: its leads, plates, and internal construction contribute a small equivalent series resistance (ESR) and an equivalent series inductance (ESL) that Book 1's idealized capacitor did not have. At low frequency, the capacitor's own reactance dominates and the part behaves almost exactly as the ideal model predicts. As frequency rises, however, the capacitive reactance 1/(ωC) shrinks while the inductive reactance ωL(ESL) grows, and at one particular frequency — the self-resonant frequency (SRF) — the two cancel exactly:
EQ: SRF = 1 / (2π√(L_ESL · C))
P: Above the SRF, the part's impedance is dominated by its parasitic inductance and it stops behaving like a capacitor at all — a critical consideration when choosing a bypass or decoupling capacitor for a high-frequency circuit, since a capacitor operated well above its own SRF provides essentially no filtering benefit.
WE: Worked Example 8.2
PROB: A capacitor has C = 100 nF and an equivalent series inductance of 5 nH. Find its self-resonant frequency.
SOL: SRF = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π√(5×10⁻⁹ × 1×10⁻⁷)) = 1/(2π√(5×10⁻¹⁶)) = 1/(2π × 2.236×10⁻⁸) ≈ 7.12 MHz. Above roughly 7 MHz, this part's parasitic inductance, not its 100 nF rating, dominates its behavior.

H2: 8.4 Capacitors in Practice: Decoupling and Real Impedance
P: Section 8.3 showed that a real capacitor has an equivalent series resistance and inductance, and that above its self-resonant frequency it behaves like an inductor. This chapter puts that model to work on the job capacitors are asked to do more than any other: holding a supply rail steady while the circuit on it draws sudden, fast-changing current. It ends with the quieter imperfections, dielectric absorption, leakage, ripple heating, and aging, that decide whether a capacitor is right for timing, sampling, or long life.
H2: 8.5 The Impedance Curve
P: The series model of Chapter 8 gives a capacitor's impedance magnitude at any frequency:
EQ: |Z| = √( ESR² + (ωL_ESL − 1/(ωC))² )
P: On a log-log plot, |Z| falls at −20 dB per decade while the capacitance dominates, flattens to the ESR at the self-resonant frequency, and rises at +20 dB per decade once the ESL dominates. Two consequences follow. A capacitor's usefulness for decoupling is bounded on both sides: at low frequency its reactance is too large, and at high frequency its inductance is. And a larger capacitor in the same package does not help at high frequency at all, because above resonance only the ESL matters, and ESL is set by the package and its mounting, not by the capacitance.
WE: Worked Example 8.3
PROB: Declare two mounted ceramic capacitors: 10 µF with ESL = 1.0 nH and ESR = 5 mΩ, and 100 nF with ESL = 0.5 nH and ESR = 20 mΩ. Find each self-resonant frequency and each impedance at 1 MHz and at 100 MHz.
SOL: For the 10 µF part, SRF = 1/(2π√(1.0×10⁻⁹ × 10×10⁻⁶)) ≈ 1.59 MHz. For the 100 nF part, SRF = 1/(2π√(0.5×10⁻⁹ × 100×10⁻⁹)) ≈ 22.5 MHz. At 1 MHz, the 10 µF part is still capacitive and presents about 11 mΩ; the 100 nF part presents about 1.59 Ω. At 100 MHz both are inductive: the 10 µF part presents about 0.63 Ω and the 100 nF part about 0.30 Ω, almost exactly in the ratio of their ESLs. Past resonance, the hundred-times-larger capacitor is the worse of the two.
H2: 8.6 Target Impedance
P: A digital load does not draw a steady current. It draws current in steps, at clock edges and whenever a block of logic wakes up. The rail must hold its voltage within a tolerance while those steps occur, and Ohm's law turns that requirement into an impedance the supply network must stay below over the whole frequency range of the steps:
EQ: Z_target = ΔV_allowed / ΔI_step
WE: Worked Example 8.4
PROB: A 1.0 V rail must stay within 3% while its load steps by 2 A. Find the target impedance. Does either capacitor of Worked Example 8.3, used alone, meet it at 10 MHz?
SOL: ΔV_allowed = 0.03 × 1.0 = 30 mV, so Z_target = 0.030/2 = 15 mΩ. At 10 MHz the 10 µF part presents about 61 mΩ and the 100 nF part about 129 mΩ. Neither comes close; meeting 15 mΩ at 10 MHz takes several capacitors in parallel, each mounted with low inductance, plus the capacitance between the board's power and ground planes. The regulator itself takes care of low frequencies, and the package and die of the load take care of the highest. The very large currents of a modern processor, and the impedance targets they imply, are taken up in Book 7; the method is the same.
H2: 8.7 Parallel Capacitors and Antiresonance
P: The obvious way to cover a wide band is to parallel a large capacitor and a small one. It works, but with a trap. Between the two self-resonant frequencies, the large capacitor has turned inductive while the small one is still capacitive. An inductor in parallel with a capacitor is a parallel resonant circuit, and at its resonance the combined impedance rises to a peak, higher than either part alone. This peak is called antiresonance. Its frequency is approximately where the small capacitor resonates with the sum of both inductances:
EQ: f_ar ≈ 1 / (2π √((L₁ + L₂) C_small))
WE: Worked Example 8.5
PROB: The two capacitors of Worked Example 8.3 are placed in parallel. Estimate the antiresonant frequency and compare the combined impedance there with each part alone.
SOL: f_ar ≈ 1/(2π√((1.0 + 0.5)×10⁻⁹ × 100×10⁻⁹)) ≈ 13.0 MHz. A numerical evaluation of the parallel combination finds the peak at about 12.9 MHz with |Z| ≈ 0.27 Ω, while at that frequency the 10 µF part alone presents about 0.080 Ω and the 100 nF part alone about 0.085 Ω. Adding the small capacitor made the network more than three times worse at 13 MHz than either capacitor by itself.
FIG: fig8_3.png | Figure 8.3 — Impedance of the 10 µF and 100 nF capacitors of Worked Example 8.3 alone and in parallel. The parallel combination is lower almost everywhere, but rises to an antiresonant peak near 13 MHz.
P: Three measures tame antiresonance. Using several capacitors of the same value lowers the impedance everywhere without creating a peak between different values. Spacing values closely, perhaps a factor of three or less apart, makes each peak shallow. And resistance damps the resonance; the low ESR of modern ceramics, so good in other respects, is exactly what makes the peak sharp, and some designs deliberately include a bulk capacitor with higher ESR, or a capacitor specified with controlled ESR, for this reason.
KEY: Above its self-resonant frequency a capacitor is an inductor, so mounting inductance, not capacitance, sets high-frequency decoupling. Parallel unlike values with care: between their resonances they form a parallel LC circuit whose antiresonant peak can exceed either part alone.
MIS: The habit of placing "a 100 nF next to every pin" is not a design. It is a reasonable default for slow logic, but for a fast load the questions are what impedance is needed, over what band, and what the mounting inductance of each capacitor is. A capacitor placed at the end of a long, thin trace adds that trace's inductance in series and may do almost nothing above a few megahertz.
H2: 8.8 Local Bulk Capacitance
P: For the slower part of a load step, before the regulator's loop can respond, local bulk capacitance supplies the charge. The required capacitance follows from the definition of capacitance: delivering a current I for a time Δt removes a charge IΔt, and the voltage falls by that charge divided by C.
EQ: C ≥ I · Δt / ΔV
WE: Worked Example 8.6
PROB: A regulator needs 10 µs to respond to a 2 A load step. The rail may sag by no more than 50 mV during that time. Find the minimum local capacitance, ignoring ESR.
SOL: C ≥ 2 × 10×10⁻⁶/0.050 = 400 µF. In practice the ESR also produces an instantaneous drop of I × ESR at the moment of the step; with 20 mΩ of total ESR, that alone would be 40 mV, leaving only 10 mV for the capacitive sag, so the low-ESR requirement can matter as much as the capacitance. Longer hold-up times, such as riding through a lost input supply, are a power-system question and belong to Book 6.
H2: 8.9 Ripple Current and Heating
P: Current flowing in and out of a capacitor heats it through its ESR, at a rate I_rms² × ESR. Electrolytic capacitors are given a ripple-current rating at a stated frequency and temperature, and their lifetime falls sharply with temperature; a common rule of thumb for aluminum electrolytics is that life roughly halves for every 10 °C rise in internal temperature. A capacitor carrying 1.5 A rms through 50 mΩ of ESR dissipates 1.5² × 0.05 ≈ 0.11 W, which in a small can is a temperature rise of several degrees and a measurable reduction in life.
H2: 8.10 Dielectric Absorption, Leakage, Microphonics, and Aging
P: Discharge a capacitor briefly to zero, remove the short, and wait: the voltage creeps back up to a fraction of its original value. This dielectric absorption comes from charge that moved slowly into the dielectric and returns slowly. In a sample-and-hold circuit or an integrator it appears as a memory of earlier values. Polypropylene and polystyrene film capacitors and C0G ceramics absorb very little; Class 2 ceramics and electrolytics absorb much more. For timing and sampling capacitors, this is as important a selection criterion as tolerance.
P: Leakage current flows through every dielectric. It is negligible for film and C0G parts, small for most ceramics, and significant for aluminum electrolytics, where it can reach microamps and rises with temperature and voltage. In a long time-constant circuit, such as a 100 s timer built from a 100 µF electrolytic and a 1 MΩ resistor, leakage comparable to the charging current makes the timing unpredictable.
P: Class 2 ceramics have two further habits. Their dielectric is piezoelectric, so they can turn mechanical vibration into a voltage, called microphonics, and turn a ripple voltage into audible sound, a whine from a power supply's output capacitors. And their capacitance ages: it falls by roughly one to a few percent per decade of time after the part was last heated above its Curie point, for example during soldering. C0G parts show neither effect to any practical degree, which is one more reason this chapter and Chapter 6 reserve them for filters and timing.
BENCH: Place decoupling capacitors so the loop from the capacitor, through the IC's power and ground pins, and back is as small as you can make it, with vias right at the pads. A 100 nF capacitor a few centimeters away behind thin traces is mostly inductance at the frequencies that matter. And derate ceramics for DC bias before you count microfarads.
