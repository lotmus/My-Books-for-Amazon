H1: Chapter 16 — Comparators, Hysteresis, and Open-Collector Outputs
P: Somewhere in almost every analog system, a continuous voltage has to become a decision: the battery is low, the temperature is too high, the zero crossing has arrived. A comparator makes that decision. Chapter 5 introduced it as an op-amp-shaped part with the feedback deliberately left off. This chapter shows why a bare comparator misbehaves on slow, noisy signals, how a little positive feedback fixes that, and how the open-collector output lets several comparators, or several chips, share one wire.
H2: 16.1 What Makes a Comparator Different
P: A comparator and an op-amp share a symbol and a differential input, but they are optimized for opposite jobs. An op-amp is internally compensated so that it stays stable under negative feedback, and that compensation slows it down. A comparator has no such compensation; its output stage is designed to swing hard from one logic level to the other and to recover quickly when the inputs cross. Its datasheet quotes a propagation delay, the time from the input crossing to the output changing state, rather than a gain-bandwidth product.
P: An op-amp can be used as a comparator in a pinch, and for slow signals it often works. Its weaknesses are real, though: it is slow to come out of saturation, its output levels may not match logic thresholds, and some op-amps have input protection diodes between the two inputs that conduct when a large differential voltage appears, which a comparator's inputs are built to tolerate.
MIS: Never put linear negative feedback around a comparator to "make it more accurate." Its lack of compensation means it will oscillate. The only feedback a comparator should see is the positive feedback of Section 16.3, which pushes it further in the direction it is already going.
H2: 16.2 Chatter on a Slow Edge
P: Feed a comparator a slowly rising voltage that carries even a few millivolts of noise. As the signal approaches the threshold, the noise carries it back and forth across the threshold many times, and the output toggles every time. Instead of one clean transition, the output produces a burst of edges, called chatter. A counter connected to that output counts dozens of events instead of one; a motor driver switches on and off at kilohertz rates and heats up. Faster comparators make chatter worse, because they respond to every crossing.
H2: 16.3 Hysteresis: Two Thresholds Instead of One
P: The cure is to give the comparator two thresholds: a higher one that the input must rise above to switch the output high, and a lower one that it must fall below to switch it back. Once the output has switched, noise smaller than the gap between the thresholds cannot switch it back. A circuit with this behavior is called a Schmitt trigger, and the gap is its hysteresis.
P: A comparator acquires hysteresis through positive feedback. In the inverting form, the input signal drives the inverting pin, and a divider sets the non-inverting pin from both a reference voltage V_ref (through R₁) and the comparator's own output (through R₂). Superposition gives the non-inverting pin's voltage, which is the threshold:
EQ: V_th = V_ref · R₂/(R₁ + R₂) + V_out · R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
P: Because the threshold depends on the output, it has two values, one for each output state. The hysteresis is the output swing multiplied by R₁/(R₁ + R₂).
WE: Worked Example 16.1
PROB: A comparator runs from 5 V with a push-pull output that swings 0 V to 5 V. The input drives the inverting pin. The non-inverting pin connects to V_ref = 2.5 V through R₁ = 10 kΩ and to the output through R₂ = 100 kΩ. Find the two thresholds and the hysteresis.
SOL: The reference contributes 2.5 × 100k/110k ≈ 2.273 V. The output contributes 5 × 10k/110k ≈ 0.455 V when high and 0 V when low. With the output high (input below threshold), the upper threshold is 2.273 + 0.455 ≈ 2.727 V; the input must rise above this to drive the output low. With the output low, the lower threshold is 2.273 V; the input must fall below this to drive the output high again. The hysteresis is 0.455 V, centered on 2.5 V. Noise up to about 0.45 V peak-to-peak can no longer cause chatter.

KEY: Hysteresis trades a small, known threshold error for immunity to noise smaller than the gap. || Size the gap larger than the peak-to-peak noise on the input and smaller than the error the application can tolerate.
P: Positive feedback is entirely legitimate here. The golden rules do not apply; the two inputs are not at the same voltage except at the instant of switching, and nothing in the circuit is trying to make them so. This is the same sign change Chapter 11 warned about, used deliberately to make the circuit decisive.
H2: 16.4 The Open-Collector Output
P: Many comparators, including the long-lived LM311 and LM339 families, have an open-collector output: a single transistor that can pull the output pin low, and nothing that can pull it high. The high level is supplied by an external pull-up resistor to whatever supply the next circuit needs. That one feature makes the part a level translator for free: a comparator powered from 12 V can drive a 3.3 V logic input simply by returning its pull-up to 3.3 V. CMOS parts with the same arrangement are called open-drain.
P: The pull-up resistor sets two competing quantities. When the transistor is on, the resistor wastes a current V_pu/R_pu. When it turns off, the resistor alone must charge the capacitance of the wire and the inputs on it, so the rising edge is an RC exponential with time constant R_pu C, and its 10% to 90% rise time is 2.2 R_pu C. Falling edges are fast, because the transistor sinks current actively.
WE: Worked Example 16.2
PROB: An open-drain output pulls up to 3.3 V through 4.7 kΩ into a line with 50 pF of total capacitance. Find the rise time and the low-state current. What largest pull-up gives a 100 ns rise time, and what current does it draw?
SOL: The time constant is 4.7 kΩ × 50 pF = 235 ns, so the rise time is 2.2 × 235 ns ≈ 517 ns. The low-state current is 3.3 V/4.7 kΩ ≈ 0.70 mA, ignoring the small output-low voltage. For a 100 ns rise, R_pu ≤ 100 ns/(2.2 × 50 pF) ≈ 909 Ω; a standard 820 Ω part gives 90 ns and draws about 4.0 mA when low. Check that the output transistor can sink that current while still meeting the receiver's maximum input-low voltage; the datasheet's V_OL at a stated sink current is the number to compare.
H2: 16.5 Wired Logic on One Line
P: Several open-collector or open-drain outputs can share one pull-up resistor and one wire. If any output turns on, the line goes low; the line is high only if every output is off. For active-low signals, such as a "fault" line that any of several monitors may assert, this is a wired-OR: the line is asserted if any monitor asserts it. In positive logic the same connection is a wired-AND. Push-pull outputs cannot be connected this way, because one driving high while another drives low creates a short circuit between the supply and ground through two output stages.
P: The arrangement is common far beyond comparators. Interrupt lines shared by several chips use it, and the I²C bus uses it for both its clock and data lines, so any device can hold the clock low to stretch it. The rise-time arithmetic of Worked Example 16.2 is exactly what limits how fast such a bus can run and how many devices it can carry.
H2: 16.6 The Window Comparator
P: Two comparators with open-collector outputs tied together make a window comparator: one fires when the input exceeds an upper limit, the other when it falls below a lower limit, and the shared line goes low if either fires. A supply monitor that flags both overvoltage and undervoltage on one pin is built exactly this way, with the two thresholds set by a single resistor ladder from a reference.

SUM: A comparator turns an analog voltage into a logic decision and must never be given linear feedback. Slow, noisy inputs make a single-threshold comparator chatter; positive feedback creates two thresholds whose gap, the hysteresis, rejects noise smaller than it. Open-collector and open-drain outputs need a pull-up that trades static current against rise time (2.2 RC), translate logic levels for free, and can share one line as wired logic.

GLOS: Hysteresis || The gap between the rising and falling thresholds of a Schmitt trigger.
