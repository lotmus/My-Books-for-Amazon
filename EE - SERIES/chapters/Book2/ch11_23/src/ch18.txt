H1: Chapter 18 — PID, Lead, and Lag: Shaping the Loop
P: Chapter 17 ended with two facts in tension. Proportional control alone leaves a steady error, and an integrator that removes the error adds phase lag that can make the loop ring. Compensation is the art of resolving that tension: adding gain where the loop needs accuracy, removing phase lag where the loop needs stability, and doing both with a small number of parts or lines of code whose settings have physical units. This chapter covers the PID controller, its cousins the lead and lag networks, and the margins that tell you how close a loop is to oscillation.
H2: 18.1 PID as Three Knobs with Units
P: The PID controller computes its command from the present error, its accumulated history, and its rate of change:
EQ: u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i ∫ e dt + K_d de/dt
P: For the motor of Chapter 17, the error is in rad/s and the command in volts, so K_p is in V per rad/s, K_i in V per rad (because integrating rad/s gives rad), and K_d in V·s² per rad. Writing the units down is not pedantry: it shows immediately that the three gains cannot be compared with one another, and it catches the common mistake of tuning K_i in one sample period's units and then changing the sample rate. An equivalent form, popular in industrial controllers, uses an integral time T_i = K_p/K_i and a derivative time T_d = K_d/K_p, both in seconds.
H2: 18.2 Designing a PI Controller by Cancellation
P: For a first-order plant P = K/(τs + 1), a PI controller C = K_p (1 + 1/(T_i s)) can be written as K_p (T_i s + 1)/(T_i s). Its zero sits at s = −1/T_i. Choosing T_i = τ places that zero exactly on the plant's pole, and the two cancel. The loop gain then collapses to a pure integrator:
EQ: L(s) = C P = K_p K / (τ s),   crossover ω_c = K_p K / τ
P: A loop gain that is a pure integrator crosses unity once, with exactly 90 degrees of lag, so its phase margin is 90 degrees, and the closed loop is a first-order system with time constant 1/ω_c. The design reduces to one decision: how fast should the loop be?
WE: Worked Example 18.1
PROB: Design a PI controller for the declared motor (K = 2 (rad/s)/V, τ = 0.1 s) that gives a closed-loop time constant of 10 ms with zero steady-state error. State K_p, T_i, and K_i with units, and the crossover frequency.
SOL: Cancel the plant pole: T_i = τ = 0.1 s. A closed-loop time constant of 10 ms means ω_c = 100 rad/s. From ω_c = K_p K/τ, K_p = 100 × 0.1/2 = 5 V per rad/s. Then K_i = K_p/T_i = 5/0.1 = 50 V per rad. The crossover is 100 rad/s, about 15.9 Hz, inside the 7 Hz to 20 Hz range Chapter 17 suggested for a 200 Hz digital loop. With an ideal continuous controller the step response has no overshoot. Chapter 19 will show what sampling does to that promise.
H2: 18.3 Derivative Action and Its Filter
P: The derivative term reacts to how fast the error is changing, and so it acts before the error has grown: it adds damping. In the frequency domain it is a zero that adds up to 90 degrees of phase lead. Two practical rules make it usable. First, a pure derivative has gain that rises without limit with frequency, so it amplifies sensor noise; every real implementation adds a low-pass filter, commonly with a corner five to twenty times above the derivative's own corner 1/T_d. Second, applying the derivative to the measurement rather than to the error avoids a huge spike in command each time the reference steps, because a step reference has an infinite derivative but a real measurement does not.
H2: 18.4 The Lead Compensator
P: A lead network is a derivative with its gain capped: a zero at ω_z followed by a pole at a higher frequency ω_p = α ω_z. Between them, the network's phase rises to a maximum and falls again:
EQ: C_lead(s) = (1 + s/ω_z)/(1 + s/ω_p),   φ_max = arcsin((α − 1)/(α + 1)) at ω = √(ω_z ω_p)
P: At the frequency of maximum phase, the network's gain is √α. The usual design places that frequency at the desired crossover, so the phase arrives exactly where the loop needs it, and then lowers the proportional gain by √α so that the crossover does not move.
WE: Worked Example 18.2
PROB: A loop has 25 degrees of phase margin at its 50 rad/s crossover and needs about 60 degrees. Design a lead network centered on the crossover.
SOL: The loop needs about 35 degrees more phase, plus a few degrees of allowance because the network will shift the crossover slightly; aim for about 37 degrees. Solving arcsin((α − 1)/(α + 1)) = 37° gives α ≈ 4: with α = 4, φ_max = arcsin(3/5) ≈ 36.9°. Center the network at 50 rad/s: ω_z = 50/√4 = 25 rad/s and ω_p = 50 × √4 = 100 rad/s. The network adds a gain of √4 = 2 (6 dB) at 50 rad/s, so reduce K_p by a factor of 2 to keep the crossover in place. A larger α, such as 10 for 55 degrees, would buy more phase at the cost of ten times more high-frequency gain, and therefore ten times more sensor noise in the command.

H2: 18.5 The Lag Compensator
P: A lag network is the mirror image: a pole at a low frequency followed by a zero a factor α higher. It raises the low-frequency gain by α, reducing steady-state error, while leaving the gain near crossover unchanged. Its cost is a small amount of phase lag that leaks up toward crossover; placing the zero a decade or more below the crossover keeps that cost to a few degrees. A PI controller is the limiting case of a lag network whose pole has moved all the way to zero frequency.
H2: 18.6 Margins You Can Measure
P: Two numbers summarize how close a loop is to oscillation. The phase margin is 180 degrees plus the phase of the loop gain at the frequency where its magnitude is 1. The gain margin is how much the loop gain could rise, at the frequency where its phase reaches −180 degrees, before its magnitude reached 1. Both can be read from a Bode plot of the loop gain, and both can be measured on the bench by injecting a small sine wave at a summing point inside the loop and comparing the signals on either side of it.
P: Phase margin also predicts the step response. For a loop whose closed-loop behavior is dominated by two poles, a phase margin of about 60 degrees corresponds to a damping ratio near 0.6 and an overshoot near 9%. About 45 degrees corresponds to an overshoot near 23%, and about 33 degrees to an overshoot near 37%. These are the numbers behind Chapter 5's rule of thumb that 60 degrees is comfortable and 30 degrees rings.
KEY: A loop's phase margin is its distance from oscillation, and it predicts overshoot. || Aim for 45 to 60 degrees, and measure it on the real hardware, because the real plant always has delay and resonance the model left out.
H2: 18.7 Integrator Windup and the Safety Layer
P: When the actuator saturates, the loop is effectively open: the command no longer changes with the controller output. The integrator does not know that. It keeps accumulating error for as long as the plant lags behind, and by the time the plant catches up, the integral holds a large stored value that drives the output well past the reference before it can unwind. This is windup, and it turns a well-damped loop into one that overshoots badly after every large step.
P: Anti-windup stops the integrator from accumulating while the actuator is saturated. The simplest form, conditional integration, freezes the integral whenever the computed command is outside the actuator's limits. A second form, back-calculation, feeds the difference between computed and applied command back into the integrator to bleed it down. Either way the principle is the one Chapter 17 ended with: the physical limit is the authority, and the controller must respect what the actuator and the safety layer actually did, not what it asked for.
WE: Worked Example 18.3
PROB: The PI controller of Worked Example 18.1 receives a 10 rad/s step. Its initial command is K_p × 10 = 50 V, but the driver saturates at 12 V. Describe what happens with and without anti-windup.
SOL: In both cases the motor accelerates at full drive, approaching the 24 rad/s that 12 V would eventually give, and crosses 10 rad/s after about τ ln(24/14) ≈ 54 ms. Without anti-windup, the integrator has meanwhile accumulated about 0.25 rad of error, worth K_i × 0.25 ≈ 12 V of stored command, which holds the drive near the rail after the crossing; a simulation of the continuous loop shows the speed overshooting to about 11.1 rad/s, 11% above the reference, before the integral unwinds. With the PI design's own 90-degree phase margin, that overshoot is caused entirely by windup. With conditional integration, the integral stays near zero during saturation, the command leaves the rail as soon as the proportional term alone falls below 12 V, and the speed approaches 10 rad/s without overshoot.
MIS: Windup is not a tuning problem. Lowering K_i reduces the overshoot only by making the loop slower everywhere, including in normal small-signal operation. The correct fix is structural: tell the integrator when the actuator is saturated.

SUM: The PID controller combines proportional, integral, and derivative action, each with physical units. For a first-order plant, a PI controller whose zero cancels the plant pole gives a pure-integrator loop with 90 degrees of phase margin and a crossover set by K_p K/τ. Lead networks add phase near crossover (maximum arcsin((α − 1)/(α + 1)) at √(ω_z ω_p)); lag networks add low-frequency gain. Phase margin predicts overshoot. Anti-windup stops the integrator from accumulating while the actuator is saturated.
GLOS: PID controller || A controller whose command is a weighted sum of the error, its integral, and its derivative.
GLOS: Lead compensator || A zero-pole pair, zero first, that adds phase near the crossover frequency.
GLOS: Lag compensator || A pole-zero pair, pole first, that adds low-frequency gain while leaving crossover nearly unchanged.

GLOS: Gain margin || The factor by which loop gain could increase, at the frequency where its phase is −180 degrees, before the loop oscillates.
