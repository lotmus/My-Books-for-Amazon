H1: Chapter 20 — The Inductor as a Part: Reading the Datasheet
P: Chapter 9 introduced the real inductor: its core can saturate, its winding has resistance, and its own capacitance gives it a self-resonant frequency. This chapter takes the next step a designer actually faces, which is choosing one particular part from a catalog of thousands. The physics of inductance stays in Book 1, and the design of the converters that use power inductors belongs to Book 6. What lives here is the datasheet: what each number means, which current waveform each one must be checked against, and which numbers are easiest to misread.
H2: 20.1 The Columns of a Power-Inductor Table
P: A power-inductor datasheet usually gives, for each part number, an inductance value with its tolerance, a DC resistance, two current ratings, and often a self-resonant frequency. The inductance is measured at a small test signal, commonly 100 kHz and 0.1 V or 1 V, with no DC current flowing. Tolerances of ±20% are normal for power parts, and ±30% is not unusual; precision is not what these parts are for.
P: The two current ratings are the ones most often confused. The saturation current I_sat is the DC current at which the inductance has fallen by a stated amount, commonly 20% or 30% below its zero-current value. It is a magnetic limit, set by the core, and it is checked against the peak current the part will ever carry. The heating current, often labeled I_rms or I_temp, is the current that raises the part's temperature by a stated amount, commonly 40 °C, through losses in its winding. It is a thermal limit, set mostly by the DC resistance, and it is checked against the rms current.
MIS: An inductor's two current ratings do not describe the same thing, and neither is a "maximum current." A part can run comfortably below its heating current while its peak current drives it into saturation on every cycle, or it can sit well below saturation while overheating. Both checks are required, each against its own measure of the current.
H2: 20.2 The Current Waveform the Part Must Survive
P: An inductor in a switching circuit carries a DC current with a triangular ripple on top of it. Chapter 9 checked a peak against I_sat; here the ripple comes from the circuit. In a buck regulator, whose full design is the subject of Book 6, the ripple's peak-to-peak value is set by the voltage across the inductor while the switch is on and the time it is on:
EQ: ΔI = (V_in − V_out) · D / (L · f_sw),   D = V_out / V_in (ideal)
P: The peak current is the DC current plus half the ripple. The rms current of a triangle riding on a DC level is only slightly above the DC value:
EQ: I_pk = I_DC + ΔI/2,    I_rms = √(I_DC² + ΔI²/12)
WE: Worked Example 20.1
PROB: A buck regulator converts 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A, switching at 500 kHz, with a 10 µH inductor. Find the ripple, the peak current, and the rms current, and state the minimum I_sat and heating-current ratings, allowing 20% margin on I_sat.
SOL: The ideal duty cycle is D = 3.3/12 = 0.275. The ripple is ΔI = (12 − 3.3) × 0.275/(10×10⁻⁶ × 500×10³) = 2.39/5 ≈ 0.479 A peak-to-peak. The peak current is 2 + 0.24 ≈ 2.24 A. The rms current is √(2² + 0.479²/12) ≈ 2.005 A, essentially the DC value. With 20% margin, I_sat should be at least 1.2 × 2.24 ≈ 2.7 A. The heating current should be at least 2 A, and more if the board around the inductor is already warm. Note also that the 10 µH could be as low as 8 µH at its −20% tolerance, which would raise the ripple to about 0.60 A and the peak to about 2.30 A; a careful selection checks the low-tolerance corner as well.
H2: 20.3 DC Resistance and Copper Loss
P: The winding loss is the rms current squared times the resistance. At the frequencies of a typical regulator, skin and proximity effects raise the winding's effective resistance for the ripple component, but because the ripple is small compared with the DC current, the DC resistance gives a good first estimate. Core loss, from the ripple's alternating flux in the ferrite, adds to it and is usually given by the manufacturer as a curve or an online calculator rather than as a single number.
WE: Worked Example 20.2
PROB: The inductor chosen for Worked Example 20.1 has a DC resistance of 30 mΩ. Estimate its copper loss and its share of a 6.6 W output.
SOL: P = I_rms² × DCR = 2.005² × 0.030 ≈ 0.121 W. That is about 1.8% of the 6.6 W output, a significant part of a regulator's total losses. A part with half the DCR is usually physically larger or has lower inductance; trading among size, loss, and ripple is the real substance of choosing a power inductor.
H2: 20.4 Soft and Hard Saturation
P: Not all cores saturate the same way. A ferrite core with a small air gap holds its inductance nearly flat up to I_sat and then falls quickly; it is said to saturate hard. A powdered-iron or metal-composite core, in which the gap is distributed through the material itself, loses inductance gradually from low currents onward; it saturates soft. A hard-saturating part must never see its peak exceed I_sat, because just beyond it the ripple grows rapidly, which raises the peak further. A soft-saturating part tolerates brief overloads gracefully but delivers less than its nominal inductance at its rated current.

H2: 20.5 Shielded or Unshielded
P: An unshielded drum-core inductor leaves part of its magnetic field outside the part, where it can couple into nearby traces, other inductors, or a sensitive feedback node. A semi-shielded part adds a magnetic coating, and a fully shielded part encloses the winding in a closed magnetic path. Shielded parts cost a little more and, for the same size, have somewhat lower inductance or current ratings, but in a dense board the reduction in coupled interference usually decides the question. Chapter 9's toroid is the same idea in its oldest form.
H2: 20.6 Ferrite Beads Are Not Small Inductors
P: A ferrite bead looks like a chip inductor and is placed in series with a supply or signal line, but it is designed to be lossy. Its datasheet gives its impedance at 100 MHz, perhaps 600 Ω, and a plot of impedance versus frequency in which a resistive part dominates above some tens of megahertz. A bead therefore absorbs high-frequency noise as heat, rather than reflecting it as an inductor would. Two cautions apply. A bead's impedance falls sharply with DC current, often to a fraction of its rated value at half its current rating. And a bead in series with a supply, followed by a decoupling capacitor, forms an LC circuit at low frequency where the bead is mostly inductive; that circuit can resonate and amplify noise near its resonance instead of removing it.
H2: 20.7 Inductors in Signal Filters
P: When an inductor is used in a filter or resonant circuit rather than a regulator, the important columns change. The quality factor Q at the operating frequency, introduced in Chapter 9, sets the loss and the sharpness of the response. The self-resonant frequency should sit well above the highest frequency the filter must handle, commonly by a factor of ten, because the inductance effectively rises as the SRF is approached. Tolerance matters again: a 5% or 2% part is chosen when the filter's corner frequency must be accurate.
KEY: Check peak current against I_sat and rms current against the heating rating, at the low-tolerance corner of the inductance. || Then decide among DCR, size, shielding, and saturation style; those trade-offs, not the inductance value, are what distinguish one catalog part from another.

SUM: A power inductor is selected against two different current checks: peak current against the saturation rating and rms current against the heating rating. The circuit sets the ripple, ΔI = (V_in − V_out)D/(L f_sw), and the peak and rms follow from it; checks should include the low-tolerance inductance. DC resistance sets copper loss. Cores saturate hard or soft; shielding limits stray field. Ferrite beads are lossy parts specified by impedance, not inductance, and can resonate with decoupling capacitors.
