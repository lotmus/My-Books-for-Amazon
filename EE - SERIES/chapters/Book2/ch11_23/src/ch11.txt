H1: Chapter 11 — Negative Feedback
P: Chapter 5 used negative feedback as a given: wire the output back to the inverting input, and the golden rules take over. That was a fair shortcut for an ideal op-amp with infinite gain. A real amplifier has a large but finite gain that wanders with temperature, supply voltage, and the individual part pulled from the reel, and the designer still expects the closed-loop gain to land within a fraction of a percent. This chapter answers the question underneath Chapter 5: why does wrapping an imprecise, high-gain amplifier in a precise feedback network produce a precise result, and what does the loop take in exchange?
H2: 11.1 The Loop in One Diagram
P: Every negative-feedback amplifier, whatever its circuit details, reduces to the same three-block picture. A forward amplifier with gain A takes an error signal and produces the output. A feedback network, usually just resistors, samples the output and returns a fraction β of it. A subtractor forms the error as the input minus that returned fraction. In an op-amp circuit the subtractor is the op-amp's own differential input, A is its open-loop gain, and β is set by the resistor divider from the output back to the inverting pin.
P: Writing the three relations down and eliminating the error signal gives the closed-loop gain:
EQ: G = Vₒᵤₜ / Vᵢₙ = A / (1 + Aβ)
P: The product T = Aβ is the loop gain. It is the gain a signal would see if you broke the loop at any point and traveled once around it. When T is large, the 1 in the denominator hardly matters, and G approaches 1/β. That limit is the whole trick. The closed-loop gain then depends on the feedback network, which is made of resistors you can buy to 0.1%, and hardly at all on A, which may be specified only as "at least 50,000."

WE: Worked Example 11.1
PROB: A non-inverting amplifier uses R_f = 99 kΩ and Rᵢₙ = 1 kΩ around an op-amp with open-loop gain A = 100,000. Find β, the ideal gain, the actual closed-loop gain, and the gain error in percent.
SOL: The feedback divider returns β = Rᵢₙ/(Rᵢₙ + R_f) = 1k/100k = 0.01 of the output. The ideal gain is 1/β = 100. The loop gain is T = Aβ = 100,000 × 0.01 = 1000. The actual gain is G = A/(1 + T) = 100,000/1001 ≈ 99.90. The error is (100 − 99.90)/100 ≈ 0.0999%, or about 1/T. That last observation generalizes: the fractional gain error of a feedback amplifier is roughly one over its loop gain.
KEY: The closed-loop gain of a negative-feedback amplifier is approximately 1/β, and its fractional error is approximately 1/T. || A loop gain of 1000 buys about 0.1% accuracy; a loop gain of 100 buys about 1%.
H2: 11.2 Desensitivity: Why the Wandering Gain Stops Mattering
P: Suppose the open-loop gain changes by some fraction. How much does the closed-loop gain change? Differentiating G = A/(1 + Aβ) with respect to A and rearranging gives a clean result:
EQ: (ΔG / G) ≈ (1 / (1 + T)) · (ΔA / A)
P: The quantity 1 + T is called the desensitivity factor, or the amount of feedback. Whatever percentage the forward gain wanders, the closed-loop gain wanders by that percentage divided by 1 + T. This is the reason a manufacturer can ship op-amps whose open-loop gain varies by a factor of three from part to part: nobody who uses them with feedback ever sees the difference.
WE: Worked Example 11.2
PROB: The amplifier of Worked Example 11.1 is heated, and its open-loop gain falls by half, from 100,000 to 50,000. Find the new closed-loop gain and its percentage change.
SOL: The loop gain falls to T = 50,000 × 0.01 = 500. The new closed-loop gain is G = 50,000/501 ≈ 99.80. The change from 99.90 is about 0.10%. The open-loop gain changed by 50%; the closed-loop gain changed by a tenth of a percent, almost exactly 50% divided by the original 1 + T = 1001. A designer who needs the gain to hold within 0.2% over temperature can therefore use this part, even though its own gain is anything but stable.
MIS: Feedback does not make the gain "exact." It makes the gain depend on the feedback network instead of the amplifier. If the feedback resistors drift by 1%, the closed-loop gain drifts by 1%, and no amount of loop gain will correct it. Precision moves into the resistors; it does not disappear from the design.
H2: 11.3 Feedback Trades Gain for Bandwidth
P: Chapter 5 declared a general-purpose op-amp whose open-loop gain is 100,000 at DC and falls at 20 dB per decade from a dominant pole at 10 Hz, crossing unity at 1 MHz. Feeding back a fraction β moves that pole. For a single-pole forward amplifier, the closed-loop gain is still single-pole, but its DC value has fallen by the factor 1 + A₀β and its pole has risen by the same factor:
EQ: f_cl = f_p · (1 + A₀β) ≈ A₀ f_p β = GBW · β
P: With β = 0.01, the 10 Hz pole moves to about 10 kHz, and the gain falls from 100,000 to 100. The product of gain and bandwidth stays at 1 MHz. Feedback did not create bandwidth; it spent gain the circuit did not need and bought bandwidth with it. Chapter 13 develops this trade into a working tool under its proper name, noise gain.
H2: 11.4 Feedback Moves Impedances
P: Feedback also changes the impedance a source and a load see, and the direction of the change depends on how the loop connects. A non-inverting amplifier samples the output voltage and subtracts a voltage in series with the input. That arrangement, called series-shunt feedback, multiplies the input impedance by 1 + T and divides the output impedance by 1 + T. The circuit becomes a better voltage amplifier on both ends: it draws less current from the source and holds its output voltage more firmly under load.
WE: Worked Example 11.3
PROB: An op-amp has a differential input resistance of 2 MΩ and an open-loop output resistance of 75 Ω. It is used in the gain-of-100 amplifier from Worked Example 11.1, where T = 1000 at DC. Estimate the closed-loop input and output resistances.
SOL: The input resistance rises to about 2 MΩ × 1001 ≈ 2 GΩ. The output resistance falls to about 75 Ω/1001 ≈ 0.075 Ω, or 75 mΩ. Both improvements shrink as frequency rises, because T falls with the open-loop gain. At 10 kHz, where this amplifier's loop gain has dropped to about 1, the output resistance is back near its open-loop value, and the output looks inductive in between, a fact that matters when Chapter 12 connects a capacitor to it.
P: The inverting amplifier connects differently. It still samples the output voltage, but it sums currents at the inverting node instead of subtracting a series voltage. The output impedance still drops by 1 + T, but the input impedance becomes simply Rᵢₙ, because the virtual ground holds one end of that resistor at 0 V. Neither result is wrong; they describe two different loops.
H2: 11.5 Distortion Is Divided Too
P: A forward amplifier that bends its transfer curve adds harmonics the input did not have. Inside a loop, that distortion appears at the output, is fed back, is subtracted from the input, and is driven back out of the output in the opposite direction. To a first approximation, any distortion generated inside the forward path is reduced by the factor 1 + T at the frequency where it appears. An output stage that would produce 5% distortion open-loop, wrapped in a loop with T = 100 at the signal frequency, delivers roughly 0.05%.
P: The qualification "at the frequency where it appears" matters. Harmonics of a 5 kHz signal land at 10, 15, and 20 kHz, where the loop gain is smaller. A loop that looks generous at the fundamental can be thin at the third harmonic, which is why audio designers chase loop gain at 20 kHz, not at 1 kHz.
H2: 11.6 The Sign Is the Whole Contract
P: Every benefit in this chapter came from one sign: the returned signal is subtracted. Return it with the opposite sign and the denominator becomes 1 − T. When T approaches 1, the gain heads toward infinity, and the circuit latches against a rail or oscillates. That is positive feedback, and Chapter 16 uses it on purpose to build a comparator with hysteresis.
P: The golden rules of Chapter 5 belong only to the negative case. A virtual short and a virtual open exist because the loop is constantly correcting the error toward zero. Remove the loop, or flip its sign, and the inputs are just two pins at whatever voltages the outside world puts on them.
MIS: A circuit wired for negative feedback is not guaranteed to have negative feedback at every frequency. Each pole in the loop adds phase lag. If the lag reaches 180 degrees at a frequency where T is still at least 1, the subtraction has turned into an addition there, and the amplifier oscillates at that frequency. Chapters 12, 13, and 18 show how to measure how far a loop is from that point.

SUM: A feedback amplifier's gain is A/(1 + Aβ), which approaches 1/β when the loop gain T = Aβ is large; its fractional gain error is about 1/T. Feedback divides gain variations and distortion by 1 + T, trades gain for bandwidth at a constant gain-bandwidth product, and moves input and output impedances in a direction set by how the loop connects. All of it depends on the returned signal being subtracted.
GLOS: Loop gain (T) || The product Aβ of forward gain and feedback fraction; the gain seen once around a feedback loop.
GLOS: Desensitivity factor || The quantity 1 + T, by which feedback divides changes in forward gain, distortion, and output impedance.
