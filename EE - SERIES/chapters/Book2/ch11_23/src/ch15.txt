H1: Chapter 15 — DC Errors: Offset, Bias Current, and Drift
P: Ground the input of a high-gain amplifier and measure its output with a good voltmeter. It will not read zero. It may read a few millivolts, or a few hundred, and the reading will creep as the board warms up. None of this is noise in the sense of Chapter 14; it is a steady error that would be there on every measurement, and it can be budgeted, predicted, and in many cases removed. This chapter builds that budget from three datasheet numbers: input offset voltage, input bias current, and their temperature drift.
H2: 15.1 Input Offset Voltage
P: The two input transistors of an op-amp are never perfectly matched. The result behaves exactly as if a small DC voltage source, the input offset voltage V_os, were connected in series with one input of an otherwise perfect op-amp. Because it sits at the input, it is amplified by the noise gain from Chapter 13, not by the signal gain:
EQ: V_out,os = V_os × NG = V_os × (1 + R_f / Rᵢₙ)
P: General-purpose bipolar op-amps have V_os in the range of 1 to 5 mV. Precision bipolar parts reach tens of microvolts. Zero-drift parts, which continually measure and cancel their own offset, reach a few microvolts.
WE: Worked Example 15.1
PROB: An inverting amplifier with gain −100 uses R_f = 1 MΩ and Rᵢₙ = 10 kΩ. The op-amp has V_os = 2 mV. Find the output error with the input grounded.
SOL: The noise gain is 1 + 1M/10k = 101. The output error is 2 mV × 101 = 202 mV. Against a full-scale output of perhaps 10 V, that is 2% of full scale before a signal has been applied, far larger than any gain error from Chapter 11.
H2: 15.2 Input Bias Current and Offset Current
P: Bipolar input transistors need base current to operate, and even FET inputs leak a little through their protection structures. This input bias current I_b flows into (or out of) both inputs. At the inverting input it has only one path, through the resistors connected there, and because the inverting node is held near ground, the current flows through R_f and appears at the output as a voltage I_b R_f.
P: The standard compensation adds a resistor R_c in series with the non-inverting input, equal to the parallel combination R_f ‖ Rᵢₙ. The bias current at that pin then produces a matching drop, and the two errors cancel to the extent that the two bias currents are equal. What remains is set by their difference, the input offset current I_os, which is typically a fraction of I_b:
EQ: Uncompensated: V_out,b ≈ I_b R_f    Compensated: V_out,b ≈ I_os R_f
WE: Worked Example 15.2
PROB: The amplifier of Worked Example 15.1 uses an op-amp with I_b = 80 nA and I_os = 20 nA. Find the output error from bias current without compensation, the value of R_c, and the error with compensation.
SOL: Without compensation, 80 nA × 1 MΩ = 80 mV. The compensation resistor is R_c = 1M ‖ 10k ≈ 9.90 kΩ, and a standard 9.76 kΩ or 10 kΩ part is close enough. With it, the error falls to I_os R_f = 20 nA × 1 MΩ = 20 mV. Chapter 5 pointed out the price: R_c adds its own Johnson noise, about 12.6 nV/√Hz at 290 K, which a low-noise design may not be able to afford. A bypass capacitor across R_c removes that noise at signal frequencies while keeping the DC correction.
H2: 15.3 Drift and the Worst-Case Budget
P: Both V_os and I_b change with temperature, and an initial offset can be trimmed out at the factory, while drift cannot. Datasheets give the offset drift as a coefficient in µV/°C, and bias current often roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in a FET-input part. A worst-case DC budget adds all contributions with their signs aligned, because nothing guarantees they will cancel:
EQ: V_out,err = NG × (V_os + ΔT · dV_os/dT) + I_os R_f + (other terms)
WE: Worked Example 15.3
PROB: The op-amp of Worked Example 15.1 has an offset drift of 10 µV/°C. The board operates from 25 °C to 75 °C. Assuming the initial 2 mV offset has been trimmed out at 25 °C and bias-current compensation is used, estimate the worst-case output error at 75 °C, ignoring the drift of I_os.
SOL: The offset drifts by 10 µV/°C × 50 °C = 0.5 mV, which appears at the output as 0.5 mV × 101 ≈ 50.5 mV. The compensated bias-current term adds 20 mV. The worst-case total is about 70.5 mV. Trimming removed the larger initial term, but drift and offset current together still produce about 0.7% of a 10 V full scale.

H2: 15.4 Supply and Common-Mode Terms
P: Two more specifications behave like extra offset. The power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR) states how much a change in supply voltage shifts the input offset; 80 dB means 100 mV of supply ripple acts like 10 µV at the input. The common-mode rejection ratio from Chapter 12 does the same for common-mode voltage. Both terms are usually small in an inverting stage and should be checked in a follower or a stage running from a noisy supply.
H2: 15.5 The Integrator Is a DC-Error Magnifier
P: An ideal integrator, a capacitor C in the feedback path and a resistor R at the input, has infinite gain at DC. Its noise gain at DC is therefore infinite too, and any offset or bias current does not produce a fixed error; it produces a ramp.
WE: Worked Example 15.4
PROB: An integrator uses R = 100 kΩ and C = 1 µF with an op-amp having V_os = 1 mV and I_b = 10 nA. With the input grounded, how fast does the output drift, and how long until it reaches a 10 V rail?
SOL: The offset forces a current V_os/R = 1 mV/100 kΩ = 10 nA through R, which must flow through C. The bias current adds another 10 nA in the worst-case direction. The capacitor charges at I/C = 20 nA/1 µF = 0.02 V/s. The output reaches 10 V in 10/0.02 = 500 s, a little over eight minutes. A practical integrator therefore needs one of three things: a reset switch across C, a large resistor across C that gives it a finite DC gain, or an outer feedback loop, such as the control loops of Chapter 17, that keeps its input centered.
KEY: An offset is amplified by the noise gain, and a bias current is converted to voltage by the feedback resistor. || Trim the initial value if you must, but budget the drift, because drift is what remains in the field.
MIS: A zero-drift op-amp does not make the whole circuit drift-free. Its own V_os may be a microvolt, but resistor ratios, thermocouple voltages at dissimilar-metal junctions on the board, and leakage across a dirty board surface can each add tens of microvolts. In precision work the op-amp is often no longer the dominant DC error once it has been chosen well.
H2: 15.6 Removing What Cannot Be Budgeted
P: When a signal carries no information at DC, AC coupling removes every DC error at once: a series capacitor and a resistor to ground form a high-pass filter whose corner sits below the lowest frequency of interest. When DC matters, the remaining choices are a better op-amp, smaller resistors where bias current dominates, a trim performed at calibration, or a chopper or auto-zero technique that measures the error periodically and subtracts it. Each choice trades cost, noise, or bandwidth; the budget tells you which term to attack first.
H2: Practice Problems
PP: A non-inverting amplifier has gain 50 and its op-amp has V_os = 3 mV. Find the output offset.
PP: An inverting amplifier uses R_f = 470 kΩ and Rᵢₙ = 4.7 kΩ. Find the value of the bias-current compensation resistor.
PP: The amplifier of the previous problem uses an op-amp with I_b = 50 nA and I_os = 5 nA. Find the output error from bias current with and without compensation.
PP: An op-amp has an offset drift of 3 µV/°C and is used at a noise gain of 200 over a 60 °C range. Find the drift-induced output change.
PP: An op-amp has PSRR = 90 dB, and its supply carries 200 mV of ripple. Find the equivalent input-referred offset.
PP: An integrator uses R = 1 MΩ and C = 100 nF with an op-amp of V_os = 0.5 mV and negligible bias current. Find the output drift rate with the input grounded.
SOLN: 3 mV × 50 = 150 mV.
SOLN: R_c = 470k ‖ 4.7k ≈ 4.65 kΩ.
SOLN: Without compensation, 50 nA × 470 kΩ = 23.5 mV. With compensation, 5 nA × 470 kΩ = 2.35 mV.
SOLN: 3 µV/°C × 60 °C × 200 = 36 mV.
SOLN: 90 dB is a ratio of about 31,600; 200 mV/31,600 ≈ 6.3 µV.
SOLN: Current = 0.5 mV/1 MΩ = 0.5 nA; drift rate = 0.5 nA/100 nF = 5 mV/s.
SUM: Offset voltage is amplified by the noise gain; bias current flowing through the feedback resistor produces an output error that a matched resistor at the non-inverting input reduces to the offset-current term. Drift remains after trimming and must be budgeted worst-case. PSRR and CMRR act as additional offsets. Integrators turn DC errors into ramps and need a reset, a DC feedback path, or an outer loop.
GLOS: Input offset voltage (V_os) || The DC voltage that must be applied between an op-amp's inputs to make its output zero; modeled as a source in series with one input.
GLOS: Input bias current (I_b) || The DC current that flows into each op-amp input.
GLOS: Input offset current (I_os) || The difference between the bias currents of the two inputs.
GLOS: Power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR) || The ratio of a supply-voltage change to the resulting change in input-referred offset.
FORM: Offset at output || V_out = V_os (1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ)
FORM: Bias-current compensation || R_c = R_f ‖ Rᵢₙ; residual ≈ I_os R_f
