H1: Chapter 12 — Followers, Buffers, and Loading
P: Chapter 5 closed with a short claim: the voltage follower is a wire, and the inverting buffer is a different machine. This chapter earns that claim with numbers. It starts from the problem a buffer exists to solve, a weak source that cannot drive the load it is asked to drive, then shows what the follower gets right, where its accuracy actually comes from, and the one load that can turn a perfectly ordinary follower into an oscillator.
H2: 12.1 Why a Source Needs a Buffer
P: Every real source has an output resistance. A thermocouple amplifier, a high-value voltage divider, a piezo sensor, or a filter built from 100 kΩ resistors all present their signal through a resistance that, together with whatever load is attached, forms a voltage divider. Book 1's divider rule gives the result directly, and it is rarely kind.
WE: Worked Example 12.1
PROB: A sensor produces a signal through a 100 kΩ output resistance. It must drive an analog-to-digital converter input that looks like 10 kΩ. Find the fraction of the signal that reaches the converter, in ratio and in decibels.
SOL: The load and source resistance form a divider: Vₗₒₐd/Vₛ = 10k/(100k + 10k) = 0.0909. In decibels, 20 log₁₀(0.0909) ≈ −20.8 dB. Ninety-one percent of the signal is lost before it is ever measured, and the loss changes whenever the sensor's resistance changes with temperature, which is worse than the loss itself.
P: The cure is a stage whose input draws almost nothing and whose output can supply the load's current from its own supply rails. That is a buffer. It provides no voltage gain at all; it provides current gain, and in doing so it separates the impedance the source must see from the impedance the load presents.
H2: 12.2 The Follower and Where Its Accuracy Comes From
P: The follower connects the output directly to the inverting input and applies the signal to the non-inverting input. The feedback fraction is β = 1, the largest a passive network can return, so the loop gain equals the open-loop gain and the closed-loop gain is A/(1 + A). With the declared A₀ = 100,000 of Chapter 5, the gain is 0.99999, an error of 10 parts per million at DC.
P: The input impedance is the op-amp's own input impedance, raised further by the loop as Chapter 11 showed. For a CMOS-input part this is effectively the leakage of its protection diodes, often well above a gigaohm. The output impedance is the open-loop value divided by 1 + A. Applied to Worked Example 12.1, a follower between sensor and converter delivers essentially the full signal: the sensor now sees a gigaohm load instead of 10 kΩ, and the converter sees a fraction of an ohm instead of 100 kΩ.
P: The follower has one accuracy limit the inverting stages do not share. Both of its inputs move with the signal, so the whole signal appears as common-mode voltage at the op-amp's inputs. Two specifications therefore matter more for a follower than for any other configuration: the input common-mode range, which must include every value the signal can take, and the common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR), which converts that common-mode swing into an input-referred error.
WE: Worked Example 12.2
PROB: A follower carries a signal that swings 2 V. The op-amp's CMRR is 90 dB. Estimate the worst-case input-referred error caused by common-mode swing.
SOL: Converting from decibels, 90 dB is a ratio of 10^(90/20) ≈ 31,600. The error is about 2 V/31,600 ≈ 63 µV, or roughly 32 parts per million of the 2 V swing. That is small, but it is larger than the 10 ppm gain error from finite open-loop gain, and it grows quickly if the signal approaches the edge of the common-mode range, where CMRR collapses.
KEY: A follower's accuracy is limited first by common-mode behavior, not by open-loop gain. || Check the input common-mode range against the full signal swing, including any overshoot, before trusting the follower's 10 ppm gain error.
H2: 12.3 The Inverting Buffer Loads Its Source
P: An inverting amplifier with R_f = Rᵢₙ has a gain magnitude of one, and it is tempting to call it a buffer. It is not one in the same sense. Its inverting input is a virtual ground, so the source sees Rᵢₙ to ground, not an open circuit. Any source resistance adds to Rᵢₙ and changes the gain.
WE: Worked Example 12.3
PROB: The 100 kΩ sensor of Worked Example 12.1 drives an inverting unity-gain stage built with Rᵢₙ = R_f = 10 kΩ. Find the actual gain from the sensor's internal voltage to the output.
SOL: The source resistance is in series with Rᵢₙ, so the effective input resistor is 110 kΩ. The gain is −R_f/(Rₛ + Rᵢₙ) = −10k/110k ≈ −0.0909. The inverting stage has reproduced exactly the same 91% loss as connecting the converter directly; it has only flipped the sign. Raising both resistors to 10 MΩ would reduce the loss, but Chapters 14 and 15 will show the noise and bias-current price of resistors that large.
P: The inverting stage still has real virtues. Its inputs sit at a fixed voltage, so common-mode range and CMRR hardly matter. It can sum several inputs at the virtual-ground node. Its noise gain is two rather than one, which halves its bandwidth compared with the follower. Choose it when you need the sign, a summing node, or a signal that would otherwise exceed the common-mode range, and when the source can afford to drive Rᵢₙ.
H2: 12.4 The Capacitive Load
P: The follower's most common failure is not inaccuracy. It is oscillation when driving a capacitor: a long cable, a converter's sampling capacitor, a reference pin's bypass capacitor, or a MOSFET gate. The cause is the op-amp's open-loop output resistance r_o. Together with the load capacitance C_L, it forms an RC low-pass filter inside the feedback loop, with a pole at
EQ: f₂ = 1 / (2π r_o C_L)
P: A follower's loop gain already crosses unity near the full gain-bandwidth product, where the op-amp's dominant pole has used up 90 degrees of phase. The new pole adds more lag, and the phase margin, the distance from −180 degrees at the crossover frequency, shrinks.
WE: Worked Example 12.4
PROB: The declared op-amp (GBW = 1 MHz, single dominant pole) has r_o = 50 Ω and is used as a follower. Estimate the phase margin when it drives 1 nF and when it drives 10 nF.
SOL: For 1 nF, f₂ = 1/(2π × 50 × 1×10⁻⁹) ≈ 3.18 MHz. The loop gain crosses unity at about 0.96 MHz, slightly below 1 MHz because the second pole has begun to reduce the gain. The extra lag there is arctan(0.96/3.18) ≈ 17°, leaving a phase margin of about 73°, which is comfortable. For 10 nF, f₂ ≈ 318 kHz, a third of the crossover frequency. Solving for the new crossover gives about 520 kHz, where the extra lag is arctan(520/318) ≈ 59°, leaving about 31° of phase margin. Chapter 5's rule of thumb says roughly 30° rings, and a step response will show exactly that: a sharp overshoot with several cycles of ringing before it settles.
FIG: fig12_1.png | Figure 12.1 — Phase margin of a follower (GBW = 1 MHz, r_o = 50 Ω) versus load capacitance; the margin drops below 45° near 4.7 nF.
P: Three remedies are standard. The first is an isolation resistor of a few tens of ohms between the op-amp output and the capacitor, outside the feedback loop. The loop then sees a resistive load at high frequency and keeps its margin, at the cost of a small DC error if the load draws current and a slower edge at the capacitor. The second, used when the capacitor's voltage must be exact, closes a DC feedback path from the far side of the isolation resistor and a high-frequency path from the op-amp output through a small capacitor. The third is simply to choose an op-amp specified as stable into the load in question; many datasheets quote the largest capacitance a unity-gain follower can drive.
MIS: A follower is not automatically the most stable configuration. It is the configuration with the least phase margin to spare, because its loop gain is the highest a passive network allows and therefore crosses unity at the highest frequency. A gain-of-ten stage driving the same capacitor crosses unity a decade lower and tolerates far more load capacitance.
H2: 12.5 Choosing Between the Two
P: The decision usually takes four questions. Can the source tolerate a resistive load? If not, use the follower. Does the signal stay inside the op-amp's common-mode range with margin? If not, use the inverting stage, or an op-amp with rail-to-rail inputs. Is a sign inversion acceptable or wanted? Is the load capacitive? If it is, budget an isolation resistor before the first prototype, not after the first oscillation.

PP: An op-amp with r_o = 40 Ω drives a 2.2 nF capacitor. Find the frequency of the pole this load adds to the loop.

SOLN: 80 dB is a ratio of 10,000; error ≈ 3 V/10,000 = 300 µV.
SOLN: Gain = −100k/(22k + 100k) ≈ −0.820.
SOLN: f₂ = 1/(2π × 40 × 2.2×10⁻⁹) ≈ 1.81 MHz.
SOLN: Above a few megahertz the resistor, not the capacitor, dominates the impedance the op-amp output sees, so the load no longer forms an extra pole inside the loop near crossover; the capacitor's pole now sits outside the loop and only slows the edge at the capacitor.
SUM: A buffer supplies current gain so a weak source is not loaded down. The follower has unity gain, an input impedance near that of the op-amp itself, and a gain error near 1/A, but it is limited by common-mode range and CMRR and has the least phase margin of any configuration when driving a capacitor. The inverting unity-gain stage loads its source with Rᵢₙ, avoids common-mode issues, and has half the follower's bandwidth. Capacitive loads are tamed with an isolation resistor, split feedback, or a part rated for the load.
GLOS: Buffer || A stage that provides current gain without voltage gain, isolating a weak source from a demanding load.
GLOS: Common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) || The ratio of an op-amp's differential gain to its common-mode gain; it converts common-mode input swing into an input-referred error.
GLOS: Isolation resistor || A small resistor placed between an op-amp output and a capacitive load, outside the feedback loop, to preserve phase margin.
FORM: Capacitive-load pole || f₂ = 1 / (2π r_o C_L)
