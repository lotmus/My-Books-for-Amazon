H1: Preface — How to Use This Book
P: This book assumes Book 1, Foundations of Electronics, as its starting point — specifically its Part III, which already covers Kirchhoff's laws, nodal and mesh analysis, Thévenin and Norton equivalents, superposition, and first-order transients in full. Rather than repeat that material, this book moves directly into what those techniques were building toward: real components with real limitations, and the analog and digital building blocks — op-amps, filters, and logic — that Book 1's idealized circuit elements were always in service of.
P: Each chapter follows the same shape as Book 1: it opens with the question the chapter answers, builds the picture in words first, works through it with fully solved examples, and closes with practice problems whose solutions appear in Appendix B. A Key Idea box marks the one fact worth carrying forward from a section; a Common Misconception box heads off a specific, predictable error before it happens.
H1: How This Book Is Organized
P: Chapters 1–2 build the vocabulary shared by every later chapter: bias points, headroom, and small-signal behavior for analog circuits, and logic levels and noise margins for digital ones. Chapters 3–4 develop digital design specifically, from Boolean algebra through sequential circuits with memory. Chapter 5 introduces the operational amplifier as an ideal building block, in the same spirit Book 1 used for the resistor, capacitor, and inductor. Chapter 6 generalizes Book 1's filter and resonance results into a full filter-design vocabulary, passive and active, and carries it through to Sallen-Key and multiple-feedback designs built from standard parts. Chapters 7–9 return to the resistor, capacitor, and inductor themselves as real, physical parts: first their types, tolerances, ratings, and parasitics, then the practical side, from heat, drift, and pulse ratings to decoupling, real impedance, and reading an inductor datasheet.
P: Chapter 10 introduces the control loop that runs underneath a robot model. Chapters 11–16 return to the op-amp with real numbers: feedback as a quantitative tool, followers and capacitive loads, bandwidth and slew rate, noise, DC errors, and comparators. Chapters 17–19 build classical control on that foundation, from plant and controller through PID and lead-lag compensation to the digital loop and its sampling delay. Chapters 20–24 add five building blocks that join the book's two halves: the instrumentation amplifier, state feedback with observers, the sample-and-hold circuit, the voltage reference, and the H-bridge motor drive written as a plant for the loops of Chapters 17–19. Chapters 25–28 complete both halves: the root locus and the stability margins and sensitivity peak on the control side, and oscillators and digital timing with metastability on the circuit side.
KEY: This book does not re-teach Book 1's Part III. Network analysis (Thévenin, Norton, nodal, mesh) and transient analysis are assumed throughout — if a technique from those chapters feels unfamiliar, that is the place to go back to, not this book.
H1: About This Series
P: This book is the second of seven, covering circuits, components, and the classical control loop that later sits under a robot model. Each book assumes only the books before it in the list below — never the ones after.
P: Book — Covers
P: 1. Foundations — Physics, mathematics, and circuit theory
P: 2. Circuits, Components, and Control (this book) — Analog and digital, op-amps, passives, the classical loop
P: 3. Semiconductor Physics and Devices — Diodes, BJTs, MOSFETs, FinFET, compound semiconductors
P: 4. RF, Microwave, and Transceivers — Lines, matching, antennas, PAs, receivers, PLLs, radar
P: 5. Communications, Wireless, and SDR — Modulation, band plans, DSP and software radio
P: 6. Power and Energy — Regulators, magnetics, the grid, the rack
P: 7. Packaging, Layout, EMC, and Test — Boards, packages, cooling, emissions, the accredited lab, robots
KEY: You do not need the other six books to use this one. This book is written to stand on Book 1's foundation alone — treat the table as a map of where you have been and where a later interest can take you, not a syllabus you must complete in order.
