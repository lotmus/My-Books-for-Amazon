H1: Chapter 7. MEMS in 2026
P: Tilt your phone and a mass of silicon smaller than a grain of sand moves by less than the width of a virus. A capacitance change of a few attofarads, read thousands of times a second, tells the phone which way is up. The same few micrometers of moving silicon now also keep time, pick up voice, and filter radio channels.
P: Where you meet this: Every phone and smartwatch, every drone's flight controller, the MPU-6050 or ICM-42688 module on a hobby robot, every MEMS microphone in a smart speaker, every SAW or BAW filter in a phone's radio, and the MEMS oscillators that are replacing quartz crystals.
P: Microelectromechanical systems are mechanical structures, springs, masses, membranes, and resonators, made with the tools of integrated circuits. They sense motion in every phone and car, select radio channels in every handset's front end, pick up voice in every earbud, and increasingly replace quartz as the timing reference in small devices. This chapter is the series' one survey of MEMS. It develops the spring-mass model that underlies most of them, computes their sensitivity and noise limits, and surveys the 2026 state of the field. The piezoelectric films they use are Book 3's materials; the radios that buy their filters are Book 5's.
H2: 7.1 The spring and the mass
P: A MEMS accelerometer is a proof mass on silicon springs. Under an acceleration a below its resonant frequency f0, the mass moves by x = a/ω0², with ω0 = 2πf0 = √(k/m). The sensor measures that displacement, usually as a change in capacitance between the mass and fixed electrodes. A higher resonant frequency gives a wider bandwidth and better shock resistance but a smaller displacement per g, which is the basic trade of accelerometer design.
EQ: x = a / ω0²,    ω0 = √(k/m),    C = ε0 A / g,    ΔC/C ≈ x/g

A stiffer spring buys bandwidth and loses signal. | Log-log falling straight line of displacement in nanometers against resonant frequency in kilohertz, with points at 62.1 nanometers for 2 kilohertz and 9.9 nanometers for 5 kilohertz.
H2: Worked Example 7.1
P: Problem. An accelerometer has f0 = 5 kHz and a sensing gap of 2 µm with 0.1 mm² of electrode area. Find the displacement at 1 g, the rest capacitance, and the capacitance change.
P: Solution. ω0 = 2π × 5000 = 31,416 rad/s, so x = 9.81/31,416² = 9.9 nm, about one two-hundredth of the gap. The capacitance is 8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 10⁻⁷/(2 × 10⁻⁶) = 443 fF, and the change is about 443 × 9.9 × 10⁻⁹/(2 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.2 fF. The readout circuit must resolve a fraction of a femtofarad, which is why the sensor and its interface chip are designed together and often packaged together.
H2: 7.2 Thermal noise
P: A damped mechanical system at temperature T is constantly shaken by the molecules around it, the mechanical counterpart of a resistor's thermal noise. The equivalent acceleration noise density is set by the mass, the resonant frequency, and the quality factor Q. Small masses are noisy; high Q, achieved by sealing the structure in vacuum, reduces the noise but leaves the resonance lightly damped, which the electronics must then control.
EQ: an = √(4 kB T ω0 / (m Q))   (m/s² per √Hz)
H2: Worked Example 7.2
P: Problem. Find the thermal noise of a 1 µg proof mass with f0 = 5 kHz at 300 K, for Q = 1 and Q = 100.
P: Solution. For Q = 1, an = √(4 × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 300 × 31,416/10⁻⁹) = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴ m/s²/√Hz, or 74 µg/√Hz. For Q = 100 the noise is ten times lower, 7.4 µg/√Hz. Consumer accelerometers in 2026 reach roughly 50 to 200 µg/√Hz overall; navigation-grade MEMS sensors use heavier masses, vacuum packaging, and closed-loop operation to go much lower.
P: Key idea. A MEMS sensor is a spring and a mass. Its sensitivity is a/ω0², its noise falls with mass and Q, and its readout must resolve femtofarads, so the mechanical and electrical designs cannot be separated.
H2: 7.3 Resonators and timing
P: A MEMS resonator is a vibrating silicon structure whose high Q gives a sharp frequency reference. Q sets the resonance's bandwidth, f0/Q: a resonator at 70 MHz with Q = 10⁵ has a bandwidth of only 700 Hz. MEMS timing parts replace quartz crystals where size, shock resistance, and stability over temperature matter more than the very lowest phase noise. In 2025 SiTime introduced its Titan family of MEMS resonators in a 0.46 × 0.46 mm chip-scale package, an outline of 0.212 mm², against 1.2 mm² for the smallest 1.2 × 1.0 mm "1210" quartz package, a ratio of 5.7 in outline; the company quotes about seven times less board area, including the land pattern. The resonators are also offered as bare dies for co-packaging with a microcontroller or radio chip.
H2: Worked Example 7.3
P: Problem. Find the bandwidth of a 32 MHz resonator with Q = 50,000. Compare the Titan package outline with a 1.6 × 1.2 mm quartz package.
P: Solution. 32 × 10⁶/5 × 10⁴ = 640 Hz. The 1.6 × 1.2 mm package is 1.92 mm², 9.1 times the Titan's 0.212 mm².
H2: 7.4 Filters, microphones, and the 2026 picture
P: The radio-frequency filters in a phone's front end are acoustic devices: surface-acoustic-wave filters below about 2 GHz and bulk-acoustic-wave filters, thin piezoelectric films resonating through their thickness, at higher frequencies up to the 5 to 7 GHz of Wi-Fi and 5G. A phone may contain dozens. Aluminum nitride has been the standard piezoelectric film; aluminum scandium nitride, AlScN, which has a much stronger piezoelectric response, moved into production on 200 mm silicon wafers, widening filter bandwidths and enabling piezoelectric MEMS microphones and ultrasonic transducers. MEMS microphones, capacitive and piezoelectric, are in nearly every phone, earbud, and laptop. Consumer motion sensors increasingly include small on-chip processors that run classification models, detecting gestures or activity without waking the main processor. These are snapshots of 2026; the spring-mass physics of this chapter will outlast them.
H2: 7.5 Reading a MEMS microphone data sheet
P: A MEMS microphone's data sheet reduces it to a few numbers. Sensitivity is the output for a 1 kHz tone at 94 dB sound pressure level, 1 pascal, quoted in dBV or dBFS for digital parts. Signal-to-noise ratio is the 94 dB reference minus the self-noise expressed as an equivalent A-weighted sound level; 65 dB SNR means a self-noise of 29 dBA, and the best parts in 2026, such as Infineon's sealed-dual-membrane designs, reach about 70 to 75 dB. The acoustic overload point, AOP, is the level at which distortion reaches 10 %, about 120 to 135 dB SPL. The dynamic range is AOP minus self-noise. Phones use several microphones and beamforming, Book 5's array gain in air, so matching sensitivity and phase between parts, typically within ±1 dB, matters as much as each part's own figures.
H2: Worked Example 7.4
P: Problem. A microphone has 65 dB SNR and an AOP of 130 dB SPL. Find its self-noise and dynamic range, and the SNR improvement from beamforming four such microphones on a distant talker, if their noise is independent.
P: Solution. Self-noise is 94 − 65 = 29 dBA, and the dynamic range 130 − 29 = 101 dB. Averaging four microphones adds the signal coherently and the self-noise in power, a gain of 10 log₁₀4 = 6 dB, to 71 dB, before any rejection of room noise from other directions.
H2: 7.6 Temperature and the frequency of a silicon resonator
P: Silicon becomes softer as it warms: its Young's modulus falls by about 60 ppm per kelvin, and because a resonator's frequency goes as the square root of stiffness, an uncompensated silicon resonator drifts by about −30 ppm/K. Over the industrial range of −40 to 85 °C that is several thousand parts per million, far outside the ±20 to ±50 ppm that timing applications need. MEMS oscillators deal with it in two ways. Passive compensation adds layers whose stiffness rises with temperature, such as silicon dioxide, or uses heavily doped silicon, flattening the curve to a few ppm over the range. Active compensation measures the temperature on the same die and corrects the output frequency with a fractional-N synthesizer, using a calibration table written at the factory. Quartz cut at the right angle is naturally flat, which is why quartz dominated timing for decades; MEMS won its place by combining compensation with small size and robustness to shock.
EQ: TCf ≈ ½ TCE ≈ −30 ppm/K  (uncompensated silicon)
H2: Worked Example 7.5
P: Problem. An uncompensated 32 MHz silicon resonator has TCf = −30 ppm/K. Find its total frequency change over −40 to 85 °C in ppm and in hertz. What residual linear coefficient would keep the total within ±20 ppm?
P: Solution. The span is 125 K, so the change is 125 × 30 = 3750 ppm, or 32 MHz × 3.75 × 10⁻³ = 120 kHz. A ±20 ppm total allows 40 ppm across 125 K, a residual of 0.32 ppm/K, about a hundred times flatter than the raw silicon, which is what compensation must achieve.
H2: 7.7 Acoustic filters in the package: cavities, power, and drift
P: The SAW and BAW filters of a phone's front end, which Book 4 designs and Book 3's piezoelectric films make possible, are MEMS in all but name, and they bring two packaging rules of their own. First, the vibrating surface must stay free: a SAW filter's interdigital fingers and a BAW resonator's membrane or Bragg-reflector stack cannot be touched by mold compound, so they are capped at the wafer level or flip-chip mounted over a sealed cavity before the module is molded. Second, a transmit filter passes the power amplifier's full output, and the decibels it loses become heat in a resonator a fraction of a millimeter across. The heat shifts the frequency through the temperature coefficient of frequency, TCF, and at high power can wear a SAW electrode by acoustic migration. As rough typical values, an uncompensated lithium-tantalate SAW drifts near −40 ppm/K, a temperature-compensated TC-SAW roughly −10 to −20 ppm/K, and an AlN BAW about −25 ppm/K; the data sheet gives the real figures.
H2: Worked Example 7.6
P: Problem. A Band 7 transmit filter (uplink 2500 to 2570 MHz, downlink 2620 to 2690 MHz) passes +29 dBm with 2.0 dB of insertion loss, and its resonators see 60 K/W to the board (an example figure). Find the heat and the self-heating. With a BAW at −25 ppm/K, how far does the upper band edge move? Compare the drift over −20 to +85 °C for SAW at −40, TC-SAW at −15, and BAW at −25 ppm/K.
P: Solution. The input is 10^(29/10) = 794 mW, and 1 − 10^(−0.2) = 36.9 % of it, 293 mW, stays in the filter. At 60 K/W the resonators run 17.6 K above the board, which moves the edge by 25 × 17.6 = 440 ppm, 1.1 MHz at 2570 MHz. Over the 105 K ambient range, the SAW drifts 4200 ppm, 10.8 MHz, a fifth of the 50 MHz gap between uplink and downlink; the TC-SAW 1575 ppm, 4.0 MHz; the BAW 2625 ppm, 6.7 MHz. The filter's guard band must absorb the ambient drift plus the self-heating, which is why the duplexers of narrow-gap bands are TC-SAW or BAW, and why the module is designed with a heat path under the filter die.
P: MEMS sensors and resonators live inside packages and next to processors. The next chapter turns to the chiplets and die-to-die links that make up the processors themselves, in Chapter 8.
BENCH: On a MEMS sensor's data sheet, read the noise density and the offset over temperature before the range and resolution; they decide what the sensor can measure. Mount it away from board-bending points such as connectors and mounting screws, because package stress shows up as offset. For a MEMS microphone, check that the sound port in the board and enclosure is sealed to the port, not to the cavity behind it.
