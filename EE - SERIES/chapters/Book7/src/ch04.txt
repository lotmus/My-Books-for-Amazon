H1: Chapter 4. Board Materials: FR-4 and Better
P: A printed circuit board is a stack of copper foils separated by dielectric laminate, and the laminate determines how fast signals travel, how much they are attenuated, how well the board handles heat, and how finely it can be built. FR-4, woven glass in epoxy resin, is the default material of electronics, cheap and well understood. This chapter shows when it stops being good enough and what to buy instead. The rule is simple: use FR-4 unless you have a reason, and the reasons are loss, stability of the dielectric constant, heat, or density.
H2: 4.1 Dielectric loss
P: A laminate is described by its relative permittivity εr, often called Dk, and its loss tangent tan δ, or Df. A wave on a line fully embedded in the dielectric, a stripline, loses power in the dielectric at a rate proportional to frequency, to √εr, and to tan δ. Because the loss grows linearly with frequency, a material that is fine at 1 GHz can be unusable at 28 GHz.
EQ: αd ≈ 8.686 π f √εr tan δ / c   dB/m   (stripline)
H2: Worked Example 4.1
WEP: Compare the dielectric loss at 10 GHz of a standard FR-4 with εr = 4.5 and tan δ = 0.020, and a low-loss hydrocarbon-ceramic laminate with εr = 3.55 and tan δ = 0.0037. Repeat at 28 GHz.
WES: For FR-4, αd = 8.686 × π × 10¹⁰ × 2.12 × 0.020/2.998 × 10⁸ = 38.6 dB/m, or 0.98 dB per inch. For the low-loss laminate, 6.3 dB/m, or 0.16 dB per inch. At 28 GHz the figures are 108 and 17.8 dB/m. A 5 cm feed line at 28 GHz loses 5.4 dB in FR-4, leaving only 29% of the transmitter's power at the antenna, and 0.9 dB in the low-loss material. Book 4's reference laminate, with εr = 3.55, is this class of material.
H2: 4.2 Datasheet values and real boards
P: A laminate's Dk and Df depend on frequency, temperature, humidity, and the ratio of glass to resin in a particular construction, and they are measured by different methods that do not always agree. A material sold as FR-4 by two mills can differ in Df by a factor of two. The designer must therefore specify Dk and Df at the frequency of interest, in the actual construction, and ask the fabricator to confirm them. The copper matters too. At 10 GHz the skin depth is only 0.66 µm, so the roughness of copper foil, which is treated to adhere to the resin and may have features of 1 to 5 µm, lengthens the current path and can add as much loss as the dielectric. Smooth, very-low-profile foils are part of every low-loss specification.
KEY: Key idea. FR-4 is the default; buy better for loss, Dk stability, heat, or density. Specify Dk and Df at the frequency you care about, in the construction you will build, because the material's name is not a specification.
H2: 4.3 Glass weave and skew
P: In woven-glass laminates, glass has εr near 6 and the resin near 3, so a trace that runs along a glass bundle sees a higher effective permittivity than one over a resin-rich gap. In a differential pair, the two lines can then travel at different speeds, and the skew between them converts the differential signal partly into common mode, which radiates and closes the eye. Spread-glass and flat-glass weaves, angled routing, and low-Dk glass reduce the effect.
H2: Worked Example 4.2
WEP: The two lines of a differential pair see effective permittivities of 3.9 and 4.2 over 5 cm. Find the skew, and compare it with the unit interval of a 56 Gbaud link.
WES: The delay difference is (√4.2 − √3.9) × 0.05/2.998 × 10⁸ = 12.4 ps. The unit interval at 56 Gbaud is 1/56 × 10⁹ = 17.9 ps, so the skew is 70% of a symbol, enough to close the eye of a 112 Gbit/s PAM4 link. At 10 Gbaud, with a 100 ps unit interval, the same skew would be tolerable.
H2: 4.4 Heat, density, and hybrids
P: FR-4's glass transition temperature, the point where the resin softens and its expansion rises sharply, is typically 130 to 180 °C, and repeated lead-free soldering near 250 °C stresses vias through the board's thickness. High-Tg and low-expansion materials are chosen for thick boards, many reflow cycles, and large packages. Fine-line, high-density interconnect boards use thin dielectrics and laser-drilled microvias. Many products use hybrid stack-ups: a low-loss laminate on the RF or high-speed layers bonded to FR-4 for the rest, which buys performance only where it is needed. Mixing materials requires care with expansion and lamination, and the fabricator must agree to it.
H2: 4.5 How much copper roughness costs
P: At high frequency the current crowds into the outermost skin depth of the conductor, 0.39 µm at 28 GHz, and if the copper surface has peaks and valleys taller than that, the current must follow them. The Hammerstad correction multiplies the smooth-conductor loss by 1 + (2/π) arctan[1.4(Δ/δ)²], where Δ is the RMS roughness, so the factor approaches 2 once the roughness is a few skin depths. More accurate models, such as Huray's snowball model, treat the nodules of electrodeposited foil as spheres, but the conclusion is the same. Board vendors therefore sell grades: standard foil with several micrometers of roughness, very-low-profile (VLP) foil near 1 µm, and hyper-very-low-profile (HVLP) foil of a few tenths of a micrometer, at progressively higher cost and with adhesion that the laminate chemistry must restore.
EQ: K_rough = 1 + (2/π) arctan[1.4 (Δ/δ)²]
H2: Worked Example 4.3
WEP: Find the roughness factor at 28 GHz for VLP foil with Δ = 1.0 µm and HVLP foil with Δ = 0.3 µm.
WES: δ = 66/√(28 × 10⁹) mm = 0.394 µm. For VLP, (1.0/0.394)² = 6.44, and K = 1 + (2/π) arctan(9.0) = 1.93. For HVLP, (0.3/0.394)² = 0.58, and K = 1 + (2/π) arctan(0.81) = 1.43. The conductor loss of the HVLP trace is 26 % lower, a saving that can be worth several decibels on a long 112 Gbit/s channel.
P: The board's materials set the loss per inch. Layout, Chapter 5, decides how the signals and their return currents use them.
