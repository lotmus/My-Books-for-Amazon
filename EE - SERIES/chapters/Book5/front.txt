H1: How to Use This Book
P: A modem turns bits into a waveform and the waveform back into bits. This book is about that waveform: how many bits it carries, how much spectrum it uses, how much noise and fading it tolerates, and how a receiver written largely in software recovers it. Part I, Chapters 1 to 8, builds a complete link. Chapters 1 to 4 build it on a channel that only adds noise: Shannon's limit, sampling and complex baseband, pulse shaping, and the constellations with their error rates. Chapters 5 to 7 add the real channel and the techniques that defeat it: multipath and diversity, OFDM, coding, and multiple antennas. Chapter 8 places the result in the 2026 band plan and in a software-defined receiver. Part II, Chapters 9 to 15, goes inside the modem: its digital filters, carrier and timing synchronization, equalization, FSK and spread spectrum, channel estimation from pilots, multiple access and scheduling, and the link adaptation and HARQ that set real throughput. Part III, Chapters 16 to 21, applies the modem to whole systems: ranging and positioning, interleaving and error detection, massive MIMO and beamforming, interference and the cellular SINR, satellite links and non-terrestrial networks, and the low-energy IoT radios. Part IV, Chapters 22 to 25, follows the same arithmetic into hardware and out to new uses: the modem in FPGA and ASIC logic, signal processing for radar, microphone arrays, and sensor fusion, communication over fiber and through the air on light, and a network of LoRa, BLE, and Wi-Fi nodes the reader can build.
P: Each chapter states its question, develops the idea in words, and works it through with declared numbers. Every number in a worked example was computed, and every practice problem is worked in full in Appendix B, with the idea, the numbers, a check, and the usual mistake. Readers should be comfortable with complex numbers, the Fourier transform at the level of Book 1, and basic probability.
P: The series gives each subject one home. Antenna gain and the Friis path factor are Book 4's. The power amplifier, transmitter watts, the synthesizer, and the receiver noise cascade are Book 4's. Emission masks, the test chamber, and certification are Book 7's. This book uses those results where it needs them and does not recompute them. Readers who want the firmware that runs these radios on a microcontroller will find it in Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics. The texts in Appendix C go deeper; this book's explanations, examples, and problems are its own.
P: The examples use real systems and real parts wherever they help: Wi-Fi 6 and 7, LTE and 5G NR numerologies, DVB-S2 satellite carriers, GPS and Bluetooth channel sounding, LoRa on the Semtech SX1276, BLE on the Nordic nRF52840, and the RTL-SDR, HackRF One, ADALM-Pluto, and USRP B210 software-defined radios. Standards revise their numbers; where a 2026 figure is quoted, the reasoning behind it is given too, so the reader can redo the arithmetic when the next release changes it. Where a circuit can do a job without a processor, the book shows that version first: an FM receiver built around an IF strip, an FSK tone decoder, a Costas loop as it was first built, an infrared link keyed by a 555 timer. Those are mostly hobby and education builds today, and the text says so where they appear; they are taught because they make the integrated chips of real products understandable. Learn it here, buy the IC at work.
