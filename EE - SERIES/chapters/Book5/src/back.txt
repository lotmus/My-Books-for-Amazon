H1: Appendix A. Constants and Formulas Used in This Book
P: Shannon capacity: C = B log₂(1 + SNR); minimum Eb/N0 = ln 2 = −1.59 dB; for spectral efficiency η, Eb/N0 ≥ (2^η − 1)/η.
P: SNR = (Eb/N0)(Rb/B); Rb = Rs log₂M; passband bandwidth ≈ Rs(1 + α).
P: Ideal converter: SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB; processing gain 10 log₁₀(fs/B) for complex sampling; jitter limit −20 log₁₀(2πf tj).
P: Q(x) = ½ erfc(x/√2). BPSK/QPSK: BER = Q(√(2Eb/N0)). Square M-QAM: SER ≈ 4(1 − 1/√M) Q(√(3Es/((M − 1)N0))).
P: Es/N0 for an uncoded symbol error rate of 10⁻³: 4-QAM 10.35 dB, 16-QAM 17.63, 64-QAM 23.97, 256-QAM 30.08, 1024-QAM 36.14, 4096-QAM 42.17 dB.
P: EVM(%) = 100 × 10^(EVM(dB)/20); −38 dB = 1.26%.
P: Coherence bandwidth Bc ≈ 1/(5στ); Doppler fd = vfc/c; coherence time Tc ≈ 0.423/fd. Rayleigh BPSK BER = ½[1 − √(γ/(1 + γ))] ≈ 1/(4γ).
P: Wi-Fi (802.11a/g): 64-point FFT at 20 MS/s, 312.5 kHz, 3.2 µs + 0.8 µs guard. Wi-Fi 6/7: 78.125 kHz, 12.8 µs + 0.8 µs = 13.6 µs. 5G NR: Δf = 15 × 2^µ kHz, 14 symbols per slot, slot = 1/2^µ ms.
P: Wi-Fi 7 paper peak: 3920 × 12 × 5/6 / 13.6 µs = 2.88 Gbit/s per stream; × 8 streams = 23.06 Gbit/s.
P: Bands: 2.4 GHz ISM 2400–2483.5 MHz; 6 GHz 5925–7125 MHz (1200 MHz: three 320 MHz channels, 240 MHz left); 5G FR1 410 MHz–7.125 GHz, FR2 24.25–71 GHz; 6G research 7.125–24.25 GHz.
P: Thermal noise −174 dBm/Hz at 290 K; sensitivity = −174 + 10 log₁₀B + NF + SNRreq.
P: FIR length N ≈ A/(22 Δf/fs); CIC bit growth N log₂(RM). Carrier offset Δf = (ppmTX + ppmRX)·10⁻⁶·fc; loop jitter σφ² ≈ BL/(C/N0).
P: ZF noise gain for h = [1, a]: 1/(1 − a²). LMS: 0 < μ < 2/(N Px). Pilots: τmax < 1/Δfp, fD,max < 1/(2Tp).
P: DSSS Gp = 10 log₁₀(Rc/Rb); LoRa Tsym = 2^SF/B. ALOHA max 18.4%, slotted 36.8%. η ≈ log₂(1 + SNR/Γ), Γ ≈ 2–3 dB.
P: Ranging: d = c·t/2 (round trip); 1 ns = 30 cm one-way. Position error ≈ DOP × σrange. Coin cell CR2032 ≈ 225 mAh × 3 V = 2430 J; Pavg = V(Ion·ton/T + Isleep). 802.15.4: 2 Mchip/s, 250 kbit/s, Gp = 9.0 dB. Repetition gain ≈ 10 log₁₀R.
P: Carson's rule B ≈ 2(Δf + fm). Bandpass sampling 2fH/n ≤ fs ≤ 2fL/(n − 1). Converter SNR with peaky signals ≈ 6.02N + 1.76 − (PAPR − 3 dB).
P: Log-distance path loss PL(d) = PL(d0) + 10n log₁₀(d/d0) + Xσ; edge-coverage margin 1.28σ (90 %), 1.64σ (95 %). Rayleigh fade margin −10 log₁₀[−ln(1 − p)] dB for availability 1 − p.
P: OFDM intercarrier interference ≈ (πε)²/3. Channel correlation J₀(2πfdτ). LLR (BPSK) = 2Ay/σ². Gray coding BER ≈ SER/log₂M.
P: Satellite link: C/N0 = EIRP − L + G/T + 228.6 dB-Hz. Footprint central angle λ = arccos[R cos ε/(R + h)] − ε, R = 6371 km. Ionospheric delay 40.3 TEC/f² m.
P: 5G peak rate (TS 38.306): v Qm f (948/1024) × 12 N_PRB × 14 × 2^µ × 10³ × (1 − OH), OH = 0.14 DL, 0.08 UL in FR1. Uplink power P = min(Pmax, P0 + 10 log₁₀M + αPL).
P: ACIR: 1/ACIR = 1/ACLR + 1/ACS. Uplink noise rise −10 log₁₀(1 − η). Reed-Solomon corrects (n − k)/2 symbols. Phase-based ranging d = cΔφ/(4πΔf).
P: Duplexer budget: allowed added noise = floor + 10 log₁₀(10^(D/10) − 1) for a desense of D dB; 0.5 dB allows −9.1 dB, 1 dB allows −5.9 dB.
P: FMCW radar: fb = 2RS/c with slope S = B/Tr; ΔR = c/(2B); vmax = λ/(4Tc); Δv = λ/(2NTc). Detection threshold for exponential noise T = −ln(Pfa) × mean; cell-averaging CFAR T = N(Pfa^(−1/N) − 1).
P: Arrays: phase step between elements 2π(d/λ) sin θ; virtual array Ntx × Nrx; resolution ≈ 2/N rad at broadside; no spatial aliasing for d ≤ λ/2. Delay-and-sum gain 10 log₁₀M in uncorrelated noise. Fusion: 1/σ² = 1/σ1² + 1/σ2²; complementary filter α = τ/(τ + Δt).
P: Fixed point: SQNR ≈ 6.02B + 1.76 − (PAPR − 3) dB; FFT word growth log₂N bits; CORDIC gain 1.6468 (scale by 0.6073), about one bit of angle per iteration. ASIC versus FPGA break-even volume = NRE/(FPGA unit cost − ASIC unit cost).
P: Optics: received power = launch − α L − connector and splice losses; photocurrent I = R P; 1 dB of optical power is 2 dB of electrical SNR in a thermal-noise-limited receiver. Spectral width Δλ ≈ λ²B/c; dispersion spread Δτ = D L Δλ, D ≈ 17 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm; reach for one symbol of spread L ≈ c/(D λ² B²). DP-16QAM carries 8 bits per symbol; PAM4 eyes cost 4.8 dB in optical power.
P: LoRa airtime: Tsym = 2^SF/B; payload symbols = 8 + ⌈(8PL − 4SF + 28 + 16 CRC − 20 IH)/(4(SF − 2DE))⌉ × (CR + 4); time on air = (preamble + 4.25 + payload symbols) Tsym. Radio horizon ≈ 4.12(√h1 + √h2) km with h in meters; first Fresnel radius at midpoint ≈ 17.32 √(d/(4f)) m with d in km and f in GHz. RSSI distance d = d0 × 10^((P0 − RSSI)/(10n)).
H1: Appendix B. Answers to the Practice Problems
P: Each answer below is worked in full: the idea that unlocks the problem, the numbers, a check by a second route or an order of magnitude, and the mistake most often made. Values are rounded at the end, not at each step.
H2: Chapter 1
P: 1. Idea: 64 symbols carry log₂64 = 6 bits each, and a root-raised-cosine pulse occupies the symbol rate times (1 + α). Rb = 10 × 10⁶ × 6 = 60 Mbit/s, and B = 10 MHz × 1.2 = 12 MHz. Check: the spectral efficiency is 60/12 = 5 bit/s/Hz, which is 6 bits per symbol reduced by the 20 % excess bandwidth. The usual mistake is to take 64 bits per symbol instead of its logarithm.
P: 2. Idea: Shannon's formula C = B log₂(1 + SNR) needs the SNR as a ratio. 10 dB is 10, so C = 5 MHz × log₂11 = 5 × 3.46 = 17.3 Mbit/s. Check: at 10 dB the efficiency of 3.46 bit/s/Hz lies between 3 bits (SNR 7) and 4 bits (SNR 15). The usual mistake is to put 10 into the logarithm as decibels and also forget the 1, getting log₂10 = 3.32.
P: 3. Idea: SNR = (Eb/N0)(Rb/B), so in decibels the spectral efficiency adds 10 log₁₀η. SNR = 8 + 10 log₁₀2 = 8 + 3.0 = 11.0 dB. Check: at 1 bit/s/Hz the SNR and Eb/N0 would be equal, and each doubling of efficiency adds 3 dB. The usual mistake is to subtract the 3 dB, confusing energy per bit with energy per symbol in the other direction.
P: 4. Idea: at the Shannon limit η = log₂(1 + SNR), so SNR = 2^η − 1. SNR = 2⁶ − 1 = 63, 10 log₁₀63 = 18.0 dB. Check: the rule of 3 dB per bit/s/Hz at high SNR gives about 6 × 3 = 18 dB. The usual mistake is to forget the −1 and quote 64, 18.06 dB, which is close here but wrong at low efficiency.
P: 5. Idea: as the spectral efficiency goes to zero, the minimum Eb/N0 = (2^η − 1)/η tends to ln 2. Eb/N0 = 0.693, 10 log₁₀0.693 = −1.59 dB. Check: at η = 0.01 the formula gives (2^0.01 − 1)/0.01 = 0.695, already within 0.3 % of the limit. The usual mistake is to answer 0 dB, the limit at 1 bit/s/Hz.
P: 6. Idea: compare how bandwidth and power enter C = B log₂(1 + SNR). Capacity is proportional to bandwidth but grows only with the logarithm of SNR, so at high SNR doubling the capacity through power alone means roughly squaring the SNR, while doubling the bandwidth doubles it at once. Check: at 20 dB, 6.66 bit/s/Hz, doubling to 13.3 bit/s/Hz needs about 40 dB, 100 times more power, although doubling the bandwidth also doubles the noise and so needs twice the power to keep the SNR. The usual mistake is to ignore that last point and claim bandwidth is free.
P: 7. Idea: Carson's rule B ≈ 2(Δf + fm). B = 2 × (2.5 + 3) = 11 kHz. Check: it fits a 12.5 kHz channel with 1.5 kHz of guard, which is why narrowband land-mobile FM uses 2.5 kHz deviation. The usual mistake is to use the deviation alone, 5 kHz, and forget the audio bandwidth.
P: 8. Idea: in AM the carrier carries power 1 and the two sidebands m²/2 together, so the sideband fraction is m²/(2 + m²). 0.25/2.25 = 0.111, 11 %. Check: even at m = 1 the fraction is only 1/3, which is why AM wastes most of its power on the carrier. The usual mistake is to use m instead of m², giving 20 %.
P: 9. Idea: PAM4 carries 2 bits per symbol, and the Nyquist frequency is half the symbol rate. 56 Gbit/s/2 = 28 GBd, Nyquist frequency 14 GHz. Check: NRZ at 56 Gbit/s would need 28 GHz, twice as much, which is why PAM4 replaced it. The usual mistake is to set the Nyquist frequency equal to the symbol rate.
P: 10. Idea: as bandwidth grows without limit, C approaches (S/N0)/ln 2 = 1.44 S/N0. 50 dB-Hz is 10⁵ Hz, so C = 1.44 × 10⁵ = 144 kbit/s. Check: Figure 1.1's 40 dB-Hz gave 14.4 kbit/s, and 10 dB more gives ten times more. The usual mistake is to treat dB-Hz as decibels of a power and convert with 20 log.
H2: Chapter 2
P: 1. Idea: a tone above fs/2 folds to |f − k fs|, the nearest image in the first Nyquist zone. 130 − 100 = 30 MHz, below fs/2 = 50 MHz, so it appears at 30 MHz. Check: a real sampler cannot tell 30 MHz from 130 MHz, since cos(2π × 130t) and cos(2π × 30t) agree at every t = n/100 µs. The usual mistake is to subtract from fs/2, giving 20 MHz.
P: 2. Idea: an ideal N-bit converter with a full-scale sine has SQNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB. 6.02 × 14 + 1.76 = 86.0 dB. Check: each bit adds about 6 dB, and 14 bits × 6 = 84 plus 1.76 is close. The usual mistake is to omit the 1.76 dB, which comes from the sine's 3 dB crest factor relative to a uniform error.
P: 3. Idea: invert SQNR = 6.02N + 1.76 to get the effective bits. ENOB = (74 − 1.76)/6.02 = 12.0 bits. Check: 12 bits ideal gives 74.0 dB. The usual mistake is to divide 74 by 6.02 without subtracting 1.76, giving 12.3 bits.
P: 4. Idea: noise spreads over the sample bandwidth, and filtering to the channel removes the rest. Processing gain = 10 log₁₀(61.44/10) = 7.9 dB. Check: 61.44/10 is a factor of about 6, and a factor of 4 would be 6 dB, 8 would be 9 dB. The usual mistake is to use fs/2 for a complex converter, whose noise spans the full fs.
P: 5. Idea: jitter limits SNR to −20 log₁₀(2π f σt). 2π × 10⁹ × 10⁻¹³ = 6.28 × 10⁻⁴, and −20 log₁₀ of that is 64.0 dB. Check: halving the jitter adds 6 dB, so 50 fs would give 70 dB, which is the class of clock that 1 GHz sampling needs. The usual mistake is to use 10 log, giving 32 dB.
P: 6. Idea: count the independent real numbers per second. Each complex sample is two real numbers, so fs complex samples per second describe a band from −fs/2 to +fs/2 without ambiguity; a real signal's spectrum is symmetric about zero, so a real sampler's fs numbers describe only 0 to fs/2. Check: both carry fs × 2 or fs real values per second and cover fs or fs/2 of bandwidth, the same ratio. The usual mistake is to think the complex sampler breaks Nyquist's rule; it uses two converters.
P: 7. Idea: for bandpass sampling with zone index n, 2fH/n ≤ fs ≤ 2fL/(n − 1). For n = 2: 2 × 160/2 = 160 MS/s to 2 × 140/1 = 280 MS/s. Check: at 200 MS/s the band 140-160 MHz lies in the second zone, 100-200 MHz, and folds to 40-60 MHz without overlap. The usual mistake is to swap fL and fH, which makes the range empty.
P: 8. Idea: the 6.02N + 1.76 figure assumes a full-scale sine, whose RMS sits 3 dB below full scale; every extra decibel of back-off costs a decibel. 13 − 3 = 10 dB of loss, so 86.0 − 10 = 76.0 dB. Check: an OFDM signal with a 13 dB crest factor needs that back-off, which costs it about 1.7 bits. The usual mistake is to subtract the full 13 dB.
P: 9. Idea: in a zero-IF radio, DC offset and LO leakage land at 0 Hz in baseband, the center subcarrier. Leaving it empty keeps that error off the data, at the cost of one subcarrier of many. Check: a 64-point Wi-Fi symbol loses 1/64 of its subcarriers, 1.6 %. The usual mistake is to blame the center subcarrier's frequency for some propagation effect.
P: 10. Idea: an N-bit accumulator divides the clock into 2^N steps. 245.76 × 10⁶/2^N < 0.1 requires 2^N > 2.46 × 10⁹; 2³¹ = 2.15 × 10⁹ is too small, so N = 32, with 0.057 Hz resolution. Check: 245.76 × 10⁶/4.29 × 10⁹ = 0.057 Hz. The usual mistake is to stop at 31 bits after a rough logarithm.
H2: Chapter 3
P: 1. Idea: occupied bandwidth = symbol rate × (1 + α). 5 × 1.22 = 6.1 MHz. Check: a 22 % roll-off spreads the 5 MHz Nyquist bandwidth by 1.1 MHz. The usual mistake is to use half the symbol rate, the one-sided baseband width.
P: 2. Idea: invert B = Rs(1 + α). 6/1.15 = 5.22 Msymbol/s. Check: 5.22 × 1.15 = 6.0 MHz. The usual mistake is to subtract 15 % from 6 MHz, giving 5.1 Msymbol/s.
P: 3. Idea: two requirements must hold at once, a matched receive filter and an overall Nyquist pulse. Splitting the raised cosine into two root-raised-cosine halves meets both, because the receive filter is then the time-reversed transmit pulse and their product is the full raised cosine. Check: a full raised cosine at the transmitter would need a flat receive filter, which passes extra noise, or more filtering, which reintroduces interference. The usual mistake is to think the split is only for implementation convenience.
P: 4. Idea: a matched filter's peak SNR is 2E/N0. 2 × 10⁻⁹/(2 × 10⁻¹²) = 1000, 30.0 dB. Check: E/N0 alone is 500, 27 dB, and the matched filter doubles it in this convention. The usual mistake is to use E/N0 and report 27 dB.
P: 5. Idea: the eye's width is time, its height is amplitude. Timing jitter closes it horizontally; noise closes it vertically. Check: an eye with ideal amplitude but 0.3 symbols of peak jitter loses 30 % of its width. The usual mistake is to blame noise for all eye closure.
P: 6. Idea: read the worst-case interference values for the two roll-offs at τ = 0.1 T from Section 3.2. 0.510/0.197 = 2.6 times. Check: smaller α means slower-decaying tails, so more interference from timing errors is expected. The usual mistake is to invert the ratio and conclude the narrower pulse is more tolerant.
P: 7. Idea: B = Rs(1 + α). 3.84 × 1.22 = 4.68 MHz, inside the 5 MHz channel. Check: the 0.32 MHz left over is the guard band between WCDMA carriers. The usual mistake is to forget α and quote 3.84 MHz.
P: 8. Idea: only the cascade of the two root filters is a Nyquist pulse. A full raised cosine after a root transmit filter gives a combined response that is not Nyquist, so intersymbol interference remains, and the receive filter is no longer matched to the pulse. Check: the combined response would be RC × RRC, which does not cross zero at the symbol instants. The usual mistake is to assume more filtering always helps.
P: 9. Idea: third-order products of a band of width W reach out to three times W. 3 × 20 = 60 MHz, centered on the signal. Check: the edges of the 20 MHz signal mix as 2f1 − f2 to land 20 MHz beyond each edge. The usual mistake is to double the bandwidth, which is the second-order case.
P: 10. Idea: an LTE resource block is 12 × 15 kHz = 180 kHz. 50 × 180 kHz = 9 MHz, 90 % of the 10 MHz channel. Check: the remaining 1 MHz is the guard band. The usual mistake is to count 10 MHz/15 kHz = 667 subcarriers instead of the 600 actually used.
H2: Chapter 4
P: 1. Idea: BPSK error rate is Q(√(2Eb/N0)). 6.8 dB is 4.786, so √9.57 = 3.094, and Q(3.094) = 9.9 × 10⁻⁴. Check: the familiar point 10⁻³ at 6.8 dB. The usual mistake is to use Q(√(Eb/N0)), which gives 1.4 × 10⁻².
P: 2. Idea: read the required Es/N0 for 64-QAM and 256-QAM from Section 4.2's table at the same symbol error rate. 30.08 − 23.97 = 6.1 dB. Check: each step of two bits per symbol in square QAM costs about 6 dB. The usual mistake is to expect 3 dB per step.
P: 3. Idea: compare the operating Es/N0 with the required value. 26 dB exceeds the 23.97 dB needed for 10⁻³, so the error rate is below 10⁻³. Check: 2 dB of margin on a steep waterfall curve lowers the error rate by more than an order of magnitude. The usual mistake is to compare against the bit-error requirement in Eb/N0.
P: 4. Idea: EVM is an amplitude ratio, so 20 log₁₀. 20 log₁₀0.02 = −34.0 dB. Check: 1 % is −40 dB, and doubling adds 6 dB. The usual mistake is 10 log, −17 dB.
P: 5. Idea: PAPR of a constellation = corner point power/average power. Corner (3, 3) gives 18; the average of 1² + 1², 1² + 3², 3² + 3² over equally likely points is 10. 18/10 = 1.8, 2.55 dB. Check: 64-QAM's ratio is 98/42 = 2.33, larger as expected. The usual mistake is to use the outer amplitude only, 3, against an average amplitude.
P: 6. Idea: QPSK is two BPSK signals on orthogonal carriers. Each carries one bit with half the symbol energy, so Eb, the noise per dimension, and each decision are identical to BPSK, and the bit error rate is the same. Check: the symbol error rate of QPSK is about twice the bit error rate because a symbol fails if either bit does. The usual mistake is to compare at equal Es/N0, where QPSK is 3 dB worse.
P: 7. Idea: with Gray coding, most symbol errors go to a neighbor and flip one of the log₂M bits. BER ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁴/6 = 1 × 10⁻⁴. Check: 64-QAM has 6 bits per symbol. The usual mistake is to take BER equal to SER.
P: 8. Idea: small phase noise of θ radians gives an error vector of length θ relative to the point. 0.3° = 5.24 mrad, 20 log₁₀(5.24 × 10⁻³) = −45.6 dB, 0.52 %. Check: 1° gives 1.75 % and −35 dB. The usual mistake is to use degrees directly.
P: 9. Idea: independent impairments add in power. 10^(−3.8) + 10^(−4.2) + 10^(−4.4) = 1.585 × 10⁻⁴ + 0.631 × 10⁻⁴ + 0.398 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.61 × 10⁻⁴, −35.8 dB. Check: the total is worse than the worst single term by 2.2 dB. The usual mistake is to add the decibel values or the percentages.
P: 10. Idea: corner power over average. Corner 2 × 7² = 98; average 42. 98/42 = 2.33, 3.68 dB. Check: larger than 16-QAM's 2.55 dB, approaching the 4.77 dB limit of large square QAM. The usual mistake is to take the average as 2 × 4² = 32.
H2: Chapter 5
P: 1. Idea: coherence bandwidth ≈ 1/(5 στ) for 0.9 correlation. 1/(5 × 1 µs) = 200 kHz. Check: a 200 kHz GSM carrier sees flat fading in such a channel, a 5 MHz LTE carrier does not. The usual mistake is to use 1/στ, 1 MHz.
P: 2. Idea: fD = v f/c. 60 km/h = 16.7 m/s, so fD = 16.7 × 5.9 × 10⁹/2.998 × 10⁸ = 328 Hz. Check: at 5.9 GHz the wavelength is 5.1 cm, and 16.7/0.051 = 328 Hz. The usual mistake is to leave the speed in km/h.
P: 3. Idea: Tc ≈ 0.423/fD. 0.423/328 = 1.29 ms. Check: a vehicle moving half a wavelength, 2.5 cm, takes 1.5 ms at 16.7 m/s, the same order. The usual mistake is to use 1/fD, 3.0 ms, which overstates the coherence.
P: 4. Idea: Rayleigh BPSK error rate ≈ 1/(4γ) at high SNR. γ = 1/(4 × 10⁻⁴) = 2500, 34.0 dB. Check: the AWGN channel needs 8.4 dB for 10⁻⁴, so fading costs about 26 dB. The usual mistake is to use 1/(2γ).
P: 5. Idea: read the required SNR per branch from Section 5.4 for one and two branches. 24.0 − 11.1 = 12.9 dB less. Check: diversity changes the slope of the error curve, so the saving is far larger than the 3 dB of array gain. The usual mistake is to answer 3 dB.
P: 6. Idea: compare the signal bandwidth with the coherence bandwidth. Indoors the coherence bandwidth is a few megahertz; a 20 MHz signal spans several and sees notches across its band, while a 300 kHz signal sits inside one and fades as a whole. Check: an RMS delay spread of 50 ns gives 1/(5 × 50 ns) = 4 MHz. The usual mistake is to think frequency selectivity depends on the carrier frequency.
P: 7. Idea: path loss beyond the reference grows as 10n log₁₀(d2/d1). 10 × 4.5 × log₁₀3 = 21.5 dB. Check: free space, n = 2, would give 9.5 dB. The usual mistake is to use 20 log, 43 dB.
P: 8. Idea: for 95 % coverage at the cell edge, the margin is the one-sided Gaussian point 1.64σ. 1.64 × 6 = 9.9 dB. Check: 90 % would need 1.28σ, 7.7 dB. The usual mistake is to use 1.96σ, the two-sided 95 % point.
P: 9. Idea: a Rayleigh signal falls below a fraction x of its mean power with probability 1 − e^(−x) ≈ x. x = 10⁻³, a margin of 30 dB. Check: each factor of ten in outage costs 10 dB in Rayleigh fading. The usual mistake is to use a Gaussian margin, which applies to shadowing, not fast fading.
P: 10. Idea: Bc ≈ 1/(5 στ). 1/(5 × 300 ns) = 667 kHz, and 100 MHz/667 kHz ≈ 150 coherence bandwidths. Check: 5G NR at 30 kHz spacing has 3276 subcarriers, about 22 per coherence bandwidth, so the channel is nearly flat across each subcarrier. The usual mistake is to use 1/στ, giving 3.3 MHz and 30.
H2: Chapter 6
P: 1. Idea: Δf = fs/N, and the useful symbol time T = 1/Δf. 15.36 MHz/1024 = 15 kHz, T = 66.7 µs. Check: LTE's spacing is 15 kHz with a 66.7 µs symbol. The usual mistake is to divide the FFT size by the sample rate.
P: 2. Idea: overhead = guard/(useful + guard). 3.2/(12.8 + 3.2) = 20 %. Check: the Wi-Fi 6 long guard costs 20 %, the 0.8 µs guard 5.9 %. The usual mistake is to divide by the useful time alone, 25 %.
P: 3. Idea: the prefix absorbs echoes delayed up to its length, a path difference of c × Tcp. 4.69 µs × 2.998 × 10⁸ = 1.41 km. Check: 1 µs is 300 m, and 4.69 × 300 = 1.41 km. The usual mistake is to halve it as if it were a round trip.
P: 4. Idea: µ = 2 means 15 × 2² kHz. 60 kHz, T = 16.7 µs, and 14 symbols per slot give 0.25 ms. Check: each step of µ halves the slot from 1 ms. The usual mistake is to use 2µ instead of 2^µ.
P: 5. Idea: N subcarriers in phase add to N times the average power. 10 log₁₀256 = 24.1 dB. Check: real signals reach this only with vanishing probability; 10⁻⁴ clipping occurs near 11-12 dB. The usual mistake is 20 log, 48 dB.
P: 6. Idea: the cyclic prefix makes the channel a circular convolution over the FFT window. The FFT turns circular convolution into a separate complex gain on each subcarrier, which one multiplication by its inverse removes. Check: without the prefix, echoes spill between symbols and the gains are no longer independent. The usual mistake is to think OFDM needs no equalizer at all.
P: 7. Idea: ICI ≈ (πε)²/3 for a normalized offset ε. (πε)²/3 = 10⁻⁴ gives ε = √(3 × 10⁻⁴)/π = 0.0055. Check: (π × 0.0055)²/3 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴. The usual mistake is to forget the square root and quote 10⁻⁴/π.
P: 8. Idea: convert ppm to hertz, then normalize. 0.2 ppm of 6 GHz is 1.2 kHz, ε = 1.2/78.125 = 0.0154, (πε)²/3 = 7.8 × 10⁻⁴, −31.1 dB. Check: the narrow Wi-Fi 7 spacing makes the same ppm four times worse in ε than at 312.5 kHz. The usual mistake is to use the 20 MHz channel bandwidth instead of the subcarrier spacing.
P: 9. Idea: common phase error rotates every subcarrier of a symbol by the same angle. The pilots in that symbol measure the angle, and the equalizer removes it before decisions. Check: only the part of phase noise that varies within a symbol, which causes ICI, is left. The usual mistake is to treat all phase noise as irreducible.
P: 10. Idea: redo Worked Example 6.5's two bounds at the new speed. 350 km/h = 97.2 m/s, Doppler 97.2 × 2 × 10⁹/3 × 10⁸ = 648 Hz, requiring a spacing of at least 648/0.02 = 32.4 kHz, above the 17 kHz cap set by the 4.7 µs echoes. The bounds cross: a high-speed train needs more prefix overhead or shorter echoes. Check: the Doppler is 350/120 = 2.92 times the 222 Hz of the example. The usual mistake is to pick 30 kHz and ignore that its 2.3 µs prefix is shorter than the echoes.
H2: Chapter 7
P: 1. Idea: information rate = coded rate × code rate. 40 × 3/4 = 30 Mbit/s. Check: the code adds one parity bit for every three information bits. The usual mistake is to divide by the code rate, 53 Mbit/s.
P: 2. Idea: minimum Eb/N0 = (2^η − 1)/η. (2⁴ − 1)/4 = 3.75, 5.74 dB. Check: at 1 bit/s/Hz the answer is 0 dB, at 2 it is 1.76 dB, rising with η. The usual mistake is to give the SNR, 15 = 11.8 dB.
P: 3. Idea: two equal orthogonal streams split the power, each at half the SNR. 30 dB = 1000, so 2 log₂(1 + 500) = 17.9 bit/s/Hz, against log₂1001 = 9.97 bit/s/Hz for one antenna each side. Check: the ratio is 1.8, approaching 2 at high SNR. The usual mistake is to give each stream the full SNR.
P: 4. Idea: rate = data subcarriers × bits per subcarrier × code rate/symbol time. 234 × 10 × 5/6/13.6 µs = 1950 bits/13.6 µs = 143.4 Mbit/s. Check: this is the published Wi-Fi 6 MCS 11 rate for 20 MHz with a 0.8 µs guard. The usual mistake is to use the 12.8 µs useful time, 152 Mbit/s.
P: 5. Idea: spatial streams are limited by the smaller antenna count. min(4, 2) = 2. Check: the channel matrix is 2 × 4 or 4 × 2 and has rank at most 2. The usual mistake is to answer 4.
P: 6. Idea: receive combining raises one stream's SNR; spatial multiplexing adds streams. A 1 × 4 link gains up to 6 dB, about two more bits per second per hertz, while a 4 × 4 link carries four streams and multiplies the capacity by about four. Check: at 20 dB, log₂(1 + 400) = 8.6 against 4 × log₂26 = 18.8. The usual mistake is to think more antennas always multiply capacity.
P: 7. Idea: four equal orthogonal paths split the SNR four ways. 20 dB = 100, so 4 log₂(1 + 25) = 18.80 bit/s/Hz, against log₂101 = 6.66 for SISO, a ratio of 2.8. Check: at higher SNR the ratio approaches 4. The usual mistake is to give each path the full 100.
P: 8. Idea: a convolutional encoder's state is its last K − 1 inputs. 2⁴ = 16 states. Check: K = 7 gives 64, the Wi-Fi code. The usual mistake is 2⁵.
P: 9. Idea: the transmitter does not know the channel, so it divides its power between the two antennas, while a receive combiner collects the full power from both. That halving is the 3 dB difference; the diversity order, two, is the same. Check: at high SNR both curves have the same slope, offset by 3 dB. The usual mistake is to think Alamouti has less diversity.
P: 10. Idea: decibels of gain convert to a power ratio by 10^(dB/10). 10^0.7 = 5.0 times. Check: 6 dB is 4 and 7 dB a little more. The usual mistake is 10^(7/20) = 2.2.
H2: Chapter 8
P: 1. Idea: the 2.4 GHz band is 83.5 MHz wide and channels are 5 MHz apart, so 20 MHz channels overlap unless separated by five steps. Channels 1, 6, and 11 fit without overlap, three in all. Check: 2412, 2437, and 2462 MHz centers, 25 MHz apart. The usual mistake is 83.5/20 = 4.
P: 2. Idea: divide the band by the channel width. 1200/80 = 15. Check: the 6 GHz band, 5925-7125 MHz, has 14 usable 80 MHz channels in the 802.11 plan because of its edges; 15 is the arithmetic ceiling. The usual mistake is to use 160 MHz channels.
P: 3. Idea: recall the 3GPP ranges. FR1 runs from 410 MHz to 7.125 GHz, FR2 from 24.25 to 71 GHz. Check: FR2 was extended from 52.6 GHz to 71 GHz in Release 17. The usual mistake is to stop FR1 at 6 GHz.
P: 4. Idea: floor = −174 + 10 log₁₀B + NF. 10 log₁₀(160 × 10⁶) = 82.0, so −174 + 82.0 + 5 = −87.0 dBm. Check: a 20 MHz channel is 9 dB lower, −96 dBm. The usual mistake is to forget the noise figure.
P: 5. Idea: sensitivity = noise floor + required SNR. −87.0 + 30 = −57.0 dBm. Check: a 20 MHz channel with the same noise figure and SNR would reach 9 dB lower, −66 dBm. The usual mistake is to subtract the SNR.
P: 6. Idea: identify where impairments land. The zero-IF receiver mixes the wanted signal directly to DC, where offsets and flicker noise sit, and relies on matched I and Q arms to reject the image at the mirror frequency, so it is the most sensitive to both. Check: a superheterodyne's IF is far from DC and its image is removed by a filter. The usual mistake is to name the superheterodyne because of its image problem.
P: 7. Idea: data rate = sample rate × bytes per sample. 16-bit I and Q are 4 bytes, so 61.44 × 10⁶ × 4 = 245.8 MB/s. Check: that is 1.97 Gbit/s, beyond USB 2.0 and within USB 3.0. The usual mistake is to count 2 bytes per complex sample.
P: 8. Idea: the strong signal controls the front end. It sets the gain and fills the 8-bit converter's full scale, leaving about 50 dB of dynamic range below it, and the tuner's limited linearity adds intermodulation on top of the wanted signal. Check: an 8-bit converter's ideal SQNR is 49.9 dB. The usual mistake is to blame the image response alone.
P: 9. Idea: a single-pole RC corner is 1/(2πτ). τ = 75 µs, f = 1/(2π × 75 µs) = 2.12 kHz. Check: Europe's 50 µs gives 3.18 kHz. The usual mistake is to give 1/τ = 13.3 kHz.
P: 10. Idea: the transmitter's noise in the receive band adds to the receiver's own floor, and a 1 dB rise allows added noise 10 log₁₀(10^0.1 − 1) = −5.9 dB relative to that floor. The floor is −174 + 5 = −169 dBm/Hz, so the added noise may reach −174.9 dBm/Hz, and the isolation must be −130 − (−174.9) = 44.9 dB. Check: Worked Example 8.6 allowed only 0.5 dB of desensitization, which costs 9.1 dB relative to the floor instead of 5.9, and needed 51.1 dB with 2 dB more amplifier noise and 1 dB less noise figure: 51.1 − 2 − 1 − 3.2 = 44.9 dB. The usual mistake is to set the added noise equal to the floor, which costs 3 dB of sensitivity.
H2: Chapter 9
P: 1. Idea: Harris's estimate, taps ≈ attenuation in dB/(22 × transition width/fs). 2/61.44 = 0.0326, and 80/(22 × 0.0326) = 112 taps. Check: halving the transition band would double the length to about 224. The usual mistake is to use the transition width in hertz without normalizing by the sample rate.
P: 2. Idea: a linear-phase FIR filter of N taps delays every frequency by (N − 1)/2 samples. 100/2 = 50 samples, and at 10 MHz each sample is 0.1 µs, so 5 µs. Check: the delay is the time to the center tap. The usual mistake is to use N/2 = 50.5 samples.
P: 3. Idea: recall the first sidelobe levels: rectangular −13 dB, Hann −31 dB, Hamming −43 dB, Blackman −58 dB. Blackman, about 58 dB down. Check: the price is the widest main lobe of the four. The usual mistake is to pick Hamming, whose first sidelobe is low but whose far sidelobes decay slowly.
P: 4. Idea: a CIC filter's gain is (RM)^N, so it grows N log₂(RM) bits. 5 × log₂8 = 5 × 3 = 15 bits. Check: a 12-bit input needs a 27-bit datapath. The usual mistake is to use log₂R only once, 3 bits.
P: 5. Idea: a decimator throws most outputs away. In polyphase form only the retained outputs are computed, and each subfilter runs at the low output rate, cutting the work by the decimation factor. Check: Worked Example 8.5 cut 307 million to 30.7 million multiplications per second by decimating by 10. The usual mistake is to think polyphase changes the frequency response.
P: 6. Idea: modems need pulse shapes preserved. Symmetric FIR filters have exactly linear phase, so all frequencies are delayed equally and pulses keep their shape, and having no feedback they are always stable. Check: an IIR filter of the same selectivity uses fewer multiplications but distorts the group delay near its edges. The usual mistake is to cite only stability.
P: 7. Idea: a product needs the sum of the two widths, and a sum of N products can grow by log₂N bits. 14 + 16 + log₂64 = 14 + 16 + 6 = 36 bits. Check: the DSP48E2's 48-bit accumulator holds it with 12 bits to spare. The usual mistake is to forget the growth from the sum.
P: 8. Idea: compare the average errors. Truncation always moves toward negative infinity, a mean bias of half an LSB that becomes a DC term and, after mixing, a spur; rounding to nearest has zero mean. Check: a 16-bit output truncated has a bias of 2^−17 of full scale, −102 dBFS, visible on an FFT. The usual mistake is to think the difference is only half an LSB of noise.
P: 9. Idea: the resampler steps through the input by the ratio of the rates. 61.44/50 = 1.2288 input samples per output sample, the same ratio as Worked Example 9.5. Check: 50 × 1.2288 = 61.44. The usual mistake is to invert the ratio to 0.814.
P: 10. Idea: a half-band filter's response satisfies H(f) + H(fs/2 − f) = 1. In the time domain that forces every coefficient at an even offset from the center to zero and the center coefficient to 1/2. Check: Worked Example 9.6's 47-tap filter has 25 nonzero coefficients including the center 0.5. The usual mistake is to think 0.5 is a normalization choice.
H2: Chapter 10
P: 1. Idea: in the worst case the two crystals err in opposite directions. 2 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ × 5.8 × 10⁹ = 116 kHz. Check: 10 ppm of 5.8 GHz is 58 kHz per radio. The usual mistake is to count one crystal only.
P: 2. Idea: a phase-locked loop's phase jitter variance is BL/(C/N0). 45 dB-Hz is 31 623 Hz, so σφ² = 100/31 623 = 0.00316 rad², σφ = 0.0562 rad = 3.2°. Check: a 10 dB stronger signal would give 1.0°. The usual mistake is to forget the square root.
P: 3. Idea: phase rotation per symbol = 360° × offset/symbol rate. 360 × 200/250 000 = 0.288°. Check: after 1000 symbols the rotation is 288°, so tracking is needed in long packets. The usual mistake is to use radians and report 0.288.
P: 4. Idea: a fractional rate difference ε slips one symbol after 1/ε symbols. 1/10⁻⁴ = 10 000 symbols. Check: at 1 Msym/s that is 10 ms. The usual mistake is to answer 100 symbols by misreading ppm as percent.
P: 5. Idea: subcarrier orthogonality holds only at exact spacing. An offset of a fraction of the spacing makes each subcarrier leak into its neighbors, intercarrier interference that the FFT cannot separate afterward, so the offset is removed in the time domain first. Check: Problem 6.7's 0.55 % residual already reaches −40 dB. The usual mistake is to think the equalizer can remove it like the common phase error.
P: 6. Idea: an offset estimate from repeated segments is unambiguous over ±1/(2T) for repetition T, and its accuracy improves with T. Short repetitions give a wide range for the coarse estimate; long ones give the accuracy for the fine estimate. Check: Wi-Fi's 0.8 µs short and 3.2 µs long symbols give ±625 kHz and ±156 kHz. The usual mistake is to think the short symbols are only for gain control.
P: 7. Idea: phase between repeats is unambiguous within ±π, so ±1/(2T). ±1/(2 × 1.6 µs) = ±312.5 kHz. Check: Wi-Fi's 0.8 µs short symbols give twice that, ±625 kHz. The usual mistake is to use 1/T.
P: 8. Idea: the primary signal carries one of 3 identities within a group, the secondary one of 336 groups. 3 × 336 = 1008 physical cell identities. Check: LTE had 504, 3 × 168. The usual mistake is to add 3 and 336.
P: 9. Idea: compare the worst-case offset with the short training field's range. 2 × 25 ppm × 2.4 GHz = 120 kHz, well within ±625 kHz. Check: even at 5.8 GHz the offset is 290 kHz, still covered. The usual mistake is to count 25 ppm once.
P: 10. Idea: drift in symbols = symbols × fractional rate difference. 2 ms × 10 MBd = 2 × 10⁴ symbols, × 4 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.8 symbol. Check: without tracking the last symbols would be sampled almost a whole symbol late. The usual mistake is to use 40 ppm as 4 × 10⁻⁴.
H2: Chapter 11
P: 1. Idea: delay spread in symbols = delay × symbol rate. 5 µs × 10⁷ = 50 symbols. Check: a 50-tap equalizer at 10 Msym/s is why such rates use OFDM. The usual mistake is to use the bit rate.
P: 2. Idea: the inverse of 1 + a z⁻¹ has taps (−a)^k, whose squares sum to 1/(1 − a²). 1/(1 − 0.49) = 1.96, 10 log₁₀1.96 = 2.9 dB. Check: an echo of 0.9 would give 5.3, 7.2 dB, rising toward infinity as the echo approaches 1. The usual mistake is to use 1/(1 − a), 3.33.
P: 3. Idea: multiply the polynomials. (1 − 0.7z⁻¹)(1 + 0.7z⁻¹ + 0.49z⁻²) = 1 + 0 + 0 − 0.343z⁻³, a residual of −0.343 three symbols later. Check: the residual is −a³ = −0.7³. The usual mistake is to drop the sign.
P: 4. Idea: LMS converges for 0 < µ < 2/(N Px). 2/(21 × 0.5) = 0.19. Check: practical step sizes are a tenth of the bound for low misadjustment, about 0.02. The usual mistake is to use 1/(N Px).
P: 5. Idea: the feedback filter uses past decisions, which carry no noise. Interference from past symbols is cancelled with noise-free decisions, so only the forward filter, which handles the remaining interference, amplifies noise. Check: when decisions are wrong the advantage turns into error propagation. The usual mistake is to think the DFE cancels noise.
P: 6. Idea: the cyclic prefix makes the channel circular over the FFT window. With a prefix longer than the delay spread, each subcarrier sees one complex gain, removed by one multiplication. Check: this is the reason behind Problem 6.6 as well. The usual mistake is to think the subcarriers are narrow enough to need no equalization.
P: 7. Idea: the trellis state is the previous L symbols for a channel spanning L + 1 symbols. L = 5, so 2⁵ = 32 states for binary symbols. Check: 4-ary symbols would need 4⁵ = 1024. The usual mistake is 2⁶.
P: 8. Idea: the FFT turns only circular convolution into multiplication. The prefix makes the channel's linear convolution circular over each block, so one complex gain per bin applies; without it, block edges see interference from the previous block. Check: the same mechanism as OFDM, with the FFT at the receiver only. The usual mistake is to think the prefix is only a guard against echoes.
P: 9. Idea: compare what makes error propagation tolerable. A SerDes channel is fixed and characterized, so the DFE taps are accurate, and strong FEC after it absorbs the short bursts a wrong feedback decision causes. Check: radio channels change with motion, so a radio DFE must track and its errors last longer. The usual mistake is to think SerDes signals have no noise.

P: 8. Idea: MSK is FSK with the smallest index that keeps the tones orthogonal, h = 0.5. The tone spacing is h × Rb = 0.5 × 1 MHz = 500 kHz. Check: ±250 kHz deviation. The usual mistake is h = 1.
P: 9. Idea: C/N0 = C − (−174 + NF). −130 − (−171) = 41 dB-Hz. Check: GPS receivers track down to about 25 dB-Hz, so there is margin. The usual mistake is to subtract a bandwidth as well.
P: 10. Idea: Bluetooth's slot is 625 µs and it hops once per slot. 1/625 µs = 1600 hops per second. Check: multi-slot packets lower the hop rate. The usual mistake is to double it for transmit and receive.

H2: Chapter 13
P: 1. Idea: pilots spaced Δ in frequency sample the channel's spectrum, which can represent impulse responses up to 1/Δ long. 1/120 kHz = 8.3 µs. Check: LTE's normal prefix of 4.7 µs is shorter. The usual mistake is to use half, 4.2 µs.
P: 2. Idea: pilots spaced T in time follow Doppler up to 1/(2T). 1/(2 × 0.25 ms) = 2000 Hz, and v = fD λ = 2000 × 0.0857 m = 171 m/s, about 617 km/h. Check: 3.5 GHz's wavelength is 8.57 cm. The usual mistake is to forget the factor of 2.
P: 3. Idea: an estimate averaged over four pilots has a quarter of the noise, which adds to the data noise. Effective SNR = SNR/(1 + 1/4) = 25 − 0.97 = 24.0 dB. Check: one pilot would cost 3 dB, sixteen 0.26 dB. The usual mistake is to subtract 6 dB, the averaging gain.
P: 4. Idea: overhead = fraction of resource elements used for pilots. 1/(4 × 7) = 1/28 = 3.6 %. Check: LTE with one antenna port is in this range. The usual mistake is 1/4 + 1/7.
P: 5. Idea: each channel from each transmit antenna must be measured separately. Each antenna needs its own orthogonal pilots, so the overhead grows with the number of antennas. Check: 8 ports use 8 times the pilot elements of one. The usual mistake is to think the receive antennas add overhead.
P: 6. Idea: decision-directed estimation uses data decisions as references. At low SNR many decisions are wrong, each feeding a wrong reference into the channel estimate, which causes more errors. Check: at high SNR the error rate is low and the method works. The usual mistake is to blame noise averaging alone.
P: 7. Idea: the limit is 1/(pilot spacing in hertz). 1/(4 × 15 kHz) = 16.7 µs. Check: four times the 4.17 µs of a 60 kHz pattern. The usual mistake is to use the subcarrier count without the spacing.
P: 8. Idea: per-pilot MMSE error = 1/(1 + SNR), least squares 1/SNR, for unit channel power. At 0 dB, 1/2 = 0.5 against 1.0, a 3 dB improvement. Check: at 20 dB the two differ by 0.04 dB. The usual mistake is to expect the advantage at high SNR.
P: 9. Idea: Nyquist in time, T ≤ 1/(2fD). 1/(2 × 100) = 5 ms, about 2.5 ms with a factor-of-two margin. Check: LTE's pilots every 0.5 ms are far denser. The usual mistake is 1/fD.

Atmospheric and orbit errors cancel only if both receivers see a satellite through nearly the same atmosphere; beyond a few tens of kilometers the errors decorrelate and the integer cycle counts can no longer be fixed reliably. Check: network RTK interpolates between bases to extend that. The usual mistake is to blame radio range for the corrections link.
P: 10. Idea: round-trip phase difference Δφ = 4π d Δf/c. 4π × 1 × 2 × 10⁶/2.998 × 10⁸ = 0.084 rad, 4.8°. Check: the unambiguous range is c/(2Δf) = 75 m. The usual mistake is to use 2π, the one-way case.
P: 11. Idea: σd ∝ 1/√SNR. A tenfold SNR improves it by √10 = 3.16. Check: the bandwidth term is unchanged. The usual mistake is to expect a factor of 10.
P: 12. Idea: FMCW range resolution is c/(2B), and the beat frequency is the sweep slope times the round-trip delay. ΔR = 2.998 × 10⁸/(2 × 4 × 10⁹) = 3.75 cm. The slope is 4 GHz/40 µs = 10¹⁴ Hz/s, the delay 2 × 6/2.998 × 10⁸ = 40.0 ns, so fb = 10¹⁴ × 40.0 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.00 MHz. Check: Worked Example 16.8 uses the same sweep. The usual mistake is to use the one-way delay and get 2.00 MHz.
H2: Chapter 17
P: 1. Idea: a random error pattern passes an r-bit CRC with probability 2^−r. 2^−16 = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵. Check: a 24-bit CRC gives 6 × 10⁻⁸. The usual mistake is to think a CRC catches every error.
P: 2. Idea: rate = information/transmitted. To go from 1/3 to 1/2, keep 2 of every 3 coded bits, a fraction of 2/3. Check: 1 information bit/(3 × 2/3) = 1/2. The usual mistake is to keep 1/2.
P: 3. Idea: from rate 1/3, rate 3/4 needs 4/3 coded bits per information bit, 4/9 of the 3. 1 − 4/9 = 5/9, 55.6 %, punctured. Check: 1/(3 × 4/9) = 3/4. The usual mistake is 1/4.
P: 4. Idea: bits lost = fade duration × rate. 2 ms × 10⁷ = 20 000 bits. Check: an interleaver must spread codewords over much more than 20 000 bits. The usual mistake is to use symbols.
P: 5. Idea: the receiver must collect a whole codeword before decoding. Its bits are spread across the interleaver's span, so the decoder waits for the full span. Check: a 20 ms interleaver adds about 20 ms each way. The usual mistake is to blame processing time.
P: 6. Idea: spread in frequency instead of time. Each codeword covers subcarriers separated by more than the coherence bandwidth, so one fade hits only some bits. Check: a slow fade in a static channel would defeat a time interleaver anyway. The usual mistake is to think OFDM removes fading.
P: 7. Idea: a maximal-length n-bit LFSR cycles through every nonzero state. 2¹⁵ − 1 = 32 767 bits. Check: the all-zero state is excluded because it would stay zero. The usual mistake is 2¹⁵.
P: 8. Idea: 5G uses base graph 2 for small blocks and low rates, base graph 1 for large blocks and high rates. A 300-bit block at rate 1/5 uses base graph 2. Check: base graph 2 serves blocks up to 3840 bits and rates down to 1/5. The usual mistake is to choose by rate alone.
P: 9. Idea: undetected errors per second = failed blocks × 2^−16. 1000 × 1.53 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.0153 per second, × 86 400 = about 1320 per day. Check: a 24-bit CRC would let through about 5 per day. The usual mistake is to forget that a CRC failure rate applies to every failed block.
P: 10. Idea: the link stays busy if processes cover the round trip. 4/0.5 = 8 processes. Check: LTE uses 8 for its 8 ms round trip at 1 ms. The usual mistake is to add one for the current slot when the round trip already includes it.
P: 11. Idea: RS(n, k) corrects (n − k)/2 symbols. (255 − 223)/2 = 16 bytes. Check: up to 128 consecutive bit errors within 16 bytes. The usual mistake is to correct n − k = 32.
H2: Chapter 18
P: 1. Idea: array gain = 10 log₁₀M. 10 log₁₀256 = 24.1 dB. Check: 64 elements give 18.1 dB. The usual mistake is 20 log.
P: 2. Idea: zero-forcing gain ≈ M − K + 1. 32 − 4 + 1 = 29, 14.6 dB. Check: with one user it equals the array gain, 15.1 dB. The usual mistake is M/K.
P: 3. Idea: overhead = pilots/coherence block. 16/500 = 3.2 %. Check: overhead scales with users, not antennas, in TDD. The usual mistake is to use antennas.
P: 4. Idea: channel hardening makes the variance of the normalized gain fall as 1/M. 64/4 = 16 times. Check: the standard deviation falls by 4. The usual mistake is to answer 4.
P: 5. Idea: who must learn which channels. In TDD, reciprocity lets the base station learn all antennas' channels from uplink pilots, with overhead proportional to users; FDD would need downlink pilots per antenna and feedback. Check: Problem 3's 3.2 % would grow with the antenna count in FDD. The usual mistake is to cite spectrum efficiency alone.
P: 6. Idea: count the cost per chain. Each digital chain needs its own converters and mixers, whose power and cost at millimeter wave are too high for every element. Check: a 256-element array with 4 chains uses phase shifters for the rest. The usual mistake is to think digital beamforming is impossible at millimeter wave.
P: 7. Idea: M − K + 1. 32 − 12 + 1 = 21, 13.2 dB. Check: compare 14.6 dB with 4 users. The usual mistake is to forget the +1.
P: 8. Idea: what dominates at low SNR. Noise dominates interference, so spending array gain to null interference wastes signal; regularization relaxes the nulls and moves the precoder toward maximum-ratio transmission. Check: at high SNR regularized ZF converges to ZF. The usual mistake is to think regularization only adds numerical stability.
P: 9. Idea: one burst per beam. 16 bursts, 320 ms at a 20 ms period. Check: hierarchical search cuts it. The usual mistake is 16 × 16 for both ends when only the handset sweeps here.
P: 10. Idea: gain loss ≈ e^(−σ²). e^(−σ²) = 10^(−0.05) = 0.891, σ² = 0.115, σ = 0.34 rad = 19°. Check: 1 dB costs 0.48 rad, 28°. The usual mistake is to use degrees in the exponent.
H2: Chapter 19
P: 1. Idea: SIR = (D/r)^n for one interferer. (1/0.5)⁴ = 16, 12.0 dB. Check: n = 2 would give 6 dB. The usual mistake is to use r/D.
P: 2. Idea: add interference and noise in milliwatts. 10^(−8.5) + 10^(−9.5) = 3.48 × 10⁻⁹ mW, −84.6 dBm, so SINR = −80 − (−84.6) = 4.6 dB. Check: noise adds 0.4 dB to the interference. The usual mistake is to add the dB values.
P: 3. Idea: Shannon with the SINR. 10.5 dB = 11.2, log₂(12.2) = 3.61 bit/s/Hz. Check: 10 dB gives 3.46. The usual mistake is to use decibels in the logarithm.
P: 4. Idea: invert SIR = (D/r)^n. (D/r) = 10^(1/3.5) = 1.93. Check: 1.93^3.5 = 10. The usual mistake is 10^(10/3.5).
P: 5. Idea: ratios are unchanged when everything scales. Signal and interference both scale with transmit power, so SIR stays the same and only noise shrinks. Check: an interference-limited user gains only the small noise share. The usual mistake is to think the edge user benefits.
P: 6. Idea: bandwidth beats SINR in Shannon's formula. Using the full bandwidth everywhere gives most users more throughput than a fraction of it at higher SINR, and edge users are protected by coordination. Check: reuse 3 gives a third of the bandwidth for a few decibels. The usual mistake is to think reuse 1 maximizes SINR.
P: 7. Idea: 1/ACIR = 1/ACLR + 1/ACS in linear terms. 10⁻³ + 5.01 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.50 × 10⁻³, ACIR = 28.2 dB. Check: below the weaker term, 30 dB. The usual mistake is to take the smaller dB value.
P: 8. Idea: noise rise = −10 log₁₀(1 − η). −10 log₁₀0.4 = 4.0 dB. Check: 50 % load gives 3 dB. The usual mistake is to use η directly.
P: 9. Idea: in a parallel sum the weaker term dominates. The base station's ACLR, 45 dB, far exceeds the handset's ACS, 33 dB, so the ACIR is near 33 dB. Check: 1/ACIR = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ + 5.0 × 10⁻⁴, 32.7 dB. The usual mistake is to blame the transmitter.
P: 10. Idea: capacity per area scales with cells per area, the inverse square of spacing. (400/100)² = 16. Check: SINR stays the same in an interference-limited network. The usual mistake is to answer 4.
P: 11. Idea: deferral radius ∝ threshold^(−1/n). 10^(10/30) = 2.15 times smaller. Check: n = 2 would give 3.16. The usual mistake is to divide by 10.
H2: Chapter 20
P: 1. Idea: slant range d = √((R + h)² − (R cos e)²) − R sin e. With R = 6371 km, h = 550 km, e = 10°: √(6921² − 6274²) − 1106 = 2921 − 1106 = 1815 km. Check: at zenith it is 550 km. The usual mistake is to use h/sin e, 3170 km, the flat-earth answer.
P: 2. Idea: delay = distance/c. 1815 km/2.998 × 10⁵ km/s = 6.05 ms. Check: about 3.3 ms per 1000 km. The usual mistake is to double it.
P: 3. Idea: Doppler scales with carrier frequency. Worked Example 20.2's radial velocity of 6986 m/s gives 12 × 10⁹ × 6986/2.998 × 10⁸ = 280 kHz. Check: 46.6 kHz at 2 GHz × 6 = 280 kHz. The usual mistake is to use the orbital speed.
P: 4. Idea: GEO is 35 786 km up. Round trip user to satellite and back, 2 × 35 786/2.998 × 10⁵ = 239 ms. Check: a full user-to-user hop through the satellite is twice that. The usual mistake is to use the slant range at the edge without saying so.
P: 5. Idea: the satellite receives many users at once. Each has different Doppler; precompensation aligns them in frequency so their subcarriers stay orthogonal. Check: Doppler of tens of kilohertz is several subcarrier spacings. The usual mistake is to think the satellite can correct each user separately before the FFT.
P: 6. Idea: compare path loss and delay. LEO's path loss is about 36 dB lower and its delay milliseconds, not a quarter second, which a handset can tolerate. Check: 20 log₁₀(35 786/550) = 36.3 dB. The usual mistake is to cite only coverage.
P: 7. Idea: C/N0 = EIRP − L + G/T − k, with −k = 228.6 dB. 40 − 190 + 5 + 228.6 = 83.6 dB-Hz. Check: a 10 MHz channel would see 13.6 dB of SNR. The usual mistake is to subtract 228.6.
P: 8. Idea: precompensation needs geometry. The handset must precompensate the satellite's delay and Doppler, and to compute them it needs its own position as well as the satellite's ephemeris. Check: the Doppler varies by tens of kilohertz across a pass. The usual mistake is to think GNSS is for timing only.
P: 9. Idea: trade footprint against slant range. A lower minimum elevation enlarges each footprint, so fewer satellites cover the Earth, but edge users see a longer slant range, more atmosphere, and more obstructions. Check: 550 km at 10° is 1815 km, at 30° about 1000 km. The usual mistake is to think elevation affects only coverage.
P: 10. Idea: specific attenuation γ = k R^α. 0.09 × 25^1.06 = 0.09 × 30.3 = 2.7 dB/km. Check: 5 km of rain path costs 14 dB. The usual mistake is to multiply 25 by 1.06.
H2: Chapter 21
P: 1. Idea: average current = active current × duty cycle + sleep current. 8 mA × 2 ms/10 s = 1.6 µA, plus 2 µA = 3.6 µA; × 3 V = 10.8 µW. Check: the sleep current is more than half the total. The usual mistake is to ignore sleep.
P: 2. Idea: life = energy/power. 2430 J/10.8 µW = 2.25 × 10⁸ s, about 7.1 years. Check: self-discharge limits coin cells near 10 years. The usual mistake is to use mAh and forget the voltage.
P: 3. Idea: BLE has 40 channels at 2 MHz; three are for advertising, 37, 38, and 39. Check: they sit between the busy Wi-Fi channels 1, 6, and 11. The usual mistake is 37, the data channels.
P: 4. Idea: S = 8 coding sends 8 symbols per bit, 8 times the energy per bit. 10 log₁₀8 = 9.0 dB. Check: the rate falls to 125 kbit/s. The usual mistake is to expect more from coding gain.
P: 5. Idea: processing gain = chip rate/bit rate. 2 × 10⁶/250 × 10³ = 8, 9.0 dB. Check: 802.15.4 maps 4 bits to 32 chips. The usual mistake is to use symbol rate.
P: 6. Idea: repetition gain = 10 log₁₀N. 10 log₁₀32 = 15.1 dB. Check: 16 repetitions give 12 dB. The usual mistake is 20 log.
P: 7. Idea: Tsym = 2^SF/B. 1024/125 000 = 8.19 ms. Check: as in Problem 12.3. The usual mistake is SF/B.
P: 8. Idea: life = capacity/current. 225 mAh/0.01 mA = 22 500 h, about 2.6 years. Check: 10 µA × 3 V = 30 µW, and 2430 J/30 µW = 2.6 years. The usual mistake is to use 1 mA.
P: 9. Idea: airtime is the shared resource. A device moved to a smaller spreading factor occupies the channel for much less time, so the gateway receives more messages before collisions rise, and different spreading factors are nearly orthogonal. Check: SF7 is 28 times shorter than SF12 in Worked Example 21.4. The usual mistake is to see only battery life.
P: 10. Idea: free-space range scales as 10^(ΔdB/20). 10^(6/20) = 2.0 times. Check: 6 dB is a factor of 4 in power, and power falls as distance squared. The usual mistake is 10^(6/10) = 4.
H2: Chapter 22
P: 1. Idea: complex data with real coefficients run the filter twice, once on I and once on Q, at the input rate. 2 × 48 × 30.72 × 10⁶ = 2.95 × 10⁹ real multiplications per second. Check: Worked Example 22.1 had 2.56 × 10⁹ for 32 taps at 40 MS/s, and 48 × 30.72/(32 × 40) = 1.15 times that is 2.95 × 10⁹. The usual mistake is to count one rail only and come out a factor of two low.
P: 2. Idea: each hard multiplier performs one multiplication per clock, so divide the rate by the clock. 2.95 × 10⁹/3.6864 × 10⁸ = 8.0, so 8 multipliers. Check: 8 × 368.64 MHz = 2.95 × 10⁹ per second exactly, since 30.72 × 96/368.64 = 8. The usual mistake is to give each tap its own multiplier, 96 in all, when the clock is twelve times the sample rate and lets each multiplier serve twelve taps.
P: 3. Idea: a pre-adder sums the two samples that share a symmetric coefficient, so a 48-tap filter needs 24 multiplications per output per rail. The count halves to 4 multipliers. Check: 2 × 24 × 30.72 × 10⁶ = 1.47 × 10⁹ per second, and 1.47 × 10⁹/3.6864 × 10⁸ = 4.0. The usual mistake is to assume the saving applies to the adders too; the pre-adder adds an addition per pair.
P: 4. Idea: each micro-rotation lengthens the vector by √(1 + 2^−2i), and the product converges. K = 1.6468, removed by one multiplication by 1/K = 0.6073 or folded into a later gain stage. Check: the first two factors alone are √2 × √1.25 = 1.581, already within 4 % of the limit. The usual mistake is to forget the gain and find every NCO output 4.3 dB too large.
P: 5. Idea: the ideal 6.02B + 1.76 dB assumes a sine with 3 dB PAPR; a peakier signal loses the excess. 6.02B + 1.76 − (10 − 3) ≥ 35 gives B ≥ (35 − 1.76 + 7)/6.02 = 6.68, so 7 bits. Check: 7 bits give 42.1 + 1.76 − 7 = 36.9 dB, above 35; 6 bits give 30.9 dB, below. The usual mistake is to ignore the PAPR and choose 6 bits.
P: 6. Idea: the ASIC's fixed cost is recovered by its lower price per unit. 1.5 × 10⁶/(35 − 5) = 50 000 units. Check: at 50 000 units the FPGA costs 1.75 million dollars and the ASIC 1.5 + 0.25 = 1.75 million, equal. The usual mistake is to divide the NRE by the ASIC's unit cost instead of by the difference in unit costs.
P: 7. Idea: raw video rate is pixels × bytes per pixel × frames per second. 640 × 480 × 2 × 30 = 18.4 MB/s, 147 Mbit/s. Check: the 1600 × 1200 frame of Worked Example 22.6 has 6.25 times the pixels at half the frame rate, 3.1 times the rate, and 147 × 3.1 = 461 Mbit/s. The usual mistake is to forget the factor of 8 between bytes and bits.
P: 8. Idea: power is operations per second times energy per operation. 10⁹ × 3 pJ = 3 mW; 10⁹ × 70 pJ = 70 mW. Check: a picojoule per operation at a gigaoperation per second is a milliwatt. The usual mistake is to compare only the arithmetic and forget that the processor also spends energy on every load and store, which can double its figure.
P: 9. Idea: every path between two registers must settle within one clock period. A long adder tree has many adders in series, and its delay exceeds the period at high clock rates; registers between levels cut it into short stages, so the clock can run fast at the cost of a few cycles of latency. Check: Worked Example 22.3's CORDIC is pipelined the same way, one iteration per clock and 16 cycles of latency. The usual mistake is to lower the clock instead, which forces more parallel copies of the whole filter.
P: 10. Idea: a full-scale tone puts all its energy into one bin, and each radix-2 stage can double the magnitude. log₂64 = 6 bits. Check: the DFT of a tone of amplitude A has a bin of magnitude NA = 64A, and 64 = 2⁶. The usual mistake is to allow only half a bit per stage, the growth for noise, and overflow on the first pure tone.
H2: Chapter 23
P: 1. Idea: range resolution depends only on the swept bandwidth. ΔR = c/(2B) = 2.998 × 10⁸/(2 × 10⁹) = 15.0 cm. Check: Worked Example 16.8's 4 GHz gives 3.75 cm, and a quarter of the bandwidth gives four times the cell, 15 cm. The usual mistake is to use the carrier frequency, which sets the wavelength but not the range resolution.
P: 2. Idea: the echo lags by 2R/c, and the sweep moves S hertz per second in that time. fb = 2RS/c = 2 × 5 × 5 × 10¹³/2.998 × 10⁸ = 1.67 MHz. Check: the delay is 33.4 ns, and 33.4 ns × 50 MHz/µs = 1.67 MHz. The usual mistake is to drop the factor of 2 for the round trip.
P: 3. Idea: phase between chirps is unambiguous up to ±π, which sets vmax = λ/(4Tc). λ = 2.998 × 10⁸/77 × 10⁹ = 3.89 mm, so vmax = 3.89 mm/(4 × 40 µs) = 24.3 m/s. Check: at that speed the round trip changes by 2 × 24.3 × 40 µs = 1.95 mm per chirp, half a wavelength, a phase change of π. The usual mistake is to use λ/(2Tc), which counts one-way motion and doubles the answer.
P: 4. Idea: noise-only cell power is exponential, so Pfa = e^(−T). T = −ln(10⁻⁴) = 9.21, or 9.6 dB above the mean. Check: Worked Example 23.1's 10⁻⁶ needed 13.8; each factor of 100 in Pfa adds ln 100 = 4.6, and 13.8 − 4.6 = 9.2. The usual mistake is to treat the threshold as a number of standard deviations of a Gaussian amplitude, which gives about 3.7 for 10⁻⁴ and far too many false alarms on power.
P: 5. Idea: every transmitter-receiver pair is a virtual element. 2 × 4 = 8 elements, and Δθ ≈ 2/8 = 0.25 rad = 14.3°. Check: the 12-element array of Worked Example 23.2 gave 9.5°, and 9.5 × 12/8 = 14.3°. The usual mistake is to add the antennas, 6, instead of multiplying them.
P: 6. Idea: spacing must not exceed half the shortest wavelength. λ = 343/16 000 = 2.14 cm, so d ≤ 1.07 cm. Check: Worked Example 23.3's 4 cm spacing aliased above 4.29 kHz, and 4 cm × 4.29/16 = 1.07 cm. The usual mistake is to use the speed of light out of habit from radio arrays.
P: 7. Idea: the wanted sound adds in amplitude, uncorrelated noise in power, so the SNR gains a factor of M. 10 log₁₀8 = 9.0 dB. Check: four microphones gave 6.0 dB, and doubling to eight adds 3 dB. The usual mistake is to use 20 log₁₀M, which would claim 18 dB.
P: 8. Idea: the light travels out and back. t = 2 × 2/2.998 × 10⁸ = 13.3 ns. Check: 30 cm per nanosecond one way means 15 cm of range per nanosecond of round trip, and 200 cm/15 = 13.3 ns. The usual mistake is to forget the return path and get 6.7 ns.
P: 9. Idea: inverse variances add. 1/σ² = 2/16, so σ = 4/√2 = 2.83 m. Check: averaging two equal, independent measurements divides the variance by two. The usual mistake is to halve the standard deviation, which would require four measurements.
P: 10. Idea: α = τ/(τ + Δt) with Δt = 10 ms. α = 1/(1 + 0.01) = 0.990. Check: the accelerometer's share, 1 − α = 0.0099 per step, accumulates with a time constant of Δt/(1 − α) = 1.01 s. The usual mistake is to use the sample rate, 100, in place of the sample interval.
H2: Chapter 24
P: 1. Idea: current is proportional to optical power, electrical power to current squared. Optical power falls 3 dB and electrical signal power 6 dB. Check: (1/2)² = 1/4, and 10 log₁₀4 = 6.0 dB. The usual mistake is to quote 3 dB for both and underestimate the SNR loss of a thermal-noise-limited receiver.
P: 2. Idea: I = R P with P in watts. −10 dBm = 100 µW, so I = 0.9 × 100 µW = 90 µA. Check: Worked Example 24.1 had 9 µA at −20 dBm, and 10 dB more light gives ten times the current. The usual mistake is to convert dBm to milliwatts and then treat the result as microwatts.
P: 3. Idea: a signal of B baud occupies about B hertz, which at wavelength λ is Δλ = λ²B/c. (1550 × 10⁻⁹)² × 25 × 10⁹/2.998 × 10⁸ = 0.200 nm. Check: 1 nm at 1550 nm is 125 GHz, and 25 GHz is a fifth of that. The usual mistake is to divide by λ instead of λ², which gives an answer off by nine orders of magnitude.
P: 4. Idea: spread equals dispersion × length × spectral width. 17 × 2 × 1 = 34 ps. Check: a 25 GBd symbol lasts 40 ps, so a 1 nm-wide source over 2 km nearly closes the eye, which is why such links use narrow lasers or the 1310 nm band. The usual mistake is to forget the length and quote 17 ps.
P: 5. Idea: divide the band by the grid spacing. 4800 GHz/75 GHz = 64 channels. Check: the 100 GHz grid gave 48, and 48 × 100/75 = 64. The usual mistake is to count edge channels twice or forget that a channel needs its full width inside the band.
P: 6. Idea: PAM4 carries two bits per symbol. 4 × 53.125 × 2 = 425 Gbit/s. Check: this is the DR4 rate of Worked Example 24.4, which carries 400 Gbit/s Ethernet plus FEC. The usual mistake is to count 4 bits per symbol because there are four levels.
P: 7. Idea: subtract every loss from the launch power, then compare with sensitivity. −1 − 3.5 − 1.0 = −5.5 dBm received, and the margin is −5.5 − (−12) = 6.5 dB. Check: Worked Example 24.2 had 9.1 dB with 1 dB more launch power, 2 dB more sensitivity, and 0.4 dB more splice loss: 9.1 − 1 − 2 + 0.4 = 6.5 dB. The usual mistake is to subtract the sensitivity the wrong way and report a negative margin.
P: 8. Idea: cycles equal duration times frequency. 562.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 38 × 10³ = 21.4 cycles. Check: one cycle at 38 kHz lasts 26.3 µs, and 562.5/26.3 = 21.4. The usual mistake is to assume the receiver needs only a cycle or two; its bandpass filter needs about ten to respond.
P: 9. Idea: line rate = symbol rate × bits per symbol × polarizations. 59.84375 × 10⁹ × 4 × 2 = 478.75 Gbit/s. Check: this is the 400ZR line rate of Worked Example 24.4. The usual mistake is to forget the second polarization and get half the rate.
P: 10. Idea: 50 km × 0.2 dB/km is 10 dB of loss, a transmittance of 10^(−1). One photon in ten, 10 %, survives. Check: each 15 km more adds 3 dB and halves the rate again, which is why QKD links stop near 100 km. The usual mistake is to treat the loss as electrical decibels and get 31.6 %.

P: 1. Idea: a LoRa symbol holds 2^SF chips at one chip per hertz of bandwidth. Tsym = 2048/250 000 = 8.19 ms. Check: SF7 at 250 kHz is 0.512 ms, and four steps up multiply it by 16. The usual mistake is to use SF instead of 2^SF.
P: 2. Idea: sensitivity = thermal floor + 10 log₁₀B + NF + required SNR. −174 + 54.0 + 6 − 17.5 = −131.5 dBm. P: 9. Idea: average current is charge per cycle divided by the cycle time. 200 mA × 2 s/600 s = 0.667 mA. Check: Worked Example 25.5's 180 mA for 1 s gave 0.30 mA, and 400/180 × 0.30 = 0.667. The usual mistake is to average the awake current over the awake time only.

H1: About the Author
P: Lothar J. Musiol is a graduate of Munich University of Applied Sciences who spent more than forty years in the semiconductor industry, first at Germany's largest company in the field, then at a string of American start-ups. He studied physics alongside all of it, seriously enough to know exactly how much he is simplifying. Dual citizenship, put to its most sensible use, lets him split his time between San Clemente, California, and Passau, Bavaria, where Austria begins at the garden fence. The household includes one married daughter, one grumpy Teacup Pomeranian named Gucci, and eleven chickens, listed in order of seniority rather than volume.
P: He writes to make complex ideas clearer and more engaging than most treatments manage, without ever pretending they are simpler than they actually are.
