H1: Chapter 3. Pulse Shaping and the Matched Filter
P: A symbol is not a point; it is a pulse with a duration and a spectrum. If pulses are too narrow, they occupy too much bandwidth. If they are too wide, they overlap, and each symbol's decision is disturbed by its neighbors, which is intersymbol interference. This chapter shows how the raised-cosine family of pulses avoids both, why the filtering is split equally between transmitter and receiver, and why the receive filter that best fights noise is the one matched to the pulse.
H2: 3.1 Nyquist's condition for zero interference
P: Symbols sent every T seconds can be separated perfectly if the overall pulse, from transmitter through receiver, is one at its own sampling instant and zero at every other multiple of T. The narrowest spectrum that does this is the sinc pulse, whose spectrum is a rectangle of width 1/(2T) at baseband. The sinc is impractical: its tails decay only as 1/t, so it must be very long, and a small timing error lets the tails of many symbols add up. The raised-cosine pulse keeps the zero crossings of the sinc but rounds the edges of its spectrum, spending an extra fraction α of bandwidth, the roll-off, to make the tails decay as 1/t³.
EQ: h(t) = sinc(t/T) × cos(παt/T) / [1 − (2αt/T)²],   bandwidth = (1 + α)/(2T) at baseband
H2: Worked Example 3.1
WEP: A satellite link sends 30 Msymbol/s. Find the occupied radio-frequency bandwidth with α = 0.35, 0.20, and 0.05. How fast could it signal in a 36 MHz transponder at each of the first two roll-offs?
WES: On a carrier the occupied bandwidth is Rs(1 + α): 30 × 1.35 = 40.5 MHz, 30 × 1.20 = 36.0 MHz, and 30 × 1.05 = 31.5 MHz. In a 36 MHz transponder the symbol rate can be 36/1.35 = 26.7 Msymbol/s at α = 0.35 or 36/1.20 = 30.0 Msymbol/s at α = 0.20, a 12.5% gain in throughput from the filter alone. The DVB-S2 standard offered roll-offs of 0.35, 0.25, and 0.20, and its S2X extension added 0.15, 0.10, and 0.05 for exactly this reason.
H2: 3.2 The price of a small roll-off
P: A sharp roll-off saves bandwidth but makes the pulse longer and the link more sensitive to timing error. The sensitivity can be computed directly: sample the raised-cosine pulse a tenth of a symbol late and add the magnitudes of all the neighboring pulses at that instant, which is the worst case of their interference. For α = 1.0 the sum is 0.079, for α = 0.5 it is 0.197, for α = 0.25 it is 0.331, and for α = 0.1 it is 0.510, against a main tap of about 0.98. A system that uses a small roll-off therefore needs good timing recovery and an equalizer, and the filters need more taps.
H2: 3.3 The matched filter
P: In white noise, the receive filter that maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio at the sampling instant is the time-reversed copy of the received pulse, the matched filter. Its peak output SNR depends only on the pulse energy E and the noise density, not on the pulse's shape: 2E/N0 for a real pulse, or E/N0 per complex dimension in the convention of this book. That is why error rates are written in terms of Eb/N0.
EQ: hMF(t) = p(T0 − t),   SNRmax = 2E / N0
P: The two requirements fit together. The raised-cosine response is split into two root-raised-cosine filters, one in the transmitter and one in the receiver. The receiver's filter is then matched to the transmitted pulse, and the product of the two is the raised cosine, which meets Nyquist's condition. Every modern single-carrier standard, from satellite to the cable modem, uses this arrangement.
KEY: Key idea. Split the pulse shaping into two equal root-raised-cosine halves. The transmit half limits the spectrum, the receive half is the matched filter that maximizes SNR, and together they make a raised cosine with no interference at the sampling instants.
H2: 3.4 Eye diagrams and timing
P: An eye diagram overlays the received waveform symbol period by symbol period. The opening of the eye at the sampling instant is the margin against noise; its width is the margin against timing error. Intersymbol interference closes the eye vertically, and jitter closes it horizontally. The receiver's timing loop steers the sampling point to the widest opening. With complex baseband, an eye is drawn for I and for Q separately, and the constellation of Chapter 4 is the same information seen at one instant.
H2: Worked Example 3.2
WEP: A rectangular pulse of amplitude 0.2 V and duration 1 µs is received in white noise with N0 = 10⁻¹⁰ V²/Hz (one-sided, for a 1 Ω reference). Find the matched-filter output SNR at its peak.
WES: The pulse energy is E = A²T = 0.04 × 10⁻⁶ = 4 × 10⁻⁸ V²·s. The peak SNR is 2E/N0 = 8 × 10⁻⁸/10⁻¹⁰ = 800, or 29.0 dB. Any other filter with the same noise gives a lower peak SNR; an integrate-and-dump circuit over exactly the pulse duration is the matched filter in this case.
H2: 3.5 The raised-cosine family in practice
P: The raised-cosine pulse is chosen not only because it meets Nyquist's condition but because its tails die quickly. A pulse with a brick-wall spectrum, α = 0, decays as 1/t, so a filter that approximates it must span dozens of symbols, and a small timing error lets the sum of many slowly decaying tails pile up into large interference. With α > 0 the tails decay as 1/t³. At α = 0.25 the response has fallen below 1 % of its peak within about three symbols of the center, so a filter spanning eight to twelve symbols is adequate.
P: Real systems split the raised cosine into two root-raised-cosine halves, one in the transmitter and one in the receiver, so that the receive filter is also the matched filter. Each half alone does not meet the zero-interference condition; only their cascade does. That is why a test receiver that uses a plain raised cosine on a root-raised-cosine signal reports a poor EVM even when the transmitter is perfect. The roll-off values are part of each standard: 0.22 for WCDMA, 0.35, 0.25, or 0.20 for DVB-S2, down to 0.05 in DVB-S2X, and 0.5 for TETRA's π/4-DQPSK.
H2: 3.6 Spectral regrowth: when the amplifier undoes the filter
P: Pulse shaping confines the spectrum only as long as the chain after it is linear. A power amplifier driven near compression multiplies the signal by itself, and every product of the in-band spectrum with itself lands outside it: the third-order products fill a band three times as wide as the original, centered on it, and the fifth-order products a band five times as wide. The leakage into the adjacent channel is the adjacent-channel leakage ratio, ACLR, the power in the neighbor's band relative to the wanted power. A smaller roll-off makes the envelope peakier, so the same amplifier regrows more. The amplifier's linearization belongs to Book 4 and the emission mask to Book 7. What belongs here is the trade: a tighter roll-off saves bandwidth but asks more of the amplifier.
KEY: Key idea. The pulse-shaping filter sets the spectrum only up to the first nonlinearity. Every dB of roll-off saved in bandwidth must be paid for in amplifier back-off or linearization.
H2: Worked Example 3.3
WEP: A 36 MHz satellite transponder carries a single DVB-S2 carrier. Find the highest symbol rate for roll-offs of 0.35, 0.20, and 0.05, and the rate gain of the smallest over the largest.
WES: The occupied band is Rs(1 + α), so Rs = 36 MHz/(1 + α). That gives 26.7 MBd at α = 0.35, 30.0 MBd at 0.20, and 34.3 MBd at 0.05. Going from 0.35 to 0.05 carries 34.3/26.7 = 1.29 times the data in the same transponder, 29 % more, which is why DVB-S2X introduced the small roll-offs once satellite amplifiers could be linearized well enough to keep the regrowth inside the guard band.
H2: Worked Example 3.4
WEP: A root-raised-cosine transmit filter for a 30 MBd carrier spans 8 symbols at 4 samples per symbol. Find the number of taps and the multiply rate for a direct implementation of the I and Q filters, and the rate when the upsampling is done in polyphase form.
WES: Eight symbols at 4 samples per symbol is 32 sample intervals, so 33 taps. Running at 120 MS/s, each rail needs 33 × 120 × 10⁶ = 3.96 × 10⁹ multiplications per second, 7.9 × 10⁹ for I and Q. Three of every four input samples to a 4× interpolating filter are zeros, so the polyphase form of Chapter 9 skips them: each output needs only about 33/4 ≈ 8 or 9 multiplications, cutting the total to about 2.0 × 10⁹ per second.
H2: 3.7 Occupied bandwidth and channel utilization
P: A regulator does not ask for the roll-off; it asks for the occupied bandwidth, defined as the band holding 99 % of the power, and for the emissions outside the channel. The two numbers differ because a root-raised-cosine spectrum is not perfectly square and because an OFDM spectrum's sinc skirts fall slowly. Standards fix the fraction of a channel that the signal may fill. An LTE 20 MHz carrier uses 100 resource blocks of 180 kHz, 18 MHz, 90 % of the channel. 5G NR, with steeper filtering applied to its OFDM signal, fills 273 resource blocks of 360 kHz, 98.28 MHz, of a 100 MHz channel at 30 kHz spacing, about 98 %. The difference, 8 % of the spectrum, is capacity recovered by better transmitter filtering, at the price of more demanding filters in both transmitter and receiver.
H2: Worked Example 3.5
WEP: How much more data does a 100 MHz carrier carry at 98.3 % utilization than it would at LTE's 90 %, all else equal?
WES: The ratio is 98.28/90 = 1.092, so 9.2 % more. Across an operator's 100 MHz of mid-band spectrum, that is the same as being given another 9 MHz of spectrum for free.
P: Nyquist's condition, the matched filter, and the eye diagram have turned a stream of symbols into a band-limited waveform and back. What the symbols are, and how often they are misread in noise, is the subject of Chapter 4.
