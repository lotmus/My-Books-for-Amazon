H1: Chapter 23. Signals That Sense: Radar Detection, Microphone Arrays, and Fusion
P: A 60 GHz radar chip the size of a fingernail can tell a sleeping person from an empty bed. When a chest rises by a millimeter, the round trip of the echo grows by two, and at a wavelength of 4.84 mm the echo's phase turns by 149°. No camera, no contact, and no light are needed; the sensor reads breathing from phase, with the same arithmetic as a carrier loop.
P: Where you meet this: Presence and occupancy sensors, automotive radar, smart speakers and conference phones with microphone arrays, laser time-of-flight sensors in phones and robots, BLE direction finding, and every drone or phone that fuses GNSS with an inertial sensor.
P: Sensing is communication in which the transmitter's message is known and the channel is the unknown. Every tool of this book applies: matched filters, FFTs, pilots, beamforming, and the estimation of time, frequency, and phase. Section 16.8 built the range-Doppler map of an FMCW radar. This chapter decides which of its cells hold a target, measures angle with an array, does the same with microphones in air, times light with a single-photon detector, and combines several imperfect sensors into one better estimate. The analog sensor interfaces are Book 2, Circuits, Components, and Control; the firmware that reads the sensors is Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics.
H2: 23.1 Detection and the constant false-alarm rate
P: A range-Doppler map holds thousands of cells, and most contain only noise. A detector compares each cell with a threshold. In complex Gaussian noise the power of a noise-only cell is exponentially distributed, so the probability that it exceeds a threshold T times the mean noise power is e^(−T): a false-alarm probability of 10⁻⁶ needs a threshold 13.8 times, 11.4 dB, above the mean. The difficulty is that nobody knows the mean in advance; it changes with clutter, temperature, and gain. A cell-averaging constant-false-alarm-rate detector, CA-CFAR, estimates it from N neighboring reference cells and scales the estimate. Because the estimate is itself noisy, the scale factor must be larger than the known-noise threshold, a CFAR loss that shrinks as N grows.
EQ: Pfa = e^(−T)  (known noise);   CA-CFAR: T = N (Pfa^(−1/N) − 1)
H2: Worked Example 23.1
P: Problem. Find the threshold above the mean noise power for a false-alarm probability of 10⁻⁶ when the noise level is known, and the scale factor of a CA-CFAR detector with 16 reference cells. What is the CFAR loss?
P: Solution. With known noise, T = −ln(10⁻⁶) = 13.8, 11.4 dB. With 16 reference cells, T = 16 × (10^(6/16) − 1) = 16 × 1.371 = 21.9, 13.4 dB. The CFAR loss is 2.0 dB: a target must be 2 dB stronger to be detected with the same probability, the price of not knowing the noise. With 32 reference cells it falls to about 1 dB, but a wider window is more easily confused by a second target or a clutter edge.
H2: 23.2 Angle from an array, real and virtual
P: A wave arriving at angle θ from broadside reaches two antennas d apart with a path difference d sin θ, a phase step of 2π(d/λ) sin θ. A third FFT across the antennas of a radar, or a phase measurement between two BLE antennas, turns that step into angle. With N elements spaced λ/2 the angular resolution at broadside is about 2/N radians. A MIMO radar multiplies its aperture without more receivers: each of Ntx transmitters sends a separable signal, by time or code, and each of Nrx receivers hears all of them, so the receiver sees Ntx × Nrx virtual elements. The IWR6843 of Worked Example 16.8 has 3 transmitters and 4 receivers and so forms 12. Bluetooth 5.1 adds the same idea to BLE: a constant tone extension at the end of a packet lets an array of antennas measure angle of arrival, the basis of the indoor positioning of Chapter 25. As with the antennas of Book 4, spacing above λ/2 creates grating lobes, angles the array cannot tell apart.
EQ: Δφ = 2π (d/λ) sin θ;   Nvirtual = Ntx × Nrx;   Δθ ≈ 2/N rad
H2: Worked Example 23.2
P: Problem. Find the angular resolution at broadside of the IWR6843's 12-element virtual array at λ/2 spacing, and the angle that gives a 60° phase step between adjacent elements. A BLE locator has antennas 4 cm apart at 2.44 GHz and measures a 60° phase difference. Find the angle.
P: Solution. Δθ ≈ 2/12 = 0.167 rad, 9.5°. At d = λ/2 the phase step is π sin θ, so 60° = π/3 gives sin θ = 1/3 and θ = 19.5°. For the BLE locator, λ = 2.998 × 10⁸/2.44 × 10⁹ = 12.29 cm and d/λ = 0.326, so sin θ = (60/360)/0.326 = 0.512 and θ = 30.8°. The same measured phase means a different angle because the spacing in wavelengths differs, which is why every array's calibration records its geometry.
H2: 23.3 Microphone arrays
P: Sound is slow, 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, so the same array arithmetic works with millimeters and microseconds instead of millimeters and picoseconds. A delay-and-sum beamformer delays each microphone's signal so that sound from the chosen direction lines up, then adds them: the wanted sound adds in amplitude and uncorrelated noise in power, a gain of 10 log₁₀M for M microphones. The spacing rule is the radio one: to avoid spatial aliasing at the highest frequency of interest, the spacing must not exceed half a wavelength, 2.1 cm at 8 kHz. At low frequencies the problem reverses: a small array is a fraction of a wavelength long and can barely form a beam, which is why smart speakers combine beamforming with adaptive noise cancellation. The steering delays are fractions of a sample at audio rates, and the Farrow structure of Section 9.6 provides them. Digital MEMS microphones with I2S or PDM outputs connect directly to a microcontroller such as the ESP32-S3.
EQ: d ≤ λmin/2 = c/(2 fmax);   gain = 10 log₁₀M;   τmax = (M − 1)d/c

H2: Worked Example 23.3
P: Problem. A linear array of four MEMS microphones spaced 4 cm apart samples at 48 kHz. Find the highest frequency free of spatial aliasing for steering over all angles, the largest steering delay across the array in samples, and the beamforming gain against uncorrelated noise.
P: Solution. Spatial aliasing starts where d = λ/2, at f = 343/(2 × 0.04) = 4.29 kHz. Sound arriving along the array's axis crosses its 12 cm span in 0.12/343 = 350 µs, 16.8 samples at 48 kHz, so the steering delays need fractional-sample resolution. The gain against uncorrelated noise, such as microphone self-noise, is 10 log₁₀4 = 6.0 dB; against a diffuse room noise field it is less at low frequencies, where the microphones' noise is correlated.
H2: 23.4 Timing light: optical time of flight
P: A laser time-of-flight sensor such as the STMicroelectronics VL53L1X sends 940 nm infrared pulses from a vertical-cavity laser and times the returning photons with an array of single-photon avalanche diodes, measuring up to 4 m at up to 50 readings per second. Light covers a millimeter of round trip in 6.67 ps, far below the timing jitter of any single photon detection. The sensor reaches millimeter precision by statistics: it fires many pulses, builds a histogram of photon arrival times, and finds the center of the peak, whose uncertainty falls as the square root of the number of detected photons. Ambient sunlight adds photons at random times, raising the histogram's floor, which is why such sensors lose range outdoors. The same principle at higher power and with scanning optics is automotive lidar.
EQ: t = 2d/c;   σcenter ≈ σsingle/√N
H2: Worked Example 23.4
P: Problem. A target is 4 m away. Find the round-trip time. If a single photon's arrival is timed with 200 ps rms jitter, how many detected photons give a 1 mm range precision?
P: Solution. t = 2 × 4/2.998 × 10⁸ = 26.7 ns. A millimeter of range is a round-trip time of 6.67 ps, so the histogram center must be found to 6.67 ps: N = (200/6.67)² = 899, about 900 detected photons. At one detected photon per few pulses that means thousands of pulses per reading, which a sensor firing at megahertz rates collects within a 20 ms measurement.
H2: 23.5 Fusing imperfect sensors
P: No sensor is good at everything. GNSS gives absolute position with meters of noise and no drift; an inertial sensor gives smooth short-term motion but drifts without bound; a radar or lidar sees obstacles but not its own position. Fusion combines them so that each covers the other's weakness. The simplest case is two independent measurements of the same quantity with variances σ1² and σ2²: weighting each by the inverse of its variance gives a combined variance smaller than either. A Kalman filter applies that rule recursively, alternating a prediction from a motion model, whose uncertainty grows, with an update from a measurement, whose weight, the Kalman gain, is set by the two variances. A complementary filter does the same job for tilt with fixed weights: it trusts the gyroscope over short times and the accelerometer over long ones, with a crossover set by a time constant. The full treatment, with code, is Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, chapter "Sensor Fusion: IMU Filters, Kalman, and SLAM Basics".
EQ: 1/σ² = 1/σ1² + 1/σ2²,   x = (x1/σ1² + x2/σ2²) σ²;   complementary α = τ/(τ + Δt)
H2: Worked Example 23.5
P: Problem. A GNSS fix has a standard deviation of 3 m, and a dead-reckoning prediction from wheel speed and heading has 2 m. Find the fused standard deviation and the weight given to the GNSS fix. A complementary tilt filter runs at 100 Hz with a 0.5 s time constant. Find its coefficient.
P: Solution. 1/σ² = 1/9 + 1/4 = 0.361, so σ = 1.66 m, better than either. The GNSS weight is (1/9)/(1/9 + 1/4) = 4/13 = 0.31, and the prediction gets 0.69 because it is the better of the two at this moment. For the tilt filter, α = 0.5/(0.5 + 0.01) = 0.980: each step keeps 98 % of the gyro-propagated angle and takes 2 % from the accelerometer.

H2: Worked Example 23.6
P: Problem. A 60 GHz presence sensor watches a sleeping person whose chest moves by 1 mm with each breath. Find the change in the echo's phase, and say why phase, not range, is measured.
P: Solution. The round trip changes by 2 mm, so the phase changes by 4π × 1 mm/4.84 mm = 2.60 rad, 149°. The range resolution of even a 4 GHz sweep is 3.75 cm, 37 times the motion, so the range bin does not change at all; the phase of that bin, tracked chirp after chirp like a carrier in Chapter 10, carries the breathing waveform. The same phase tracking detects heartbeats, whose chest motion is a fraction of a millimeter.
BENCH: When a radar or microphone array reports targets that are not there, look at the threshold before the algorithm. Plot the noise in the reference cells over a minute: if it drifts with temperature or with a fan's speed, a fixed threshold will either miss targets or invent them, and a CFAR window that is too short will be pulled up by the target itself. For arrays, verify the geometry first; one channel with swapped polarity or a few millimeters of spacing error moves every angle the array reports.

P: Who uses this: radar and sensor signal-processing engineers in automotive, industrial automation, smart-building, and consumer-electronics companies, and audio DSP engineers who design voice capture.
