H1: Chapter 10. Carrier and Timing Synchronization
P: Two Wi-Fi radios that both meet their ±20 ppm crystal specification can disagree on 5.8 GHz by 232 kHz. For Wi-Fi 5 that is about three quarters of a subcarrier spacing; for Wi-Fi 6, with its narrower subcarriers, it is about three spacings. Yet every packet decodes. The first few microseconds of the preamble exist to measure that error and remove it before a single data bit is read.
P: Where you meet this: Every Wi-Fi preamble, every 5G synchronization signal, every Costas loop and timing recovery block in an SDR flowgraph, and every crystal specification in parts per million.
P: The constellations of Chapter 4 assume that the receiver knows the carrier's exact frequency and phase and samples each symbol at exactly the right instant. It knows neither. The transmitter's and receiver's oscillators differ by tens of parts per million, the channel adds phase, and the two sample clocks drift apart. Synchronization is the set of loops and estimators that recover those quantities from the signal itself. This chapter follows the practical approach of Rice's Digital Communications and Johnson, Sethares, and Klein's Software Receiver Design.
H2: 10.1 The size of the offsets
P: A crystal oscillator is typically accurate to 10 to 20 ppm. At a 2.4 GHz carrier, 20 ppm is 48 kHz, and since the transmitter and receiver can err in opposite directions, the receiver may see up to 96 kHz of carrier offset. An uncorrected offset rotates the constellation steadily: an offset Δf advances the phase by 360° × Δf × Ts each symbol. In OFDM, an offset that is a significant fraction of the subcarrier spacing destroys orthogonality, so it must be estimated and removed before the FFT.
EQ: Δφ per symbol = 360° × Δf × Ts,   Δf = (ppmTX + ppmRX) × 10⁻⁶ × fc
H2: Worked Example 10.1
P: Problem. Two 2.4 GHz radios each have ±20 ppm crystals. Find the worst-case carrier offset and compare it with the subcarrier spacings of Wi-Fi 5 (312.5 kHz) and Wi-Fi 6 (78.125 kHz).
P: Solution. Each radio may be off by 48 kHz, so the worst-case difference is 96 kHz. That is 0.31 of a Wi-Fi 5 subcarrier spacing and 1.23 spacings in Wi-Fi 6. A Wi-Fi 6 receiver must therefore estimate the offset coarsely to the nearest spacing as well as finely within one, which its preamble is designed to allow.
H2: 10.2 Coarse and fine estimation from a preamble
P: Burst systems like Wi-Fi start each packet with a known preamble. A repeated section lets the receiver estimate frequency without knowing the data: the phase difference between two copies a time D apart is 2πΔfD, so the offset follows directly. Short repetitions give a wide range and coarse accuracy; long ones give a narrow range and fine accuracy, so preambles use both. The same correlation detects the packet's arrival and gives a coarse timing estimate. Continuous systems like 5G and broadcast use synchronization signals and pilots at regular intervals instead.
EQ: Δf̂ = ∠(Σ r*[n] r[n + D]) / (2πD Ts),   unambiguous for |Δf| < 1/(2D Ts)
P: Analog first. The Costas loop was first an analog circuit: John Costas described it in 1956, with two multipliers, two low-pass filters, a third multiplier forming the phase error, and a voltage-controlled oscillator. A hobbyist can still build a phase-locked loop from a CD4046 chip and a handful of resistors and capacitors, and watch it lock on an oscilloscope. Every loop in this chapter keeps that structure; only the multipliers have become arithmetic.
H2: 10.3 Phase tracking: the Costas loop
P: After the coarse correction a residual offset and the oscillator's phase noise remain, and a phase-locked loop tracks them. For suppressed-carrier signals like QPSK the loop cannot lock to a carrier tone, so it uses a phase detector that removes the modulation, either by decisions or by raising the signal to the fourth power; the Costas loop is the classic form. The loop's noise bandwidth BL trades two errors: a wide loop follows phase noise and offset changes but passes more noise, while a narrow one is quieter but slower. For a loop tracking a signal with carrier-to-noise density C/N0, the phase jitter variance is about BL/(C/N0).
EQ: σφ² ≈ BL / (C/N0)  rad²
H2: Worked Example 10.2
P: Problem. A carrier loop has a noise bandwidth of 10 kHz and tracks a signal at a C/N0 of 60 dBHz. Find the phase jitter.
P: Solution. σφ² = 10⁴ / 10⁶ = 0.01 rad², so σφ = 0.1 rad = 5.7°. For QPSK, whose decision boundaries are 45° away, that is acceptable; for 256-QAM, whose corner point has a neighbor only 4.1° away in angle, so that about 2° of rotation already puts it halfway to an error, it is far too large, so a high-order constellation needs a narrower loop, a cleaner oscillator, or pilot-aided phase correction.
P: Key idea. Synchronization is estimation: the receiver measures frequency, phase, and timing from known preambles, pilots, or the data itself, and the loop bandwidth trades how quickly it follows changes against how much noise it lets in.
H2: 10.4 Symbol timing
P: The receiver's sample clock also differs from the transmitter's symbol clock. A timing error detector estimates the error from the samples, the Gardner detector using two samples per symbol and needing no carrier lock, and a loop adjusts either the ADC clock or, more often, an interpolator that computes samples at the right instants from free-running ones. Even a small rate mismatch accumulates: a 50 ppm difference slips one full symbol every 20,000 symbols, so the loop must track continuously, not only once at the start.
H2: Worked Example 10.3
P: Problem. A 1 Msym/s link has a residual carrier offset of 1 kHz after coarse correction. How much does the constellation rotate per symbol and over a 100-symbol block?
P: Solution. 360° × 10³ × 10⁻⁶ = 0.36° per symbol, and 36° over 100 symbols. For QPSK, that would push points across a decision boundary within about 125 symbols, so the fine loop or pilot tracking must remove it.
H2: 10.5 Synchronizing a Wi-Fi packet, step by step
P: The 802.11a/g/n/ac/ax preamble shows how the estimation pieces of this chapter fit together in less than 20 µs. The legacy short training field repeats a 0.8 µs pattern ten times. The receiver correlates the incoming samples with a copy delayed by 0.8 µs; the correlation magnitude rises as soon as a packet arrives, which detects it and gives a coarse timing, and its phase gives a coarse frequency estimate with a range of ±1/(2 × 0.8 µs) = ±625 kHz. After that correction, the long training field repeats a 3.2 µs symbol twice. The same correlation over the longer lag refines the frequency with a range of ±156 kHz and about four times better accuracy, and a cross-correlation with the known symbol fixes the FFT window to within a sample. The pilots in every following symbol track the residual phase, as Section 10.3 describes.
FIG: fig10_1.png | Figure 10.1 — Coarse and fine frequency estimates in a Wi-Fi preamble. The short training field repeats every 0.8 µs and reads offsets unambiguously to ±625 kHz; the long field repeats every 3.2 µs and is four times more precise but wraps at ±156 kHz. A 232 kHz offset, 40 ppm at 5.8 GHz, reads correctly on the short field and as −80.5 kHz on the long field alone.
H2: 10.6 Finding a 5G cell
P: A 5G handset that switches on knows neither the cell, nor the timing, nor its own frequency error. The base station broadcasts a synchronization signal block, four OFDM symbols over 240 subcarriers, every 20 ms by default. Its primary synchronization signal is one of three length-127 m-sequences; the handset correlates for all three at a few candidate frequencies, which gives the symbol timing, a coarse frequency offset, and the cell's identity within its group. The secondary signal, a length-127 Gold sequence, gives one of 336 groups, so 3 × 336 = 1008 physical cell identities in all. The broadcast channel in the same block then delivers the frame timing and the system information needed to read the rest. Because the block sits on a coarse global raster rather than in the middle of every carrier, the search is limited to a few thousand candidate frequencies across all bands.
H2: Worked Example 10.4
P: Problem. A 5.8 GHz Wi-Fi link has crystals of ±20 ppm at both ends. Find the worst-case frequency offset, and say whether the long training field alone could estimate it.
P: Solution. The worst case is 40 ppm of 5.8 GHz, 232 kHz. The long training field's range is ±156 kHz, so it alone would alias; the short training field's ±625 kHz covers it with margin. After the coarse correction, the residual error, a few kilohertz, is well inside the long field's range, which then refines it. This two-stage design is why the standard allows ±20 ppm crystals.
H2: Worked Example 10.5
P: Problem. A handset searches for the primary synchronization signal at 3.5 GHz with a 10 ppm crystal and 30 kHz subcarrier spacing. How many frequency hypotheses must it test if each covers ±7.5 kHz?
P: Solution. The uncertainty is ±35 kHz, 70 kHz wide. Each hypothesis covers 15 kHz, so ⌈70/15⌉ = 5 hypotheses, each correlated against three sequences, 15 correlations per timing position. The handset's temperature-compensated oscillator, often ±2 ppm, cuts this to ±7 kHz and a single hypothesis, which is why every phone has one.
H2: 10.7 Timing loops and clock drift
P: Symbol timing must not only be found but held. The transmitter's symbol clock and the receiver's sampling clock come from different crystals, so even after acquisition the ideal sampling instant slides at the rate of their frequency difference. A timing-error detector produces a signal proportional to the timing error once per symbol; the Gardner detector does this with two samples per symbol and without knowing the carrier phase, which lets it run before the carrier loop has locked. A loop filter integrates the error and steers the Farrow interpolator of Chapter 9 so that its fractional delay μ ramps at the rate of the drift. When μ wraps past one sample, the interpolator inserts or drops a sample, and the loop continues without a glitch.
H2: Worked Example 10.6
P: Problem. A receiver samples a 1 MBd signal with a clock that differs from the transmitter's by 100 ppm. How often does the timing slip by a whole symbol, and how fast does μ ramp?
P: Solution. The timing slides by 10⁻⁴ of a symbol per symbol, so a whole symbol slips every 10⁴ symbols, every 10 ms. At two samples per symbol, μ ramps by 2 × 10⁻⁴ of a sample per symbol and wraps every 5000 symbols, 5 ms. A burst of 1000 symbols drifts by only 0.1 symbol, so a short Wi-Fi packet can get by on its initial estimate and pilot tracking, while a continuous broadcast stream needs a working loop.
P: Measure it yourself. Crystal offsets in parts per million, and the references that measure them, are in Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, chapter "Counters, Timers, and Frequency References".
P: Synchronization brings the samples onto the right frequency and the right time. What remains of the channel's echoes must be undone by an equalizer, the subject of Chapter 11.
BENCH: A constellation that rotates slowly is a residual frequency offset; one that smears into arcs is phase noise; one that blurs into a cloud as the packet goes on is a sampling-clock offset the receiver is not tracking. Measure the offset between your two radios with a tone before you debug the loop, and remember that a 40 ppm worst-case pair is legal.
