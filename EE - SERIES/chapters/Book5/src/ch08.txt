H1: Chapter 8. The 2026 Band Plan and the Software-Defined Receiver
P: A 30-dollar TV dongle tunes from 24 to 1766 MHz and streams its samples over USB. With a few hundred lines of free software it becomes an FM radio, an aircraft tracker on 1090 MHz, a weather-satellite receiver, or a spectrum analyzer. The radio in it is mostly arithmetic, and this chapter follows that arithmetic from the antenna to the sound card.
P: Where you meet this: Every SDR from an RTL-SDR dongle to a USRP, every GNU Radio flowgraph and PySDR notebook, and every Wi-Fi 7 and 5G channel number in a 2026 band plan.
P: The previous chapters built a waveform without asking where in the spectrum it would go. This chapter answers that question for the systems most readers will build or test in 2026, Wi-Fi and cellular, and then follows the signal into the receiver that turns it back into bits. The receiver is described as a software-defined radio, the architecture in which a fixed analog front end feeds a converter and everything else is code, because that is both how commercial chips are now built and how a student can experiment with every idea in this book. Emission masks and the test chamber belong to Book 7, and the power amplifier and synthesizer to Book 4.
H2: 8.1 Unlicensed and licensed spectrum
P: Wi-Fi in the bands discussed here operates under license-exempt rules: compliant devices may transmit without an individual spectrum license, subject to band-specific power, equipment, and sharing limits, and must tolerate interference from other authorized users. Cellular networks often use licensed spectrum; the applicable license and service rules define the permitted service, area, and interference rights, and licensing mechanisms vary by band. 5G NR also supports unlicensed operation called NR-U. Both systems use the same RF physics but operate under different authorization and interference rules. The 2.4 GHz band, 2400 to 2483.5 MHz, is 83.5 MHz wide and holds only three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels, 1, 6, and 11, shared with Bluetooth, microwave ovens, and much else. It is crowded. The 5 GHz band, with several hundred megahertz depending on the country, is the workhorse of Wi-Fi 5 and 6, with 80 and 160 MHz channels. This chapter is a 2026 snapshot; verify current national allocations and operating rules before transmitting.
H2: 8.2 The 6 GHz band
P: National allocations and equipment classes differ. The European Union has harmonized the lower 6 GHz band, 5945–6425 MHz (480 MHz), for WAS/RLAN.
P: In the United States, Wi-Fi devices use several unlicensed classes across 5925–7125 MHz: low-power indoor (LPI) access points and very-low-power (VLP) devices do not use AFC, but have different operating limits; standard-power access points and eligible fixed clients use AFC in U-NII-5 (5925–6425 MHz) and U-NII-7 (6525–6875 MHz). Effective 27 April 2026, FCC rules also permit geofenced variable-power (GVP) devices in those two sub-bands under geofencing controls that protect incumbent services. Check the local frequency, power, equipment class, and AFC or geofencing requirements before transmitting.
P: This band makes Wi-Fi 7 320 MHz channels, and the 23 Gbit/s figure in Chapter 7, possible; national rules determine where and how devices may use them.

H2: Worked Example 8.1
P: Problem. How many 320 MHz and 160 MHz channels fit in the full 6 GHz band, and how many 320 MHz channels in a 480 MHz national allocation?
P: Solution. 1200/320 = 3.75, so three 320 MHz channels fit with 1200 − 960 = 240 MHz left, and the remainder holds one 160 MHz channel and one 80 MHz channel. Of 160 MHz channels alone, 1200/160 = 7.5, so seven fit. A 480 MHz allocation holds one 320 MHz channel with 160 MHz left. In practice the standard fixes the channel centers, and the 320 MHz channels may overlap in two staggered sets, but the counts of non-overlapping channels are as calculated.
H2: 8.3 The cellular ranges
P: 5G NR defines two frequency ranges: FR1, from 410 MHz to 7.125 GHz, and FR2, from 24.25 to 71 GHz, the millimeter-wave range, itself split at 52.6 GHz. 5G-Advanced, the 3GPP releases from 18 onward, extracts more from those same bands rather than adding new ones. The research toward 6G around 2030 targets the gap between them, 7.125 to 24.25 GHz, sometimes called FR3, where wider channels than FR1 are possible with better coverage than FR2; candidate bands in that range are on the agenda of the 2027 World Radiocommunication Conference. Sub-terahertz frequencies of roughly 90 to 300 GHz remain a study item, not a 2026 phone band. Wi-Fi at 6 GHz and licensed cellular in the upper 6 GHz range sit next to each other in some countries, and power limits and coordination are how they share.
P: Key idea. The waveform is this book; the license is the band table. A design starts by asking which band, how wide a channel, and under what rules, and the answers fix the symbol rate and constellation before a line of code is written.
H2: 8.4 Receiver architectures
P: Four architectures cover nearly all receivers. The superheterodyne mixes to a fixed intermediate frequency, filters there sharply, and then converts to baseband; it has the best selectivity and the most parts. The zero-IF, or direct-conversion, receiver mixes straight to complex baseband with a quadrature mixer. It is cheap and integrates well, and it suffers from DC offset, flicker noise, and the I/Q imbalance of Chapter 2. The low-IF receiver mixes to a small offset to avoid the DC problems and relies on digital image rejection. Direct-RF sampling digitizes the antenna signal, after filtering and amplification, with a multi-gigasample converter; the clock jitter limit of Chapter 2 is its constraint. Most phones and Wi-Fi chips use zero-IF or low-IF; laboratory SDRs such as the ADALM-Pluto use a zero-IF transceiver chip with 12-bit converters.
H2: 8.5 Sensitivity
P: A receiver's sensitivity is the weakest signal it can demodulate at a target error rate. It is set by the thermal noise floor, −174 dBm/Hz at room temperature, plus 10 log₁₀B for the bandwidth, plus the receiver's noise figure, plus the SNR the modulation and code require. The noise figure itself, and how it builds up through the chain of stages, is Book 4's subject.
EQ: Psens (dBm) = −174 + 10 log₁₀B + NF + SNRreq
H2: Worked Example 8.2
P: Problem. A 20 MHz receiver has a 7 dB noise figure. Find its noise floor, and its sensitivity for a mode that needs 25 dB of SNR.
P: Solution. 10 log₁₀(20 × 10⁶) = 73.0 dB, so the floor is −174 + 73.0 + 7 = −94.0 dBm. With 25 dB of required SNR the sensitivity is −69 dBm. The 802.11a standard requires −65 dBm for its 54 Mbit/s mode, so this receiver would pass with 4 dB of margin. The same receiver needs only about −90 dBm for BPSK at rate 1/2, which is why the rate falls step by step as a user walks away from an access point.
H2: 8.6 SDR as a laboratory
P: A software-defined radio lets every chapter of this book be tried on real signals. The PySDR online textbook and the GNU Radio framework are good laboratories, though they are not substitutes for this book's theory. A useful sequence of exercises is: capture a broadcast FM station and display its spectrum; decimate the capture and plot the eye of a known digital signal; build a raised-cosine transmitter and matched-filter receiver in loopback; measure EVM against transmit gain to find where the amplifier starts to compress; and decode the beacon frames of a Wi-Fi access point, which use the 312.5 kHz OFDM of Chapter 6. Transmitting requires care: outside licensed amateur bands or a shielded enclosure, the laboratory should be receive-only.
H2: 8.7 The SDR you can buy
P: Four families of hardware cover almost all software-defined radio work in 2026. At the bottom, the RTL-SDR dongles combine an R820T2 or R828D tuner with the RTL2832U demodulator chip, originally made for DVB-T television, to give an 8-bit receiver from about 24 to 1766 MHz at up to 2.4 MS/s for about 30 dollars. The HackRF One covers 1 MHz to 6 GHz, transmits as well as receives, but only one at a time, and samples at up to 20 MS/s with 8-bit converters. The ADALM-Pluto wraps Analog Devices' AD9363 transceiver: 12-bit converters, 325 MHz to 3.8 GHz officially, 20 MHz of bandwidth, full duplex, but a USB 2.0 link that limits continuous streaming to a few megasamples per second. The Ettus USRP B210 uses the AD9361: 70 MHz to 6 GHz, two receive and two transmit channels, 56 MHz of instantaneous bandwidth, and a USB 3.0 link. Above that sit the networked USRPs and the FPGA boards with RF-sampling converters.
P: The software is layered in the same way. GNU Radio connects processing blocks into flowgraphs in Python or a graphical editor; SoapySDR and UHD hide the hardware differences; srsRAN and OpenAirInterface implement complete 4G and 5G base stations and handsets on a USRP. The limit is usually not the code but the transport: every complex sample at 16 bits per rail is 4 bytes, and the bus must carry them all without dropping one.
H2: Worked Example 8.3
P: Problem. A USRP B210 streams two receive channels at 30.72 MS/s with 16-bit I and Q. Find the data rate over USB, and the rate if the 8-bit sample format is chosen.
P: Solution. Each complex sample is 4 bytes, so 30.72 × 10⁶ × 4 × 2 channels = 245.8 MB/s. USB 3.0's practical throughput of roughly 300 to 400 MB/s carries it, with little room for anything else on the same controller. The 8-bit format halves it to 122.9 MB/s at the cost of about 48 dB of quantization SNR instead of 96 dB, still enough for most demodulation once a strong blocker is filtered out in the analog front end.
H2: Worked Example 8.4
P: Problem. An RTL-SDR samples at 2.4 MS/s with an 8-bit converter. Estimate its ideal dynamic range across the full band, and in a 200 kHz FM broadcast channel.
P: Solution. The ideal 8-bit SNR is 6.02 × 8 + 1.76 = 49.9 dB across the 2.4 MHz complex band. Filtering to 200 kHz gives a processing gain of 10 log₁₀(2.4/0.2) = 10.8 dB, so about 60.7 dB in the channel. Real converters lose a bit or so to their nonlinearity and the tuner's gain control has to keep the strongest station a little below full scale, which is why a nearby strong transmitter swamps such a cheap receiver.
P: Analog first. Before the software receiver, the hardware one. A classic broadcast FM receiver is a superheterodyne: an RF stage, a mixer to a 10.7 MHz intermediate frequency, a ceramic filter about 180 to 280 kHz wide that sets the selectivity, a limiting amplifier that strips off amplitude noise, and a quadrature detector whose output is the audio. No line of code is involved, and every stage can be probed with an oscilloscope. Building one is hobby and education work today, and Book 4, RF, Microwave, and Transceivers, builds receivers of this kind in "Receivers and Transmitters You Can Build". A 2026 product uses a single-chip receiver such as the Silicon Labs Si47xx family instead, and inside that chip is a digital chain much like the one below: learn the stages here, buy the IC at work.
H2: 8.8 A complete receiver in software: broadcast FM
P: The simplest real flowgraph shows every piece of Part I at once. An RTL-SDR tuned to a station delivers complex samples at 2.4 MS/s. A low-pass filter and a decimation by 10 keep the 200 kHz station at 240 kS/s. The FM demodulator takes the angle of each sample times the conjugate of the previous one, which is the phase step and so the instantaneous frequency. A second filter and decimation by 5 give 48 kS/s audio, a de-emphasis filter with a 50 µs time constant in Europe or 75 µs in the Americas restores the treble balance, and the result goes to the sound card. Each stage is a block from Chapters 2, 3, and 9; the whole receiver is a few dozen lines of Python with GNU Radio and runs on a laptop with most of a core to spare.

The RTL-SDR delivers 2.4 MS/s of complex samples; a low-pass FIR decimates by 10, the discriminator recovers the audio, a second filter decimates by 5 to 48 kS/s, and de-emphasis restores the tonal balance. Polyphase decimation cuts the first filter's work from 307 to 30.7 million multiplications per second. | Flow diagram of six boxes connected by arrows: RTL-SDR complex IQ at 2.4 megasamples per second, low-pass FIR divide by 10 to 240 kilosamples per second, FM discriminator computing the angle of x of n times the conjugate of x of n minus 1, low-pass divide by 5 to 48 kilosamples per second, de-emphasis 75 microseconds or 50 in Europe, and sound card. A note below gives 64 taps times 240 kilosamples per second times 2 rails as 30.7 million multiplications per second instead of 307 million.
H2: Worked Example 8.5
P: Problem. The first filter in this receiver is a 64-tap FIR running before the decimation by 10. Compare its multiply rate for complex data in direct form and in polyphase decimating form.
P: Solution. Direct form computes 64 complex-by-real products at 2.4 MS/s, 2 × 64 × 2.4 × 10⁶ = 307 million real multiplications per second, and then discards nine outputs of every ten. The polyphase form computes only the kept outputs, at 240 kS/s, so 2 × 64 × 240 × 10³ = 30.7 million. A tenfold saving for a change in the order of operations is why every SDR library implements decimation that way.
H2: 8.9 Filters and duplexers in the system
P: Everything in this chapter assumed that the receiver hears only the band it wants. A phone breaks that assumption with its own transmitter. In frequency-division duplex the handset transmits and receives at the same moment on two frequencies: in LTE Band 7 and NR n7 the uplink is 2500 to 2570 MHz and the downlink 2620 to 2690 MHz, a duplex gap of only 50 MHz. The transmitter delivers about 23 dBm at the antenna, so its power amplifier puts out about 26 dBm, while the receiver must find a signal near −100 dBm. Two things leak across. The transmit carrier itself, 50 MHz or more away, can drive the low-noise amplifier into compression or mix with other signals; the receive filter must suppress it. And the amplifier's own wideband noise, which falls inside the receive band, adds directly to the receiver's noise floor; only the transmit filter can remove that, because once it is in the receive band no later filter can separate it from the wanted signal.
P: The two filters together are the duplexer, and its isolation in each band is a system number that follows from the sensitivity budget of Section 8.5. Time-division duplex, the 3.5 GHz band n78 for example, avoids the duplexer by switching between transmit and receive, but it still needs a filter against the neighbors. Coexistence inside one phone adds more cases: Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz sits directly against LTE Band 40 below it and within 13 MHz of Band 41 above it, so a phone that runs both at once needs filters with steep skirts in both radios. Carrier aggregation multiplies the problem, because several bands must be received at once through one antenna, and the duplexer becomes a quadplexer or a multiplexer. A 2026 flagship phone carries dozens of such filters. Their technology, the surface- and bulk-acoustic-wave resonators that make the steep skirts possible in a few square millimeters, is Book 4, RF, Microwave, and Transceivers, in its chapter "Acoustic and Micro-Mechanical Filters: SAW, BAW, XBAR, and the Smartphone Front End"; what this section adds is where the isolation requirement comes from.
EQ: allowed noise = floor + 10 log₁₀(10^(D/10) − 1),   isolation = PA noise density − allowed noise
H2: Worked Example 8.6
P: Problem. A Band 7 handset receiver has a 4 dB noise figure and may lose at most 0.5 dB of sensitivity to its own transmitter. The power amplifier's output noise in the receive band is −128 dBm/Hz, and its carrier leaves the amplifier at +26 dBm. The low-noise amplifier tolerates a blocker of −30 dBm at its input. Find the receive-band isolation the duplexer's transmit filter must provide, and the transmit-band rejection its receive filter must provide.
P: Solution. The receiver's noise floor is −174 + 4 = −170 dBm/Hz. A 0.5 dB desense allows added noise of 10 log₁₀(10^0.05 − 1) = −9.1 dB relative to the floor, −179.1 dBm/Hz. The transmit filter must therefore attenuate the amplifier's −128 dBm/Hz in the receive band by 51.1 dB. The carrier must fall from +26 dBm to −30 dBm at the low-noise amplifier, 56 dB of rejection at the transmit frequency in the receive path. Both figures are in the range a good acoustic duplexer delivers, 50 to 60 dB, and neither leaves much margin, which is why the antenna, the duplexer, and the amplifier's noise are specified together.

BENCH: An SDR overloads more easily than its specification suggests: one strong broadcast station or cell tower anywhere in its input bandwidth eats the dynamic range for everything else. If weak signals vanish when the gain is high, lower the gain until the noise floor drops by less than the signal does, and add a band filter in front. Start every new setup by recording a known strong station, so you know the chain works before you hunt weak ones.
P: On the Job
P: Who uses this: wireless system engineers at phone, router, and module makers; SDR and test engineers at instrument companies; and the developers of open-source receivers. Typical chips are the AD9361-class transceivers, Qualcomm, MediaTek, and Broadcom Wi-Fi and cellular chipsets, and RTL-SDR, HackRF, ADALM-Pluto, and USRP boards in the lab. The tools are GNU Radio, Python with NumPy, MATLAB, and a vector signal analyzer. A realistic task: a new Wi-Fi 7 router fails its 4096-QAM rate at 3 m; capture its signal with a USRP, compute EVM against the −38 dB requirement of Chapter 4, and find which term of the EVM budget, phase noise, amplifier, or I/Q imbalance, is over its share.
