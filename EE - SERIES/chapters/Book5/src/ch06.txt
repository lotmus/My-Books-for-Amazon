H1: Chapter 6. OFDM: Many Slow Carriers Instead of One Fast One
P: Chapter 5 showed that a wideband signal in a multipath channel sees a frequency response full of notches, and that a single fast carrier would need a long and power-hungry equalizer to undo them. Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing takes the opposite approach. It divides the band into many narrow subcarriers, each slow enough to see a flat channel, and sends a separate QAM symbol on each. A fast Fourier transform builds and separates them, a short guard interval absorbs the echoes, and equalization becomes one complex division per subcarrier. Wi-Fi, 4G, 5G, digital television, and DSL all use it.
H2: 6.1 Orthogonal subcarriers
P: N subcarriers spaced Δf apart are orthogonal over a symbol of duration T = 1/Δf: over that interval each completes a whole number of cycles more than its neighbor, so their product integrates to zero. An inverse FFT of N QAM values produces exactly such a sum, sampled at N Δf. The receiver's FFT separates them again without any analog filter. Each subcarrier's spectrum is a sinc that overlaps its neighbors, but at the center of each subcarrier all the others pass through zero, so there is no interference.
EQ: Δf = fs / N,    T = 1 / Δf
H2: Worked Example 6.1
WEP: The original 802.11a/g Wi-Fi uses a 64-point FFT at 20 MS/s with a 0.8 µs guard interval, and 48 data subcarriers plus 4 pilots. Find the subcarrier spacing, the useful symbol time, the total symbol time, the guard overhead, and the top rate with 64-QAM at code rate 3/4.
WES: Δf = 20 MHz/64 = 312.5 kHz, so T = 3.2 µs, and with the guard the symbol is 4.0 µs. The guard takes 0.8/4.0 = 20% of the time, a loss of 10 log₁₀(1/0.8) = 0.97 dB. The 52 used subcarriers occupy 52 × 312.5 kHz = 16.25 MHz of the 20 MHz channel. The rate is 48 × 6 bits × 3/4 / 4.0 µs = 54 Mbit/s, the familiar top figure of 802.11a/g.
H2: 6.2 The cyclic prefix
P: The guard interval is filled with a copy of the end of the symbol, a cyclic prefix. As long as every echo arrives within the prefix, the receiver's FFT window contains a whole number of cycles of every delayed copy, and the channel's effect on each subcarrier is a single complex gain, H(k). The receiver measures H(k) from known pilots and divides it out: one complex division per subcarrier replaces the long equalizer. The 0.8 µs Wi-Fi prefix covers path differences up to 0.8 µs × c = 240 m, ample indoors. The cost is the prefix overhead and the fact that subcarriers in a notch carry little; coding across subcarriers, Chapter 7, recovers that loss as frequency diversity.
KEY: Key idea. OFDM turns a frequency-selective channel into N flat ones. The cyclic prefix makes the channel's convolution circular, so the FFT diagonalizes it, and each subcarrier needs only one complex gain to equalize.
H2: 6.3 Wi-Fi 6 and 7, and 5G numerology
P: Later Wi-Fi generations kept the idea and changed the numbers. 802.11ax and 802.11be use a spacing of 78.125 kHz, a quarter of the original, so the useful symbol is 12.8 µs, and guard intervals of 0.8, 1.6, or 3.2 µs; with the 0.8 µs guard the symbol is 13.6 µs. The longer symbol makes the fixed guard a smaller fraction of the time. 5G NR instead defines a family of numerologies with spacing Δf = 15 × 2^µ kHz for µ = 0 to 4 and beyond, keeping 14 symbols per slot, so a slot lasts 1/2^µ ms.
P: The useful symbol times are 66.7, 33.3, 16.7, 8.33, and 4.17 µs for spacings of 15, 30, 60, 120, and 240 kHz. The normal cyclic prefix at 15 kHz is 144 samples at 30.72 MS/s, 4.69 µs, and it shrinks in the same proportion as the symbol. Wide spacings tolerate Doppler and phase noise, which matter at millimeter wave; narrow spacings tolerate long delay spreads, which matter in large outdoor cells. A 100 MHz carrier at 30 kHz uses 273 resource blocks of 12 subcarriers, 3276 in all, occupying 98.28 MHz, and a 4096-point FFT at 122.88 MS/s.
H2: Worked Example 6.2
WEP: Find the slot length and useful symbol time for 5G numerology µ = 1 and µ = 3, and say which would be used in a 28 GHz cell.
WES: µ = 1 gives 30 kHz spacing, a 33.3 µs useful symbol, and a 0.5 ms slot. µ = 3 gives 120 kHz, 8.33 µs, and a 0.125 ms slot. At 28 GHz the Doppler shift at a given speed is eight times that at 3.5 GHz and oscillator phase noise is worse, so the wider 120 kHz spacing is used; the shorter prefix is acceptable because the narrow beams of millimeter-wave arrays cut the delay spread.
H2: 6.4 Peak-to-average power
P: An OFDM signal is the sum of many independent subcarriers, so by the central limit theorem its samples are nearly Gaussian, with occasional large peaks. The peak-to-average power ratio is the price OFDM pays, and the transmitter's power amplifier pays it in back-off and efficiency, a subject Book 4 owns. The theoretical maximum is 10 log₁₀N, 18.1 dB for 64 subcarriers, but it almost never occurs. Under the Gaussian approximation, the probability that a symbol's PAPR exceeds z is about 1 − (1 − e^(−z))^N. The level exceeded by one symbol in a thousand is 10.4 dB for N = 64, 11.0 dB for 256, and 11.6 dB for 2048. The figure grows only slowly with N. Clipping and filtering, tone reservation, and the DFT-spread OFDM of the 4G and 5G uplink reduce it.
EQ: P(PAPR > z) ≈ 1 − (1 − e^(−z))^N
H2: 6.5 Frequency offset and phase noise in OFDM
P: OFDM's orthogonality rests on every subcarrier completing a whole number of cycles in the FFT window. A residual carrier frequency offset of a fraction ε of the subcarrier spacing breaks that. Every subcarrier then leaks into its neighbors, intercarrier interference, and the wanted subcarrier loses a little amplitude. For small ε the leaked power relative to the signal is about (πε)²/3. Oscillator phase noise does two things. Its slow part rotates every subcarrier of a symbol by the same angle, the common phase error, which the pilots measure and remove. Its fast part, wider than the subcarrier spacing, behaves like a random frequency offset and adds intercarrier interference that no pilot can undo. That is why the millimeter-wave numerologies of Section 6.3 use wide spacings and why 5G NR added the phase-tracking reference signal for them.
EQ: ICI/S ≈ (π ε)² / 3,    ε = residual offset / Δf
H2: 6.6 DFT-spread OFDM for the uplink
P: The handset's amplifier pays OFDM's peak-to-average ratio with battery life and range. The 4G uplink and the 5G coverage-limited uplink therefore precode the M data symbols with an M-point DFT before mapping them onto contiguous subcarriers. The inverse FFT nearly undoes the DFT, and the transmitted signal is close to a single carrier with the original constellation's low PAPR, while the receiver still equalizes it with one complex gain per subcarrier and an inverse DFT. For QPSK the 0.1 % PAPR falls from about 8.5 dB for OFDM to about 6 dB, and π/2-BPSK in 5G brings it lower still. The price is that a deep notch spreads its damage over all symbols instead of hitting a few, so the receiver needs an MMSE equalizer, Chapter 11, rather than simple division.
H2: Worked Example 6.3
WEP: A 5G handset at 3.5 GHz uses 30 kHz subcarrier spacing. After synchronization its residual frequency error is 0.1 ppm. Find ε and the intercarrier interference level.
WES: 0.1 ppm of 3.5 GHz is 350 Hz, so ε = 350/30 000 = 0.0117. The interference is (π × 0.0117)²/3 = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴, or −33.5 dB, which caps the SNR of the link at about 33.5 dB, enough for 256-QAM but marginal for 1024-QAM. Tighter frequency tracking, Chapter 10, is the fix.
H2: Worked Example 6.4
WEP: Switching a handset's uplink from OFDM to DFT-spread OFDM reduces the PAPR by 2.5 dB, and the amplifier can raise its average output by the same amount. With a path-loss exponent of 3.5, how much do the range and the coverage area grow?
WES: The range scales as 10^(2.5/35) = 1.18, an 18 % gain, and the area as 1.18² = 1.39, 39 % more. That is the whole reason a more complicated waveform exists for the uplink.
H2: 6.7 Choosing OFDM parameters from first principles
P: The numbers of an OFDM system follow from the channel in three steps. The cyclic prefix must exceed the longest significant echo. The useful symbol must then be long enough that the prefix is a small overhead, typically under 10 %, which caps the subcarrier spacing from above. The spacing must also be large compared with the Doppler shift and the oscillator's phase noise, so that intercarrier interference stays small, which bounds it from below. If the two bounds cross, the channel is too hostile for OFDM with those numbers, and the designer must accept more overhead or shorten the echoes with directional antennas.
H2: Worked Example 6.5
WEP: Design the subcarrier spacing for a 10 MHz outdoor system whose echoes last up to 4.7 µs and whose users move at up to 120 km/h at 2 GHz, keeping the prefix overhead below 7.5 % and the Doppler below 2 % of the spacing.
WES: The prefix is 4.7 µs, so the useful symbol must be at least 4.7/0.075 − 4.7 = 58 µs, and the spacing at most 17 kHz. The Doppler is 33.3 m/s × 2 × 10⁹/3 × 10⁸ = 222 Hz, which requires a spacing of at least 222/0.02 = 11.1 kHz. Any spacing between 11.1 and 17 kHz works; 15 kHz, with a 66.7 µs symbol and a 4.69 µs prefix, is LTE's choice, and the overhead is 4.69/71.4 = 6.6 %.
P: OFDM splits the channel into flat pieces, but some of those pieces still fade. Chapter 7 adds the coding that spreads each bit across many of them and the multiple antennas that turn the channel's richness into extra capacity.
