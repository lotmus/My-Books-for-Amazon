H1: Chapter 24. Communicating over Light: Fiber Links, Data-Center Optics, and Optical Wireless
P: Almost every bit in this book spends most of its journey as light. A phone's video leaves the antenna as radio, but within a few kilometers it enters a fiber, and inside a data center it crosses dozens of optical links at 400 or 800 Gbit/s each. The modulation, dispersion, equalization, and link budgets of the earlier chapters all return, with a carrier near 193 THz.
P: Where you meet this: Fiber to the home, cellular fronthaul and backhaul, data-center switches and AI clusters, submarine cables, infrared remote controls, optical wireless links, and the quantum-safe networks now being planned.
P: An optical link has a transmitter, a laser or LED whose light is modulated by the data; a channel, glass fiber or free space; and a receiver, a photodiode followed by a transimpedance amplifier. The devices themselves, lasers, photodiodes, and modulators, are the subject of Book 3, Semiconductor Physics and Devices, and the Book 4 chapter "Microwave Photonics: Radio over Light and Optical Phased Arrays" carries radio signals over fiber. This chapter treats light as a communication channel: its link budget, its dispersion, the modulation formats of data-center and long-haul links, the infrared links in every living room, and the quantum key distribution that some operators are adding to fibers.
H2: 24.1 Optical power and the photodiode
P: A photodiode produces a current proportional to the optical power falling on it, I = R P, with a responsivity R near 0.9 A/W for an InGaAs diode at 1550 nm. The electrical signal power is proportional to the square of that current. A 1 dB loss of light is therefore a 2 dB loss of electrical signal, and optical link budgets are kept in optical decibels, dBm of light, to avoid confusion. When the receiver's noise is the thermal noise of its amplifier, every decibel of light lost costs two decibels of electrical SNR; when the noise is the light's own shot noise, as in coherent receivers with a strong local oscillator, it costs one.
EQ: I = R P;   1 dB optical = 2 dB electrical (thermal-noise limited)
H2: Worked Example 24.1
P: Problem. A receiver with R = 0.9 A/W receives −20 dBm of light. Find the photocurrent. If 3 dB more fiber loss is added, how much does the electrical SNR of a thermal-noise-limited receiver fall?
P: Solution. −20 dBm is 10 µW, so I = 0.9 × 10 µW = 9 µA. Three decibels of optical loss halves the current, and the electrical signal power, proportional to I², falls by a factor of four, 6 dB. Into a 5 kΩ transimpedance amplifier the 9 µA becomes 45 mV, a level the analog front end of Book 2, Circuits, Components, and Control handles with ordinary op-amp techniques at low rates and with dedicated TIAs at gigabit rates.
H2: 24.2 The fiber link budget
P: A fiber budget is the radio budget of Chapters 5 and 8 without the spreading loss. Single-mode fiber loses about 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm and 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm; each mated connector costs up to about 0.5 dB and each fusion splice about 0.1 dB. The transmitter's launch power minus all losses must stay above the receiver's sensitivity, with a margin for aging, repairs, and temperature. Because the loss is so low, a 10 km link loses less than a two-meter radio link at 2.4 GHz loses to spreading alone.
EQ: Prx = Ptx − α L − Σ connectors − Σ splices;   margin = Prx − sensitivity
H2: Worked Example 24.2
P: Problem. A 10 km link at 1310 nm launches 0 dBm. The fiber loses 0.35 dB/km, the two connectors 0.5 dB each, and two splices 0.2 dB each. The receiver needs −14 dBm. Find the received power and the margin.
P: Solution. Losses are 3.5 + 1.0 + 0.4 = 4.9 dB, so the receiver sees −4.9 dBm, and the margin is −4.9 − (−14) = 9.1 dB. A dirty connector, a common fault, can take 1 to 3 dB of that by itself; cleaning and inspecting connectors with a fiber microscope is the first step in every optical repair.

H2: 24.3 Dispersion: the fiber as a multipath channel
P: Light of different wavelengths travels through glass at slightly different speeds. Standard single-mode fiber has a chromatic dispersion of about 17 ps per nanometer of source width per kilometer at 1550 nm and nearly zero at 1310 nm. A modulated signal of B baud occupies a spectral width of about λ²B/c, 0.2 nm at 25 GBd, so its pulses spread by D L Δλ. When that spread approaches a symbol, the fiber has become the multipath channel of Chapter 11. Because both the spectral width and the symbol rate scale with B, the dispersion-limited reach falls as the square of the symbol rate: a 10 GBd signal travels about 73 km at 1550 nm before it spreads by one symbol, a 53 GBd signal about 2.6 km. Short data-center links therefore use the O band near 1310 nm, where dispersion is small, and long links either compensate it optically or equalize it in DSP.
EQ: Δλ ≈ λ² B/c;   Δt = D L Δλ;   L(one symbol) ≈ c/(D λ² B²)
H2: Worked Example 24.3
P: Problem. A 400ZR coherent signal at 59.84 GBd crosses 80 km of standard fiber at 1550 nm. Find its spectral width, the dispersion spread, and the spread in symbols.
P: Solution. Δλ = (1550 nm)² × 59.84 × 10⁹/2.998 × 10⁸ = 0.48 nm. The spread is 17 × 80 × 0.48 = 652 ps, and at 59.84 GBd one symbol lasts 16.7 ps, so each symbol overlaps 39 others. No receiver could ignore that, and a coherent receiver does not try: it undoes the dispersion with the single-carrier frequency-domain equalizer of Section 11.7, because dispersion is a known all-pass phase response.

The 400ZR signal of Worked Example 24.3 crosses 80 km, 39 symbols of spread, by equalizing dispersion in DSP.
H2: 24.4 Data-center optics: PAM4 and coherent
P: Inside a data center, cost and power rule, so links detect light intensity directly, IM-DD. Each 100 Gbit/s lane uses PAM4 at 53.125 GBd: four light levels carry two bits per symbol. A 400GBASE-DR4 module sends four such lanes over four fibers in each direction at 1310 nm for up to 500 m, a line rate of 425 Gbit/s that includes the Reed-Solomon FEC of Section 17.7. PAM4's three eyes each span a third of the swing that on-off keying would use, a penalty of 10 log₁₀3 = 4.8 dB of optical power, recovered by FEC and by DSP in the module: a feed-forward equalizer and often a maximum-likelihood sequence detector of the kind in Chapter 11. Between data centers, 400ZR modules in the same pluggable size use coherent detection: dual-polarization 16-QAM at 59.84 GBd, 8 bits per symbol, 478.75 Gbit/s on the line, for links up to about 120 km. The receiver mixes the signal with a local laser, recovers I and Q on both polarizations, equalizes dispersion as in Worked Example 24.3, and separates the two polarizations, which the fiber mixes continuously, with a 2 × 2 adaptive MIMO equalizer that is the MIMO receiver of Chapter 7 in glass.
P: Wavelength-division multiplexing, WDM, puts many such signals on one fiber. The erbium-doped fiber amplifier's C band spans about 191.3 to 196.1 THz, 4.8 THz, and a 100 GHz channel grid fits 48 channels in it; at 400 Gbit/s each that is 19.2 Tbit/s per fiber, and long-haul systems with wider bands and denser formats carry several times more. The measurement of optical signals, power meters, optical spectrum analyzers, and time-domain reflectometers, is Book 9, Test and Measurement Instruments, chapter "Optical, Thermal, and EMC Precompliance Instruments".
EQ: PAM4 optical penalty = 10 log₁₀3 = 4.8 dB;   coherent line rate = 2 polarizations × log₂M × symbol rate
H2: Worked Example 24.4
P: Problem. Find the line rate of a 400GBASE-DR4 module and of a 400ZR module, and the payload capacity of a 100 GHz grid in a 4.8 THz C band at 400 Gbit/s per channel.
P: Solution. DR4: 4 lanes × 53.125 GBd × 2 bits = 425 Gbit/s. 400ZR: 59.84 GBd × 2 polarizations × 4 bits = 478.75 Gbit/s. Both carry 400 Gbit/s Ethernet; the rest is FEC and framing, 6 % for DR4 and about 20 % for 400ZR, whose stronger code buys the sensitivity for 120 km. The C band holds 4800/100 = 48 channels, 19.2 Tbit/s per fiber.
H2: 24.5 Light in the living room: infrared and optical wireless
P: Analog first. The infrared remote control is the oldest optical wireless link in most homes and can be built from a 555 timer and an infrared LED. It flashes the LED at a carrier near 38 kHz, so that the receiver can reject the steady light of the sun and lamps, and switches the carrier on and off to send pulses. A receiver module such as the Vishay TSOP38238 contains the photodiode, an automatic gain control, a bandpass filter at 38 kHz, and a demodulator, and delivers clean logic pulses to a microcontroller. The common NEC protocol starts with a 9 ms burst and a 4.5 ms space, then sends 32 bits, address, inverted address, command, and inverted command, each beginning with a 562.5 µs burst and coded by the length of the following space.
P: Optical wireless at data rates is now standardized as IEEE 802.11bb, light communication within the Wi-Fi family, published in 2023: it uses infrared light, modulated with OFDM of the kind in Chapter 6, between ceiling units and devices, in rooms where radio is unwanted or crowded. Free-space optical links between buildings and between satellites use narrow laser beams, with the pointing and atmospheric problems that the Book 4 chapter "Antennas and the Sky: LoRa, GPS, Satellites, and the Hydrogen Line" meets at radio wavelengths.
H2: Worked Example 24.5
P: Problem. Find the length of an NEC frame whose 32 bits hold 16 zeros and 16 ones (bit zero 1.125 ms, bit one 2.25 ms), the resulting bit rate, and the number of 38 kHz carrier cycles in one 562.5 µs burst.
P: Solution. 9 + 4.5 + 16 × 1.125 + 16 × 2.25 = 67.5 ms, plus a final 562.5 µs burst. Because each byte is followed by its inverse, every frame holds exactly 16 ones and 16 zeros and lasts the same time. 32 bits in 67.5 ms is 474 bit/s. A burst contains 562.5 µs × 38 kHz = 21.4 cycles, enough for the receiver's bandpass filter to respond, which is why the protocol's time unit is not shorter.
H2: 24.6 Viewpoint: quantum key distribution
P: Quantum key distribution, QKD, sends single photons whose polarization or phase carries key bits, following the BB84 protocol of Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard from 1984. An eavesdropper who measures the photons disturbs them in a way the two ends can detect by comparing a sample, so the security rests on physics rather than on a hard mathematical problem. Its limits are the fiber's: photons cannot be amplified without destroying their quantum state, so the key rate falls with the channel's transmittance, a tenth after 50 km of fiber at 0.2 dB/km, and deployed networks bridge distances beyond about 100 km with trusted relay nodes, where the key exists in ordinary form. Some security agencies, among them the U.S. NSA and the U.K. NCSC, have stated that they prefer post-quantum cryptography for this reason: algorithms such as ML-KEM, standardized by NIST as FIPS 203 in 2024, run on existing networks and protect against future quantum computers without new hardware. QKD networks are in trial service in Europe and Asia; whether they become a mass technology or remain a specialty for links that must be secure against any future mathematics is open. The optical engineering, single-photon detectors, low-loss components, and stable interferometers, is the same as in the rest of this chapter.
EQ: QKD key rate ∝ channel transmittance = 10^(−αL/10)
BENCH: When an optical link fails, measure the light before suspecting the electronics. A handheld power meter at the receiving connector tells you in seconds whether the budget of Worked Example 24.2 still holds; if the power is low, inspect and clean every connector along the path with a fiber microscope before replacing anything. Never look into a fiber or a module's port: data-center lasers are infrared and invisible, and a phone camera, which sees some near infrared, is a safer way to check that a short-reach 850 nm transmitter is on.
P: On the Job
P: Who uses this: optical and photonics engineers at module makers, network-equipment vendors, cloud operators, and telecom carriers, and fiber technicians who install and repair links. Typical devices are QSFP-DD and OSFP modules, 400ZR and 800ZR coherent pluggables, PAM4 DSP chips, and EDFAs. The tools are optical power meters, OTDRs, optical spectrum analyzers, fiber microscopes, and bit-error-rate testers. A realistic task: a 400G link in an AI cluster shows rising corrected FEC errors; read the module's diagnostic monitoring, measure received power per lane, find the lane whose connector is contaminated, and verify the pre-FEC error rate after cleaning.
