H1: Chapter 11. Equalization
P: Here is a myth: a strong echo is harmless once an equalizer has cancelled it. With an echo half as strong as the direct signal, a zero-forcing equalizer pays only 1.25 dB of extra noise. With an echo of 0.9, it pays 7.2 dB, more than five times the noise power, because it boosts the frequencies where the echo cancels the signal. Undoing a channel is not free, and how much it costs depends on how deep the notches are.
P: Where you meet this: Every high-speed serial link, every single-carrier modem, the one-tap equalizer of every OFDM receiver, and every adaptive filter in a modem.
P: Chapter 5 showed that multipath spreads each symbol in time. When the delay spread is a large fraction of the symbol period, each received sample contains contributions from several symbols, the intersymbol interference that the matched filter of Chapter 3 cannot remove. An equalizer is a filter that undoes the channel. This chapter covers the main equalizer types for single-carrier signals, the adaptive algorithms that set their coefficients, and the much simpler equalizer that OFDM allows.
H2: 11.1 How much interference?
P: The number of symbols a channel smears together is the delay spread divided by the symbol period. A 2 µs delay spread, common outdoors, spans 40 symbols at 20 Msym/s, so a single-carrier equalizer for that channel needs at least 40 taps and must adapt them as the channel changes. At 100 ksym/s the same channel spans a fifth of a symbol and barely needs equalization at all. This scaling is the reason wideband systems moved to OFDM.
H2: 11.2 Zero forcing and noise enhancement
P: The zero-forcing equalizer applies the inverse of the channel, cancelling the interference completely. Its weakness is noise: where the channel's frequency response is weak, the inverse is strong, and it amplifies the noise in that band along with the signal. For a two-path channel h = [1, a], the inverse is the series 1 − a z⁻¹ + a² z⁻² − …, and the noise power gain is 1/(1 − a²). As a approaches 1, the channel's response has a deep null and the noise enhancement grows without limit. A finite equalizer truncates the series and leaves a residual interference term.
EQ: W(z) = 1/H(z),   for h = [1, a]: noise gain = Σ a²ᵏ = 1/(1 − a²)

Right: for a = 0.9 the zero-forcing equalizer boosts the band edges by up to 20 dB where the channel is deep; MMSE at 10 dB SNR backs off there.
H2: Worked Example 11.1
P: Problem. A channel has an echo half as strong as the direct path, one symbol later. Find the noise enhancement of the ideal zero-forcing equalizer and the residual interference of a three-tap version. Repeat the enhancement for an echo at 0.9.
P: Solution. For a = 0.5 the noise gain is 1/(1 − 0.25) = 1.333, a 1.25 dB penalty. The three-tap equalizer [1, −0.5, 0.25] convolved with [1, 0.5] gives [1, 0, 0, 0.125]: the interference is cancelled except for a residual of 0.125 three symbols later. For a = 0.9 the noise gain is 1/(1 − 0.81) = 5.26, a 7.2 dB penalty, and the truncated series converges slowly.
H2: 11.3 MMSE and decision feedback
P: The minimum mean-square error equalizer does not insist on cancelling the interference completely. It minimizes the sum of interference and noise, so it backs off its gain where the channel is weak and the noise would dominate. At high SNR it approaches zero forcing; at low SNR it approaches the matched filter. A decision-feedback equalizer adds a second filter that subtracts the interference from past symbols using the receiver's own decisions, which are noise-free if they are correct, so only the forward filter enhances noise. Its weakness is error propagation: a wrong decision feeds back wrong interference. Maximum-likelihood sequence estimation, the Viterbi algorithm applied to the channel, is optimal but grows exponentially in complexity with the channel length.
EQ: W(f) = H*(f) / (|H(f)|² + N0/Es)   (MMSE)
H2: 11.4 Adaptive equalizers
P: The channel is unknown and changes, so the equalizer learns it. During a known training sequence it compares its output with the known symbols and adjusts its taps to reduce the error; afterwards it can continue in decision-directed mode using its own decisions as the reference. The least-mean-squares algorithm updates each tap by μ times the error times the corresponding input sample. It is simple and robust, but converges slowly; the step size must be below about 2 divided by the total input power in the equalizer, and in practice much smaller for low residual error. Recursive least squares converges far faster at a higher cost per update.
EQ: w ← w + μ e* x,   0 < μ < 2 / (N Px)
H2: Worked Example 11.2
P: Problem. An 11-tap LMS equalizer has unit-power input. What is the upper bound on its step size, and why would a designer choose a much smaller one?
P: Solution. μ < 2/(11 × 1) = 0.18. Near that bound the algorithm converges fastest but its taps jitter around their optimum, adding misadjustment noise roughly proportional to μ. A step of 0.01 to 0.02 converges in a few hundred symbols with small excess error, a common compromise.
P: Key idea. An equalizer inverts the channel, but where the channel is weak, inversion amplifies noise. MMSE and decision-feedback designs limit that penalty, and OFDM sidesteps the problem by turning one long equalizer into many one-tap ones.
H2: 11.5 The OFDM equalizer
P: OFDM with a cyclic prefix longer than the delay spread turns the channel's convolution into a multiplication on each subcarrier, as Chapter 6 showed. The equalizer is then a single complex division per subcarrier by the channel estimate of Chapter 13. A subcarrier in a deep fade still loses its SNR, because the scalar inverse amplifies its noise along with the signal, but the damage is confined to that subcarrier, and the decoder of Chapter 7, told which subcarriers are unreliable, corrects the errors there from the others. A subcarrier with |H|² = 0.01, a 20 dB fade, at an average SNR of 20 dB has an SNR of 0 dB after equalization, nearly useless on its own, but it is one of hundreds.
H2: 11.6 Maximum-likelihood sequence estimation
P: A linear equalizer inverts the channel and pays in noise enhancement; a decision-feedback equalizer pays in error propagation. The optimum receiver for a channel with a short, known memory does neither. It asks which whole sequence of symbols, passed through the measured channel, would best explain the received samples, and the Viterbi algorithm of Chapter 7 finds it, with the channel's memory playing the role of the encoder's shift register. For a binary modulation and a channel that spans L + 1 symbols, the trellis has 2^L states. GSM is the classic case: its symbols last 3.69 µs and a hilly-terrain echo profile spans about four symbols beyond the main one, so GSM receivers run a 16-state Viterbi equalizer on every burst, using the 26-bit training sequence in the middle of the burst to measure the channel first. The cost grows as M^L, so MLSE is practical only for small constellations and short channels; for 64-QAM over ten symbols it would need 10¹⁸ states.
H2: 11.7 Frequency-domain equalization for a single carrier
P: The OFDM equalizer of Section 11.5 is cheap because the cyclic prefix lets the FFT turn the channel into a set of complex gains. A single-carrier signal can borrow the same trick. The transmitter sends blocks of N symbols, each with a cyclic prefix; the receiver takes an FFT of the block, multiplies each bin by an MMSE coefficient H*(k)/(|H(k)|² + σ²/Es), and returns to the time domain with an inverse FFT before deciding the symbols. This is single-carrier frequency-domain equalization. It keeps the low peak-to-average ratio of a single carrier, which is why it underlies the DFT-spread OFDM uplink of Section 6.6 and the 802.11ad millimeter-wave single-carrier mode, and its cost per symbol grows only as log N, however long the echo.
H2: Worked Example 11.3
P: Problem. A 100 MBd single-carrier link sees echoes up to 2 µs. Compare the complex multiplications per symbol of a time-domain equalizer with twice as many taps as the channel span and of a frequency-domain equalizer on 1024-symbol blocks.
P: Solution. The channel spans 2 µs × 100 MBd = 200 symbols, so the time-domain equalizer has about 400 taps and needs about 400 multiplications per symbol. A 1024-point FFT and inverse FFT need about 2 × 512 × 10 = 10 240 multiplications, plus 1024 for the equalizer coefficients, 11 264 per block, or 11 per symbol: 36 times fewer. The prefix of at least 200 symbols costs 200/1224 = 16 % of the time, the price of the saving.
H2: Worked Example 11.4
P: Problem. How many states does an MLSE equalizer need for QPSK over a channel spanning three symbols, and for 8-PSK over the same channel?
P: Solution. The memory is L = 2 symbols. QPSK gives 4² = 16 states and 8-PSK 8² = 64 states. The EDGE extension of GSM used 8-PSK and so moved most receivers to reduced-state sequence estimators that keep only the most likely few states.
H2: 11.8 Equalization in a wireline SerDes
P: The PAM4 links of Chapter 1 depend on equalizers as much as any radio. A copper channel at 26.6 GHz can lose 30 dB or more, and the loss rises steadily with frequency, so a pulse smears over many symbols. A 112 Gbit/s receiver therefore stacks three equalizers. A transmitter feed-forward equalizer of three to five taps pre-distorts each symbol. A continuous-time linear equalizer, an analog filter with a zero below the Nyquist frequency, lifts the high frequencies by 10 to 20 dB. Then the receiver samples the signal, often with a 6- or 7-bit converter running at the symbol rate, and a digital FFE and DFE remove the rest. The DFE is particularly effective here because the channel's postcursor interference is long but predictable, and its error propagation is contained by the strong forward error correction that follows.
H2: Worked Example 11.5
P: Problem. A backplane channel loses 12 dB at 6.6 GHz and 30 dB at 26.6 GHz. The receiver's linear equalizer provides 15 dB of boost at 26.6 GHz relative to low frequencies. How much loss must the digital equalizer still handle at the Nyquist frequency of a 53.125 GBd PAM4 signal, measured from the 6.6 GHz point?
P: Solution. The channel's slope between 6.6 and 26.6 GHz is 30 − 12 = 18 dB. The CTLE removes 15 dB, leaving about 3 dB of residual tilt, plus whatever the CTLE's boost does below 6.6 GHz, which a few DFE and FFE taps handle easily. Without the CTLE, the digital equalizer would face 18 dB of tilt and would amplify the converter's noise by roughly that much at high frequencies, the noise enhancement of Section 11.2.
P: Equalization completes the work of undoing a single carrier's channel. Chapter 12 turns to a different strategy: waveforms that are spread out deliberately, so that narrowband interference, fading, and even other users matter less.
BENCH: When an equalizer improves the eye but the error rate barely changes, it is probably trading intersymbol interference for noise. Switch from zero forcing to MMSE if the receiver allows it, and look at the channel's frequency response. A deep notch near the band center is a sign that no linear equalizer will help much; decision feedback or OFDM will.
