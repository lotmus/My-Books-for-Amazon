PART: Part II. Inside the Modem
H1: Chapter 9. Digital Filters in the Modem
P: Divide a sample stream by 16 with a filter that has no multipliers at all, only adders and delays. The cascaded integrator-comb filter does exactly that, and it is why nearly every SDR chip can decimate from hundreds of megasamples per second at low power. The catch is a sag of 3.6 dB at the edge of the band that a short correction filter has to straighten afterward.
P: Where you meet this: Every decimation and interpolation stage in an SDR or a radio chip, every FIR coefficient file, and every 'number of taps' setting in a filter-design tool.
P: Almost every block in a modern modem is a digital filter: the pulse shaper of Chapter 3, the channel selector after the ADC, the decimators that bring a wideband sample stream down to the symbol rate, and the equalizer of Chapter 11. This chapter covers the two filter families, the way their length is estimated, and the multirate structures that make high sample rates affordable. It follows the practical treatment in Lyons's Understanding Digital Signal Processing.
P: Analog first. Every digital filter in this chapter sits behind an analog one. Before the converter, only an analog filter can stop a signal from folding onto the wanted band, and its order sets how much the digital filters can relax: a fourth-order Butterworth falls 24 dB per octave beyond its corner, so a converter sampling at four times the corner frequency sees the band that folds onto its passband, which starts at three times the corner, 38 dB down from that filter alone. The active filters themselves, Sallen-Key and multiple-feedback stages built with an op-amp, are the subject of Book 2, Circuits, Components, and Control.
H2: 9.1 FIR and IIR filters
P: A finite impulse response, FIR, filter forms each output as a weighted sum of the last N input samples. With symmetric coefficients its phase is exactly linear, so every frequency is delayed by the same (N − 1)/2 samples and the pulse shapes of Chapter 3 pass undistorted. An infinite impulse response, IIR, filter feeds outputs back and reaches a given selectivity with far fewer coefficients, but its phase is nonlinear and it can become unstable or ring if its coefficients are quantized carelessly. Modems use FIR filters wherever phase matters, which is nearly everywhere in the signal path, and IIR filters for loop filters and DC removal.
EQ: y[n] = Σ h[k] x[n − k],  k = 0 … N − 1;   group delay = (N − 1)/2 samples
H2: 9.2 How long must the filter be?
P: The length of an FIR filter grows with the attenuation wanted and shrinks with the width of the transition band, measured as a fraction of the sample rate. A useful estimate, due to fred harris, is N ≈ A/(22 Δf/fs), with A the stopband attenuation in decibels. Halving the transition band doubles the length, and with it the number of multiplications per second. Window design trades the same quantities through the choice of window: the first sidelobe of a rectangular window is only 13 dB down, a Hann window's 31 dB, a Hamming window's 42 dB, and a Blackman window's 58 dB, at the cost of a wider main lobe and so a wider transition band. Optimal equiripple designs by the Parks-McClellan algorithm meet a specification with the fewest taps.
EQ: N ≈ A / (22 Δf / fs)
H2: Worked Example 9.1
P: Problem. A channel filter runs at 100 MHz. It must pass the band and reach 60 dB of attenuation within a 5 MHz transition band. Estimate its length, its group delay, and its multiplication rate.
P: Solution. N ≈ 60 / (22 × 5/100) = 54.5, so about 55 taps. The delay of a 55-tap linear-phase filter is 27 samples, 270 ns. At 100 MHz it needs 55 × 10⁸ = 5.5 × 10⁹ multiplications per second, or half that if the symmetric coefficients are exploited by adding the paired samples first.
P: Key idea. An FIR filter's length is set by the attenuation wanted divided by the transition band as a fraction of the sample rate. Sharp filters at high sample rates are expensive, which is why modems lower the sample rate as early as possible.
H2: 9.3 Decimation and polyphase filters
P: When a filter's output will be decimated, keeping only every Mth sample, there is no need to compute the samples that are discarded. A polyphase structure splits the filter into M shorter subfilters, each running at the output rate, so the cost falls by a factor of M. Interpolation, the reverse operation used before a DAC, has the same structure. Large rate changes are done in stages, because the early stages, at the high rate, can have wide transition bands and short lengths, while the final stage, at the low rate, does the sharp filtering cheaply.
H2: Worked Example 9.2
P: Problem. The filter of Example 9.1 is followed by decimation by 4. What multiplication rate does a polyphase implementation need?
P: Solution. Each output still needs 55 multiplications, but only 25 million outputs are computed each second: 55 × 2.5 × 10⁷ = 1.375 × 10⁹ per second, a quarter of the direct form.
H2: 9.4 The CIC filter
P: The first decimation stage after a fast ADC, running at hundreds of megasamples or gigasamples per second, often uses a cascaded integrator-comb, CIC, filter. It needs no multipliers at all: N integrators at the input rate, a rate change by R, and N combs at the output rate. Its response is a sinc function raised to the Nth power, which has nulls exactly at the frequencies that alias onto the passband, but it droops across the passband, so a short compensation FIR follows it. Its gain is (RM)^N, with M the comb delay, so the registers must grow by N log₂(RM) bits.
EQ: |H(f)| = |sin(πfRM/fs) / (R M sin(πf/fs))|^N,   bit growth = N log₂(RM)
FIG: fig9_1.png | Figure 9.1 — Response of a four-stage CIC decimator by 16. It needs no multipliers, but it droops 3.63 dB at a passband edge of a quarter of the output rate, and the band that aliases onto that edge is suppressed only to −41.7 dB. Nulls fall at every multiple of the output rate.
H2: Worked Example 9.3
P: Problem. A four-stage CIC filter decimates by 16 with M = 1. Find its bit growth, its droop at a quarter of the output rate, and its attenuation of the band that aliases onto that frequency.
P: Solution. Bit growth = 4 × log₂16 = 16 bits. At f = 0.25/16 of the input rate the response is (sin(π/4)/(16 sin(π/64)))⁴ = 0.658, a droop of 3.63 dB, which the compensation filter must correct. The input at 0.75/16 of the input rate aliases onto the same frequency after decimation; there the response is −41.7 dB. If more rejection is needed, more stages or a narrower passband are used.
H2: 9.5 Fixed-point arithmetic and word growth
P: A filter designed in floating point runs in an FPGA or an ASIC on integers, and three numbers must be chosen: the coefficient width, the data width, and the accumulator width. Coefficient quantization moves the zeros of the filter and raises its stopband floor; as a working rule each coefficient bit buys about 5 to 6 dB of attainable stopband attenuation, so a filter that must reject an adjacent channel by 80 dB needs coefficients of at least 15 or 16 bits. The product of a B_x-bit sample and a B_c-bit coefficient has B_x + B_c bits, and summing N such products can add log₂N more. If the accumulator holds all of them, the sum is exact and only the final rounding adds noise.
EQ: accumulator bits ≥ Bx + Bc + ⌈log₂N⌉
P: Real hardware is built around those numbers. The DSP48E2 slice of AMD's UltraScale+ FPGAs has a 27 × 18-bit multiplier feeding a 48-bit accumulator, and Intel's variable-precision DSP blocks offer 18 × 19 and 27 × 27 modes. A filter designer who keeps samples to 16 bits and coefficients to 18 has 14 bits of growth to spare in a 48-bit accumulator, enough for any practical FIR length. The final output is rounded, not truncated, because truncation adds a DC bias of half a least significant bit that would appear as a spur at 0 Hz after the next mixer.
H2: 9.6 Fractional resampling and the Farrow filter
P: Not every rate change is a ratio of small integers. Converting the 30.72 MS/s of an LTE carrier to the 25 MS/s of a particular converter is a ratio of 625 to 768, and a polyphase filter with 625 branches would be absurd. The Farrow structure instead approximates the ideal interpolating filter by a polynomial in the fractional delay μ, between 0 and 1. A handful of fixed FIR filters run on the input, and each output sample combines their outputs as a polynomial evaluated at the current μ, which an accumulator advances by the rate ratio for every output. A cubic Farrow filter uses four fixed filters and three multiplications by μ per output. The same structure does the fine timing correction of Chapter 10, where μ comes from the timing loop instead of a fixed ratio.
H2: Worked Example 9.4
P: Problem. A 128-tap FIR filter takes 16-bit samples and 18-bit coefficients. Find the accumulator width for exact arithmetic, and check that it fits a DSP48E2 slice.
P: Solution. The products have 16 + 18 = 34 bits; summing 128 of them adds ⌈log₂128⌉ = 7 bits, so 41 bits are needed. The 48-bit accumulator holds them with 7 bits to spare. The 18-bit coefficients support roughly 90 to 100 dB of stopband attenuation, so the filter's floor is set by its design, not its arithmetic.
H2: Worked Example 9.5
P: Problem. A Farrow resampler converts 30.72 MS/s to 25 MS/s. Find the increment of its fractional-delay accumulator per output sample, and the integer and fractional input positions of the first four outputs.
P: Solution. Each output advances the input position by 30.72/25 = 1.2288 input samples. Starting at 0, the outputs fall at 0, 1.2288, 2.4576, and 3.6864, so the integer parts 0, 1, 2, 3 select the input samples and the fractions μ = 0, 0.2288, 0.4576, and 0.6864 are the values at which the Farrow polynomial is evaluated. The fraction wraps every time the accumulator passes an integer, and occasionally the filter skips one input sample, here after the fifth output, because 5 × 1.2288 = 6.144.
H2: 9.7 Half-band filters
P: The most common decimate-by-two stage has a special shape. A half-band filter's frequency response is symmetric about a quarter of the sample rate, with its passband edge and stopband edge equally spaced from fs/4. That symmetry forces every second coefficient to zero, except the center one, which is exactly one half. A half-band filter of N taps therefore needs only about N/4 multiplications per output when its coefficient symmetry is also used, and it decimates by two after that. Cascading several half-band stages, each running at half the rate of the one before, is how digital down-converters in chips such as the AD9081 move from gigasamples to megasamples with little arithmetic.
H2: Worked Example 9.6
P: Problem. A 47-tap half-band filter decimates a 491.52 MS/s complex stream by two. How many of its coefficients are nonzero, and how many real multiplications per second does it need with symmetry exploited?
P: Solution. Of 47 taps, the 22 at even offsets from the center are zero, leaving 25 nonzero: 12 symmetric pairs plus the center tap of 0.5. Folding the pairs gives 12 multiplications plus a shift for the center, per output. At the output rate of 245.76 MS/s and two rails, 2 × 12 × 245.76 × 10⁶ = 5.9 × 10⁹ multiplications per second, about an eighth of the 46 × 10⁹ that a general 47-tap filter computing every output at the input rate would need.
P: Where this continues. Chapter 22 puts these filters into FPGA and ASIC logic, where the word lengths of Section 9.5 become numbers of DSP slices and milliwatts. The firmware view, fixed-point formats and filters on a microcontroller, is Book 8, Firmware and Software for Electronics, chapter "Fixed-Point Math and DSP".
P: Digital filters condition the samples. The next problem is to find the symbols in them, which means estimating the carrier frequency, its phase, and the symbol timing that the channel and the oscillators have disturbed.
BENCH: Estimate a FIR filter's length before you design it: taps ≈ attenuation in dB/(22 × transition width as a fraction of the sample rate). If the answer is in the thousands, filter in stages and decimate between them. A polyphase decimator does the same work at the output rate, so measure your processor load after the change; a factor of ten saving is normal.
