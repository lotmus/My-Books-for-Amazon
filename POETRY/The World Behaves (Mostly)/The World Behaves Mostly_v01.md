# The World Behaves (Mostly)

**Original poems by Kira Vale**

*Working title and opening draft*

This is a new, original poetry collection. It stands apart from the companion nonsense book: the poems here should make sense on a first reading, have a clear emotional or comic point, and use rhyme that sounds natural aloud. The subjects can range freely—from love and family to work, weather, technology, the natural world, history, and space travel. The collection will mix funny, serious, intelligent, and thought-provoking work. Most poems will rhyme, using varied schemes and forms such as ballads and sonnets; blank verse and free verse can appear when they suit the subject. Some lyrics may draw on the broad tradition of reflective poems about nature, human feeling, and meaning, while speaking in a distinctly modern voice. Each poem should earn its ending.

## Love and Company

### Love, With the Dishes

The plates were stacked beside the sink;
We stood too tired to stop and think.
You washed; I dried. We spoke of rain,
The restless cat, the passing train.

No violins came through the wall;
The kettle clicked. That was all.
You handed me a chipped blue cup;
I held it while you filled it up.

The years leave quiet evidence there:
In simple things we might ignore:
A coat held open at the door,
A light left on across the floor.

So if they ask what love can do,
It knows which cup belongs to you.
It dries the plate. It knows the way
Back to the sink at end of day.

### The Map Between Us

We took the road that crossed the hill
And missed the turn beside the mill.
The map announced we’d gone astray;
You said the map could lead the way.

The road grew narrow, then it bent
Past fields where evening shadows lent
The barns a roof of copper light,
The hedges held the last of night.

We found the inn, an hour late,
Its supper cold, its welcome great.
The map lay folded by the bed,
Still certain where we ought to head.

Next morning, neither hurried on.
The road had somewhere else to run.
We let the careful blue line cease
And took the longer way to peace.

## Daily Life and Its Machinery

### The Instructions

The parcel promised, in bold type,
A table of the simplest type.
The diagram showed one polished board,
Six screws, one wrench, and no discord.

The first page said, “Begin with A.”
The pieces looked the other way.
The screw marked C refused to start;
The wrench developed thoughts of art.

At last the legs stood four-square, proud,
The shelf sat level, if allowed.
We set one book upon the top;
The table made a thoughtful stop.

It held the book. It held the tea.
It held our small victory.
The leftover screw, though none could say,
Became a keepsake straightaway.

### The Meeting That Could Have Been an Email

We gathered round the polished screen
To choose between two plans, both lean.
One chart compared the plans by price;
The next made savings look precise.

A voice said, “We should circle back.”
The clock performed a quiet crack.
We circled once, then circled twice,
And called the loop a fresh device.

At last someone leaned toward the mic:
“Which plan, then? We could make this quick.”
The chair said, “A.” The room agreed.
No further charts were then required.

We wrote it down and sent it round.
The email landed safe and sound.
The meeting link stayed open wide,
In case the thought came back inside.

### A Small Repair

The gate had sagged a little low;
The latch complained when winds would blow.
I found the screwdriver and went
To fix the thing I’d long resented.

One screw came free; the next one spun.
The job had scarcely yet begun.
I braced the post, then tried once more;
The gate swung true as years before.

A neighbor watched me test the latch.
“You fixed it?” “Yes.” “Then fix the patch
Of fence beside it.” I looked around.
The whole street leaned a half-inch down.

I shut the gate. It clicked in place.
The fence could wait. It had all day.
A small repair, done well and slow,
Can hold more than the boards may show.

## Earth, Sky, and Space

### The Astronaut’s First Morning

The alarm went off; it did not ring.
It drifted past like a small king.
The astronaut reached out one hand;
The clock escaped his command.

His coffee hovered, brown and round,
A little planet, unbound.
He sipped it through a straw and saw
Earth turned beneath him, vast and raw.

His home turned blue and green below;
The cloudbanks caught the morning glow.
No street or face came into view;
Yet all he loved was there, he knew.

He checked each gauge, bolt, and seal,
The things that made the silence real.
Then sent one message, short and clear:
“The stars are bright. I wish you here.”

### The Moon Keeps Office Hours

I asked the moon to lend some light;
It said, “I’m booked most every night.”
“I rise at six, I set at four;
I have a tide to mind offshore.”

“I’ve craters stacked from here to there,
And half the world to help repair.
The poets call me cold and bright;
They never see my inbox at night.”

I offered it a cup of tea.
It said, “That’s kind. It isn’t free.
The stars have all requested cream;
I’m short one comet for the team.”

Then clouds rolled in and closed the show.
The moon said, “Sorry. Time to go.”
I went to bed, the curtains drawn,
And left it to the coming dawn.

## Time and Perspective

### The Light That Arrives Late

We point at stars and give them names,
As if a name could bring them near;
Their light, that crossed the dark like flame,
Left home before our own was here.

Some stars are gone; their light goes on
Across the dark in silver streams.
The present holds a vanished dawn
And reads the past in borrowed beams.

We, too, keep voices after years,
Though time remakes the things they mean;
A phrase once heard through joy or tears
Can tint each room we have ever seen.

We cannot bring the source back near,
Or make its vanished hour our own;
But something reaches us from there,
And makes the dark feel less alone.
## Love and Company: Further Poems

### The Unsent Message

At half past one, the room was blue;
I typed three words: “I miss you.”
I watched them glow against the night,
As if the words could choose to go.

The distance was a modest kind:
A train, two stops, a change of mind.
No border, storm, or ocean lay
Between the things I meant to say.

I feared the phrase would sound too small,
Or like a hook, or nothing at all.
I set the phone beside the bed;
The unsent words stayed where they’d been.

At dawn, I wrote: “I’m coming by.”
The morning did not ask me why.
You opened up. We made some tea.
The words were there, but so were we.

## Daily Life and Its Machinery: Further Poems

### Instructions for a Modern Oracle

I asked the search bar, “What is true?”
It showed me seven points of view,
A sponsored link, a sponsored guide,
And fourteen ways to pick a side.

I asked it how to sleep at night.
It offered lamps with softer light,
A watch that counts my every dream,
And tea that costs as much as steam.

I asked, “What should I do today?”
It drew a map, then moved the day.
I closed the screen and made some bread;
The oracle approved the spread.

### A Sonnet for the Unread Email

The subject line announced a brief reply;
The message filled a page before the day.
I read the opening, then let it lie,
And watched its little red flag hold its sway.

It asked for one small answer, yes or no;
I drafted six, each longer than the last.
I weighed the tone, the commas, where to go,
Then chose the safest phrase and pressed “reply.”

The cursor blinked, a patient tiny eye.
I wrote: “That works.” I checked it twice again.
A thought occurred: perhaps I should explain
The context, caveat, timeline, and the why.

I sent the line. The screen returned to blue.
The whole long afternoon had fit in two.

### The Cat on the Lecture Notes

I spread my notes across the floor
And left the office door ajar.
The cat sat squarely on the chart,
And covered up the crucial part.

I moved him gently. He returned
And chose the graph I most had learned.
He pinned the proof beneath one paw,
As if he’d found a useful flaw.

I asked him what the symbols meant.
He blinked, which seemed a firm dissent.
Then stretched, and with one lazy sweep,
Erased the theorem. Went to sleep.

## Earth, Sky, and Space: Further Poems

### The Mountain at the Edge of Town

The mountain keeps the evening light
Long after rooftops lose the day.
It does not hurry into night
Or ask the clouds to look its way.

We build our roads along its side,
We name its bends, we mark its height;
The mountain lets our measures slide
And wears the weather as it might.

I went there with a crowded head,
A list of tasks, a half-made plan.
The pines let all the wind pass through;
The mountain did not ask who ran.

I came back down. The work remained.
The sky had changed. I had, a little.
The mountain held its quiet place,
Unmoved, though every leaf could tremble.

### Orbit

The planet turned beneath the ship,
A blue and clouded turning stone.
The astronaut let silence slip
Between the stars and thoughts of home.

No border showed from up that high,
No fence, no flag, no dotted line.
The rivers wound beneath the sky;
The night side glittered, bright as wine.

The radio returned a voice,
A simple check: “Your systems good?”
He answered yes. The signal passed.
The little human world still stood.

He watched the continents revolve,
Their storms and coastlines in one view.
The questions did not all resolve;
But one grew harder to ignore:
What do we owe this world we share,
When every home is turning there?

### The Last Tree in the Lot

They marked the tree with orange paint
And posted plans beside the gate.
The lot would soon be shops and homes;
The tree stood where the curb would go.

A child had tied a ribbon there,
A faded strip of summer air.
The foreman read the posted sheet;
The branches stirred above the street.

No speech could make the numbers lie:
The town needed rooms, the costs ran high.
They shifted one small line of stone;
The tree stayed standing, not alone.

A new path curved around its shade.
The plans were altered, not unmade.
The child came back at closing time
And found the ribbon, leaf, and sign.

### Snow in April

By noon the blacktop had turned silver.
The tulips wore small white caps.
One bird stood on the fence,
looking offended by the calendar.

The forecast called it a brief return.
The branches held their breath.
Then a drop let go,
bright as a bead from a loosened sleeve.

No season apologized.
The snow thinned into the soil.
The bird lifted off,
carrying no explanation.

## Time and Perspective: Further Poems

### The Museum at Closing Time

At closing, every footstep slows;
The guards begin their quiet rounds.
A king in paint keeps watch below
A ceiling full of borrowed clouds.

A helmet rests behind its glass,
A cup a potter shaped by hand.
The years have made the makers pass;
Their fingerprints still understand.

The lights go out from room to room.
The old things keep their patient place.
We leave the dark to hold the room
And take our briefness through the gate.

### A Good Question

A child asked why the stars stay bright
Though none of them can hear us call.
I had a fact, concise and right;
I let it wait. I heard them all.

We stood beneath the winter sky,
The answer small against the cold.
The child kept looking, asking why;
The stars kept what they had not told.

A fact can open one clear door;
A question keeps the hallway wide.
We walked back home, and asked once more
What else the dark might hold inside.
## Love and Company: More Poems

### The Phone at Dinner

The phone lay face-up by the plate,
A second guest who never ate.
It lit the table blue and cold
And scrolled the worries bought and sold.

You turned it over. “There,” you said.
The room grew warm; the screen went dead.
The soup had cooled. The bread was tough.
The evening, somehow, was enough.

We spoke of work, the neighbor’s tree,
The small repairs we’d yet to see.
The phone stayed quiet in its place.
I learned again your listening face.

### A Houseplant Complains

I bought a plant to learn to care
For something rooted, green, and fair.
It drooped each time I missed a day;
I took the hint and changed my way.

I moved it closer to the light,
Then moved it back; the sun was bright.
I watered twice. It looked distressed.
I asked the florist what was best.

“Leave it alone,” the florist said.
I gave it room and let it be.
It found its shape beside the light;
I stopped to help. It did all right.

## Daily Life and Its Machinery: More Poems

### The Missing Sock Inquiry

The laundry basket has a trick:
It takes one sock; the rest go quick.
I questioned every drawer and shoe;
The dryer hummed, but never knew.

I blamed the dog. He looked offended.
The cat looked pleased. The case was pending.
I checked the sleeves, the towel, the hall;
One sock had vanished, that was all.

I bought a dozen, paired and bright.
By Thursday, half had left from sight.
I keep one lonely sock on show:
A small white flag to those who go.

### How to Leave a Party

You say, “We really must be going,”
While neither of your coats is showing.
Your host begins a tale at length;
You nod, and summon hidden strength.

You stand. You sit. You praise the cheese.
The host asks, “Tea?” “Oh, yes, please.”
A friend arrives; you greet them all;
The doorway vanishes down the hall.

At last you say, “We have to run.”
The host says, “Wait, we’ve just begun.”
You wave, escape, and reach the street—
Then text: “That was a lovely evening. We should meet.”

## Earth, Sky, and Space: More Poems

### The Ferry at First Light

The ferry eased away from shore
Before the town began to stir.
A gull kept pace, then asked for more;
The harbor lights began to blur.

We stood beneath the salt-stiff air,
The deck still dark, the coffee strong.
A woman held her ticket there
As if it knew where she belonged.

The far coast gathered from the gray:
A line of roofs, a church, a crane.
We crossed a strip of ordinary sea
And found the day had changed again.

### A Sonnet for Mars

We point our instruments at rust-red ground,
And send a little rover through the cold.
It reads the stones without a human sound,
While we await the pictures it will hold.

We map the valleys, name each distant rise,
And test the dust for traces left behind.
The planet keeps its billion-year disguise;
Our questions travel faster than our kind.

A bootprint there would matter, yet how much?
The world is not a prize we get to claim.
We bring our hunger, tools, and need to touch,
And leave our fingerprints beside a name.

Perhaps the wisest mission we can send
Is learning what we owe before we land.

### The Weather Has Its Own Grammar

Cloud gathers over the ridge.
The leaves turn their pale sides out.
A crow abandons the fence
and takes the long way home.

No forecast says what a field knows:
the pressure in its grasses,
the smell of rain still miles away,
the small birds going quiet.

Then weather writes across the window
in a hand no one can keep.
By morning, every branch has learned
another shape for green.

## Time and Perspective: More Poems

### The Laundromat at Midnight

At twelve, the dryers turn like moons
Above the rows of empty chairs.
A man folds shirts beneath the tunes
Of radio hosts and late-night cares.

He sorts each sleeve, each seam, each pair,
The ordinary made precise.
A child asleep against a chair
Has dreamed the hum a lullaby.

The washers finish, one by one;
The glass doors open, warm and wide.
The night has done what nights have done:
Returned the small things, clean and dried.

### Two Minutes After Midnight

The date changed while I washed a cup.
The clock advanced; the sink stayed full.
The year began; the kettle shut.
The house remained its usual self.

No trumpet sounded in the hall.
No hidden door swung open wide.
The old year left its coat behind;
The new one wore it, tried the stride.

I dried the cup and set it down.
The first small act was just the same.
Yet somewhere, past the sleeping town,
A light came on and called its name.

### After the Rain

The pavement keeps the sky awhile,
In broken pools along the street.
The traffic slows; a child can smile
At clouds reflected near their feet.

The storm has rinsed the plane-tree leaves;
The gutters carry twigs and grit.
A sparrow tests the air, then leaves
A wet stone ledge to rise from it.

No one can hold the rain in place,
Or ask the shining day to stay.
But for a moment, every face
Can see the world refreshed by gray.

### The Choice

A road divided at the hill:
One climbed toward noise, one curved below.
The faded sign stood silent still;
No arrow said which way to go.

I asked a walker passing by.
She said, “I took the lower track.”
I asked if she was glad. “Sometimes.”
She smiled, then turned and wandered back.

The choice was not a test of worth,
Nor proof one path was false or true.
We choose a road; we make its meaning
By what we notice as we move.


## Love and Company: A Quiet Poem

### The Toast

You did not say everything heals.
You put the kettle on.
You set a plate beside my chair
and left the toast where I could reach it.

The rain took its time at the glass.
You stayed until the room changed color.
I do not remember what we said.
I remember the butter softening,
the quiet shared without being solved.
## About the Poet

Kira Vale writes poems that move between the intimate and the immense: a small kindness at the kitchen sink, a difficult change, the old light of a distant star. Her work can be wry, tender, or questioning, and looks for meaning in ordinary details without pretending every question has an easy answer. She writes for readers who want a poem to leave them with something to feel, consider, or carry into the day.
