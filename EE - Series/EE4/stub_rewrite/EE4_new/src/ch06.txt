H1: Chapter 6. Resonators, Filters, and Waveguide
P: A filter is a matching network that refuses to match outside its passband. Inside the band it presents the source impedance and passes the signal; outside it presents a reactance and reflects it. This chapter builds filters in three steps: the low-pass prototype that sets the shape of the response, the transformations that turn it into a real low-pass or band-pass circuit, and the resonator quality factor that decides how much of the passband signal the filter loses. The last of those steps leads from microstrip to cavities and waveguide, which is where high-power and low-loss filters live.
H2: 6.1 The low-pass prototype
P: Filter design starts from a normalized ladder of alternating series inductors and shunt capacitors, designed for a 1 Ω termination and a cutoff of 1 rad/s. Its element values gₖ are tabulated for each response type. A Butterworth (maximally flat) filter of order n has a monotonic response that falls by 3 dB at cutoff and then by 20n dB per decade. A Chebyshev filter allows a specified ripple in the passband in exchange for a steeper fall just beyond cutoff. The third-order values are g = 1, 2, 1 for Butterworth and g = 1.0316, 1.1474, 1.0316 for a Chebyshev response with 0.1 dB ripple. Scaling to a real impedance R0 and cutoff frequency ωc gives the components.
EQ: Lₖ = gₖ R0 / ωc,    Cₖ = gₖ / (R0 ωc),    Butterworth: |S₂₁|² = 1 / (1 + (ω/ωc)^(2n))
H2: Worked Example 6.1
WEP: Design a third-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 1 GHz cutoff in a 50 Ω system, as shunt capacitor, series inductor, shunt capacitor. Find its attenuation at 2 GHz and at 2.45 GHz.
WES: ωc = 2π × 10⁹ rad/s. C₁ = C₃ = 1/(50 × 2π × 10⁹) = 3.18 pF; L₂ = 2 × 50/(2π × 10⁹) = 15.9 nH. At 2 GHz, ω/ωc = 2 and the attenuation is 10 log₁₀(1 + 2⁶) = 18.1 dB. At 2.45 GHz it is 10 log₁₀(1 + 2.45⁶) = 23.4 dB. A harmonic filter after a 1 GHz amplifier must suppress the second harmonic by more than this, so a real design would use a higher order or a Chebyshev response, and would check the inductor's self-resonance, which for 15.9 nH in a small package can fall near the frequencies the filter is meant to stop.
H2: 6.2 Band-pass filters and resonator Q
P: A band-pass filter replaces each series inductor of the prototype with a series resonator and each shunt capacitor with a parallel resonator, all tuned to the center frequency f0. The fractional bandwidth FBW = Δf/f0 scales the element values: the narrower the band, the higher the stored energy in each resonator relative to the power passing through. That is why narrow filters are lossy. Each resonator has an unloaded quality factor Qu, the ratio of stored energy to energy lost per radian, set by its conductors and dielectric. Cohn's estimate gives the midband insertion loss of a filter from its prototype values, its bandwidth, and its resonators' Qu.
EQ: IL₀ ≈ 4.343 × Σ gₖ / (FBW × Qu)   dB
H2: Worked Example 6.2
WEP: A three-pole 0.1 dB Chebyshev band-pass filter is centered at 2.45 GHz with a 100 MHz bandwidth. Estimate its midband loss with microstrip resonators of Qu = 200 and with cavity resonators of Qu = 2000.
WES: FBW = 100/2450 = 0.0408 and Σgₖ = 3.21. With Qu = 200, IL₀ ≈ 4.343 × 3.21/(0.0408 × 200) = 1.71 dB. With Qu = 2000 it is 0.17 dB. In a receiver the 1.5 dB difference adds directly to the noise figure; after a transmitter it is heat in the filter: at 100 W, a 1.71 dB loss burns 33 W, and a 0.17 dB loss 3.9 W. This is why filters that follow power amplifiers are cavities, as Book 6 assumes.
KEY: Key idea. A filter's shape comes from its prototype, but its loss comes from its resonators. Narrowing the bandwidth or adding poles multiplies the stored energy, and the loss rises in proportion unless the resonator Q rises with it.
H2: 6.3 Waveguide
P: A hollow metal pipe guides waves without a center conductor, with lower loss and higher power capacity than any coaxial line of similar size. It has a cutoff frequency below which nothing propagates. In a rectangular guide of broad dimension a, the dominant TE₁₀ mode cuts off where half a free-space wavelength equals a. Above cutoff the wave travels with a guide wavelength longer than the free-space wavelength, and the guide is used from about 1.25 to 1.9 times its cutoff, where only the dominant mode propagates and the loss is low.
EQ: fc = c / (2a),    λg = λ0 / √(1 − (fc/f)²)
H2: Worked Example 6.3
WEP: WR-90 waveguide has a = 22.86 mm. Find its cutoff frequency and its guide wavelength at 10 GHz. Then find the length d of a rectangular TE₁₀₁ cavity in the same guide that resonates at 10 GHz.
WES: fc = 2.998 × 10⁸/(2 × 0.02286) = 6.56 GHz, so the guide is used across the X band, 8.2 to 12.4 GHz. At 10 GHz, λ0 = 29.98 mm and λg = 29.98/√(1 − 0.430) = 39.7 mm. The cavity resonates when its length is half a guide wavelength, d = λg/2 = 19.9 mm; equivalently f = (c/2)√((1/a)² + (1/d)²) = 10 GHz with d = 19.85 mm. Such a cavity in copper has an unloaded Q of several thousand, ten times a microstrip resonator. At millimeter wave the same formula sets the size of the guide: WR-28, for 26.5 to 40 GHz, has a = 7.11 mm and a cutoff of 21.1 GHz.
H2: 6.4 Which technology at which frequency
P: Lumped LC filters work to a few gigahertz, limited by component self-resonance and Q near 50 to 100. Microstrip and stripline filters, built from coupled line resonators, work from about 1 GHz into the millimeter range, with Qu of 100 to 300. Ceramic coaxial resonators reach Qu near 500 to 1000 in a small volume for handset and base-station front ends. Acoustic filters, surface and bulk acoustic wave devices, are tiny and steep up to about 6 GHz and dominate mobile phones; their resonators are mechanical, which is why the MEMS survey in Book 9 claims them too. Cavities and waveguide, with Qu in the thousands, take the high-power and lowest-loss jobs. A filter is chosen by the loss it can afford, the power it must carry, and the space it may occupy, roughly in that order.
