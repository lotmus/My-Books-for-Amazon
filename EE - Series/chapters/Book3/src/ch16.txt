H1: Chapter 16. How a Transistor Remembers: SRAM, DRAM, and Flash
P: Logic forgets its inputs as soon as they change. Memory must not. There are three standard ways to make transistors hold a bit, and each trades area, speed, and permanence in a different way. The static cell holds a bit in a loop of two inverters, the dynamic cell in a charge on a capacitor, and the flash cell in electrons trapped on an insulated gate. Each has one governing equation, and each of those equations sets a hard limit that the memory industry engineers around.
H2: 16.1 The six-transistor SRAM cell
P: Two inverters connected in a ring have two stable states: if the first output is high, the second is low, which holds the first high. That latch is the bit. Two access transistors connect its two nodes to a pair of bit lines when the word line is raised, for six transistors in all. Reading precharges both bit lines high and lets the cell discharge one of them slightly; a sense amplifier detects the difference. Writing drives one bit line low hard enough to overpower the latch.
P: The two operations pull the sizing in opposite directions. During a read, the access transistor and the cell's pull-down NMOS form a divider on the node that holds a zero. If the access device is too strong, the node rises past the other inverter's switching threshold and the read flips the cell. The cell ratio, the pull-down's W/L divided by the access device's, is therefore kept at about 1.5 to 2. During a write, the access transistor must overpower the PMOS pull-up, so the pull-up is made weakest of the three. Random threshold mismatch from Chapter 12 makes these margins statistical: a large SRAM has billions of cells, and the worst cell, not the average, must work, so read and write margins are designed to many standard deviations.
H2: Worked Example 16.1
P: During a read, the access transistor (treated as a resistance Racc) and the pull-down transistor (Rpd) divide the 0.8 V precharged bit line onto the storage node that holds zero. The opposite inverter switches at 0.35 V. Find the largest ratio Rpd/Racc that keeps the node below half the switching threshold, a common margin target.
P: The node voltage is 0.8 × Rpd/(Rpd + Racc). Setting this to 0.175 V gives Rpd/(Rpd + Racc) = 0.219, so Rpd/Racc = 0.219/0.781 = 0.28. The pull-down must be about 3.6 times stronger than the access device in this resistive picture. Square-law and velocity-saturation effects make the real ratio smaller, which is why the practical cell ratio is 1.5 to 2, but the direction is the same: the read margin is bought with a stronger pull-down.
H2: 16.2 The one-transistor DRAM cell
P: A DRAM cell is a capacitor Cs and one access transistor. A one is a charged capacitor and a zero an empty one. To read, the bit line, with its much larger capacitance Cbl, is precharged to half the array voltage, and the access transistor connects the cell to it. The charge shares between the two capacitors and moves the bit line by a small amount that a sense amplifier detects and restores. The read is destructive, so every read is followed by a write-back.
EQ: ΔVbl = (Vcell − Vpre) × Cs / (Cs + Cbl)
H2: Worked Example 16.2
P: A DRAM cell has Cs = 20 fF on a bit line of Cbl = 100 fF. The array stores 1.0 V for a one and precharges to 0.5 V. Find the read signal. Then find the largest cell leakage that lets the cell lose no more than 0.15 V in the standard 64 ms refresh interval.
P: ΔVbl = 0.5 × 20/120 = 83 mV, a small signal that the sense amplifier must resolve against its own offset and the noise of neighboring lines. A leakage I discharges the cell at I/Cs, so the limit is I = Cs ΔV/t = 20 fF × 0.15 V/64 ms = 47 fA. Leakage roughly doubles with every 10 °C, and the DRAM standards halve the refresh interval to 32 ms above 85 °C for that reason. Every row must be refreshed within the interval whether or not it is used, which is a share of the chip's power and bandwidth that grows with capacity.
P: The ratio Cs/Cbl is the central problem of DRAM scaling. The cell area shrinks, but the capacitance cannot shrink with it without losing the read signal, so DRAM capacitors have become tall cylinders with aspect ratios beyond 50 to 1 and high-permittivity dielectrics. The access transistor must leak femtoamperes when off, so it is built quite unlike a logic transistor, with a buried, recessed gate and a long effective channel.
KEY: Key idea. SRAM stores a bit in feedback and needs power to keep it but no refresh; DRAM stores it in charge, which leaks and must be refreshed; flash stores it in trapped charge that stays without power. Each cell's density is set by the element that holds the bit.
H2: 16.3 The flash cell
P: A flash cell is a MOSFET with an extra gate, floating and completely surrounded by insulator, or, in most current NAND flash, a charge-trapping silicon-nitride layer that plays the same role. Electrons placed on the floating gate or in the trap layer shield the channel from the control gate and raise the threshold voltage seen from it. Programming forces electrons through the thin tunnel oxide by Fowler-Nordheim tunneling with a high field, about 15 to 20 V across the stack; erasing pulls them out. The stored state is read by applying a control-gate voltage between the programmed and erased thresholds and asking whether the cell conducts.
EQ: ΔVth = −Q / Ccg   (Q is the trapped charge, negative for electrons)
H2: Worked Example 16.3
P: A NAND cell's effective coupling capacitance between control gate and storage layer is 0.1 fF. How many electrons shift its threshold by 1 V? A triple-level cell (TLC) places 8 threshold levels in a 6 V window. Find the level spacing in volts and in electrons.
P: N = Ccg ΔVth/q = 0.1 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 1/1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 624 electrons per volt. Eight levels have seven spaces, so the spacing is 6/7 = 0.86 V, about 535 electrons. Losing a few tens of electrons over ten years of retention moves the threshold a noticeable fraction of the spacing, which is why multi-level cells need strong error-correcting codes and why each additional bit per cell (QLC has 16 levels and spacing of 0.4 V in the same window) costs endurance and retention.
P: Each program-erase cycle drives electrons through the tunnel oxide and leaves some trapped damage, so flash wears out: endurance is quoted in program-erase cycles, tens of thousands for single-level cells and roughly a thousand for TLC, and controllers spread writes evenly across the chip (wear leveling). Modern NAND stacks more than two hundred layers of cells vertically, because shrinking the cell laterally had reached the point where a few electrons decided a bit.
H2: 16.4 How long a DRAM cell remembers
P: Section 16.2 described the DRAM cell as a capacitor guarded by a transistor. The guard is imperfect: subthreshold leakage through the access transistor, junction leakage at the storage node, and leakage through the capacitor's dielectric all drain the stored charge. The JEDEC standards require every cell to hold its data for 64 ms at normal temperature, so the memory controller refreshes every row within that interval, and halves the interval above 85 °C, where the ni² leakage of Section 1.6 has grown by roughly an order of magnitude.
P: The required leakage is astonishingly small. A storage capacitor of about 10 fF that may lose 0.3 V before the sense amplifier of Section 16.2 misreads it must leak less than C ΔV / t = 10×10⁻¹⁵ × 0.3 / 0.064 = 47 fA. Achieving that in every one of tens of billions of cells is why DRAM uses its own process, with deep recessed-channel access transistors and tall, high-permittivity capacitors, and why it cannot simply be built on a logic chip. Refresh costs power and bandwidth: in a large DRAM device several percent of the time goes to refreshing rows nobody is reading.
H2: 16.5 The memories that want to replace these three
P: Each of the three classic memories has a flaw: SRAM is large, DRAM forgets, and flash wears out after thousands to around a hundred thousand program-erase cycles and writes slowly. Several newer cells store data as a physical state instead of a charge. Magnetic RAM, in its spin-transfer-torque form, stores a bit in the relative orientation of two magnetic layers in a tunnel junction, read as a resistance difference; it is nonvolatile, fast, and nearly unlimited in endurance, and it is in production as embedded memory in 22 nm and smaller logic processes, replacing embedded flash in microcontrollers. Resistive RAM switches a filament in an oxide between conducting and insulating states. Ferroelectric memories store a bit in the polarization of a film; the discovery that hafnium oxide, already in every gate stack, becomes ferroelectric when doped has revived them as ferroelectric transistors and capacitors compatible with CMOS lines.
P: None has displaced DRAM or NAND flash for bulk storage, because those two have decades of cost reduction behind them. The new memories win where their particular strength matters: nonvolatility at logic speed in a microcontroller, or radiation hardness in space. The DRAM cell, meanwhile, keeps moving up into the package, stacked in the high-bandwidth memory that Book 7 opens.
KEY: Key idea. A DRAM cell must leak less than about fifty femtoamps to survive its 64 ms refresh interval. Newer nonvolatile cells, MRAM foremost, win as embedded memory, not yet as bulk storage.
H2: Worked Example 16.1
P: A DRAM cell of 8 fF stores 1.0 V and is misread if it falls below 0.6 V. The refresh interval is 32 ms at high temperature. What is the largest allowed leakage, and how does it compare with the allowance at 64 ms?
P: I = C ΔV/t = 8×10⁻¹⁵ × 0.4 / 0.032 = 100 fA. At 64 ms the allowance would be 50 fA. Halving the interval at high temperature doubles the allowed leakage, which roughly matches the leakage increase between 85 °C and 95 °C under the doubling rules of Chapter 1, the reason for the standard's two refresh rates.
P: Memory is where the transistor's imperfections, leakage and wear, become the specification. Chapter 17 takes up the other ways a chip fails.
