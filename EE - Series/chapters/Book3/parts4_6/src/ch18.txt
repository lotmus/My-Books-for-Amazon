PART: Part VI. Light and Power
PARTP: The last part takes the junction of Part I in two directions the earlier parts did not. The first is light: the recombination that the diode equations treated as a bookkeeping term turns out to emit photons in some crystals and absorb them in all of them, which gives the LED, the photodiode, and the solar cell. The second is high voltage: the breakdown mechanism Chapter 6 introduced becomes the design constraint of every power device, from the power MOSFET to the thyristor and the IGBT. Appendix C then hands the reader on to the rest of the series.
H1: Chapter 18. Recombination and Light: LEDs, Photodiodes, and Solar Cells
P: Chapter 5 derived the diode law by counting minority carriers injected across a junction and noting that they disappear by recombination within a diffusion length. Where the energy of each recombination goes, nothing in the circuit needed to know. In silicon it goes into heat. In gallium arsenide or gallium nitride much of it leaves as light. Run the process backward, and absorbed light creates carriers that a junction collects as current. One set of numbers, the bandgap and the carrier lifetime, connects all three devices in this chapter.
H2: 18.1 Lifetime and diffusion length
P: An excess minority carrier survives for an average time τ, the minority-carrier lifetime, before it recombines. While it survives it diffuses, and the average distance it covers is the diffusion length. Lifetimes range from nanoseconds in heavily doped or deliberately damaged silicon to milliseconds in the purest solar-grade material. The shortest lifetimes are engineered on purpose: fast-recovery diodes are doped with gold or platinum or irradiated, creating recombination centers that shorten the reverse-recovery tail of Chapter 6.
EQ: L = √(D τ)
H2: Worked Example 18.1
WEP: Using the declared diffusion constants Dn = 26.0 and Dp = 12.48 cm²/s, find the diffusion length of electrons with τ = 1 µs and of holes with τ = 10 µs.
WES: Ln = √(26.0 × 10⁻⁶) cm = 5.1 × 10⁻³ cm = 51 µm. Lp = √(12.48 × 10⁻⁵) cm = 1.12 × 10⁻² cm = 112 µm. Both are long compared with the base of a transistor, which is why nearly every carrier injected into a thin base survives to the collector, and short compared with a solar wafer's thickness, which is why solar silicon needs long lifetimes.
H2: 18.2 Three ways to recombine
P: Recombination happens by three routes. In Shockley-Read-Hall recombination an electron and a hole meet at a defect level in the gap and their energy goes into lattice vibrations, heat. It dominates in silicon and depends on purity. In radiative recombination the electron drops directly across the gap and emits a photon of energy close to the bandgap. In Auger recombination the energy is handed to a third carrier, which matters at very high carrier densities, as in a heavily driven LED. Whether radiative recombination is efficient depends on the band structure. In a direct-gap semiconductor such as GaAs or GaN, the lowest conduction state and the highest valence state have the same crystal momentum, and an electron can fall straight down emitting only a photon. In an indirect-gap semiconductor such as silicon, a lattice vibration must take part to conserve momentum, the process is thousands of times slower, and defect recombination wins.
EQ: λ (nm) ≈ 1240 / E (eV)
H2: Worked Example 18.2
WEP: Find the wavelength corresponding to the bandgaps of silicon (1.12 eV), GaAs (1.42 eV), and GaN (3.4 eV), and say what each means for light emission and detection.
WES: Silicon: 1240/1.12 = 1107 nm, in the near infrared. Silicon cannot emit efficiently, but it absorbs every wavelength shorter than about 1.1 µm, so it detects visible and near-infrared light and is transparent beyond that. GaAs: 873 nm, the classic infrared LED and laser material. GaN: 365 nm, ultraviolet; alloying it with indium lowers the gap into the blue, around 450 nm, which with a phosphor makes the white LED. Telecommunication wavelengths of 1310 and 1550 nm lie beyond silicon's edge, so their detectors are InGaAs grown on InP.
H2: 18.3 The LED
P: An LED is a forward-biased junction in a direct-gap material, usually a heterostructure that confines electrons and holes in a thin quantum-well layer where they recombine. Its forward voltage is roughly the photon energy in electron-volts plus resistive and heterojunction drops: about 1.8 to 2.2 V for red, about 3 V for blue. Its external quantum efficiency (EQE) is the fraction of injected electrons that produce a photon leaving the chip, and its wall-plug efficiency is the optical power out divided by the electrical power in.
EQ: Popt = EQE × (I/q) × hν,    ηwall = Popt / (I VF)
H2: Worked Example 18.3
WEP: A blue LED emits at 450 nm, runs at 20 mA with VF = 3.0 V, and has an EQE of 50%. Find the optical power and the wall-plug efficiency.
WES: The photon energy is 1240/450 = 2.76 eV. The photon rate is 0.5 × 0.020/q, and each photon carries 2.76 eV, so Popt = 0.5 × 0.020 A × 2.76 V = 27.6 mW. The electrical input is 0.020 × 3.0 = 60 mW, so ηwall = 46%. The remaining 32 mW heats the junction, and because efficiency falls with temperature and at high current density (efficiency droop, mostly Auger recombination), LED drivers regulate current and LED luminaires are designed around heat sinking.
KEY: Key idea. The bandgap sets the color an LED emits and the longest wavelength a detector absorbs, through λ ≈ 1240/E. Whether a material emits efficiently is a question of direct versus indirect gap, not of the gap's size.
H2: 18.4 The photodiode
P: A reverse-biased junction that absorbs a photon in or near its depletion region collects the electron-hole pair as current. The quantum efficiency η is the fraction of incident photons that produce a collected pair, and the responsivity is the current per watt of light. The photocurrent is linear in light power over many decades, which is why photodiodes are run into the transimpedance amplifiers of Book 2 rather than read as voltages. Reverse bias widens the depletion region, which lowers the capacitance and speeds the response; an intrinsic layer between p and n (the PIN diode) does the same by design. The dark current, the junction's reverse leakage, sets the noise floor through its shot noise.
EQ: R = η q λ / (h c) ≈ η λ(nm) / 1240   (A/W)
H2: Worked Example 18.4
WEP: A silicon photodiode has η = 0.6 at 940 nm. An InGaAs photodiode has η = 0.7 at 1310 nm. Find both responsivities and the photocurrent from 50 µW on the InGaAs diode.
WES: Silicon: 0.6 × 940/1240 = 0.45 A/W. InGaAs: 0.7 × 1310/1240 = 0.74 A/W. The photocurrent is 0.74 × 50 µW = 37 µA. Longer wavelengths give more current per watt because each watt contains more photons, each with less energy, until the wavelength passes the bandgap edge and the response falls to zero.
H2: 18.5 The solar cell
P: A solar cell is a large-area photodiode operated in the fourth quadrant, where it delivers power. Under illumination its current is the diode law minus a photocurrent IL. At open circuit the photocurrent is balanced entirely by forward diode current, which sets the open-circuit voltage. The maximum-power point lies a little below it, at about 80% of Voc in a good silicon cell. The Shockley-Queisser limit for a single junction, set by the bandgap and the solar spectrum, is about 33%; commercial silicon modules reach 20–24%.
EQ: Voc = VT ln(IL/IS + 1)
H2: Worked Example 18.5
WEP: A small silicon cell has IS = 1 pA and delivers IL = 35 mA in full sun. Find Voc. How much does Voc change if the light is halved?
WES: Voc = 26 mV × ln(3.5 × 10¹⁰) = 26 mV × 24.3 = 0.631 V. Halving the light halves IL and lowers Voc by VT ln 2 = 18 mV, to 0.613 V. The voltage depends only logarithmically on light, while the current is proportional to it, which is why a panel's voltage stays near its rating from bright sun to overcast and its power falls mostly through current.
