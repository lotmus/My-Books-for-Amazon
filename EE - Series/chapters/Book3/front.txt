HOW: Book 1 taught the circuit laws and the passive parts. Book 2 treated a diode, a transistor, and an op-amp as blocks you could drop into a schematic. This book opens those blocks. By the end of Part I you can say, with a number, how many free carriers a piece of doped silicon has, how that number moves with temperature, how a Hall voltage or a capacitance measurement checks it, why a pn junction grows a built-in voltage with nobody applying a battery, and why the phrase "the diode turns on at 0.7 volts" is a useful sketch and a bad physical claim.
HOW: Two numbers are fixed for the whole book, so that a later chapter cannot quietly adopt a different room temperature. Thermal voltage VT is 26 mV. The intrinsic carrier concentration of silicon, ni, is 1.0×10^10 per cubic centimeter. Real tables scatter a little around both of those, because the bandgap model and the exact temperature move them. Hand work in this book uses the declared pair. When a vendor model quotes a different ni, use the vendor number for that part and keep this pair for the teaching problems.
HOW: Electron and hole mobilities depend on doping and temperature. Whenever a problem in this book does not hand you a mobility, use μn = 1000 cm²/V·s and μp = 480 cm²/V·s. Those are round working values for lightly doped silicon near room temperature, not a promise about a particular wafer.
HOW: Part II takes up the bipolar transistor and Part III the MOSFET, down to the fin and the nanosheet. Part IV builds the op-amp of Book 2 from those transistors and derives the noise they make. Part V treats CMOS as a chip: gate delay and power, memory cells, and the failures that end a chip's life. Part VI turns the junction into a source and detector of light and into a high-voltage switch. Part VII follows a transistor through the fab, builds the piezoelectric thin films and acoustic resonators behind every phone's filters, and explains the compact models and process design kits that carry all of these devices into a simulator. The circuit check for Parts II to IV is Tietze, Schenk, and Gamm, Halbleiter-Schaltungstechnik, 16th edition (2019), whose op-amp chapter was rewritten for that edition. High-speed boards, radio layout, switching regulators, and software-defined radio are later books in this series, named in Appendix C so this book does not pretend to cover them.
HOW: Each chapter opens with the question it answers, develops the physics in words, and then works it through with declared numbers and, where it helps, a real part: the 1N4148 and SS34 diodes, the 2N3904 transistor, the μA741 and OPA1612 op-amps, the BPW34 photodiode, and the 650 V switches of 2026. Every chapter ends with practice problems, and every answer is in Appendix B. Appendix A collects the declared constants; Appendix D lists the sources the prose was checked against.

HANDOFF: This book runs from the crystal to the chip: carriers and junctions (Part I), the bipolar transistor (Part II), the MOSFET to the nanosheet (Part III), the amplifier built from them (Part IV), CMOS as a chip (Part V), the junction as a source and detector of light and as a high-voltage switch (Part VI), and how devices are made, modeled, and extended to piezoelectric films (Part VII). Each later book of the series starts from a device this one has explained and stops explaining it.
HANDOFF: Book 4 takes the transistor's small-signal model, the GaAs, GaN, and InP devices and the load line of Chapter 7, the noise of Chapter 14, the power-device limits of Chapter 19, and the acoustic filters of Chapter 21 into matching networks, power amplifiers, receivers, and synthesizers. Book 5 uses the converters and sample-and-hold circuits of Book 2 and the noise of Chapter 14 to build modems and software-defined radios. Book 6 takes the Schottky and fast-recovery diodes of Chapter 6 and the power MOSFET, superjunction, SiC, GaN, and IGBT of Chapter 19 into converters, magnetics, and the grid. Book 7 picks up the chip of Part V at the package: interposers and chiplets (Chapter 20's yield argument), high-bandwidth memory built from the DRAM cells of Chapter 16, the 875 A of a modern accelerator, MEMS on the films of Chapter 21, and the ESD and latch-up of Chapter 17 as system-level immunity.
HANDOFF: Two habits travel with the reader. First, every quantity in this book was computed from a declared hand value; when a vendor model or a process design kit gives a different number, the vendor number wins for that part. Second, below about 20 nm the PDK outranks every textbook, including this one.
