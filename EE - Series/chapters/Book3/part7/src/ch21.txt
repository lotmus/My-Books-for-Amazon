H1: Chapter 21. Piezoelectric Films and Acoustic Resonators
P: A phone contains dozens of radio filters, and almost none of them are made from inductors and capacitors. They are acoustic: a piezoelectric crystal or film converts the electrical signal into a sound wave, the sound resonates in a structure a few micrometers in size, and the film converts it back. Because sound travels about 26,000 times slower than light in this material, an acoustic resonator at 2.45 GHz is micrometers long where an electromagnetic one would be centimeters, with a Q in the thousands. This chapter explains piezoelectricity, the bulk and surface acoustic resonators built on it, and why aluminum scandium nitride, AlScN, became the film of choice for the newest high-frequency filters. The survey of 2026 MEMS products is Book 7's.
H2: 21.1 Piezoelectricity
P: In a crystal without a center of symmetry, such as quartz, lithium tantalate, or the wurtzite form of aluminum nitride, straining the lattice shifts the positive and negative ions differently and produces a polarization; conversely, an applied field strains the crystal. The strength of this coupling between electrical and mechanical energy is expressed by an electromechanical coupling coefficient, k², the fraction of stored energy that converts between the two forms. Aluminum nitride, sputtered as a thin film compatible with CMOS fabs, has a thickness-mode k² of about 6.5%. Replacing part of the aluminum with scandium softens the lattice and raises the piezoelectric response substantially, so that AlScN films with scandium fractions of 20% to 30% reach roughly twice that coupling, at the cost of more difficult deposition and somewhat higher acoustic loss.
H2: 21.2 The bulk acoustic resonator
P: A film bulk acoustic resonator, FBAR, or its solidly mounted cousin, SMR, is a thin piezoelectric layer between two metal electrodes. A voltage across it launches a longitudinal sound wave through its thickness, and the structure resonates when the thickness is half an acoustic wavelength. In an FBAR the film is suspended over an air gap; in an SMR it sits on an acoustic mirror of alternating quarter-wave layers. Both trap the energy in the film. The electrodes add mass and lower the frequency, so real films are thinner than the simple formula gives, and the final frequency is trimmed by adjusting a thin layer on top.
EQ: f ≈ v / (2t),   λacoustic = v / f
H2: Worked Example 21.1
WEP: Aluminum nitride has a longitudinal sound velocity of about 11,350 m/s. Find the film thickness for a 2.45 GHz and a 5.5 GHz resonator, ignoring the electrodes, and compare the acoustic and electromagnetic wavelengths at 2.45 GHz.
WES: t = 11,350/(2 × 2.45 × 10⁹) = 2.32 µm, and 11,350/(2 × 5.5 × 10⁹) = 1.03 µm. The acoustic wavelength at 2.45 GHz is 4.63 µm; the free-space wavelength is 122 mm, 26,400 times longer. That ratio is the whole reason acoustic filters fit in a phone.
H2: 21.3 The resonator as a circuit
P: Near resonance, an acoustic resonator behaves like the Butterworth-Van Dyke circuit: a series branch of Lm, Cm, and Rm, representing the mechanical resonance, in parallel with the plate capacitance C0 of the electrodes. The series branch resonates at fs, where the impedance is minimum, and with C0 it produces a parallel resonance at fp = fs√(1 + Cm/C0), where the impedance is maximum. The spacing between them is set by the coupling: kt² ≈ (π²/4)(fp − fs)/fp. A ladder filter of series and shunt resonators, offset in frequency, places its passband between these resonances, so the coupling sets the widest bandwidth a filter can have.
EQ: fp = fs √(1 + Cm/C0),   kt² ≈ (π²/4)(fp − fs)/fp
H2: Worked Example 21.2
WEP: A 2.45 GHz resonator has a series resonance fs = 2.45 GHz. Find fp and the spacing for kt² = 6.5% (AlN) and 13% (AlScN). Find Cm/C0 for the AlN case. Then size an AlN resonator, εr = 9.5, so that C0 has a reactance of 50 Ω at 2.45 GHz.
WES: For AlN, (fp − fs)/fp = 0.065 × 4/π² = 0.0263, so fp = 2.45/(1 − 0.0263) = 2.516 GHz, a spacing of 66 MHz, and Cm/C0 = (2.516/2.45)² − 1 = 0.055. For AlScN at 13%, fp = 2.586 GHz, a spacing of 136 MHz, wide enough for the broad 5G bands that AlN cannot cover. For 50 Ω, C0 = 1/(2π × 2.45 × 10⁹ × 50) = 1.30 pF; with t = 2.32 µm, the area is C0t/(ε0εr) = 0.036 mm², a square 189 µm on a side.
KEY: Key idea. An acoustic resonator is a piezoelectric film half a sound wavelength thick. Its coupling coefficient sets the spacing of its two resonances and so the widest filter it can make, which is why higher-coupling AlScN films matter for wideband 5G and Wi-Fi bands.
H2: 21.4 Surface acoustic waves
P: A surface acoustic wave, SAW, device launches a wave along the surface of a piezoelectric crystal, usually lithium tantalate or lithium niobate, with interleaved comb electrodes. The electrode period is one acoustic wavelength, so with a velocity near 4000 m/s a 1 GHz device has a 4 µm period and 1 µm fingers. SAW filters are cheap and dominate below about 2 GHz; above that, the fingers become too fine and lossy, and BAW takes over. Temperature-compensated SAW adds a silicon dioxide layer whose stiffness changes in the opposite direction, and thin-film SAW on bonded substrates extends the technology upward.
H2: 21.5 Temperature and power
P: Acoustic velocities change with temperature, so filters drift: aluminum nitride's temperature coefficient of frequency is about −25 ppm/K, which moves a 2.45 GHz filter by 4.9 MHz over an 80 K range, a significant fraction of a narrow guard band. Oxide compensation layers reduce the drift. Power handling is the other limit: a transmit filter carries watts in a volume of a fraction of a cubic millimeter, and heating and acoustic stress at the edges set its maximum power, which is why duplexers for high-power user equipment use larger or cascaded resonators.
