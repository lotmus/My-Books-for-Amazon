H1: Chapter 2. Sampling and Complex Baseband
P: A modern radio does almost nothing in analog form. It amplifies, filters, and shifts the signal down in frequency, and then it samples it, and everything after the sampler is arithmetic. This chapter covers the three ideas that make that possible: the sampling theorem and the aliasing it warns of, the complex-baseband representation that lets a radio-frequency signal be processed at a low rate, and the two imperfections that limit what the sampled signal can show, the converter's quantization noise and the mismatch between the in-phase and quadrature paths.
H2: 2.1 The sampling theorem and aliasing
P: A signal containing no frequencies above B can be reconstructed exactly from samples taken at any rate fs greater than 2B. When the condition is broken, energy at a frequency f appears after sampling at the distance from f to the nearest multiple of fs, and nothing in the samples can tell the two apart. That folding is aliasing, and the only defense is a filter in front of the converter that removes what would fold before it is sampled. The same folding is used deliberately in bandpass sampling, where a narrow signal at a high intermediate frequency is sampled below its carrier and lands, intact, at a low frequency.
EQ: falias = | f − k fs |, with k chosen so that 0 ≤ falias ≤ fs/2
H2: Worked Example 2.1
WEP: A converter samples at 50 MS/s. Where does an interferer at 70 MHz appear? Where does a 140 MHz intermediate frequency appear when sampled at 61.44 MS/s?
WES: 70 − 50 = 20 MHz, inside the first Nyquist zone of 0 to 25 MHz, so the interferer lands at 20 MHz, on top of any wanted signal there. For the intermediate frequency, 140 − 2 × 61.44 = 17.12 MHz, which is below fs/2 = 30.72 MHz, so the signal appears at 17.12 MHz. The anti-alias filter in that case must be a bandpass around 140 MHz that rejects everything in the neighboring zones, not a lowpass.
H2: 2.2 Complex baseband
P: A radio signal at carrier fc can always be written as x(t) = I(t) cos 2πfct − Q(t) sin 2πfct, where I and Q are two slowly varying real signals. Together they form the complex envelope s(t) = I(t) + jQ(t), and all the information is in it. A quadrature mixer recovers I and Q by multiplying the received signal by cosine and by minus sine at the carrier and lowpass filtering each product. The pair can then be sampled at a rate set by the signal's bandwidth, not its carrier. A 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel at 5.5 GHz needs about 20 million complex samples per second, not 11 billion real ones.
P: Because s is complex, its spectrum need not be symmetric, and negative frequencies mean something: they are the part of the band below the carrier. That is why a complex sampler at fs covers a band fs wide, centered on the carrier, while a real sampler at the same rate covers only fs/2. The constellation diagrams of Chapter 4 are plots of s at the symbol instants.
KEY: Key idea. Complex baseband separates the carrier from the information. Once the signal is shifted down and split into I and Q, every modulation, filter, and equalizer in this book acts on a complex number per sample, at a rate set by the bandwidth alone.
H2: 2.3 Converter noise and processing gain
P: An ideal N-bit converter driven by a full-scale sine wave has a signal-to-quantization-noise ratio of 6.02N + 1.76 dB, spread evenly from 0 to fs/2. If the wanted signal occupies only a bandwidth B, a digital filter removes the noise outside it and the ratio within B rises by the processing gain 10 log₁₀(fs/2B) for a real converter, or 10 log₁₀(fs/B) for a complex pair. Real converters fall short of the ideal because of thermal noise, nonlinearity, and clock jitter. Jitter tj on the sampling clock limits the SNR for an input at frequency f to −20 log₁₀(2πftj), independent of the number of bits.
EQ: SNRq = 6.02 N + 1.76 dB,    processing gain = 10 log₁₀(fs / B)
H2: Worked Example 2.2
WEP: A 12-bit complex converter samples at 122.88 MS/s, and the wanted channel is 20 MHz wide. Find the ideal quantization SNR over the full band and in the channel. What clock jitter keeps the jitter limit at 64 dB for a 100 MHz input?
WES: Over the full band, 6.02 × 12 + 1.76 = 74.0 dB. Filtering to 20 MHz adds 10 log₁₀(122.88/20) = 7.9 dB, for 81.9 dB in the channel. For the jitter, −20 log₁₀(2π × 100 MHz × tj) = 64 dB gives tj = 1.0 ps rms. A 1 GHz input would need 100 fs for the same figure, which is why direct-RF sampling places hard demands on the clock, a subject Book 4 takes up with the synthesizer.
H2: 2.4 I/Q imbalance
P: The two arms of a quadrature mixer are never identical. If the Q arm has a gain g relative to the I arm and a phase error φ away from 90°, each frequency in the band leaks an image of itself into the mirror frequency on the other side of the carrier. The image-rejection ratio measures the leak. A 1% gain error and 1° of phase give about 40 dB, which is adequate for QPSK but not for 1024-QAM, whose decisions need errors near −36 dB or better in total. Modern receivers estimate the imbalance from the signal and correct it digitally, reaching 60 dB or more.
EQ: IRR = 10 log₁₀[(1 + 2g cos φ + g²) / (1 − 2g cos φ + g²)]
H2: Worked Example 2.3
WEP: Find the image-rejection ratio for g = 1.01 and φ = 1°, for g = 1.001 and φ = 0.1°, and for g = 1.05 and φ = 3°.
WES: Substituting, the three cases give 40.0 dB, 60.0 dB, and 28.9 dB. Each tenfold reduction in both errors buys about 20 dB. The third case, typical of an uncalibrated low-cost receiver, leaves an image only 29 dB down, enough to corrupt a 64-QAM signal on the opposite side of the band.
H2: 2.5 Bandpass sampling
P: The sampling theorem asks for a rate above twice the bandwidth, not twice the highest frequency. A signal confined to a band from fL to fH can be sampled below 2fH, as long as no alias of the band lands on top of another. The band is then folded down intact to a lower frequency. The valid rates are those that satisfy the inequality below for some integer n from 1 up to the largest value of fH/(fH − fL). For even n the folded band is spectrally inverted, which the digital processing simply corrects by negating Q.
EQ: 2fH / n ≤ fs ≤ 2fL / (n − 1)
P: Bandpass sampling is how many receivers digitize an intermediate frequency directly, and how direct-RF converters such as the AD9082 or the RF-sampling front ends of the AMD RFSoC take a carrier in the second or third Nyquist zone. Two costs come with it. The converter's analog input bandwidth and its clock jitter must suit the real carrier frequency, not the folded one, so the jitter limit of Section 2.3 is set by fH. And the anti-alias filter now must be a bandpass filter with stopbands that protect the neighboring zones.
H2: 2.6 DC offset, LO leakage, and the zero-IF receiver
P: The zero-IF receiver of Chapter 8 mixes the carrier straight to 0 Hz. Any local-oscillator energy that leaks back to the antenna and re-enters the mixer is mixed with itself and appears as a DC offset, often millivolts at the mixer output when the wanted signal is microvolts. Second-order intermodulation from a strong neighbor also lands at DC. Integrated transceivers such as the AD9361 run continuous DC-offset and quadrature-tracking loops for this reason, and OFDM standards leave the center subcarrier empty so that the residual offset falls where no data are. A low-IF receiver moves the wanted signal a few hundred kilohertz off zero, which avoids the DC problem at the price of a stricter image rejection.
H2: 2.7 Quantizing a signal with peaks
P: The ideal converter formula, 6.02N + 1.76 dB, assumes a full-scale sine wave, whose peak-to-average power ratio is 3 dB. A communication signal has more. If the converter's full scale must hold the peaks of a signal whose PAPR is P dB, the average power sits P − 3 dB further below full scale than a sine's would, and the SNR falls by the same amount. Clipping rarer peaks recovers some of that loss but adds clipping noise of its own. The usual design point for OFDM is to put full scale near the level exceeded once in 10⁴ samples, about 4 times the RMS value or 12 dB above the mean.
EQ: SNR ≈ 6.02N + 1.76 − (PAPR − 3 dB)
H2: Worked Example 2.4
WEP: An IF band from 60 to 80 MHz is to be sampled directly. List the valid sample-rate ranges, and pick one with margin for a realistic anti-alias filter.
WES: Here fH/(fH − fL) = 80/20 = 4, so n runs from 1 to 4. For n = 1 any rate above 160 MS/s works. For n = 2 the range is 80 to 120 MS/s; for n = 3, 53.3 to 60 MS/s; for n = 4 only exactly 40 MS/s, which leaves no guard band at all. A rate of 100 MS/s sits in the middle of the n = 2 range. The band folds to 20 to 40 MHz, inverted, with 20 MHz of guard to the folded edges at 0 and 50 MHz, room for a real bandpass filter.
H2: Worked Example 2.5
WEP: A 12-bit converter digitizes an OFDM signal with full scale set 12 dB above the RMS level. Find the SNR over the full Nyquist band, and the SNR in a 20 MHz channel if the sample rate is 122.88 MS/s.
WES: The ideal full-scale sine SNR is 6.02 × 12 + 1.76 = 74.0 dB. The OFDM signal sits 12 − 3 = 9 dB lower relative to its quantization noise, so the SNR is 65.0 dB across the full 0 to 61.44 MHz Nyquist band. Filtering to 20 MHz adds 10 log₁₀(61.44/20) = 4.9 dB of processing gain, giving 69.9 dB, far more than the 30 to 40 dB that even 4096-QAM needs. The margin is what lets the same converter handle a strong and a weak signal at once.
H2: 2.8 The digital down-converter
P: In a modern receiver the last mixer is a multiplication. After a wideband converter, a numerically controlled oscillator, NCO, produces samples of e^(−j2πf₀n/fs) and multiplies them into the data, shifting the wanted channel to 0 Hz, and a decimating filter of Chapter 9 then reduces the rate. The NCO is a phase accumulator, usually 32 or 48 bits wide, that adds a frequency word every sample; its top bits address a sine table or drive a CORDIC rotator. The accumulator width sets the tuning resolution, fs/2^N, and the number of phase bits used for the lookup sets the spurious level, about 6 dB per bit. Because everything is digital, eight or sixteen such channels can be tuned independently from one converter, which is how a base station receives several carriers with one RF chain.
EQ: Δf = fs / 2^N,   spur ≈ −6.02 × (phase bits) dBc
H2: Worked Example 2.6
WEP: An NCO runs at 122.88 MS/s with a 32-bit accumulator and 16 bits of phase into its sine generator. Find its tuning resolution and the approximate worst spur.
WES: The resolution is 122.88 × 10⁶/2³² = 0.029 Hz, fine enough to cancel any oscillator error. Truncating the phase to 16 bits gives spurs near −6.02 × 16 = −96 dBc, below the noise floor of a 14-bit converter in a wide band. Dithering the phase, or a CORDIC with more phase bits, lowers them further when needed.
P: Sampling, complex baseband, converter noise, and I/Q imbalance define the numbers a modem receives. Chapter 3 shapes the symbols themselves so that they fit a channel and can be pulled out of it cleanly.
