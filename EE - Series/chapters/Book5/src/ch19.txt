H1: Chapter 19. Interference and the Cellular SINR
P: A single link is limited by noise. A network is limited by itself: every base station is an interferer to the users of its neighbors. The quantity that sets a cellular user's rate is the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio, SINR, and its distribution across the cell, especially at the edge, sets the network's capacity. This chapter computes SINR in simple geometries, shows the trade made by frequency reuse, and outlines the coordination techniques that modern networks use.
H2: 19.1 SINR
P: The SINR is the wanted power divided by the sum of all interference and noise. In the interior of a dense network, interference dominates and noise is irrelevant; raising every base station's power then changes nothing, because signal and interference rise together. With a path-loss exponent n, typically 3 to 4 in urban environments, a user at distance r from its serving station and D from an equal-power interferer has an SIR of (D/r)^n. At the boundary between two cells, r = D and the SIR is 0 dB, before counting any other interferers.
EQ: SINR = S / (Σ Ii + N),   SIR (one interferer) = (D/r)^n
H2: Worked Example 19.1
WEP: With n = 3.5, a user is 0.8 km from its station and 1.2 km from the nearest interferer. Find the SIR and the Shannon spectral efficiency, ignoring noise and other interferers.
WES: SIR = 1.5^3.5 = 4.13, or 6.2 dB, and log₂(1 + 4.13) = 2.36 bit/s/Hz. At the cell edge, SIR = 0 dB and the efficiency falls to log₂2 = 1 bit/s/Hz, or less with several interferers. The edge, not the center, sets the coverage guarantee.
H2: 19.2 Frequency reuse
P: Older networks gave neighboring cells different frequencies, so the nearest co-channel interferer was several cells away. With a reuse factor of 3, each cell gets a third of the spectrum, and the interference falls sharply. Modern OFDMA networks use reuse 1, every cell on every frequency, because the full bandwidth at a lower SINR yields more throughput for most users than a third of it at a higher SINR. Reuse 1 makes the cell edge harder, which the techniques of the next section address.
H2: Worked Example 19.2
WEP: A cell-edge user has SINR 0 dB with reuse 1. With reuse 3 the SINR rises to 10 dB, but the user gets a third of the bandwidth. Compare their throughputs per hertz of total spectrum.
WES: Reuse 1: log₂(1 + 1) = 1.0 bit/s/Hz over the full band. Reuse 3: log₂(1 + 10) = 3.46 bit/s/Hz over a third of it, 1.15 bit/s/Hz of total spectrum. The edge user gains 15% from reuse 3, but users nearer the center, who already have high SINR, would lose nearly two-thirds of their rate, so reuse 1 wins overall, with the edge handled separately.
KEY: Key idea. In a cellular network, interference, not noise, limits most users. Reuse 1 maximizes total throughput but weakens the cell edge, which coordination, power control, and beamforming then repair.
H2: 19.3 Coordination
P: Networks protect the cell edge in several ways. Soft or fractional frequency reuse gives edge users a protected part of the band. Inter-cell interference coordination schedules neighboring cells so that their edge users avoid the same resources. Coordinated multipoint transmission has several stations serve one user jointly. Beamforming, Chapter 18's subject, reduces interference by directing energy only where it is wanted. Uplink power control keeps near users from swamping far ones, a necessity inherited from the CDMA systems of Chapter 12.
