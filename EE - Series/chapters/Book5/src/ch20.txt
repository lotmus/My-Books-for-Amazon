H1: Chapter 20. Satellites and Non-Terrestrial Networks
P: Since 3GPP Release 17, the cellular standards include non-terrestrial networks: phones and IoT devices connecting directly to satellites, and satellite constellations carrying 5G-style waveforms. Low-Earth-orbit broadband constellations have meanwhile put millions of terminals in service. The waveform of this book survives the trip to orbit largely unchanged, but three things are very different from a terrestrial link: the delay, the Doppler shift, and the path loss. This chapter computes each for low-Earth and geostationary orbits.
H2: 20.1 Geometry and delay
P: A geostationary satellite sits 35,786 km above the equator, so a signal takes 119 ms to reach it even at zenith, and a transparent link from user to satellite to gateway and back takes nearly half a second. A low-Earth-orbit satellite at 550 km is only 1.8 ms away overhead, and its slant range grows to about 1000 km at a 30° elevation angle. The slant range for an elevation e and altitude h is d = √((Re + h)² − (Re cos e)²) − Re sin e, with Re = 6371 km. HARQ timers, random-access windows, and timing advance in the standards had to be extended to accommodate these delays.
EQ: d = √((Re + h)² − (Re cos e)²) − Re sin e,   τ = d / c
H2: Worked Example 20.1
WEP: Find the one-way delay to a 550 km LEO satellite at zenith and at 30° elevation, and the round trip through a geostationary satellite between user and gateway.
WES: At zenith, 550 km / c = 1.83 ms. At 30°, d = 993 km and the delay is 3.31 ms. For GEO at zenith, each hop is 119.4 ms, and the user-satellite-gateway-satellite-user round trip is four hops, 477 ms. Interactive applications notice the GEO delay; LEO's few milliseconds are comparable to a terrestrial network's.
H2: 20.2 Doppler
P: A LEO satellite moves at about 7.6 km/s. A ground user sees a radial velocity up to about v Re/(Re + h), 7.0 km/s near the horizon, and the carrier shifts by f v/c, tens of kilohertz at 2 GHz and hundreds at Ka band, changing continuously through the pass. The subcarrier spacing of Chapter 6 cannot tolerate that offset uncompensated, so terminals use their own GNSS position and the satellite's broadcast ephemeris to precompensate the Doppler and delay before transmitting, leaving only a small residual for the synchronization loops of Chapter 10.
EQ: fD,max ≈ f × (v Re/(Re + h)) / c
H2: Worked Example 20.2
WEP: Find the orbital speed at 550 km, the maximum radial velocity seen from the ground, and the maximum Doppler at 2 GHz. Compare it with a 15 kHz subcarrier spacing.
WES: v = √(GM/(Re + h)) = √(3.986 × 10¹⁴ / 6.921 × 10⁶) = 7589 m/s. The maximum radial velocity is 7589 × 6371/6921 = 6986 m/s, and the Doppler is 2 × 10⁹ × 6986 / 2.998 × 10⁸ = 46.6 kHz, more than three subcarrier spacings. Without precompensation the receiver could not even find the right subcarriers.
KEY: Key idea. Non-terrestrial links keep the terrestrial waveform but face large delays, large and changing Doppler, and long paths. Terminals precompensate delay and Doppler using their own position and the satellite's orbit.
H2: 20.3 Path loss and the direct-to-device link
P: The free-space loss grows with the square of the distance, so GEO links lose about 36 dB more than LEO links at 550 km for the same frequency. That is why direct-to-phone services, which must close a link to a handset's small antenna and 23 dBm transmitter, use LEO satellites with very large deployable antennas, and why they start with text messages and low-rate data. Book 4's link budget applies unchanged: the satellite's G/T and EIRP make up for the handset's weak antenna.
EQ: ΔL = 20 log10(dGEO / dLEO)
H2: Worked Example 20.3
WEP: Compare the free-space loss at zenith for GEO and for a 550 km LEO satellite at the same frequency.
WES: 20 log10(35,786/550) = 36.3 dB. A LEO link at a 30° elevation, at 993 km, loses 5.1 dB more than at zenith, still 31 dB less than GEO.
H2: 20.4 The satellite link budget in C/N0
P: Satellite engineers write the link budget in a form that separates the pieces each party controls. The transmitter is described by its EIRP, the power times the antenna gain in the direction of the receiver, in dBW. The path is the free-space loss, which Book 4 derives, plus atmospheric and rain losses. The receiver is described by a single figure of merit, G/T, its antenna gain divided by its system noise temperature, in dB/K. Their combination gives the carrier-to-noise-density ratio, C/N0, from which the SNR in any bandwidth follows by subtracting 10 log₁₀B. The constant 228.6 is −10 log₁₀ of Boltzmann's constant in dBW/(K·Hz).
EQ: C/N0 = EIRP − L_path + G/T + 228.6   (dB-Hz)
P: The form explains the design of every satellite service. A geostationary television satellite radiates about 50 to 55 dBW toward its coverage area, so that a 60 cm dish with a G/T of about 15 dB/K can close the link at 40 000 km. A LEO broadband terminal uses a flat phased array of a few hundred elements with a G/T of roughly 10 dB/K but sits thirty-six decibels closer. A direct-to-phone service turns the asymmetry around: the phone's antenna has a G/T near −30 dB/K, so the satellite must carry a very large deployable array to make up the difference in both directions.
H2: 20.5 Timing advance and Doppler pre-compensation
P: A terrestrial handset learns its timing advance from the base station, which measures the round trip on a random-access preamble. Over a LEO link the round trip is several milliseconds and changes by tens of microseconds per second, far beyond the range a terrestrial preamble can measure. Release 17 NTN therefore makes the handset do most of the work: it knows its own position from GNSS and the satellite's from broadcast ephemeris, computes the delay and the Doppler of the service link, and pre-compensates both before it transmits. The network then corrects only the residual and the feeder-link delay. The same knowledge lets the handset predict when the satellite will set and prepare the handover to the next one, often every few minutes.
H2: Worked Example 20.4
WEP: A geostationary satellite at 12 GHz radiates 52 dBW toward a receiver 38 000 km away with a G/T of 15 dB/K. Find C/N0, the C/N for a 30 MBd DVB-S2 carrier, and the margin over 32APSK at rate 3/4, which needs Es/N0 = 10.2 dB.
WES: The free-space loss is 92.45 + 20 log₁₀38 000 + 20 log₁₀12 = 205.6 dB with distance in km and frequency in GHz. C/N0 = 52 − 205.6 + 15 + 228.6 = 90.0 dB-Hz. In 30 MHz, 74.8 dB-Hz, C/N = 15.2 dB, a clear-sky margin of 5.0 dB over 32APSK 3/4. A heavy rain cell at 12 GHz takes that margin, and DVB-S2's adaptive coding steps down to 8PSK or QPSK until it passes.
H2: Worked Example 20.5
WEP: A LEO satellite at 550 km, directly overhead, receives a handset's uplink at 2 GHz. The handset transmits 23 dBm (−7 dBW) from a 0 dBi antenna, and the satellite array has an assumed G/T of 10 dB/K. Find the C/N0 and the C/N in one 180 kHz resource block, before body and polarization losses.
WES: The path loss is 92.45 + 20 log₁₀550 + 20 log₁₀2 = 153.3 dB. C/N0 = −7 − 153.3 + 10 + 228.6 = 78.3 dB-Hz, and in 180 kHz, 52.6 dB-Hz, C/N = 25.7 dB. Body loss, polarization mismatch, and low elevation can take 10 to 15 dB of that, and a slant range at 30° elevation adds about 5 dB more, which is why direct-to-device services offer messaging and modest data rates first.
H2: 20.6 Footprints and constellation size
P: How many satellites a constellation needs depends on how much of the sky a user can use. A satellite at altitude h is visible above an elevation angle ε within a cap on the ground of Earth-central angle λ = arccos[R cos ε/(R + h)] − ε, with R = 6371 km. Low minimum elevation enlarges the footprint but forces the link through longer slant ranges, more atmosphere, and more obstruction by buildings and trees. Coverage alone sets a floor on the number of satellites; capacity sets the real number. One satellite's beams can serve only so many users, so a broadband constellation for millions of subscribers needs far more satellites than coverage alone would.
EQ: λ = arccos[R cos ε / (R + h)] − ε,   footprint radius = R λ
H2: Worked Example 20.6
WEP: Find the footprint radius and the maximum slant range of a satellite at 550 km for a minimum elevation of 25°, and estimate the minimum number of satellites for single coverage of the whole Earth.
WES: arccos(6371 × cos 25°/6921) = arccos(0.8343) = 33.45°, so λ = 8.45° = 0.1476 rad and the footprint radius is 940 km. The slant range at 25° is 1123 km, about twice the altitude. Each footprint covers about π × 940² = 2.78 × 10⁶ km², and the Earth's 5.1 × 10⁸ km² needs at least 184 of them, in practice two to three times as many for overlapping footprints and orbital geometry. Broadband constellations fly thousands because of capacity, not coverage.
H2: 20.7 Rain fade
P: Above about 10 GHz, rain decides a satellite link's availability. Raindrops absorb and scatter the signal with a specific attenuation that ITU-R Recommendation P.838 expresses as k R^α dB per kilometer, where R is the rain rate in millimeters per hour and k and α depend on frequency and polarization. For horizontal polarization, k is about 0.024 and α about 1.18 at 12 GHz; about 0.09 and 1.06 at 20 GHz; and about 0.24 and 0.95 at 30 GHz. The attenuation on a path is the specific attenuation times an effective path length through the rain, a few kilometers at moderate elevation. A climate that sees 40 mm/h for 0.01 % of the year, about 53 minutes, costs a few decibels at Ku band and well over ten at Ka band, which is why Ka systems rely on adaptive coding and modulation and why gateways are sited with diversity a few tens of kilometers apart.
EQ: γR = k R^α  (dB/km)
H2: Worked Example 20.7
WEP: Using the approximate coefficients above, find the rain attenuation for a 40 mm/h rain rate over an effective path of 4 km at 12 GHz and at 30 GHz.
WES: At 12 GHz, γ = 0.024 × 40^1.18 = 1.86 dB/km, so 7.5 dB over 4 km, more than the 5 dB clear-sky margin of Worked Example 20.4, so that link drops to a lower modulation for those minutes. At 30 GHz, γ = 0.24 × 40^0.95 = 8.0 dB/km and the attenuation is 32 dB, which no practical margin covers; the link instead accepts the outage or switches the user to a gateway outside the rain cell.
P: Satellite links bring the same physical layer into a geometry where every number is extreme: delay, Doppler, path loss, and the antenna that compensates for them. The last chapter goes to the other extreme, radios that must spend almost nothing.
