H1: Chapter 15. Link Adaptation, HARQ, and Real Throughput
P: The channel of Chapter 5 changes from moment to moment, and a fixed modulation and code would be either too fragile when the channel is poor or too timid when it is good. Modern links therefore choose their modulation and coding scheme, the MCS, every few milliseconds from the receiver's reports, and repair failed blocks with hybrid automatic repeat request, HARQ. This chapter shows how those mechanisms turn the Shannon bound of Chapter 1 into the throughput a user actually sees.
H2: 15.1 The gap to Shannon
P: Practical modulation and coding fall short of Shannon's capacity by a roughly constant SNR gap: the achievable spectral efficiency is about log₂(1 + SNR/Γ). With modern LDPC and polar codes at the block error rates links use, Γ is about 2 to 3 dB. The MCS table of a standard is a ladder of rates spaced across that curve. 5G NR's highest entry for 256-QAM uses a code rate of 948/1024, which carries 8 × 948/1024 = 7.41 bits per symbol.
EQ: η ≈ log₂(1 + SNR/Γ),   Γ ≈ 2 to 3 dB
H2: Worked Example 15.1
WEP: A link has an SNR of 15 dB. Find the Shannon spectral efficiency and the practical one with a 3 dB gap.
WES: SNR = 31.6, so log₂(32.6) = 5.03 bit/s/Hz. With the 3 dB gap, log₂(1 + 31.6/2.0) = 4.07 bit/s/Hz. The scheduler would pick the MCS whose efficiency is just below 4.07, a 64-QAM entry with a code rate near two-thirds.
H2: 15.2 Choosing the MCS
P: The receiver measures its SNR or SINR, usually from the pilots of Chapter 13, and reports a channel-quality index; the transmitter maps it to an MCS. Because the report is delayed and noisy, the link deliberately aims at a target block error rate, typically 10%, rather than at zero errors: an occasional error repaired by HARQ costs less than the throughput lost by always choosing a robust MCS. An outer loop corrects the mapping by watching the acknowledgments. Each failure raises an SNR backoff by a step Δup; each success lowers it by Δdown = Δup × BLER/(1 − BLER), so that at the target rate the two balance.
EQ: throughput = R (1 − BLER),   Δdown = Δup × BLER/(1 − BLER)
H2: Worked Example 15.2
WEP: At the current channel, MCS A delivers 4.0 bit/s/Hz with a 30% block error rate, and MCS B delivers 3.3 bit/s/Hz with 5%. Which gives more throughput? Find the outer-loop down step for a 10% target with a 0.5 dB up step.
WES: A: 4.0 × 0.70 = 2.80 bit/s/Hz. B: 3.3 × 0.95 = 3.14 bit/s/Hz. The more robust MCS wins here, even before counting the latency of retransmissions. For the outer loop, Δdown = 0.5 × 0.1/0.9 = 0.056 dB: nine successes at 0.056 dB balance one failure at 0.5 dB.
KEY: Key idea. Links aim at a moderate block error rate, not zero, and adapt the MCS to the measured channel. HARQ makes the occasional failure cheap by combining the retransmission with what was already received.
H2: 15.3 Hybrid ARQ
P: A plain retransmission discards the failed block. HARQ keeps it. In chase combining, the retransmission repeats the same coded bits, and the receiver adds the two copies, doubling the energy: a 3 dB gain in effective SNR. In incremental redundancy, the retransmission sends new parity bits, lowering the effective code rate; after two transmissions of a rate-3/4 code, the combined code has rate 3/8, a much stronger code than simple repetition provides. 5G and LTE use incremental redundancy with up to four transmissions and run several HARQ processes in parallel so the link stays busy while acknowledgments travel.
H2: Worked Example 15.3
WEP: A block fails on its first transmission 10% of the time and always succeeds on the second. What fraction of the first-transmission rate does the link deliver?
WES: The average number of transmissions per block is 1 + 0.1 = 1.1, so the throughput is 1/1.1 = 0.909 of the first-transmission rate, and the 10% of blocks that need a second transmission arrive one HARQ round trip later, a few milliseconds in LTE and as little as a fraction of a millisecond in 5G.
H2: 15.4 From PHY rate to user throughput
P: A user's throughput is the PHY rate of Chapter 7 reduced in steps: by the MCS the channel actually supports, by the block error rate, by pilot and control overhead, by the share of resources the scheduler of Chapter 14 assigns, and by protocol overhead above the PHY. In a loaded cell a user may see a tenth or less of the peak rate on the box. The engineering lesson is that the peak rates of Chapter 7 describe the best case of one link, while the averages and the cell-edge rates describe the network.
H2: 15.5 The peak rate a standard promises
P: 3GPP publishes a formula, in TS 38.306, for the maximum data rate a 5G NR carrier can support, and the headline figures in every handset announcement come from it. It multiplies the number of MIMO layers, the bits per symbol of the highest modulation, the highest code rate, 948/1024, and the number of subcarriers, and divides by the average OFDM symbol duration including the cyclic prefix, 10⁻³/(14 × 2^µ) seconds. A final factor removes the overhead of reference signals and control: 14 % in the downlink and 8 % in the uplink below 6 GHz. The result is a ceiling for one user next to the tower with the whole carrier, perfect hardware, and nothing else to do. Section 15.4 already showed how far below it a real user sits.
EQ: R = v Qm f Rmax × 12 N_PRB / [10⁻³/(14 × 2^µ)] × (1 − OH)
H2: 15.6 Latency and the bandwidth-delay product
P: Throughput is half the story; the other half is how long a packet takes. In 5G NR the air-interface delay is built from the slot length, the scheduling request and grant, the HARQ round trip, and the processing time at each end. At 30 kHz spacing a slot is 0.5 ms and a HARQ retransmission adds about 2 to 3 ms, so a user-plane delay of 4 to 10 ms is typical, and 1 ms needs mini-slots of 2, 4, or 7 symbols, wider spacing, and pre-granted uplink resources. The transport layer then multiplies the effect. A TCP connection can have no more data in flight than its window, so its rate is at most the window divided by the round-trip time. Filling a fast radio link therefore needs a window as large as the rate times the round trip, the bandwidth-delay product, and every lost packet that TCP mistakes for congestion halves the window. Link-layer HARQ and ARQ exist so that TCP almost never sees a radio error.
EQ: window ≥ R × RTT
H2: Worked Example 15.4
WEP: Find the 38.306 peak downlink rate of a 100 MHz carrier at 30 kHz spacing, 273 resource blocks, with 4 layers of 256-QAM.
WES: The average symbol lasts 10⁻³/28 = 35.7 µs. R = 4 × 8 × 1 × 0.926 × 273 × 12 / 35.7 µs × 0.86 = 2.34 Gbit/s. Two such carriers aggregated give the 4.7 Gbit/s quoted for many 2026 flagship modems; real users see a few hundred megabits.
H2: Worked Example 15.5
WEP: A user downloads over a 1 Gbit/s link with a 20 ms round-trip time. Find the TCP window needed to fill it, and the rate achieved with the once-common 64 KiB default window.
WES: The bandwidth-delay product is 10⁹ × 0.02 / 8 = 2.5 MB. A 65 536-byte window allows 65 536 × 8 / 0.02 = 26.2 Mbit/s, under 3 % of the link. Window scaling, standard in every modern operating system, removes the limit, but a large window also means that a single loss takes many round trips to recover from.
H2: 15.7 Outage: choosing a rate for a fading channel
P: Shannon's capacity assumes the SNR is known and fixed. In a fading channel with a slow-moving user and no time to adapt within a packet, a rate chosen for the average SNR fails whenever the channel fades below it. The outage capacity at probability p is the highest rate that the channel supports in all but a fraction p of its fades. For Rayleigh fading the instantaneous SNR is exponential, so the SNR exceeded with probability 1 − p is −ln(1 − p) times the mean. The penalty is severe without diversity and shrinks quickly with it, which is the deeper reason modern systems spread each codeword across frequency, time, and antennas: diversity makes the outage capacity approach the ergodic capacity.
EQ: C_out = log₂(1 − ln(1 − p) × SNR̄)    (Rayleigh, per hertz)
H2: Worked Example 15.6
WEP: A slow Rayleigh-fading link has a mean SNR of 20 dB. Find the 10 % outage capacity and compare it with the capacity at the mean SNR and with the ergodic capacity, 5.9 bit/s/Hz.
WES: The SNR exceeded 90 % of the time is −ln(0.9) × 100 = 10.5, 10.2 dB, so C_out = log₂(11.5) = 3.53 bit/s/Hz, and 90 % of transmissions deliver it, 3.18 bit/s/Hz on average. A fixed AWGN channel at 20 dB would give 6.66 bit/s/Hz. Diversity and fast link adaptation together recover most of the factor of nearly two between them.
H2: 15.8 Why Wi-Fi aggregates
P: Every Wi-Fi transmission pays a fixed overhead of time that does not depend on its length: the contention wait of Section 14.3, a DIFS of 34 µs plus a random backoff averaging 67.5 µs in a fresh contention window, a preamble of roughly 40 µs for an 802.11ax frame, a SIFS of 16 µs, and the block acknowledgment. At a PHY rate of a gigabit per second, a single 1500-byte packet occupies the air for only 10 µs, so the overhead dominates. 802.11n introduced aggregation, which packs many packets into one transmission, an A-MPDU, behind one preamble and one acknowledgment; 802.11ax allows up to 256 packets and 802.11be up to 1024. The effective throughput then approaches the PHY rate, and the acknowledgment bitmap lets the receiver request only the packets that failed.
H2: Worked Example 15.7
WEP: A Wi-Fi 6 link runs at a PHY rate of 1201 Mbit/s (two streams, 80 MHz, 1024-QAM rate 5/6). Take a fixed overhead of 34 µs DIFS, 67.5 µs average backoff, 40 µs preamble, 16 µs SIFS, and 44 µs block acknowledgment. Find the throughput for one 1500-byte packet per transmission and for an aggregate of 64.
WES: The overhead totals 201.5 µs. One packet takes 12 000 bits/1201 Mbit/s = 10.0 µs, so a transmission lasts 211.5 µs and the throughput is 12 000/211.5 µs = 57 Mbit/s, under 5 % of the PHY rate. Sixty-four packets take 639.5 µs, the transmission 841 µs, and the throughput 913 Mbit/s, 76 % of the PHY rate. Aggregation is worth a factor of 16 here, more than any modulation step.
