H1: Chapter 8. The 2026 Band Plan and the Software-Defined Receiver
P: The previous chapters built a waveform without asking where in the spectrum it would go. This chapter answers that question for the systems most readers will build or test in 2026, Wi-Fi and cellular, and then follows the signal into the receiver that turns it back into bits. The receiver is described as a software-defined radio, the architecture in which a fixed analog front end feeds a converter and everything else is code, because that is both how commercial chips are now built and how a student can experiment with every idea in this book. Emission masks and the test chamber belong to Book 7, and the power amplifier and synthesizer to Book 4.
H2: 8.1 Unlicensed and licensed spectrum
P: Wi-Fi uses unlicensed spectrum: anyone may transmit, within power and etiquette rules, and the cost is that nobody is protected from anyone else. Cellular uses licensed spectrum, bought at auction for exclusive use in a region, plus some unlicensed operation called NR-U. The two share physics and do not share regulators. The 2.4 GHz band, 2400 to 2483.5 MHz, is 83.5 MHz wide and holds only three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels, 1, 6, and 11, shared with Bluetooth, microwave ovens, and much else. It is crowded. The 5 GHz band, with several hundred megahertz depending on the country, is the workhorse of Wi-Fi 5 and 6, with 80 and 160 MHz channels.
H2: 8.2 The 6 GHz band
P: Where it is fully open, as in the United States, the 6 GHz band runs from 5925 to 7125 MHz, 1200 MHz in all. A 320 MHz channel fits three times, with 240 MHz left over for narrower channels. Many countries, including those of the European Union, opened only the lower part, about 500 MHz; a slice of that size holds one 320 MHz channel. Indoor low-power operation needs no coordination. Standard-power operation, including outdoors, is permitted under an automated frequency coordination system that checks a database of incumbent microwave links before allowing a device to transmit. The band is the reason Wi-Fi 7's 320 MHz channels, and so the 23 Gbit/s of Chapter 7, are possible at all. Wi-Fi 8, the 802.11bn project expected around 2028, aims at reliability and latency rather than another doubling of the constellation or the channel.
H2: Worked Example 8.1
WEP: How many 320 MHz and 160 MHz channels fit in the full 6 GHz band, and how many 320 MHz channels in a 480 MHz national allocation?
WES: 1200/320 = 3.75, so three 320 MHz channels fit with 1200 − 960 = 240 MHz left, and the remainder holds one 160 MHz channel and one 80 MHz channel. Of 160 MHz channels alone, 1200/160 = 7.5, so seven fit. A 480 MHz allocation holds one 320 MHz channel with 160 MHz left. In practice the standard fixes the channel centers, and the 320 MHz channels may overlap in two staggered sets, but the counts of non-overlapping channels are as calculated.
H2: 8.3 The cellular ranges
P: 5G NR defines two frequency ranges: FR1, from 410 MHz to 7.125 GHz, and FR2, from 24.25 to 71 GHz, the millimeter-wave range, itself split at 52.6 GHz. 5G-Advanced, the 3GPP releases from 18 onward, extracts more from those same bands rather than adding new ones. The research toward 6G around 2030 targets the gap between them, 7.125 to 24.25 GHz, sometimes called FR3, where wider channels than FR1 are possible with better coverage than FR2; candidate bands in that range are on the agenda of the 2027 World Radiocommunication Conference. Sub-terahertz frequencies of roughly 90 to 300 GHz remain a study item, not a 2026 phone band. Wi-Fi at 6 GHz and licensed cellular in the upper 6 GHz range sit next to each other in some countries, and power limits and coordination are how they share.
KEY: Key idea. The waveform is this book; the license is the band table. A design starts by asking which band, how wide a channel, and under what rules, and the answers fix the symbol rate and constellation before a line of code is written.
H2: 8.4 Receiver architectures
P: Four architectures cover nearly all receivers. The superheterodyne mixes to a fixed intermediate frequency, filters there sharply, and then converts to baseband; it has the best selectivity and the most parts. The zero-IF, or direct-conversion, receiver mixes straight to complex baseband with a quadrature mixer. It is cheap and integrates well, and it suffers from DC offset, flicker noise, and the I/Q imbalance of Chapter 2. The low-IF receiver mixes to a small offset to avoid the DC problems and relies on digital image rejection. Direct-RF sampling digitizes the antenna signal, after filtering and amplification, with a multi-gigasample converter; the clock jitter limit of Chapter 2 is its constraint. Most phones and Wi-Fi chips use zero-IF or low-IF; laboratory SDRs such as the ADALM-Pluto use a zero-IF transceiver chip with 12-bit converters.
H2: 8.5 Sensitivity
P: A receiver's sensitivity is the weakest signal it can demodulate at a target error rate. It is set by the thermal noise floor, −174 dBm/Hz at room temperature, plus 10 log₁₀B for the bandwidth, plus the receiver's noise figure, plus the SNR the modulation and code require. The noise figure itself, and how it builds up through the chain of stages, is Book 4's subject.
EQ: Psens (dBm) = −174 + 10 log₁₀B + NF + SNRreq
H2: Worked Example 8.2
WEP: A 20 MHz receiver has a 7 dB noise figure. Find its noise floor, and its sensitivity for a mode that needs 25 dB of SNR.
WES: 10 log₁₀(20 × 10⁶) = 73.0 dB, so the floor is −174 + 73.0 + 7 = −94.0 dBm. With 25 dB of required SNR the sensitivity is −69 dBm. The 802.11a standard requires −65 dBm for its 54 Mbit/s mode, so this receiver would pass with 4 dB of margin. The same receiver needs only about −90 dBm for BPSK at rate 1/2, which is why the rate falls step by step as a user walks away from an access point.
H2: 8.6 SDR as a laboratory
P: A software-defined radio lets every chapter of this book be tried on real signals. The PySDR online textbook and the GNU Radio framework are good laboratories, though they are not substitutes for this book's theory. A useful sequence of exercises is: capture a broadcast FM station and display its spectrum; decimate the capture and plot the eye of a known digital signal; build a raised-cosine transmitter and matched-filter receiver in loopback; measure EVM against transmit gain to find where the amplifier starts to compress; and decode the beacon frames of a Wi-Fi access point, which use the 312.5 kHz OFDM of Chapter 6. Transmitting requires care: outside licensed amateur bands or a shielded enclosure, the laboratory should be receive-only.
