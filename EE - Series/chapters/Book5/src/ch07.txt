H1: Chapter 7. Coding and Multiple Antennas
P: The modulations of Chapter 4 are several decibels from Shannon's limit, and the fading channel of Chapter 5 is far worse. Two techniques close most of the gap. Error-correcting codes add structured redundancy so the receiver can repair errors, and they recover most of the distance to the limit. Multiple antennas at both ends open several parallel paths through the same bandwidth, and they raise the limit itself. This chapter shows what each buys and finishes with the calculation behind Wi-Fi 7's headline figure.
H2: 7.1 What a code does
P: A code of rate R = k/n maps k information bits to n coded bits. The extra n − k bits let the decoder detect and correct errors, at the cost of a factor R in throughput or a factor 1/R in bandwidth. The simplest example is the Hamming (7,4) code: four data bits and three parity bits, any single error in the seven corrected. With a raw bit error rate of 10⁻², the probability that a 7-bit block contains two or more errors, and so is not corrected, is 2.0 × 10⁻³. Codes are judged by their coding gain, the reduction in the Eb/N0 needed for a target error rate, with the rate loss already counted because Eb is energy per information bit.
H2: 7.2 Convolutional, turbo, and LDPC codes
P: For decades the standard was the rate-1/2 convolutional code with constraint length 7, decoded by the Viterbi algorithm. It gives a coding gain of about 5 dB at a bit error rate of 10⁻⁵ and is still in the 802.11 standard. Turbo codes, announced in 1993, and low-density parity-check codes, invented by Gallager in 1962 and rediscovered in the 1990s, decode iteratively and come within about a decibel of the limit for long blocks. LDPC codes are used in Wi-Fi from 802.11n onward, in DVB-S2, and for the 5G data channels; 5G uses polar codes for its control channels. The limit itself, for a spectral efficiency η, requires Eb/N0 of at least (2^η − 1)/η.
EQ: Eb/N0 ≥ (2^η − 1) / η
H2: Worked Example 7.1
WEP: Find the minimum Eb/N0 for spectral efficiencies of 1, 2, 6, and 10 bit/s/Hz.
WES: For η = 1, (2 − 1)/1 = 1, or 0 dB. For η = 2, 3/2 gives 1.76 dB. For η = 6, 63/6 gives 10.2 dB. For η = 10, 1023/10 gives 20.1 dB. As η falls toward zero the bound tends to ln 2, the −1.59 dB of Chapter 1. Uncoded BPSK needs 9.6 dB for a bit error rate of 10⁻⁵ at η = 1, so a good code on such a link has up to about 9.6 dB to recover; practical LDPC codes recover about 8.
KEY: Key idea. Coding moves a link toward Shannon's limit, and multiple antennas move the limit. Modern links use both: an LDPC code within a decibel or two of capacity, on top of several spatial streams.
H2: 7.3 MIMO
P: With Nt transmit and Nr receive antennas in a rich scattering environment, the channel is a matrix H, and the link can carry up to min(Nt, Nr) independent spatial streams through the same bandwidth. The capacity is the sum over the channel's eigenmodes. In the ideal case of equal, independent eigenmodes and the transmit power split equally, an N × N link at total SNR ρ has a capacity of N log₂(1 + ρ/N) bit/s/Hz. At ρ = 20 dB this is 6.66 bit/s/Hz for one antenna at each end, 11.3 for 2 × 2, and 18.8 for 4 × 4. One transmit antenna with four receive antennas gives only log₂(1 + 4ρ) = 8.6: receive combining raises SNR, but only spatial multiplexing multiplies the number of streams.
EQ: C ≈ N log₂(1 + ρ/N)  bit/s/Hz  (N × N, ideal)
P: Real channels have correlated antennas and unequal eigenmodes, and line-of-sight links with closely spaced antennas may support only one useful stream. Multiple antennas can also be used for diversity, as in the Alamouti space-time code, or for beamforming toward one user or several users at once, which is multiuser MIMO; the arrays themselves are Book 4's subject.
H2: Worked Example 7.2
WEP: An 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) link uses 80 MHz, which has 980 data subcarriers, 1024-QAM with rate-5/6 coding, and a 13.6 µs symbol. Find its rate per spatial stream. Find the 160 MHz, eight-stream peak.
WES: Per stream, 980 × 10 bits × 5/6 / 13.6 µs = 600.5 Mbit/s, the figure printed on most Wi-Fi 6 adapters as 600 Mbit/s for one stream. At 160 MHz there are 1960 data subcarriers, and with eight streams the rate is 1960 × 10 × 5/6 / 13.6 µs × 8 = 9.61 Gbit/s, Wi-Fi 6's quoted peak of 9.6 Gbit/s.
H2: Worked Example 7.3
WEP: Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) uses 320 MHz channels with 3920 data subcarriers, 4096-QAM at rate 5/6, a 13.6 µs symbol, and up to eight spatial streams. Find its paper peak rate.
WES: 4096-QAM carries 12 bits, so per stream the rate is 3920 × 12 × 5/6 / 13.6 µs = 2.88 Gbit/s. Eight streams give 23.06 Gbit/s, the "about 23 Gbit/s" of the specifications. It is a paper figure. It needs a clean 320 MHz channel, which exists only in the 6 GHz band of Chapter 8, an SNR above about 38 dB on every stream, and eight antennas at each end; a phone with two antennas reaches a quarter of it at best.
H2: 7.4 Decoding: the Viterbi trellis and soft information
P: A convolutional encoder is a shift register; its state is the last K − 1 input bits, so the Wi-Fi code with constraint length K = 7 has 64 states. The decoder looks for the path through the trellis of states whose output sequence is closest to what was received. Viterbi's insight was that at every step only one surviving path into each state can be part of the best overall path, so the work grows linearly with the message length instead of exponentially: 64 add-compare-select operations per bit for Wi-Fi's code. Fed soft values, the log-likelihood ratios of Chapter 4, the closeness measure becomes a correlation, and the decoder gains about 2 dB over hard decisions. Turbo and LDPC decoders go further by passing soft information back and forth iteratively, which is how they reach within 1 dB of Shannon's limit.
H2: 7.5 Polar codes and the 5G split
P: 5G NR uses two code families. LDPC carries the data, because its decoder parallelizes well at gigabit rates. Polar codes, proposed by Arıkan in 2009 and the first family proven to reach capacity, carry the short control messages and the broadcast channel. A polar code combines N = 2ⁿ copies of the channel recursively so that some of the synthesized bit channels become almost perfect and the rest almost useless; the encoder puts information on the good ones and fixed, frozen bits on the bad ones. Successive-cancellation list decoding, with a list of 8 and a CRC to pick the right candidate, makes short polar codes, a few dozen to a few hundred bits, better than LDPC or turbo codes of the same length.
H2: 7.6 MIMO capacity and the Alamouti code
P: Spatial multiplexing turns a channel with a rich scattering environment into several parallel channels, as many as the smaller of the transmit and receive antenna counts. If those channels are equally strong, splitting the transmit power among them multiplies the capacity almost by their number at high SNR, because capacity grows only logarithmically with power but linearly with the number of channels. When the channel offers diversity but the receiver has only one antenna, the Alamouti code sends two symbols from two antennas over two symbol periods, s₁ and s₂ first, then −s₂* and s₁*. A single-antenna receiver recovers both with simple linear combining and gets full second-order diversity, the same slope as two receive antennas, but 3 dB less array gain, because the transmitter, not knowing the channel, splits its power between the antennas.
EQ: C = Σᵢ log₂(1 + λᵢ SNR / Nt)
H2: Worked Example 7.4
WEP: A channel at 20 dB SNR offers two equal, orthogonal spatial paths, each with the gain of the single-antenna channel. Compare the capacity of a 2 × 2 MIMO link that splits its power between them with the single-antenna capacity.
WES: SISO: log₂(1 + 100) = 6.66 bit/s/Hz. MIMO: each stream gets half the power, SNR 50, so C = 2 log₂(1 + 50) = 11.34 bit/s/Hz, 1.7 times as much with no more power and no more bandwidth. At 30 dB the ratio approaches 1.8, and in the limit 2.
H2: Worked Example 7.5
WEP: A Viterbi decoder runs at 1 Gbit/s for a K = 7 code. How many add-compare-select operations per second does it perform, and how does a K = 9 code change that?
WES: 64 states at 10⁹ bits per second is 6.4 × 10¹⁰ operations per second, which is why fast Viterbi decoders run several radix-4 units in parallel. A K = 9 code has 256 states, four times the work, for about 1 dB more coding gain, which is why K = 7 has been the standard compromise since the Voyager probes.
H2: 7.7 Coding gain in the link budget
P: The value of a code appears in the link budget as coding gain: the reduction in Eb/N0 needed for a target error rate, compared with the uncoded modulation at the same information rate. Uncoded BPSK or QPSK needs Eb/N0 = 10.5 dB for a bit error rate of 10⁻⁶. A rate-1/2 convolutional code with K = 7 and soft Viterbi decoding needs about 5 dB, a gain of 5.5 dB. A long rate-1/2 LDPC code reaches the same error rate at about 1.5 dB, a gain of 9 dB, within about 1.7 dB of the Shannon limit for a binary-input channel at rate 1/2, which is 0.19 dB. In a power-limited link, 9 dB is a factor of 8 in transmitter power or 2.8 in range in free space, bought with arithmetic in the receiver.
H2: Worked Example 7.6
WEP: A QPSK link closes at a bit error rate of 10⁻⁶ uncoded with 2 dB of margin. Adding a rate-1/2 LDPC code with a 9 dB coding gain halves the information rate in the same bandwidth. By how much can the free-space range grow if the information rate is kept by doubling the bandwidth?
WES: Doubling the bandwidth to keep the rate doubles the noise, a 3 dB loss, so the net improvement is 9 − 3 = 6 dB. In free space, range scales as 10^(6/20) = 2.0: the code doubles the range at the same transmitter power and bit rate, at the cost of twice the spectrum.
P: Pulse shapes, constellations, OFDM, coding, and MIMO are the pieces of a modern physical layer. Chapter 8 puts them where they are used: in the frequency bands of 2026 and in the software-defined radio that turns them into code.
