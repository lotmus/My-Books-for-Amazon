PART: Part II. Active Circuits: Amplifiers, Receivers, Oscillators, and Synthesizers
H1: Chapter 20. The Transmitter Chain and Its Devices
P: Book 5 built the waveform. This book makes it powerful enough to cross a room, a city, or the distance to a satellite, and then receives it again when it arrives weak and noisy. The transmitter is a chain, and every link in it either delivers watts or loses them: the device, the load line it is designed to, the amplifier class, the output match, the bias, the heat sink, the combiner, the isolator, the harmonic filter, the feed line, the duplexer, and the antenna, whose output is the effective isotropic radiated power that the regulator limits. The receiver half of a radio, the low-noise amplifier, the noise cascade, and the oscillator that tunes both halves sit in this book as well, because they share the same box and the same engineering. Why gallium nitride withstands the voltage is Book 3's question, antenna gain is Part I's, and the test chamber is Book 7's. This book owns the watts.
H2: 20.1 Power in decibels
P: Radio engineers count power in dBm, decibels relative to one milliwatt: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW). One watt is 30 dBm, 100 W is 50 dBm, and 1 kW is 60 dBm. Gains and losses in decibels add. The effective isotropic radiated power is the transmitter output, minus the loss between the amplifier and the antenna, plus the antenna gain in dBi. A loss of 1 dB is a factor of 10^(−0.1) = 0.794 in power: a fifth of the watts, gone as heat in a cable.
EQ: EIRP (dBm) = Pout (dBm) − Lfeed (dB) + Gant (dBi)
H2: Worked Example 20.1
WEP: A 100 W amplifier feeds a 17 dBi sector antenna through 1 dB of cable and connectors. Find the power at the antenna and the EIRP in dBm and watts.
WES: 100 W is 50 dBm. After 1 dB the power is 49 dBm, or 100 × 0.794 = 79.4 W. The EIRP is 49 + 17 = 66 dBm, which is 10^(3.6) mW = 3981 W, about 4 kW. The 20.6 W lost in the feed heats the cable and buys nothing; one decibel saved after the last amplifier is worth exactly as much as one decibel of extra amplifier output, and it is almost always cheaper.
KEY: Key idea. One decibel after the last amplifier is one decibel of EIRP. Every loss between the device and the antenna must be paid for by a bigger, hotter, more expensive amplifier.
H2: 20.2 The device families
P: Five technologies cover nearly all power amplifiers in 2026. Silicon LDMOS, the laterally diffused MOS transistor, dominates from HF to about 3 GHz: it is cheap, rugged, and runs from 28 or 50 V supplies, and it powers broadcast transmitters, industrial heating, and most sub-3 GHz cellular base stations built before gallium nitride took over. Gallium nitride on silicon carbide, GaN-on-SiC, covers roughly 0.5 to 40 GHz. Its wide bandgap lets it run at 28 to 50 V with power densities several times those of gallium arsenide per millimeter of gate, and its silicon-carbide substrate conducts heat well; it is the default for new base stations, radar, and satellite links. Gallium arsenide, as heterojunction bipolar transistors and pHEMTs, supplies drivers and the few-watt amplifiers in phones and Wi-Fi. Indium phosphide offers the highest transistor speed and is used for sub-terahertz work and low noise, not for kilowatts. Vacuum tubes, the traveling-wave tube, klystron, and magnetron, remain where kilowatts to megawatts are needed at high frequency, because combining enough transistors becomes more expensive than one tube.
H2: 20.3 Supply voltage and power
P: A transistor's output power is set by how much voltage and current it can swing. The voltage is limited by breakdown, which sets the supply, and the current by the device's size. Chapter 21 makes this precise with the load line, but the consequence is already clear: at a given power, a higher supply means a lower current and a higher optimum load resistance, which is closer to the 50 Ω of the outside world and easier to match. That is the main reason high-voltage gallium nitride displaced gallium arsenide in base stations, and why a phone amplifier running from a 3.4 V battery must transform its load by a large ratio, losing efficiency in the process.
H2: Worked Example 20.2
WEP: A radar transmitter needs 2 kW peak at the antenna through 0.6 dB of loss. Its GaN modules each deliver 250 W at the combiner input, and the combiner loses 0.4 dB. How many modules are needed?
WES: The total loss is 1.0 dB, a factor of 0.794, so the modules together must supply 2000/0.794 = 2518 W. At 250 W each, that is 10.07 modules, so eleven, or in a binary combiner tree, sixteen. A tree of eight would deliver only 8 × 250 × 0.794 = 1589 W. The arithmetic shows why combining loss limits the solid-state route: every doubling of the module count adds a combining stage and its loss.
