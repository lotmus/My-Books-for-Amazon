H1: Chapter 21. The Load Line
P: A small-signal amplifier is matched for gain: its load is chosen so that the most power is transferred, which means a conjugate match to the device's output impedance. A power amplifier is matched for power, and that is a different load. The device can swing its voltage only between a knee, below which its current collapses, and a maximum set by the supply and breakdown, and it can swing its current only between zero and a maximum. The load that lets it use the whole of both ranges at once is the optimum load, Ropt, and finding it is the first step of every power-amplifier design. The method is due to Steve Cripps, whose RF Power Amplifiers for Wireless Communications remains the reference.
H2: 21.1 The optimum load
P: With a supply VDD and a knee voltage Vknee, the drain voltage can swing by VDD − Vknee on either side of the supply, so the peak fundamental voltage is VDD − Vknee. With a sinusoidal current, the fundamental output power is the product of the peak voltage and peak current divided by two. For a target output power P, the optimum load resistance follows directly, and it is a resistance at the intrinsic drain, behind the device's output capacitance and package parasitics, which the matching network must also absorb.
EQ: Ropt = (VDD − Vknee)² / (2P)
H2: Worked Example 21.1
WEP: Find Ropt for a 100 W amplifier on a 50 V GaN process with a 5 V knee, and on a 28 V LDMOS process with a 3 V knee. Find the ratio of 50 Ω to each, and the peak fundamental current in each case.
WES: At 50 V, Ropt = 45²/200 = 10.125 Ω, which this book writes as 10.13 Ω. At 28 V, Ropt = 25²/200 = 3.125 Ω, written 3.13 Ω. The transformation ratios to 50 Ω are 4.9 and 16. The peak fundamental currents are 2P/(VDD − Vknee) = 200/45 = 4.44 A and 200/25 = 8.0 A. The 28 V design needs nearly twice the current and so a larger device, with more output capacitance to tune out, and a matching network with three times the impedance ratio.
H2: 21.2 Why the conjugate match is wrong
P: The device's output resistance, the slope of its current against drain voltage, is typically hundreds of ohms for a large transistor. A conjugate match would present that resistance as the load, and the voltage swing would hit the knee and the supply long before the current reached its maximum: the device would deliver a small fraction of the power it could. The load-line match accepts a lower gain in exchange for using the full current and voltage range. The difference between the two, often 2 to 3 dB of output power, is the reason power amplifiers are designed by load-pull measurements, which sweep the load and map power and efficiency contours, rather than by small-signal S-parameters.
KEY: Key idea. A power amplifier is matched for power, not gain. The optimum load is set by the voltage and current the device can swing, and at a given power it scales with the square of the voltage.
H2: 21.3 The matching network
P: The output network must transform 50 Ω down to Ropt and absorb the device's output capacitance. For a single L-section, Chapter 3's result gives Q = √(Rhigh/Rlow − 1). For the 50 V design, Q = √(4.94 − 1) = 1.98; for the 28 V design, Q = √(16 − 1) = 3.87. A higher Q means a narrower bandwidth, larger circulating currents, and more loss in the network's finite-Q components. Wideband or high-ratio designs use two or more sections, each with a lower ratio, or a transmission-line transformer. In a phone the problem is worse: at 3.4 V with a 0.3 V knee, 1 W needs Ropt = 3.1²/2 = 4.8 Ω, and 2 W needs 2.4 Ω, a 21:1 ratio.
H2: Worked Example 21.2
WEP: A 200 W design on the 50 V process uses the same 5 V knee. Find Ropt, the single-section Q to 50 Ω, and compare it with the 100 W design.
WES: Ropt = 45²/400 = 5.06 Ω. The ratio is 50/5.06 = 9.9, so Q = √(9.9 − 1) = 2.98. Doubling the power at the same voltage halves Ropt and raises the single-section Q from 1.98 to 2.98. To get back to the 100 W impedance level, the designer would combine two 100 W devices, Chapter 24's subject, or move to a higher supply.
H2: 21.4 Raising the rail
P: The scaling Ropt ∝ V²/P is the main argument of high-voltage design. Doubling the supply at constant power quadruples the optimum load and halves the current. Gallium nitride's breakdown field, about ten times that of silicon, permits 48 to 50 V supplies at microwave frequencies, and some designs use 65 V or more. The cost is that the knee voltage, the drain-source capacitance, and the trapping effects that cause memory in the amplifier's response all change with voltage, and the designer must characterize them at the operating point.
