PART: Part I. Passive Structures: Lines, Matching, Filters, and Antennas
H1: Chapter 1. When a Wire Stops Being a Wire
P: Book 1 solved circuits in which a wire has no length: the voltage at one end equals the voltage at the other, at every instant. That assumption is excellent as long as a signal's wavelength is much longer than the circuit. At radio frequencies it fails, and the failure is the whole subject of this book. A trace becomes a transmission line, a capacitor becomes an inductor, and a connection becomes a radiator. This chapter sets out where the boundary lies, fixes the units the rest of the book uses, and makes one claim that shapes the series: millimeter wave is not a separate subject. It is this subject at a shorter wavelength, with less forgiveness.
H2: 1.1 Wavelength sets the scale
P: A signal of frequency f travels at the speed of light in vacuum, c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s, and more slowly in a dielectric. In a line fully surrounded by a dielectric of relative permittivity εr, such as a stripline buried inside a circuit board, the speed falls by √εr, and so does the wavelength. A partially surrounded line, such as a microstrip on the surface of a board, uses an effective permittivity εeff between 1 and εr, which Chapter 2 calculates.
EQ: λ0 = c / f,    λg = λ0 / √εeff
H2: Worked Example 1.1
WEP: Find the free-space wavelength at 2.45 GHz, 28 GHz, and 77 GHz, and the guided wavelength at 2.45 GHz in a stripline on a laminate with εr = 3.55.
WES: λ0 = 2.998 × 10⁸/2.45 × 10⁹ = 12.24 cm at 2.45 GHz, 10.7 mm at 28 GHz, and 3.89 mm at 77 GHz. In the stripline, λg = 12.24/√3.55 = 12.24/1.884 = 6.49 cm. A Wi-Fi signal's wavelength on the board is the size of a credit card; a 77 GHz automotive radar's wavelength in air is smaller than a fingernail.
P: A common rule of thumb treats a circuit as lumped when its largest dimension is below about one tenth of the guided wavelength. A 1 cm trace in the stripline above reaches that limit at the frequency where λg = 10 cm, which is 2.998 × 10⁸/(1.884 × 0.10) = 1.6 GHz. Below that, Book 1's circuit laws work with small corrections. Above it, the trace must be treated as a line with a characteristic impedance and a delay.
KEY: Key idea. A wire is a wire only when it is short compared with a wavelength. Shorter wavelength is not a new subject; it is the same subject with less forgiveness.
H2: 1.2 Decibels and dBm
P: Radio engineering adds and subtracts in decibels because gains and losses multiply along a chain and span many orders of magnitude. A power ratio in decibels is 10 log₁₀(P₂/P₁). Absolute power is expressed relative to one milliwatt: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW). In a 50 Ω system, 0 dBm is 0.224 V rms, and every 20 dB is a factor of ten in voltage. Antenna gain relative to an isotropic radiator is written dBi. A decibel figure is always a ratio; adding two dBm values is meaningless, while adding a dBm value and a dB gain gives a dBm result.
H2: Worked Example 1.2
WEP: A transmitter delivers 20 dBm. It passes through a cable with 3 dB of loss and an antenna with 6 dBi of gain. Find the power at the antenna input in milliwatts and the effective isotropic radiated power in dBm. Then express −100 dBm as a voltage across 50 Ω.
WES: 20 dBm is 100 mW. After the cable, 20 − 3 = 17 dBm, which is 50 mW. The radiated power referred to an isotropic antenna is 17 + 6 = 23 dBm. For the received signal, −100 dBm is 10⁻¹³ W, and V = √(PR) = √(10⁻¹³ × 50) = 2.24 µV rms. A receiver works with microvolts while the transmitter beside it handles volts, a range of 120 dB that every layout decision must respect.
H2: 1.3 Parts that are not what they say
P: Every real component carries parasitic elements that a schematic leaves out, and at radio frequencies those parasitics decide what the part does. A capacitor in a small surface-mount package has an equivalent series inductance (ESL) of roughly 0.5 to 1 nH from its terminations and mounting. A resistor has a shunt capacitance across its body. An inductor has capacitance between its turns. Each part therefore has a self-resonant frequency (SRF), above which it behaves as its opposite.
EQ: fSRF = 1 / (2π √(ESL × C))
H2: Worked Example 1.3
WEP: A 10 pF capacitor in an 0603 package has 0.8 nH of ESL as mounted. Find its self-resonant frequency and its net reactance at 2.45 GHz.
WES: fSRF = 1/(2π √(0.8 × 10⁻⁹ × 10 × 10⁻¹²)) = 1.78 GHz. At 2.45 GHz the capacitive reactance is 1/(2π × 2.45 GHz × 10 pF) = 6.50 Ω and the inductive reactance is 2π × 2.45 GHz × 0.8 nH = 12.32 Ω, so the net reactance is +5.82 Ω: the part is an inductor of about 0.38 nH. As a DC block that is harmless; in a matching network it is a different component from the one drawn. RF designers choose capacitors by their SRF, often using a value whose series resonance falls at the operating frequency, where the part is nearly a short circuit.
H2: 1.4 The plan of this book
P: Chapter 2 develops the transmission line, reflection, and standing waves. Chapters 3 and 4 match one impedance to another, first with lumped components and then with lines and the Smith chart, which Book 1 introduced and this book uses as a working tool. Chapter 5 describes networks by their scattering parameters, the language of every RF datasheet and network analyzer. Chapter 6 builds resonators, filters, and waveguide. Chapter 7 treats the antenna, and Chapter 8 the array and the millimeter-wave link. Chapters 9 to 19 extend the toolkit: couplers, dividers, and hybrids; microstrip and stripline design; broadband matching and the Bode-Fano limit; cavities and dielectric resonators; small antennas, monopoles, loops, and horns; propagation beyond free space; ferrites, circulators, and isolators; antenna measurement; noise in passive networks; a bandpass filter carried from specification to layout; and RF switches. Part II, Chapters 20 to 40, turns to the active radio: amplifiers, receivers, oscillators, synthesizers, and the measurements that prove them. Transmitter power, power-amplifier design, and receiver noise belong to Part II; laminate properties at 10 GHz, the package, and emissions limits belong to Book 7. This book supplies the passive structure that connects them, and it owns the antenna gains that every link budget in the series uses.
