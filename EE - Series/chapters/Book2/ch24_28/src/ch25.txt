H1: Chapter 25 — State Feedback and Observers
P: Chapters 17 and 18 shaped a loop by looking at one signal, the error, and filtering it with proportional, integral, and derivative terms. This chapter looks at the plant from the inside instead. A motor or a moving mass has a small number of internal quantities, its states, whose present values completely determine what it will do next. Feeding every state back with its own gain lets a designer place the closed-loop poles exactly where they are wanted, and an observer supplies the states that no sensor measures. The method is the foundation of modern control, and for the second-order plants in this book it turns out to be a precise form of something already familiar.
H2: 25.1 States and the State Equation
P: A state is a variable whose current value, together with the future inputs, is enough to predict the system's future. For a mass m pushed by a force u along a frictionless track, position x and velocity v are the states: knowing both at one instant, and the force from then on, fixes the motion. Newton's law gives two first-order equations, which are collected into a vector form.
EQ: ẋ = v,   v̇ = u/m      →      d/dt [x; v] = A [x; v] + B u,   A = [0 1; 0 0],   B = [0; 1/m]
P: Every linear plant in this book can be written as ż = Az + Bu with an output y = Cz. The poles of the open-loop plant are the eigenvalues of A, the roots of det(sI − A) = 0. For the mass both eigenvalues are zero: a double integrator, which drifts away from any position under the smallest constant force.
H2: 25.2 Pole Placement
P: State feedback makes the input a weighted sum of all the states, u = −Kz + k_r·r, where K is a row of gains and r is the reference. Substituting gives ż = (A − BK)z + Bk_r·r, so the closed-loop poles are the eigenvalues of A − BK. For a second-order plant the characteristic polynomial has two free coefficients and K has two gains, so any pair of poles can be reached. For the mass, with u = −k₁x − k₂v + k₁r, the closed-loop polynomial is s² + (k₂/m)s + k₁/m, and matching it to the standard second-order form s² + 2ζωₙs + ωₙ² gives the gains directly.
EQ: k₁ = m ωₙ²,    k₂ = 2 ζ ωₙ m
WE: Worked Example 25.1
PROB: A 2 kg carriage on an air-bearing track is driven by a linear motor. Choose state-feedback gains that give ωₙ = 10 rad/s and ζ = 0.7, and predict the step response.
SOL: k₁ = 2 × 10² = 200 N/m and k₂ = 2 × 0.7 × 10 × 2 = 28 N·s/m. The closed-loop polynomial is s² + 14s + 100. A damping ratio of 0.7 gives an overshoot of 100·exp(−ζπ/√(1 − ζ²)) = 4.6%. A simulation of the loop settles to within 2% in 0.60 s; the familiar 4/(ζωₙ) estimate gives 0.57 s. The reference gain k_r = k₁ makes the steady-state position equal r exactly, because at rest v = 0 and the force −k₁x + k₁r must be zero.

KEY: For a plant whose states are a position and its velocity, state feedback is PD control with a measured derivative. || The gain on position is the proportional gain and the gain on velocity is the derivative gain, but the velocity comes from a sensor or an estimator instead of from differentiating a noisy error signal.
P: The same algebra works when the plant has its own dynamics. A small DC motor driving a light load is well approximated by θ̈ = −(1/τ)θ̇ + (K/τ)u, with mechanical time constant τ and gain K from volts to steady speed. State feedback u = −k₁θ − k₂θ̇ + k₁r produces the closed-loop polynomial s² + (1 + Kk₂)/τ · s + Kk₁/τ.
WE: Worked Example 25.2
PROB: A motor has τ = 50 ms and K = 20 (rad/s)/V. Find position and velocity gains that give ωₙ = 40 rad/s and ζ = 0.8.
SOL: The target polynomial is s² + 64s + 1600. Matching the constant term, Kk₁/τ = 1600, so k₁ = 1600 × 0.05/20 = 4.0 V/rad. Matching the middle term, (1 + 20k₂)/0.05 = 64, so 20k₂ = 2.2 and k₂ = 0.11 V/(rad/s). The roots of s² + 64s + 1600 are −32 ± j24, which have magnitude 40 and damping 32/40 = 0.8, as required.
H2: 25.3 Controllability
P: Pole placement works only if the input can influence every state. A plant is controllable when the matrix [B AB A²B …], built from as many columns as there are states, has full rank. For the carriage, B = [0; 1/m] and AB = [1/m; 0], which are independent, so the plant is controllable. A counterexample is two identical carriages driven by the same command: their difference is never affected by the input, the controllability matrix loses rank, and no choice of gains can move the poles that describe that difference. The practical message is to check controllability before spending effort on gains, because a plant that fails it needs another actuator, not a better controller.
H2: 25.4 Observers: Estimating What Is Not Measured
P: State feedback needs every state, and most systems measure only some. An encoder gives position but not velocity, and differentiating encoder counts amplifies their quantization into a noisy velocity. An observer runs a model of the plant in software and corrects it continuously with the measured output.
EQ: dẑ/dt = A ẑ + B u + L (y − C ẑ)
P: The estimation error e = z − ẑ obeys ė = (A − LC)e, so the observer gain L places the error poles in exactly the way K places the control poles. For the carriage with position measured, C = [1 0] and L = [l₁; l₂], and the error polynomial is s² + l₁s + l₂. Observer poles are usually placed three to five times faster than the control poles, so that the estimate converges before the controller depends on it, but not so fast that the observer passes sensor noise straight through.
WE: Worked Example 25.3
PROB: Design a position-measuring observer for the carriage of Worked Example 25.1 with observer poles at ωₙ = 40 rad/s, ζ = 0.7.
SOL: The target error polynomial is s² + 2 × 0.7 × 40 s + 40² = s² + 56s + 1600, so l₁ = 56 s⁻¹ and l₂ = 1600 s⁻². The roots are −28 ± j28.6, four times the natural frequency of the control poles. The controller then uses u = −200x̂ − 28v̂ + 200r with the estimated states.
P: The separation principle makes this combination safe for linear plants: the closed-loop poles of controller plus observer are the control poles together with the observer poles, each set placed independently. What separation does not protect is robustness. A model that is wrong, for example a mass that doubles when the carriage picks up a load, shifts both sets of poles, and the margins of Chapter 18 must still be checked on the complete loop.
MIS: An observer is not a filter that can be made arbitrarily fast for free. Faster observer poles mean larger gains on the measurement error y − Cẑ, and every bit of sensor noise and quantization in y passes into the state estimate scaled by L. The observer bandwidth is a trade between tracking a changing state and rejecting a noisy sensor.
H2: 25.5 Integral Action and Disturbances
P: State feedback on position and velocity has the same weakness as PD control: a constant disturbance force leaves a constant error. In the steady state the feedback force must cancel the disturbance d, so k₁x = d and the error is d/k₁. The cure is to add a third state, the integral of the position error z_i = ∫(r − x)dt, and feed it back with a gain k_i. The closed-loop polynomial becomes third order, s³ + (k₂/m)s² + (k₁/m)s + k_i/m, and the extra pole is placed alongside the others.
WE: Worked Example 25.4
PROB: A constant 5 N force acts on the 2 kg carriage of Worked Example 25.1. Find the steady error without integral action. Then add an integral state and choose gains that keep the poles of Worked Example 25.1 and add a real pole at −10 s⁻¹.
SOL: Without integral action the error is 5/200 = 25 mm, permanently. With the integral state, the target polynomial is (s² + 14s + 100)(s + 10) = s³ + 24s² + 240s + 1000. Matching coefficients with m = 2 gives k₂ = 48 N·s/m, k₁ = 480 N/m, and k_i = 2000 N/(m·s). A simulation with the 5 N force applied at t = 0 shows a peak deflection of 8.5 mm, after which the integral state builds up exactly 5 N of opposing force and the error returns to zero.
P: The integral state needs the anti-windup protection of Chapter 18. When the actuator saturates, the integrator must stop accumulating, or it will drive a large overshoot when the actuator comes back into its linear range.
H2: 25.6 Gains Meet Actuators
P: Pole placement will return any gains asked of it, and the actuator then has to deliver them. A step in the reference demands an immediate force k₁·Δr. For the carriage with k₁ = 200 N/m, a 0.1 m step asks for 20 N at the first instant. If the motor is rated for 10 N, the loop saturates and the response is no longer the linear one designed. Two remedies keep the design honest: shape the reference into a ramp or S-curve that the actuator can follow, or choose slower poles. Pushing poles further left buys speed only until saturation, measurement noise, or unmodeled dynamics such as a flexible mount, a current-loop delay, or a sample period becomes the limit, and the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR), the usual next step beyond pole placement, chooses gains by weighing state error against control effort for exactly this reason.
PP: A 0.5 kg mass on a frictionless track is to have ωₙ = 20 rad/s and ζ = 0.7 under state feedback. Find k₁ and k₂.
PP: For the gains of the previous problem, find the steady-state error caused by a constant 2 N disturbance force without integral action.
PP: A motor with τ = 0.1 s and K = 10 (rad/s)/V is to have closed-loop poles with ωₙ = 30 rad/s and ζ = 1. Find k₁ and k₂.
PP: An observer for a double integrator measured in position is to have both error poles at −50 s⁻¹. Find l₁ and l₂.
PP: The carriage of Worked Example 25.1 is commanded with a 0.03 m step. Find the initial force demanded, and state whether a 10 N actuator saturates.
PP: Explain in a sentence or two why measuring velocity directly or estimating it with an observer is preferable to differentiating the encoder position.
SOLN: k₁ = 0.5 × 20² = 200 N/m; k₂ = 2 × 0.7 × 20 × 0.5 = 14 N·s/m.
SOLN: Error = d/k₁ = 2/200 = 10 mm.
SOLN: Target s² + 60s + 900. Kk₁/τ = 900 gives k₁ = 900 × 0.1/10 = 9.0 V/rad. (1 + 10k₂)/0.1 = 60 gives 10k₂ = 5 and k₂ = 0.5 V/(rad/s).
SOLN: (s + 50)² = s² + 100s + 2500, so l₁ = 100 s⁻¹ and l₂ = 2500 s⁻².
SOLN: k₁ × Δr = 200 × 0.03 = 6 N, within the 10 N rating, so the actuator does not saturate.
SOLN: Differentiating position amplifies high-frequency noise in proportion to frequency, and with an encoder it turns each count of quantization into a spike of size one count per sample period. A tachometer or an observer gives a velocity whose bandwidth is chosen by design.
SUM: A plant's states determine its future; linear plants are written ż = Az + Bu, y = Cz, with open-loop poles at the eigenvalues of A. State feedback u = −Kz + k_r·r places the closed-loop poles at the eigenvalues of A − BK; for a mass, k₁ = mωₙ² and k₂ = 2ζωₙm, which is PD control with a measured derivative. Pole placement requires controllability. An observer dẑ/dt = Aẑ + Bu + L(y − Cẑ) estimates unmeasured states with error poles set by L, typically three to five times faster than the control poles, and the separation principle lets the two be designed independently. An integral state removes steady error from constant disturbances. Actuator limits and noise, not algebra, bound how fast the poles can be placed.
GLOS: State || One of the minimum set of internal variables whose present values, with future inputs, determine a system's future behavior.

GLOS: Observer || A software model of the plant corrected by the measured output, used to estimate states that are not measured.
