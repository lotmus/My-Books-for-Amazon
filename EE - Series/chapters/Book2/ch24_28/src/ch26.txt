H1: Chapter 26 — The Sample-and-Hold Circuit
P: Chapter 19 treated the hold as a mathematical step: a value is captured and held as a staircase. This chapter opens the box. A sample-and-hold is a switch, a capacitor, and a buffer, and each of those three parts limits the accuracy of every analog-to-digital converter built on it. The useful skill is to put a number on each limit, compare it with one least significant bit (LSB), and choose the hold capacitor that balances them.
H2: 26.1 The Circuit and Its Two Modes
P: In track mode the switch is closed and the capacitor voltage follows the input through the switch's on-resistance and the driving source's output resistance. At the hold command the switch opens and the capacitor keeps the last value while the converter measures it, through a buffer whose input draws almost no current. Most modern converters use the converter's own capacitor array as the hold capacitor, so the circuit is inside the chip, but its limits still appear in the datasheet and still dictate how the input must be driven.
P: The errors fall into four groups: how long tracking takes to settle (acquisition), what happens at the instant the switch opens (pedestal and aperture effects), what happens during the hold (droop and feedthrough), and the noise frozen onto the capacitor. Nearly every one of them improves with a larger capacitor except acquisition, which gets worse. That single tension sets the design.
H2: 26.2 Acquisition Time
P: While tracking, the capacitor charges through the total series resistance R as a single RC network. After an input step, the remaining error falls as exp(−t/RC). To settle within half an LSB of an N-bit converter, the error must fall by a factor of 2^(N+1).
EQ: t_acq = R C ln(2^(N+1)) = R C (N + 1) ln 2
WE: Worked Example 26.1
PROB: A 12-bit converter's hold capacitor is 100 pF, the switch has 100 Ω on-resistance, and the source driving it has 50 Ω output resistance. Find the time constant and the acquisition time to ½ LSB.
SOL: R = 150 Ω and RC = 150 × 100 pF = 15 ns. The settling factor is ln(2¹³) = 9.01, so t_acq = 9.01 × 15 ns = 135 ns. At 16 bits the factor rises to ln(2¹⁷) = 11.8 and the time to 177 ns. Each extra bit adds 0.69 time constants, so resolution is cheap in settling time; it is expensive in everything else below.
P: The same formula sets the driver's requirement. An op-amp driving the hold capacitor must itself settle within the acquisition window, and its closed-loop output resistance and bandwidth from Chapter 13 are part of R. A driver that slews during a full-scale step loses time before the exponential settling even begins.
H2: 26.3 kT/C Noise
P: Any resistor charging a capacitor leaves a random voltage on it when the switch opens. Integrating the resistor's thermal noise over the RC bandwidth gives a result that does not depend on the resistance at all: a larger resistor is noisier but narrows the bandwidth by exactly the same factor.
EQ: v_n,rms = √(kT / C)
WE: Worked Example 26.2
PROB: Find the sampled noise at 290 K for hold capacitors of 1 pF, 10 pF, and 100 pF, and compare each with the LSB of a 16-bit converter spanning 5 V.
SOL: With k = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K, kT = 4.00 × 10⁻²¹ J. The noise is 63 µV rms at 1 pF, 20 µV at 10 pF, and 6.3 µV at 100 pF. The 16-bit LSB is 5/65,536 = 76 µV. A 1 pF capacitor adds noise comparable to an LSB on every sample, and 10 pF is marginal; precision 16- and 18-bit converters use tens of picofarads or more for this reason.
KEY: kT/C noise depends only on the capacitor and the temperature. || The resistance that charges a hold capacitor cannot be chosen to reduce the noise frozen on it; only a larger capacitor, or averaging several samples, does that.
H2: 26.4 What Happens When the Switch Opens
P: A MOSFET switch holds charge in its channel while it is on. When it turns off, part of that charge is pushed onto the hold capacitor, and the gate-drive edge couples more through the gate-to-drain capacitance. The result is a step, the pedestal, of size ΔV = Q/C. It is largely constant and appears as an offset, but the channel charge depends on the input voltage, so a residual signal-dependent part appears as gain error and distortion. Differential sampling, where two matched switches inject nearly equal charge into two capacitors and the difference cancels, removes most of it.
EQ: ΔV_pedestal = Q_inj / C
P: The switch also does not open at a perfectly defined instant. Random variation in the sampling instant, aperture jitter, converts a changing signal's slope into voltage error. For a full-scale sine wave of frequency f and an rms jitter σ_t, the best achievable signal-to-noise ratio is set by the jitter alone.
EQ: SNR_jitter = −20 log₁₀(2π f σ_t)
WE: Worked Example 26.3
PROB: An ideal 12-bit converter has SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 = 74.0 dB. Find the largest rms jitter that keeps the jitter-limited SNR at this level for input frequencies of 10 MHz and 100 MHz.
SOL: Setting −20 log₁₀(2πfσ_t) = 74.0 gives 2πfσ_t = 10^(−74.0/20) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴. At 10 MHz, σ_t = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴/(2π × 10⁷) = 3.2 ps. At 100 MHz, σ_t = 0.32 ps. A 1 ps clock at 100 MHz input limits the converter to 64 dB, about 10.3 effective bits, no matter how good the rest of it is. At audio frequencies the same arithmetic allows nanoseconds, which is why jitter is a radio-frequency and high-speed-converter problem.
H2: 26.5 Droop and Feedthrough During Hold
P: During the hold, leakage currents from the switch, the buffer input, and the board discharge or charge the capacitor at a constant rate, droop = I_leak/C. Signal also couples across the open switch through its off-capacitance, so that a large, fast input partly appears on the held value; this hold-mode feedthrough is quoted in decibels and improves with a larger hold capacitor in the ratio C_off/(C_off + C).
WE: Worked Example 26.4
PROB: A discrete sample-and-hold for a 16-bit, 5 V system uses a 1 nF C0G capacitor charged through 50 Ω (switch plus driver). The switch injects 2 pC and total leakage is 100 pA. The converter takes 5 µs to measure the held value. Find the acquisition time, the noise, the pedestal, and the droop over the conversion, and compare each with the 76 µV LSB.
SOL: Acquisition: RC = 50 ns, and 11.8 × 50 ns = 0.59 µs. Noise: √(kT/C) = 2.0 µV rms, negligible. Pedestal: 2 pC/1 nF = 2 mV, about 26 LSB, but almost entirely a constant offset that calibration removes. Droop: 100 pA/1 nF = 0.1 V/s, which over 5 µs is 0.5 µV, also negligible. With 100 pF instead, acquisition would shrink to 59 ns, but the pedestal would grow to 20 mV, the droop to 1 V/s (5 µV over the conversion), and the noise to 6.3 µV. The larger capacitor is the right choice whenever the sample rate allows its acquisition time. The C0G dielectric matters as much as the value, for the reasons given in Chapter 23.

H2: 26.6 Driving a Switched-Capacitor Converter Input
P: A successive-approximation converter samples onto its internal capacitor array, often 20–60 pF. At the start of each acquisition that array still holds the previous sample, so connecting it to the input is a step of charge sharing, not a gentle tracking. The standard input network is a small resistor and a large capacitor between the driving op-amp and the converter pin. The external capacitor supplies the sudden charge, and the op-amp then restores the external capacitor within the acquisition time.
WE: Worked Example 26.5
PROB: A 16-bit SAR converter spanning 5 V has a 30 pF sampling array. An external 2.7 nF capacitor sits at its input. Find the worst-case charge-sharing kick and how many time constants the driver network needs to recover it to ½ LSB.
SOL: In the worst case the array held 0 V and the input is at 5 V. Sharing charge drops the input by 5 × 30/(2700 + 30) = 55 mV, which is about 1,440 times the ½-LSB level of 38 µV. Recovering requires ln(1,440) = 7.3 time constants of the external RC and driver within the acquisition window. A larger external capacitor reduces the kick but slows recovery and loads the op-amp's output, and Chapter 12 showed what a capacitive load does to the phase margin; the series resistor exists to isolate it. Datasheets of precision converters recommend specific values because the trade is particular to each array.
MIS: Sampling a slow signal does not make the input-driving problem go away. A 1 Hz thermocouple voltage still meets the switched capacitor's charge kick once every conversion. A high-impedance source without a driver amplifier and input capacitor cannot recover from it, and the result is a gain error that looks like a calibration problem.
PP: A 14-bit converter's hold capacitor is charged through 200 Ω total and is 20 pF. Find the acquisition time to ½ LSB.

PP: Explain in a sentence or two why a larger hold capacitor is not always better.
SOLN: ln(2¹⁵) = 10.4; RC = 200 × 20 pF = 4 ns; t_acq = 41.6 ns.
SOLN: √(4.00 × 10⁻²¹/5 × 10⁻¹²) = 28 µV rms.
SOLN: 0.5 pC/50 pF = 10 mV.
SOLN: 2πfσ_t = 10^(−86/20) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵, so σ_t = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵/(2π × 5 × 10⁶) = 1.6 ps.
SOLN: 1 nA/470 pF = 2.13 V/s; over 2 µs the change is 4.3 µV.
SOLN: Acquisition time grows in proportion to C, and the driver must charge it, so a large capacitor limits the sample rate and burdens the driving amplifier; the best value is the smallest one that brings noise, pedestal, droop, and feedthrough below the error budget.
SUM: A sample-and-hold is a switch, a capacitor, and a buffer. Tracking settles as an RC network, needing (N + 1) ln 2 time constants to reach ½ LSB. The capacitor freezes kT/C noise independent of resistance. Opening the switch injects a pedestal Q/C, and aperture jitter limits SNR to −20 log₁₀(2πfσ_t). During hold, leakage causes droop I/C and the off-switch passes feedthrough. A larger capacitor improves every error except acquisition time. Switched-capacitor converters draw a charge-sharing kick each sample, so they need a driver amplifier with an isolating resistor and an external charge reservoir.
GLOS: Acquisition time || The time a sample-and-hold needs, after a step, to track the input to within a stated error before it can hold.
GLOS: Pedestal || The step in held voltage caused by charge injected from the switch as it turns off, ΔV = Q/C.
GLOS: Aperture jitter || Random variation in the sampling instant, which converts signal slope into voltage error.
GLOS: Droop || The steady change in a held voltage caused by leakage current, at a rate I_leak/C.
GLOS: kT/C noise || The rms noise voltage √(kT/C) frozen onto a capacitor when it is disconnected from a resistive source.
FORM: Acquisition to ½ LSB || t_acq = RC (N + 1) ln 2
FORM: Sampled noise || v_n = √(kT/C)
