H1: Chapter 27 — The Voltage Reference and the Bandgap
P: Every converter, regulator, and calibrated measurement in this book compares something against a voltage that is assumed to be known. That voltage comes from a reference, and the reference's errors pass straight into every result measured against it. This chapter explains how a stable voltage is made from parts that all drift with temperature, the bandgap circuit, and then reads a reference datasheet the way an error budget needs it read. The device physics behind the base-emitter voltage belongs to Book 3; here the junction is used as a part with two well-behaved properties.
H2: 27.1 Two Voltages That Drift in Opposite Directions
P: A forward-biased base-emitter junction at a fixed current has a voltage V_BE of about 0.65 V at room temperature, and it falls by roughly 2 mV for each kelvin of temperature rise. It is complementary to absolute temperature (CTAT). The difference between the V_BE values of two junctions run at different current densities behaves the other way. If one transistor has N times the emitter area of the other and both carry the same current, the difference is exactly proportional to absolute temperature (PTAT), because the logarithmic diode law leaves only the thermal voltage kT/q multiplied by a ratio of areas.
EQ: ΔV_BE = (kT/q) ln N,    d(ΔV_BE)/dT = (k/q) ln N
P: The PTAT slope does not depend on process details, transistor size, or current, only on the area ratio and fundamental constants. That is the property that makes the bandgap possible: one voltage with a known negative slope, one with a known positive slope, and a resistor ratio to weigh them.
WE: Worked Example 27.1
PROB: Two transistors with an area ratio N = 8 carry equal currents at 300 K. Find ΔV_BE and its temperature coefficient.
SOL: kT/q at 300 K is 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 300/1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 25.85 mV, and ln 8 = 2.079, so ΔV_BE = 53.8 mV. The coefficient is (k/q) ln 8 = 86.17 µV/K × 2.079 = 0.179 mV/K. The value at −40 °C is 41.8 mV and at 85 °C is 64.2 mV, a straight line through zero at absolute zero.
H2: 27.2 Adding Them: The Bandgap Voltage
P: To cancel a V_BE slope of −2 mV/K, the PTAT voltage must be multiplied by a gain K such that K × 0.179 mV/K = 2 mV/K, and the reference is V_REF = V_BE + K·ΔV_BE. The sum comes out close to 1.2 V regardless of the details, because it extrapolates to the bandgap energy of silicon, about 1.2 eV, divided by the electron charge. That coincidence gives the circuit its name.
EQ: V_REF = V_BE + K (kT/q) ln N,    K = |dV_BE/dT| / ((k/q) ln N)
WE: Worked Example 27.2
PROB: Using V_BE = 0.65 V with a slope of −2.0 mV/K and the N = 8 pair of Worked Example 27.1, find the gain K that gives zero slope at 300 K and the resulting reference voltage.
SOL: K = 2.0/0.179 = 11.16. The PTAT term contributes 11.16 × 53.8 mV = 0.600 V, so V_REF = 0.65 + 0.60 = 1.25 V. A larger area ratio reduces the gain needed but costs silicon area; N = 24 would need K = 7.30.

H2: 27.3 A Working Circuit
P: One common arrangement uses an op-amp to force equal voltages at the top of two equal resistors R₂, so that both branches carry the same current I. The left branch ends in a unit transistor Q₁. The right branch passes through a resistor R₁ and then an N-times transistor Q₂. Because the op-amp holds the two branches at the same voltage, the extra V_BE of Q₁ appears across R₁: I·R₁ = ΔV_BE. The current is therefore PTAT, and the output, measured at the top of the left branch, is V_BE₁ + I·R₂.
EQ: I = ΔV_BE / R₁,    V_REF = V_BE₁ + (R₂/R₁) ΔV_BE
WE: Worked Example 27.3
PROB: Design the resistors for the circuit above with N = 8 and a branch current of 10 µA at 300 K, using K = 11.16 from Worked Example 27.2.
SOL: R₁ = ΔV_BE/I = 53.8 mV/10 µA = 5.38 kΩ, and R₂ = K·R₁ = 11.16 × 5.38 kΩ = 60.0 kΩ. The output is 0.65 + 10 µA × 60 kΩ = 1.25 V. Only the ratio R₂/R₁ enters the output, so integrated references use matched resistors of the same material, and their absolute tolerance, often ±20%, does not matter. The op-amp's offset does: it adds to ΔV_BE and is multiplied by K, so a 1 mV offset shifts the output by about 11 mV and adds a slope of its own.
KEY: A bandgap reference is accurate because it depends on ratios. || The PTAT slope depends on an area ratio, and the gain on a resistor ratio, so the circuit can be made stable from parts whose absolute values vary widely.
P: The cancellation is exact at only one temperature. V_BE is not a perfectly straight line; it bends by a few millivolts over the industrial range, and the residual output looks like a shallow parabola. Uncompensated bandgaps reach 20–50 ppm/°C; curvature-corrected designs reach 2–5 ppm/°C, and the best buried-zener references, which use a subsurface zener breakdown instead of a bandgap and run from higher supplies, reach below 1 ppm/°C with lower noise.
H2: 27.4 Reading a Reference Datasheet
P: A reference's temperature coefficient is normally specified by the box method: the difference between the highest and lowest output over the temperature range, divided by the nominal voltage and by the width of the range. It describes a box that the curve fits inside, not a slope at any one temperature.
EQ: TC (ppm/°C) = (V_max − V_min) / (V_nom × (T_max − T_min)) × 10⁶
WE: Worked Example 27.4
PROB: A 1.25 V reference varies by 1.5 mV between −40 °C and 85 °C. Find its box-method temperature coefficient.
SOL: TC = 1.5 × 10⁻³/(1.25 × 125) × 10⁶ = 9.6 ppm/°C. Because the curve is a parabola, the actual change between 20 °C and 30 °C may be far smaller than 9.6 × 10 = 96 ppm; the box guarantees only the worst case over the full range.
P: The other specifications that enter a budget are initial accuracy (the tolerance at 25 °C, removable by calibration), line regulation (change with supply voltage), load regulation (change with output current), output noise (usually given peak-to-peak from 0.1 to 10 Hz, because low-frequency noise cannot be filtered without impractically large capacitors), long-term drift in ppm per 1,000 hours, and thermal hysteresis, the shift that remains after a temperature cycle. Hysteresis and long-term drift cannot be calibrated out once, which is why precision instruments re-calibrate.
WE: Worked Example 27.5
PROB: A 12-bit converter uses a 2.5 V reference rated at 0.1% initial accuracy, 10 ppm/°C, and 10 µV peak-to-peak noise. The instrument operates from 25 °C to 75 °C. Express each error in LSB.
SOL: One LSB is 2.5/4096 = 610 µV, or 244 ppm of the reference. Initial accuracy: 0.1% is 2.5 mV, or 4.1 LSB, removed by a one-time calibration. Temperature: 10 ppm/°C × 50 °C = 500 ppm, which is 1.25 mV or 2.0 LSB, not removable by a single room-temperature calibration. Noise: 10 µV is 0.016 LSB, negligible. The temperature coefficient dominates the uncalibrated budget, and a 12-bit system that must stay within 1 LSB over this range needs a reference better than about 5 ppm/°C.
MIS: A 0.1% reference does not make a 0.1% instrument. Initial accuracy is the specification that matters least once the system is calibrated. Temperature drift, hysteresis, and long-term drift remain after calibration, and over a wide temperature range they usually exceed the initial tolerance.
H2: 27.5 Series and Shunt References
P: A series reference behaves like a precision regulator: it sits between supply and load and supplies the load current. A shunt reference behaves like a precision zener: a resistor from the supply feeds it, and it sinks whatever current the load does not take, so the resistor must deliver the load current plus the reference's minimum operating current at the lowest supply voltage.
EQ: R_s ≤ (V_s,min − V_REF) / (I_load,max + I_K,min)
WE: Worked Example 27.6
PROB: A 2.5 V shunt reference needs at least 0.5 mA to regulate. The supply ranges from 4.5 V to 5.5 V, and the load draws 0 to 1 mA. Choose the series resistor and find the maximum reference current and resistor dissipation.
SOL: R_s ≤ (4.5 − 2.5)/(1.0 + 0.5) mA = 1.33 kΩ; choose 1.30 kΩ. At 4.5 V and full load the reference receives 2.0/1.30 kΩ − 1 mA = 0.54 mA, above its minimum. At 5.5 V with no load it must sink 3.0/1.30 kΩ = 2.31 mA, dissipating 2.5 × 2.31 = 5.8 mW, and the resistor dissipates 3.0²/1300 = 6.9 mW. Shunt references are simple and tolerate any supply, but they waste the full current at all times, which is why battery equipment prefers series references.

PP: A 16-bit converter uses a 4.096 V reference rated at 3 ppm/°C. Find the drift in LSB over a 40 °C temperature change.
PP: Explain in a sentence or two why an op-amp offset inside a bandgap circuit is multiplied by roughly K at the output.
SOLN: ln 24 = 3.178. ΔV_BE = 25.85 mV × 3.178 = 82.2 mV; the coefficient is 86.17 µV/K × 3.178 = 0.274 mV/K.
SOLN: K = 2.0/0.274 = 7.30.
SOLN: I = 53.8 mV/10.0 kΩ = 5.38 µA; R₂ = 11.16 × 10.0 kΩ = 112 kΩ.
SOLN: TC = 0.8 × 10⁻³/(2.048 × 70) × 10⁶ = 5.6 ppm/°C.
SOLN: Drift = 3 × 40 = 120 ppm. One LSB is 1/65,536 = 15.3 ppm, so the drift is 7.9 LSB.
SOLN: The offset adds in series with ΔV_BE across R₁, so it changes the PTAT current in the same way ΔV_BE does, and the output picks it up through the same R₂/R₁ gain that multiplies ΔV_BE.
SUM: A base-emitter voltage falls about 2 mV/K (CTAT), while the difference between two junctions at different current densities, (kT/q) ln N, rises in exact proportion to temperature (PTAT). Weighting the PTAT term by K = |dV_BE/dT|/((k/q) ln N) and adding it to V_BE gives a reference near 1.2 V with zero slope at one temperature. An op-amp bandgap sets I = ΔV_BE/R₁ and V_REF = V_BE + (R₂/R₁)ΔV_BE, so only ratios matter, while op-amp offset is amplified by K. Datasheet temperature coefficients use the box method. In an error budget, initial accuracy is calibrated away, but temperature drift, hysteresis, and long-term drift remain. A shunt reference's resistor must supply full load plus minimum current at the lowest supply.
GLOS: PTAT || Proportional to absolute temperature; describes ΔV_BE between two junctions at different current densities.
GLOS: CTAT || Complementary to absolute temperature; describes a quantity that falls as temperature rises, such as a base-emitter voltage at fixed current.
GLOS: Bandgap reference || A voltage reference that adds a PTAT voltage to a base-emitter voltage so that their temperature slopes cancel, producing about 1.2 V.
GLOS: Box method || Specifying a reference's temperature coefficient as total output variation divided by nominal voltage and temperature span.
GLOS: Shunt reference || A two-terminal reference fed through a resistor that regulates by sinking the current the load does not take.
FORM: PTAT voltage || ΔV_BE = (kT/q) ln N
FORM: Bandgap output || V_REF = V_BE + (R₂/R₁)ΔV_BE
FORM: Box-method TC || TC = ΔV/(V_nom ΔT) × 10⁶ ppm/°C
