H1: Chapter 13 — Noise Gain, Bandwidth, and Slew Rate
P: An op-amp circuit that works perfectly at 100 Hz can quietly lose accuracy at 5 kHz and distort badly at 20 kHz, with no change to its schematic. Two separate limits are responsible. One is small-signal: the open-loop gain falls with frequency, so the loop has less gain to spend on accuracy. The other is large-signal: the output can only move so many volts per microsecond. This chapter turns both limits into numbers you can compute before choosing a part.
H2: 13.1 The Declared Op-Amp
P: This book uses one declared open-loop shape, first given in Chapter 5, and it is worth restating as the starting point for every calculation here. The DC gain is A₀ = 100,000. A single dominant pole sits at f_p = 10 Hz. Above that pole the gain falls at 20 dB per decade:
EQ: A(f) = A₀ / (1 + j f/f_p),   |A(f)| ≈ GBW / f for f ≫ f_p
P: The gain-bandwidth product is GBW = A₀ f_p = 1 MHz. At 1 kHz the open-loop gain is about 1000; at 10 kHz it is 100; at 1 MHz it is 1. Every result in this chapter follows from that one straight line on a Bode plot.
H2: 13.2 Noise Gain Sets the Bandwidth
P: Chapter 11 showed that the closed-loop bandwidth is the gain-bandwidth product multiplied by the feedback fraction β. The reciprocal 1/β has its own name, the noise gain, because it is also the gain that the op-amp's own input noise and offset see on their way to the output. For both basic configurations it is the same expression:
EQ: NG = 1/β = 1 + R_f / Rᵢₙ,   f_cl ≈ GBW / NG
P: For a non-inverting amplifier the noise gain equals the signal gain. For an inverting amplifier it does not: an inverter with signal gain −R_f/Rᵢₙ has noise gain 1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ, one more than the magnitude of its signal gain. The bandwidth follows the noise gain, never the signal gain.
WE: Worked Example 13.1
PROB: Using the declared op-amp, find the closed-loop −3 dB bandwidth of (a) a non-inverting amplifier with gain 100, (b) an inverting amplifier with gain −1, and (c) an inverting amplifier with gain −10.
SOL: (a) NG = 100, so f_cl = 1 MHz/100 = 10 kHz. This is the one-home figure of this book: a noise gain of 100 leaves 10 kHz. (b) NG = 1 + 1 = 2, so f_cl = 500 kHz, half the follower's 1 MHz even though both have unity gain magnitude. (c) NG = 1 + 10 = 11, so f_cl = 1 MHz/11 ≈ 90.9 kHz, not the 100 kHz a careless reading of "gain 10" would suggest.

MIS: The −3 dB bandwidth is not the usable bandwidth. At f_cl the gain is already 29% low. If a design needs the gain to be right, it needs to know how far below f_cl it must stay, which is the subject of the next section.
H2: 13.3 Accuracy Below the Corner
P: With a single-pole loop, the closed-loop magnitude falls as |G(f)| = G₀/√(1 + (f/f_cl)²). Setting the fractional error to a specification and solving for frequency gives a useful rule. For a 1% magnitude error, f/f_cl = √(1/0.99² − 1) ≈ 0.142. For 0.1%, it is about 0.045. In words: to keep the gain within 1%, stay below about one seventh of the closed-loop bandwidth; to keep it within 0.1%, stay below about one twenty-second.
WE: Worked Example 13.2
PROB: A non-inverting stage with gain 10 is built on the declared op-amp. Over what frequency range does its gain magnitude stay within 1% of 10?
SOL: NG = 10, so f_cl = 1 MHz/10 = 100 kHz. The 1% limit is 0.142 × 100 kHz ≈ 14.2 kHz. A designer who reads the 100 kHz bandwidth and expects a flat response to 20 kHz audio will find the gain about 2% low at 20 kHz. Phase shift arrives sooner still: at 14.2 kHz the output lags the input by arctan(0.142) ≈ 8°, which matters if two channels must stay matched.
KEY: Bandwidth follows noise gain, and accuracy needs a factor of about seven below that bandwidth for 1%. || Compute f_cl = GBW/NG first, then divide again for the accuracy the application actually needs.
H2: 13.4 One Stage or Two
P: When a single stage cannot provide both the gain and the bandwidth, the gain can be split. Two stages of gain 10, each with f_cl = 100 kHz, give a total gain of 100. Each stage's response is single-pole, so the cascade is down 3 dB where each stage is down 1.5 dB. For n identical single-pole stages the overall corner is f_cl √(2^(1/n) − 1); for two stages that factor is 0.644, so the pair reaches about 64 kHz. One stage of gain 100 would have reached only 10 kHz. Splitting the gain bought more than six times the bandwidth, at the price of a second op-amp and a second set of offset and noise contributions.
H2: 13.5 Slew Rate and Full-Power Bandwidth
P: Everything so far assumed small signals. A real output stage can only change its voltage at a finite maximum rate, the slew rate SR, set inside the op-amp by how fast an internal current can charge its compensation capacitor. A sine wave of peak amplitude V_p and frequency f has a maximum rate of change 2πf V_p, at its zero crossings. If that exceeds the slew rate, the output cannot follow, and the sine wave turns into a triangle. The highest frequency at which a full-amplitude sine survives is the full-power bandwidth:
EQ: f_FP = SR / (2π V_p)
WE: Worked Example 13.3
PROB: A general-purpose op-amp has a slew rate of 0.5 V/µs. Find its full-power bandwidth for a 10 V peak output and for a 1 V peak output.
SOL: For 10 V peak, f_FP = 0.5×10⁶/(2π × 10) ≈ 7.96 kHz. For 1 V peak, f_FP ≈ 79.6 kHz. The same op-amp in the same circuit has a small-signal bandwidth of, say, 100 kHz, but cannot deliver a full 10 V sine above about 8 kHz. Which limit applies depends on the amplitude, not on the circuit.
MIS: Raising the closed-loop bandwidth does not raise the slew rate. Slew rate is a large-signal property of the op-amp's internal circuitry, independent of the feedback network. Lowering the noise gain helps small signals; only a different op-amp, or a smaller output swing, helps a slew-limited one.
H2: 13.6 A Short Design Procedure
P: Given a gain, a signal frequency range, an accuracy, and an output amplitude, the selection runs in four steps. First compute the noise gain from the resistor values, not from the signal gain. Second, divide the required accuracy into a minimum f_cl using the factor from Section 13.3, and multiply by the noise gain to get a minimum GBW. Third, compute 2πf_max V_p and require a slew rate comfortably above it, commonly by a factor of two. Fourth, check that the chosen op-amp is stable at the noise gain in use, since some fast parts are "decompensated" and are stable only above a minimum noise gain.

SUM: Closed-loop bandwidth equals the gain-bandwidth product divided by the noise gain, 1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ, which equals the signal gain only for a non-inverting stage. Holding gain within 1% requires staying below about 0.14 of that bandwidth. Splitting gain across stages extends bandwidth. Slew rate is a separate large-signal limit; the full-power bandwidth SR/(2πV_p) falls as the output amplitude rises.
GLOS: Noise gain || The reciprocal of the feedback fraction, 1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ; the gain applied to the op-amp's own input noise and offset, and the divisor of GBW in the bandwidth calculation.
GLOS: Slew rate || The maximum rate at which an op-amp output can change, in volts per microsecond.
GLOS: Full-power bandwidth || The highest frequency at which an op-amp can deliver a sine of a given peak amplitude without slew-rate distortion, SR/(2πV_p).
FORM: Closed-loop bandwidth || f_cl ≈ GBW / NG,  NG = 1 + R_f/Rᵢₙ
FORM: Full-power bandwidth || f_FP = SR / (2π V_p)
