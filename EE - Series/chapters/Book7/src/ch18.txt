H1: Chapter 18. Antenna in Package
P: At millimeter wave, the antenna is small enough to sit on the package, and the interconnect between chip and antenna is lossy enough that it should. An antenna-in-package module places the transceiver die, Book 4's subject, and an array of patch antennas, Book 4's subject, in one substrate, so the radio leaves the package as a radiated beam rather than as a signal on a board trace. This chapter explains why, sizes the array, and lists the packaging problems that come with it.
H2: 18.1 Why the antenna moves into the package
P: Transmission-line loss grows with frequency, as Chapter 14 showed for digital links. At 60 GHz a few centimeters of board trace and two connectors can cost several decibels, paid once on transmit and again on receive. Putting the antenna a couple of millimeters from the die removes most of that loss. The array also needs many feeds, one per element or subarray, and those feeds are far easier to route inside a multilayer package than across a board.
H2: Worked Example 18.1
WEP: Assume a board trace loss of 1.0 dB/cm at 60 GHz. Compare a 3 cm board route with a 2 mm in-package route.
WES: The board route costs 3.0 dB and the package route 0.2 dB, a saving of 2.8 dB at each end of the link, 5.6 dB on a two-way link. By Book 4's Friis relation, 5.6 dB of extra margin almost doubles the range, since range scales with the square root of the power ratio: 10^(5.6/20) = 1.9.
H2: 18.2 Sizing the elements and the array
P: A patch antenna resonates when its length is about half a wavelength in its substrate. Elements are spaced about half a free-space wavelength apart to avoid the grating lobes of Book 4's Chapter 8. At 60 GHz, λ₀ = 5.0 mm, so the spacing is 2.5 mm and a 4 × 4 array occupies about 10 × 10 mm, a package-sized area. At 28 GHz the spacing is 5.4 mm, and an 8 × 8 array is about 43 mm on a side, which is why many 28 GHz arrays tile several smaller modules on a board.
EQ: L_patch ≈ λ₀ / (2√εr),   d ≈ λ₀/2
H2: Worked Example 18.2
WEP: Find the approximate patch length on a substrate with εr = 3.5 at 60 GHz, and the size of a 4 × 4 array at half-wavelength spacing.
WES: L ≈ 5.0/(2 × 1.871) = 1.34 mm before the fringing correction, which shortens it slightly. The array is 4 × 2.5 = 10 mm on a side. Both dimensions are within the layer and via tolerances of a package substrate, which is why millimeter-wave arrays are built there.
H2: 18.3 EIRP of a phased array
P: In a transmitting array each element has its own amplifier. The total conducted power grows as N, and the array gain grows as N as well, so the effective isotropic radiated power grows as N². That is how arrays reach useful EIRP from small, low-voltage amplifiers on silicon.
EQ: EIRP = P_element + 20 log₁₀ N + G_element  (dBm)
H2: Worked Example 18.3
WEP: A 16-element array has 10 dBm per element and 5 dBi element gain. Find the EIRP.
WES: EIRP = 10 + 20 log 16 + 5 = 10 + 24.1 + 5 = 39.1 dBm. A single element would give 15 dBm, so the array adds 24.1 dB, half from combined power and half from gain.
KEY: Key idea. At millimeter wave the antenna belongs next to the die. Half-wavelength spacing makes the array package-sized, and EIRP grows as N² because power and gain both grow with N.
H2: 18.4 Packaging problems
P: The package substrate must now be an antenna substrate, with a controlled permittivity and low loss at the frequency of use, read from the data sheets as Chapter 4 recommends. Heat from the die must leave through the side of the package opposite the antennas, because a heatsink over the array would block it. The array's ground plane separates the radiating layers from the routing layers, and each via transition through it must be modeled as in Chapter 5. Testing changes too: the module has no RF connector, so it is measured over the air in a small anechoic chamber with the methods of Book 4's Chapter 16 and Part II's test discipline.
H2: 18.5 Element spacing and the scan range
P: A phased array steers its beam by setting the phase of each element, and the element spacing decides how far it can steer before a second, unwanted beam, a grating lobe, appears. Book 4 derives the condition; for packaging the consequence is a hard geometric limit. To scan to an angle θmax from broadside without a grating lobe, the spacing must not exceed λ/(1 + sin θmax): half a wavelength allows a full ±90°, and ±60° scanning needs about 0.54 λ. At 28 GHz that is under 6 mm, and at 60 or 140 GHz it is a few millimeters or less, so each element's feed, its RF chain, and its share of the beamformer chip must fit inside that cell. This is the deepest reason millimeter-wave arrays are built in the package rather than on the board.
EQ: d ≤ λ / (1 + sin θmax)
H2: Worked Example 18.4
WEP: Find the largest element spacing for a 28 GHz array that must scan ±60° without grating lobes, and the area per element.
WES: λ = 3 × 10⁸/28 × 10⁹ = 10.7 mm, so d ≤ 10.7/(1 + 0.866) = 5.74 mm and each element owns at most 33 mm². A beamformer chip serving four elements must route four RF feeds within 132 mm² of package, which is why such chips sit directly beneath their antennas.
P: An antenna in the package adds radiation to the package's duties. Chapter 19 adds the one duty every product shares: being built and tested in volume.
