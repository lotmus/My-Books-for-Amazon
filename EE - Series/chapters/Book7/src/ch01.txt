PART: Part I. Packages, Boards, Power, and Heat
H1: Chapter 1. Bandwidth per Watt: The 2026 Compute Picture
P: Every chip in the earlier books ends up in a package on a board, and no system shows more clearly why that step has become a first-rank engineering problem than the AI accelerator of 2026. Its performance is limited less by how fast it computes than by how fast memory can feed it, and how much current and heat its package can carry. A modern accelerator is a memory system with compute attached.
H2: 1.1 Memory bandwidth
P: An accelerator's arithmetic units can perform thousands of operations for every byte they read, but many AI workloads, particularly the inference of large language models, read the model's weights and cached context for each token generated. Their speed is then set by memory bandwidth, not arithmetic. A memory interface's bandwidth is its width in bits times its per-pin data rate, divided by eight for bytes. A conventional DDR5 channel is 64 bits wide; a high-bandwidth memory stack, HBM, is a tower of DRAM dies joined by through-silicon vias and placed beside the processor on the same package, with an interface 1024 or, in HBM4, 2048 bits wide. That width is possible only because the connections are microscopic and millimeters long, which is a packaging achievement, Chapter 2's subject.
EQ: Bandwidth (bytes/s) = width (bits) × per-pin rate (bit/s) / 8
H2: Worked Example 1.1
WEP: Find the bandwidth of one DDR5-6400 channel and of an HBM4 stack running 11 Gbit/s per pin over 2048 bits. Find their ratio, and the total for eight HBM4 stacks.
WES: DDR5-6400 transfers 6400 million times per second over 64 bits: 6.4 × 10⁹ × 64/8 = 51.2 GB/s. The HBM4 stack gives 2048 × 11 × 10⁹/8 = 2.82 × 10¹² B/s, about 2.8 TB/s, the figure Micron announced for its 12-high HBM4 in volume production in 2026. The ratio is 2.8 TB/s ÷ 51.2 GB/s = 55. Eight stacks give 22.4 TB/s. The JEDEC HBM4 baseline is 8 Gbit/s per pin, 2.0 TB/s; vendors run faster.
H2: 1.2 Energy per bit
P: Moving a bit costs energy, mostly in charging the capacitance of the wires and the input-output circuits at each end, and that energy grows with distance. Very roughly, a bit moved across a die costs a fraction of a picojoule, a bit moved to a memory stack on the same package costs a few picojoules, and a bit moved across a circuit board to a socketed memory module costs several times more. These are order-of-magnitude figures that vary with process and design; the arithmetic matters more than the exact values.
EQ: P = bit rate × energy per bit
H2: Worked Example 1.2
WEP: At a declared 2 pJ/bit, what power does a 2.8 TB/s HBM interface consume? At a declared 15 pJ/bit for a board-level link, what would the same bandwidth cost?
WES: 2.8 TB/s is 22.4 Tbit/s, so at 2 pJ/bit the power is 22.4 × 10¹² × 2 × 10⁻¹² = 44.8 W. At 15 pJ/bit it would be 336 W for a single stack's worth of bandwidth, impossible to supply and cool beside a processor. The memory had to move onto the package because the bandwidth could not be afforded any other way.
KEY: Key idea. A modern accelerator is a memory system with compute attached. Its bandwidth is bought with width, its width with packaging, and its energy per bit with short distances.
H2: 1.3 Current
P: The other limit is current. A 700 W processor core running from a 0.8 V rail draws 700/0.8 = 875 A. A thousand watts at 0.7 V would be 1429 A. That current must pass through the board, the socket or solder joints, the package substrate, and thousands of bumps into the die, with a total voltage drop of only tens of millivolts, and it must do so while the load changes by hundreds of amperes in microseconds. Book 6 designs the converters that produce the current; Chapter 3 of this book carries it to the die. The scarce inputs of the 2026 AI build-out, as the industry's own planning makes clear, are electric power, HBM, advanced packaging capacity, and optical interconnect, and two of them, memory packaging and advanced packaging capacity, are this book's subject.
H2: 1.4 What the rest of the book does
P: Chapter 2 sets out the vocabulary of packaging and the advanced packages of 2026.
