H1: Chapter 13. Crosstalk
P: Two traces that run side by side are coupled by their mutual capacitance and mutual inductance. A fast edge on one, the aggressor, induces noise on the other, the victim. Crosstalk closes eyes in serial links, corrupts quiet analog signals, and carries noise from one part of a board to another. This chapter separates the two kinds of crosstalk, near-end and far-end, computes their sizes from the coupling coefficients, and shows why spacing, stripline routing, and short parallel runs control them. The treatment follows Johnson and Graham and Bogatin.
H2: 13.1 Mutual capacitance and inductance
P: The aggressor's changing voltage drives a current through the mutual capacitance Cm into the victim, and its changing current induces a voltage on the victim through the mutual inductance Lm. The two effects are expressed relative to each line's own capacitance C and inductance L per unit length. On the victim, the capacitive and inductive currents add in the backward direction, toward the near end where the aggressor is driven, and subtract in the forward direction, toward the far end.
H2: 13.2 Near-end crosstalk
P: Near-end, or backward, crosstalk appears at the victim's end nearest the driver, as a pulse that begins with the aggressor's edge and lasts for twice the coupled length's delay. Once the coupled length exceeds the saturation length, half the distance an edge travels during its rise time, the near-end noise reaches a maximum set by the backward coefficient Kb and stays there however long the parallel run.
EQ: Kb = ¼ (Cm/C + Lm/L),    Lsat = tr v / 2
H2: Worked Example 13.1
WEP: Two microstrips have Cm/C = 0.05 and Lm/L = 0.07. Find the saturated near-end crosstalk. What is the saturation length for a 50 ps edge on a line where signals travel at half the speed of light, and for a 200 ps edge?
WES: Kb = ¼ × (0.05 + 0.07) = 0.030, so a 1 V edge produces up to 30 mV of near-end noise. The saturation length is 50 ps × 1.5 × 10⁸ m/s/2 = 3.75 mm for the 50 ps edge and 15.0 mm for the 200 ps edge. On a modern board with fast edges, almost every parallel run is longer than the saturation length, so the near-end crosstalk is at its full value and only spacing can reduce it.
H2: 13.3 Far-end crosstalk
P: Far-end, or forward, crosstalk travels with the aggressor's edge and arrives at the victim's far end at the same time as the edge does. It is proportional to the coupled length and to the edge's slope, and to the difference between the capacitive and inductive coupling ratios. In a homogeneous dielectric, as in a stripline, the two ratios are equal, and far-end crosstalk vanishes. In a microstrip, part of the field is in air, the inductive ratio exceeds the capacitive one, and far-end crosstalk grows with every centimeter of parallel run.
EQ: Vf / Va ≈ (length / (2v)) × (Cm/C − Lm/L) / tr
H2: Worked Example 13.2
WEP: The microstrips of Example 13.1 run in parallel for 10 cm on Book 4's reference microstrip, with εeff = 2.79, and the aggressor has a 50 ps edge. Find the far-end crosstalk. Repeat for 20 cm, and for 10 cm with a 100 ps edge.
WES: The speed is c/√2.79 = 1.795 × 10⁸ m/s, so length/(2v) = 0.1/(3.59 × 10⁸) = 279 ps. With Cm/C − Lm/L = −0.02, Vf/Va = 279 × (−0.02)/50 = −0.111: an inverted pulse of 11% of the aggressor's swing. At 20 cm it is 22%, and with a 100 ps edge over 10 cm it is 5.6%. The same pair routed as a stripline would have essentially no far-end crosstalk, which is the strongest reason to route long, fast buses on inner layers.
KEY: Key idea. Near-end crosstalk saturates and is controlled by spacing. Far-end crosstalk grows with length and edge rate, and vanishes in a stripline. Route long fast runs inside the board and keep aggressors away from victims.
H2: 13.4 Spacing
P: Coupling falls quickly as traces move apart. For traces at height h above a plane and separated by a distance s, a useful rule of Johnson and Graham is that crosstalk falls roughly as 1/(1 + (s/h)²). Moving from s = h to s = 3h reduces it to a fifth; s = 2h gives 40% of the s = h value. That is the origin of the common "3W" spacing guideline for traces whose width is close to their height. Bringing the reference plane closer, by thinning the dielectric, has the same effect as widening the spacing, because it confines each trace's fields.
H2: 13.5 Other coupling paths
P: Crosstalk is not only between parallel traces. Vias in a dense array couple to each other through the board; a via field under a BGA can dominate a channel's crosstalk at high data rates, and ground vias placed between signal vias reduce it. Connectors and package pins couple in the same way. Signals that share a return path, as in Chapter 9's ground bounce, couple through the common impedance. A complete crosstalk budget for a high-speed link adds all these contributions as noise sources in the receiver's eye, as Chapter 14 does.
H2: 13.6 How crosstalk falls with spacing
P: The coupling between two traces over a plane falls quickly as they move apart, because the fields of a trace over a plane are concentrated between the trace and the plane. A useful approximation for both microstrip and stripline makes the coupled noise proportional to 1/[1 + (s/h)²], where s is the center-to-center spacing and h the height above the reference plane. The rule of thumb that spaces traces three times their width apart is an expression of this: tripling s/h from 1 to 3 cuts the coupling by a factor of five. The same formula shows the alternative, often cheaper: halving the height to the plane, by a thinner dielectric, quarters the coupling at large spacings without using any more routing area.
EQ: crosstalk ∝ 1 / [1 + (s/h)²]
H2: Worked Example 13.3
WEP: Two traces 0.1 mm above their plane have 4 % near-end crosstalk at a center spacing of 0.1 mm. Estimate the crosstalk at 0.3 mm spacing, and at 0.1 mm spacing with the plane 0.05 mm below.
WES: At s/h = 1 the factor is 1/2; at s/h = 3, 1/10, so the crosstalk falls to 4 % × (1/10)/(1/2) = 0.8 %. With h = 0.05 mm at 0.1 mm spacing, s/h = 2 and the factor is 1/5, giving 1.6 %: thinning the dielectric gains a factor of 2.5 without moving the traces.
H2: 13.7 Adding up many aggressors
P: A victim trace in a dense bus has neighbors on both sides and more beyond them, and each contributes crosstalk. The worst-case budget adds their amplitudes, assuming every aggressor switches in the direction that hurts the victim at the same instant. For many aggressors with independent data that worst case is rare, and a statistical budget adds their contributions as the root of the sum of squares, which is smaller. Which one is right depends on the error rate required and the data patterns: a parallel memory bus whose lines switch together on the same clock edge can approach the linear sum, while independent serial lanes behave closer to the statistical one. Channel tools such as COM, Section 14.5, model crosstalk statistically and fold it into the noise that sets the error rate.
EQ: worst case: X = Σ|xᵢ|;    statistical: X = √(Σ xᵢ²)
H2: Worked Example 13.4
WEP: A victim has two adjacent aggressors that each couple 2 % and two next-nearest aggressors that each couple 0.5 %. Find the worst-case and statistical crosstalk, and the worst-case noise on an 800 mV swing.
WES: Worst case: 2 + 2 + 0.5 + 0.5 = 5.0 %, or 40 mV on 800 mV. Statistical: √(2 × 0.02² + 2 × 0.005²) = √(0.00085) = 2.9 %, or 23 mV. The two nearest neighbors dominate both sums, so widening their spacing, Section 13.6, buys most of the improvement.
P: Crosstalk is one way a channel loses margin. Loss, equalization, and the resulting eye, Chapter 14, are the others.
