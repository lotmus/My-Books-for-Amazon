H1: Chapter 20. Measuring the Interconnect: TDR and the VNA
P: Chapters 5, 12, 13, and 14 predicted the impedance, crosstalk, and loss of traces and vias. Those predictions must be checked on real boards, because laminate properties, etching, and plating vary. Two instruments do the checking. The time-domain reflectometer sends a fast step down a line and watches the reflections, which shows where the impedance changes. The vector network analyzer measures the S-parameters of Book 4 across frequency, which shows loss, resonances, and coupling. This chapter explains what each sees and how its accuracy is set.
H2: 20.1 The time-domain reflectometer
P: A TDR launches a step with a rise time of tens of picoseconds into the line and records the voltage at its own port. Each change in impedance reflects part of the step, with the reflection coefficient of Book 4, and the reflection arrives after the round-trip delay. Reading the trace as a function of time is reading the impedance as a function of distance. Two features close together can be told apart only if they are separated by more than about half the distance the edge travels in its rise time.
EQ: Z = Z₀(1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ),   resolution ≈ tr v / 2,   v = c/√εr
H2: Worked Example 20.1
WEP: A TDR with 35 ps rise time measures a stripline on the reference laminate, εr = 3.55. Find the spatial resolution, and the impedance where the reflection coefficient is +0.10 in a 50 Ω system.
WES: v = 2.998×10⁸/1.884 = 1.59×10⁸ m/s, so the resolution is 35×10⁻¹² × 1.59×10⁸/2 = 2.8 mm. A via shorter than that appears as a single smeared dip rather than its separate parts. With ρ = 0.10, Z = 50 × 1.10/0.90 = 61.1 Ω, a trace etched narrower than intended.
H2: 20.2 Timing and length
P: The same instrument measures delay. On the reference stripline, 100 mm of trace takes 628 ps one way, so its reflection arrives 1257 ps after the launch. Comparing the delay of two traces checks length matching for a differential pair or a memory bus directly, without knowing εr precisely, because both traces share the same laminate.
H2: 20.3 The vector network analyzer and calibration
P: The VNA measures the amplitude and phase of S-parameters from kilohertz to tens of gigahertz and beyond. Its raw measurement includes the cables, connectors, and fixture, and calibration removes them by measuring known standards. SOLT calibration uses a short, an open, a load, and a through, whose behavior must be known accurately. TRL calibration uses a through, a reflect, and a line, and needs only that the line be a clean transmission line of a different length, which makes it well suited to standards built on the board itself. A TRL line works best near a quarter wavelength longer than the through and is usable from about 20° to 160° of extra electrical length, an 8:1 frequency range per line.
EQ: usable band: 20° ≤ βΔl ≤ 160°
H2: Worked Example 20.2
WEP: Design a TRL line on the reference stripline for a 10 GHz center frequency, and find its usable band.
WES: The guided wavelength at 10 GHz is v/f = 15.9 mm, so the line is 15.9/4 = 3.98 mm longer than the through. Its 90° point is at 10 GHz, so 20° falls at 2.2 GHz and 160° at 17.8 GHz. A second, longer line extends the calibration below 2.2 GHz.
KEY: Key idea. A TDR reads impedance against distance, with a resolution set by its rise time; a VNA reads S-parameters against frequency, with an accuracy set by its calibration. On-board TRL standards move the reference planes to the structure being tested.
H2: 20.4 De-embedding and correlation
P: A test coupon on the panel edge lets each production lot be measured: impedance by TDR, loss per unit length by VNA. De-embedding subtracts the launch structures, either by TRL calibration or by measuring two lengths of the same line, so that the difference is the line alone. The measurement then correlates with the simulation of Chapter 14: if the measured loss is higher, the laminate's Df at the frequency of use or the copper roughness is worse than assumed, and the channel budget must be revisited before the product ships.
