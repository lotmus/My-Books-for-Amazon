H1: Chapter 17. Inside the HBM Stack
P: Chapter 1 counted HBM's bandwidth and Chapter 2 placed it beside the processor on an interposer. This chapter opens the stack itself. An HBM device is a column of thinned DRAM dies on a logic base die, connected vertically by thousands of through-silicon vias and joined by microbumps or, increasingly, by direct hybrid bonds. Its bandwidth comes from width, its difficulty from height, and its limits are electrical, mechanical, and thermal at once.
H2: 17.1 The structure
P: The base die at the bottom holds the interface to the processor, the test logic, and, in HBM4, more of the control functions, sometimes built in a logic process rather than a DRAM process. Above it sit eight, twelve, or sixteen DRAM dies, each thinned to a few tens of micrometers so that the whole stack fits the JEDEC height limit of a few hundred micrometers. Through-silicon vias, copper columns a few micrometers across, pass signals and power through each die. HBM4 doubles the interface to 2048 data bits per stack, so at 11 Gbit/s per pin the stack delivers 2048 × 11/8 = 2.8 TB/s.
EQ: bandwidth = width × rate / 8 = 2048 × 11 Gbit/s / 8 = 2.8 TB/s
H2: 17.2 Through-silicon vias
P: A TSV is short and thick compared with a wire bond, so its inductance and resistance are small, but it is surrounded by silicon, a lossy semiconductor, separated only by a thin oxide liner. Its capacitance, tens of femtofarads, and the coupling between neighbors through the silicon set the signaling energy. Its copper also expands more than the silicon around it, so the keep-out zone around each TSV, where transistor behavior shifts with stress, costs die area.
EQ: R = ρL / A
H2: Worked Example 17.1
WEP: Find the resistance of a copper TSV 5 µm in diameter and 50 µm long.
WES: A = π(2.5×10⁻⁶)² = 1.96×10⁻¹¹ m², so R = 1.72×10⁻⁸ × 50×10⁻⁶ / 1.96×10⁻¹¹ = 0.044 Ω. That is negligible for a signal, but a power path through sixteen dies passes through many such vias in series per layer, so power is delivered through hundreds or thousands of TSVs in parallel, just as Chapter 3 spreads 875 A across thousands of bumps.
H2: 17.3 Bonding: microbumps and hybrid bonds
P: Conventional HBM joins the dies with microbumps at a pitch of about 40 to 55 µm and fills the gaps with underfill or a non-conductive film. Hybrid bonding joins copper pads directly to copper and oxide directly to oxide, with no solder and no gap, at pitches of about 10 µm and below. By Chapter 2's density formula, a 55 µm pitch gives about 330 connections per square millimeter, and a 9 µm pitch gives about 12,300, more than thirty times as many, while removing the low-conductivity underfill layers from the heat path.
EQ: density = 10⁶ / p²  per mm²  (p in µm)
H2: Worked Example 17.2
WEP: Heat generated in the base die must flow up through sixteen bonding layers. Each underfill layer is 10 µm thick with k = 0.5 W/(m·K) over 100 mm². Find the thermal resistance of the sixteen layers, and the temperature rise for 10 W.
WES: Each layer gives θ = t/(kA) = 10×10⁻⁶/(0.5 × 100×10⁻⁶) = 0.2 K/W, and sixteen in series give 3.2 K/W. For 10 W the rise is 32 K, a large share of the budget of Chapter 6 before the heat reaches the lid. Hybrid bonding replaces those layers with silicon and copper, whose conductivity is hundreds of times higher, which is one of its main motivations.
KEY: Key idea. HBM's bandwidth comes from a very wide interface, and its difficulty from height: the TSVs, the bonds, and the heat path through sixteen thinned dies. Hybrid bonding raises connection density and lowers thermal resistance together.
H2: 17.4 Energy per bit and stack power
P: An HBM interface is short, so its energy per bit is a few picojoules, far less than a DDR channel across a board. Even so, at 2.8 TB/s the interface power is large, because Chapter 1's rule multiplies the energy per bit by a very large bit rate. Refresh, the DRAM arrays, and the base die add more. The stack's power, concentrated in a small footprint next to the processor, is why HBM temperature, not only processor temperature, limits AI accelerators.
H2: Worked Example 17.3
WEP: Assume an interface energy of 2 pJ/bit. Find the interface power of one HBM4 stack at 2.8 TB/s, and of eight stacks.
WES: The bit rate is 2.816 TB/s × 8 = 22.5 Tbit/s, so P = 22.5×10¹² × 2×10⁻¹² = 45 W per stack, and 360 W for eight stacks. The assumed energy per bit is the lever: every 0.5 pJ/bit saved removes about 90 W from an eight-stack accelerator.
H2: 17.5 Known-good dies and test
P: A sixteen-high stack is only as good as its worst die and its worst bond. If each die and its bonding has a yield of 99%, the stack yield is 0.99¹⁶ = 85%, before the stack meets the processor. Each die is therefore tested before stacking, spare rows and TSVs are built in for repair, and the base die includes test logic so the assembled stack can be checked through the interposer. Chapter 8's yield arithmetic applies vertically as well as horizontally.
