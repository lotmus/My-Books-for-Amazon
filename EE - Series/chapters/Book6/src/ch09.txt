PART: Part II. Inside the Converter
P: Part II goes inside: where the watts are lost and how to measure them, how to compensate the loop, what happens at light load, how batteries are charged and managed, and how inverters drive motors.
H1: Chapter 9. Where the Watts Go: Loss in a Switching Converter
P: Chapter 3 treated the buck converter's switches as ideal. Real ones lose power in five ways: resistance while on, overlap of voltage and current while switching, charging and discharging their own capacitance, driving their gates, and conducting through a diode during the dead time between switches. Adding these up predicts the efficiency of Chapter 1's formula, and it shows why raising the switching frequency, which shrinks the inductor, costs efficiency, and why gallium nitride changes the trade. The device physics is Book 3's; this chapter uses the datasheet numbers.
H2: 9.1 Conduction loss
P: A MOSFET that is on behaves as a resistance RDS(on). In a buck converter the high-side switch carries the load current for a fraction D of each period and the low-side, synchronous switch for the rest, so their conduction losses are I²R weighted by those fractions. The current's ripple adds a small term, I²rms = I² + ΔI²/12, usually a few percent. Because the low-side switch conducts most of the time in a large step-down, designers give it the lower resistance and accept more capacitance.
EQ: Pcond = I² RDS(on),HS D + I² RDS(on),LS (1 − D)
H2: 9.2 Switching, capacitance, gate, and dead-time loss
P: During each transition the switch briefly carries the full current while the full voltage is still across it. Approximating the overlap as linear gives an energy of ½VI(tr + tf) per cycle. The device's output capacitance, charged to the input voltage each cycle, dumps ½CossV² into the channel when it turns on. Driving the gate costs QgVg per switch per cycle, delivered by the driver. Finally, both switches must be off briefly between transitions to avoid shoot-through, and during that dead time the low-side switch's body diode conducts with a forward drop VF. All four terms scale with the switching frequency.
EQ: Psw = ½ V I (tr + tf) fs,   PCoss = ½ Coss V² fs,   Pgate = Qg Vg fs,   Pdead = 2 VF I tdt fs
H2: Worked Example 9.1
WEP: A buck converter steps 12 V to 1 V at 20 A and 500 kHz. The high-side switch has 5 mΩ, the low-side 2 mΩ. Take tr + tf = 20 ns, Coss = 1 nF, Qg = 20 nC per switch at a 5 V drive, a dead time of 20 ns per transition, and VF = 0.7 V. Find each loss and the efficiency, ignoring the inductor.
WES: D = 1/12. Conduction: 400 × 0.005 / 12 = 0.167 W high side, 400 × 0.002 × 11/12 = 0.733 W low side. Switching: ½ × 12 × 20 × 20 ns × 500 kHz = 1.20 W. Coss: ½ × 1 nF × 144 × 500 kHz = 0.036 W. Gates: 2 × 20 nC × 5 V × 500 kHz = 0.10 W. Dead time: 2 × 0.7 × 20 × 20 ns × 500 kHz = 0.28 W. The total is 2.52 W against a 20 W output, so η = 20/22.52 = 88.8%. Switching overlap is the largest single loss.
H2: Worked Example 9.2
WEP: The same converter runs at 1 MHz to halve its inductor. Find the new loss and efficiency. Then repeat with GaN switches of the same resistance, tr + tf = 4 ns, Qg = 5 nC, a 5 ns dead time, and VF = 2 V for the reverse-conduction drop.
WES: At 1 MHz with silicon, every frequency-dependent loss doubles: 2.40 + 0.072 + 0.20 + 0.56 = 3.23 W, plus 0.90 W of conduction, total 4.13 W and η = 82.9%. With GaN at 1 MHz: switching ½ × 12 × 20 × 4 ns × 1 MHz = 0.48 W, gates 0.05 W, dead time 2 × 2 × 20 × 5 ns × 1 MHz = 0.40 W, Coss 0.072 W, conduction 0.90 W, total 1.90 W and η = 91.3%, better than silicon at half the frequency.
KEY: Key idea. Conduction loss depends on resistance; switching, capacitance, gate, and dead-time losses scale with frequency. Faster devices let a converter switch faster and shrink its magnetics without paying the frequency penalty in full.
H2: 9.3 Reverse recovery and soft switching
P: When a silicon diode or MOSFET body diode that has been conducting is turned off, stored charge must be swept out before it blocks, and the opposite switch carries that reverse-recovery charge Qrr at full voltage, adding QrrV fs of loss and a sharp current spike that Book 7's EMC chamber will see. Silicon carbide Schottky diodes and GaN transistors have essentially no reverse-recovery charge, a major reason they dominate high-voltage power-factor stages and fast converters. Soft-switching topologies, such as the LLC converter of Chapter 5 and phase-shifted bridges, arrange for the voltage across each switch to fall to zero before it turns on, eliminating overlap and Coss loss at the cost of circulating current.
H2: 9.4 Thermal limits
P: The losses become heat in packages whose thermal resistance, junction to ambient, may be tens of kelvin per watt for a small surface-mount part, or below 1 K/W for a module on a heat sink. The junction temperature, ambient plus power times thermal resistance, must stay below its rating with margin, and RDS(on) rises with temperature, about 1.5 to 2 times from 25 °C to 125 °C for silicon, so the loss estimate must use the hot value. A good design iterates: estimate the loss, compute the temperature, recompute the resistance, and repeat.
H2: 9.5 The capacitors' share: ripple current and life
P: Capacitors lose power in their equivalent series resistance, I²rms × ESR, and for aluminum electrolytics that heat also sets the life. The electrolyte slowly evaporates through the seal, faster when hot, and the rated life, typically 2000 to 10 000 hours at 105 °C, roughly doubles for every 10 °C cooler the core runs. A capacitor rated 5000 h at 105 °C and operating with its core at 65 °C therefore lasts about 80 000 h, nine years. The ripple current raises the core above the ambient, so the life calculation must use the core temperature, and the ripple current at the actual frequency, because ESR falls with frequency. Polymer aluminum and ceramic capacitors have no liquid electrolyte and no such wear-out, which is why they replaced electrolytics on processor boards, but electrolytics remain unbeaten for bulk energy storage at 400 V.
EQ: L = L0 × 2^((T0 − T)/10)
H2: 9.6 Measuring efficiency honestly
P: A converter at 98 % efficiency loses 2 % of its power, and measuring that loss as the difference of two large numbers magnifies every error. A power analyzer with 0.1 % accuracy on both input and output power can be wrong by up to 0.2 % of the throughput, which is 10 % of the loss. Accurate work therefore uses an analyzer with matched channels and calibrated current shunts, measures at the converter's terminals with Kelvin connections, waits for thermal equilibrium, and reports the loss directly when the efficiency exceeds about 99 %. For the best converters, calorimetry, placing the converter in an insulated box and measuring the heat it produces, is the only method whose error scales with the loss rather than the throughput.
H2: Worked Example 9.3
WEP: A 470 µF electrolytic rated 5000 h at 105 °C sits in a 55 °C enclosure, and its ripple current heats its core 10 °C above that. Estimate its life.
WES: The core runs at 65 °C, 40 °C below the rating, so L = 5000 × 2^(40/10) = 80 000 h, about nine years of continuous operation. Moving it next to a heatsink at 85 °C would cut the life to 5000 × 2^1 = 10 000 h, just over a year: placement decides the product's warranty.
H2: Worked Example 9.4
WEP: A 1 kW converter is measured at 98.0 % efficiency with an analyzer accurate to ±0.1 % of reading on each channel. Find the uncertainty in the loss.
WES: The input reads about 1020.4 W ± 1.02 W and the output 1000 W ± 1.00 W. The loss, 20.4 W, could be off by up to 2.0 W, ±10 %, or about ±1.4 W if the errors are independent. The efficiency is then known only to between 97.8 and 98.2 %, a range wider than the difference between many competing designs.
H2: 9.7 Choosing a heatsink
P: Once the losses are known, the thermal path must remove them. The junction temperature is the ambient plus the loss times the sum of thermal resistances in the path: junction to case, from the data sheet; case to heatsink, set by the interface material, typically 0.1 to 0.5 °C/W for a TO-247 with a good pad or grease; and heatsink to ambient, which the designer chooses. Subtracting the first two from the total allowed gives the heatsink's required resistance. Forced air halves or quarters a heatsink's resistance, and liquid cold plates reach 0.01 to 0.05 °C/W for large modules, which is why every high-power converter and every accelerator board above a few hundred watts now uses one or the other.
EQ: θSA ≤ (TJ,max − TA)/P − θJC − θCS
H2: Worked Example 9.5
WEP: A SiC MOSFET in TO-247 dissipates 25 W with θJC = 0.6 °C/W and θCS = 0.3 °C/W. The junction must stay below 125 °C at 50 °C ambient. Find the largest allowed heatsink resistance.
WES: The total allowed is (125 − 50)/25 = 3.0 °C/W, so θSA ≤ 3.0 − 0.6 − 0.3 = 2.1 °C/W, a medium extruded heatsink in natural convection or a small one with a fan. If two such devices share one heatsink, each adds its loss to the heatsink's temperature rise, and the requirement halves to about 1.05 °C/W.
