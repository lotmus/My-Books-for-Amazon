H1: Chapter 5. Isolated Converters
P: The converters of Chapters 3 and 4 share a ground between input and output. Anything connected to the mains must not: a fault in the converter must never put line voltage on a connector a person can touch. Isolated converters pass power through a transformer, which also allows large voltage ratios that a buck or boost could not handle efficiently. This chapter covers the three main isolated topologies, the flyback, the forward, and the resonant LLC, and the wide-bandgap switches that let them run fast and efficiently. The transformer itself is in Chapter 6; safety standards for isolation, such as IEC 62368-1, set the creepage and insulation that the transformer must meet.
H2: 5.1 The flyback
P: The flyback is a buck-boost whose inductor has a second winding. When the primary switch is on, the coupled inductor stores energy in its magnetizing inductance, and the secondary rectifier is reverse-biased. When it switches off, the stored energy flows out of the secondary to the load. It needs only one switch, one magnetic component, and one rectifier, which makes it the standard topology for chargers and auxiliary supplies up to about 100 W. Its drawbacks are that all the energy must be stored in the transformer's gap each cycle, and that the leakage inductance between windings causes a voltage spike when the switch turns off, which a clamp or snubber must absorb.
EQ: Vout = Vin × D / [(1 − D) n],   n = Np/Ns;   switch stress ≈ Vin + n Vout + spike
H2: Worked Example 5.1
WEP: A flyback runs from a 375 V rectified bus to 12 V with a 15:1 turns ratio in continuous conduction. Find the duty cycle, the reflected voltage, and the switch's off-state voltage before the leakage spike.
WES: D/(1 − D) = nVout/Vin = 15 × 12/375 = 0.48, so D = 0.324. The output voltage reflected to the primary is 15 × 12 = 180 V, so the switch must block 375 + 180 = 555 V, plus the leakage spike. A 650 V gallium-nitride switch or an 800 V silicon MOSFET is the usual choice. A higher turns ratio would raise the duty cycle and the switch stress; a lower one would lower the stress but increase the secondary current and its loss.
H2: Worked Example 5.2
WEP: A 24 W flyback running in discontinuous conduction at 100 kHz has a primary magnetizing inductance of 500 µH. Find the energy stored per cycle and the peak primary current, neglecting losses.
WES: The energy per cycle is P/fsw = 24/10⁵ = 240 µJ. With E = ½LIpk², Ipk = √(2 × 240 × 10⁻⁶/500 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.98 A. The core and its gap must store this energy without saturating, which Chapter 6 shows sets the core size of a flyback.
H2: 5.2 The forward converter
P: The forward converter is an isolated buck. The transformer transfers energy directly while the switch is on, and an output inductor smooths it, so the output is Vout = DVin/n. The transformer stores little energy, but its magnetizing current must be reset during each off-time, by a reset winding, an active clamp, or a second switch, and the reset limits the duty cycle, typically to below 0.5 for the simplest reset. Forward converters and their two-switch and active-clamp variants serve the range from about 100 W to 1 kW. Full-bridge and phase-shifted full-bridge converters take over above that.
EQ: Vout = D Vin / n   (forward)
H2: 5.3 The LLC resonant converter
P: The LLC converter drives a series resonant tank, a resonant inductor Lr, a capacitor Cr, and the transformer's magnetizing inductance, with a half-bridge or full-bridge of switches at close to its resonant frequency fr = 1/(2π√(LrCr)). Near resonance the switches turn on with zero voltage across them, and the secondary rectifiers turn off at zero current, so switching losses nearly vanish. The output is regulated by moving the switching frequency around resonance. LLC converters dominate server power supplies and the high-efficiency stages of data-center shelves, where they reach 97 to 98%. A fixed-ratio form, the DC transformer or bus converter, runs exactly at resonance and converts 48 V to 12 V, or higher bus voltages down, at a fixed ratio with very high efficiency.
EQ: fr = 1 / (2π √(Lr Cr))
H2: Worked Example 5.3
WEP: An LLC has Lr = 50 µH and Cr = 22 nF. Find its resonant frequency. It runs from a 400 V bus with a half-bridge to produce 12 V. What transformer turns ratio gives unity tank gain at resonance?
WES: fr = 1/(2π√(50 × 10⁻⁶ × 22 × 10⁻⁹)) = 152 kHz. A half-bridge applies a square wave of amplitude Vin/2 = 200 V to the tank, so for 12 V at unity gain the ratio is 200/12 = 16.7. Designers then add the rectifier drop and choose an integer or convenient ratio, letting the frequency control absorb the difference.
KEY: Key idea. A transformer buys isolation and large ratios. The flyback stores energy in it, the forward converter passes energy through it, and the LLC uses resonance so that its switches turn on at zero voltage and off at low loss.
H2: 5.4 Wide-bandgap switches
P: Gallium-nitride transistors at 650 V and silicon-carbide MOSFETs at 650 V to 1700 V and beyond have transformed isolated converters. GaN has very low capacitance and no reverse-recovery charge, so it switches in a few nanoseconds and enables flybacks and LLCs at several hundred kilohertz to megahertz in small chargers. SiC withstands higher voltage and temperature and dominates electric-vehicle traction inverters, onboard chargers, and solar inverters at 800 V. Both demand careful gate drive and layout, because their fast edges excite every parasitic inductance; the hot-loop rule of Chapter 3 applies with greater force. Indium phosphide, the high-frequency compound of Book 4, has no role in power conversion.
H2: 5.5 Designing a flyback: turns ratio, stress, and leakage
P: A flyback design starts from one choice, the reflected voltage VR, the output voltage plus the rectifier drop multiplied by the turns ratio Np/Ns. While the secondary conducts, VR appears on the primary on top of the input. A larger VR lowers the secondary's stress and allows a larger duty cycle at low line, but raises the primary switch's voltage. The switch must withstand the peak rectified line, the reflected voltage, and the spike produced by the transformer's leakage inductance, which no transformer can avoid entirely. That spike is clamped by an RCD snubber, which dissipates the leakage energy, ½LlkIpk² per cycle and somewhat more in practice because the clamp also discharges part of the reflected voltage, or by an active clamp that returns it to the input.
EQ: VDS,max = √2 Vac,max + VR + Vspike,   P_leak ≈ ½ Llk Ipk² fsw
H2: 5.6 The modern USB-C charger
P: A 65 W laptop charger of 2026 is a quasi-resonant or active-clamp flyback with a 650 V GaN switch, a synchronous rectifier on the secondary, and a USB Power Delivery controller that negotiates 5 to 20 V, or up to 48 V under Extended Power Range. Power Integrations' InnoSwitch, Navitas's GaNFast, and the Infineon and TI active-clamp controllers integrate most of it. GaN's small output charge lets the switch run at 100 to 300 kHz with low loss, the active clamp recycles the leakage energy, and the smaller transformer and capacitors shrink the charger to a third of the volume of a silicon design of 2015, at power densities above 1.5 W/cm³.
H2: Worked Example 5.4
WEP: A 65 W, 20 V flyback runs from 90 to 264 VAC with VR = 100 V and a 0.5 V synchronous rectifier drop. Find the turns ratio, the duty cycle at the 100 V valley of the bulk capacitor at low line, assuming boundary conduction, and the switch voltage before the spike at high line.
WES: Np/Ns = 100/20.5 = 4.88. At the boundary of conduction, volt-second balance gives D = VR/(VR + Vin) = 100/200 = 0.50. At 264 VAC the bulk capacitor reaches 373 V, so the switch sees 373 + 100 = 473 V before the leakage spike; with 80 to 100 V of spike, a 650 V GaN switch keeps about 15 % margin.
H2: Worked Example 5.5
WEP: For the same converter at 92 % efficiency and 100 kHz, find the primary peak current at the 100 V valley, and the leakage energy lost per second if Llk = 3 µH and the clamp simply dissipates it.
WES: The input power is 70.7 W and the average input current 0.707 A. In boundary conduction at D = 0.5 the input current is a triangle averaging ½IpkD, so Ipk = 2 × 0.707/0.5 = 2.83 A. The leakage loss is ½ × 3 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.83² × 10⁵ = 1.2 W, nearly 2 % of the output and more in an RCD clamp. An active clamp recovers most of it, which is a large part of why GaN chargers reach 93 to 94 %.
H2: 5.7 Designing the LLC tank
P: The LLC converter of Section 5.3 is defined by three tank elements: the resonant inductance Lr, often the transformer's leakage, the resonant capacitor Cr, and the magnetizing inductance Lm. At the series resonant frequency fr = 1/(2π√(LrCr)) the tank's gain is one, set by the turns ratio alone, and the converter is most efficient, with zero-voltage switching on the primary and zero-current switching of the rectifiers. Below fr the magnetizing inductance joins the resonance and the gain rises; above it the gain falls below one. The ratio Ln = Lm/Lr, typically 4 to 10, trades the gain range against magnetizing current: a small Ln gives more gain range for a narrow frequency sweep but more circulating current. Data-center bus converters run their LLC exactly at fr as a fixed-ratio DC transformer and leave regulation to the next stage.
EQ: fr = 1/(2π √(Lr Cr)),   Ln = Lm / Lr
H2: Worked Example 5.6
WEP: An LLC tank has Lr = 60 µH, Cr = 47 nF, and Lm = 300 µH. Find the series resonant frequency and Ln, and the characteristic impedance √(Lr/Cr).
WES: fr = 1/(2π√(60 × 10⁻⁶ × 47 × 10⁻⁹)) = 94.8 kHz, Ln = 300/60 = 5, and √(Lr/Cr) = √(1277) = 35.7 Ω. The characteristic impedance, compared with the load reflected through the transformer, sets the tank's quality factor and so how steeply the gain varies with frequency.
P: The isolated converters bring a transformer into the design, and with it the magnetic component that every switching converter depends on. Chapter 6 sorts those components into families and shows how to choose and size them.
