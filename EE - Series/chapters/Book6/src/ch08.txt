H1: Chapter 8. High Voltage and Pulsed Power
P: Most of this book deals with voltages below a kilovolt and continuous power. Two specialties go beyond that. High-voltage engineering deals with the insulation, spacing, and breakdown of systems at tens to hundreds of kilovolts: X-ray sources, electrostatic precipitators, particle accelerators, and the transmission grid. Pulsed power stores energy slowly and releases it in microseconds or nanoseconds, at peak powers of megawatts to terawatts, to drive radar tubes, lasers, and research machines. Both share a concern that ordinary converters can mostly ignore: the electric field, and what happens when it exceeds what the insulation can stand. Safety is the first consideration in both. Charged capacitors at these energies are lethal, and the work requires interlocks, grounding sticks, and training that no book replaces.
H2: 8.1 Breakdown and insulation
P: Air at atmospheric pressure breaks down at about 3 kV/mm in a uniform field, less in the concentrated field at a sharp point or edge, which also produces corona, a continuous partial discharge that erodes insulation and creates interference. Paschen's law describes how breakdown depends on the product of pressure and gap: there is a minimum, near 330 V for air, below which no gap breaks down, and the breakdown voltage rises at both lower and higher pressure-gap products. Solid insulation fails by partial discharge in voids, tracking along surfaces, and slow aging. Designs therefore set two distances: clearance through air, and creepage along surfaces, given by safety standards such as IEC 60664-1 for the voltage, pollution degree, and material group. Edges are rounded and fields graded, so that no point sees much more than the average field.
H2: Worked Example 8.1
WEP: What is the smallest uniform-field air gap that would withstand 25 kV, and why is a practical design much larger?
WES: At 3 kV/mm, 25/3 = 8.3 mm. A practical design uses several times that, because real electrodes have field enhancement at edges, humidity and contamination lower the threshold, transients exceed the working voltage, and safety standards add margins. Rounding electrodes, adding solid barriers, or immersing the assembly in oil or a pressurized insulating gas reduces the size.
H2: 8.2 Storing and releasing energy
P: Pulsed power begins with a store, usually capacitors, charged over milliseconds or seconds, and a switch, a spark gap, thyratron, or stack of solid-state devices, that discharges it in microseconds. The energy in a capacitor is ½CV². A Marx generator charges n capacitors in parallel and then switches them in series, multiplying the voltage by n: ten stages charged to 30 kV give 300 kV. Pulse-forming networks shape the release into a flat-topped pulse.
EQ: E = ½ C V²,    VMarx ≈ n Vcharge
H2: Worked Example 8.2
WEP: Find the energy stored in a 1 µF capacitor at 10 kV and in a 2 µF capacitor at 20 kV. Find the output of an eight-stage Marx charged to 40 kV.
WES: ½ × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁸ = 50 J and ½ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 4 × 10⁸ = 400 J. The Marx produces about 8 × 40 = 320 kV, less in practice because of stray capacitance and switch losses. 50 J delivered in 1 µs is 50 MW of peak power, from a supply that might be rated at only a few hundred watts.
KEY: Key idea. Pulsed power trades time for power. Energy is collected slowly at modest power and released quickly at enormous power, and the design problem is the switch, the pulse shape, and the field the insulation must withstand.
H2: 8.3 The line-type modulator
P: A radar transmitter built around a magnetron or klystron, Book 4's subject, needs a rectangular high-voltage pulse at each transmit instant. A line-type modulator charges a pulse-forming network, a ladder of inductors and capacitors that approximates a transmission line of impedance Z0 = √(L/C) and delay τ/2, to a voltage V, and discharges it through a switch into a matched load. The network delivers a pulse of width τ at V/2 across the load. Its total capacitance and inductance follow from the impedance and pulse width. A pulse transformer, shelf C of Chapter 6, steps the pulse up to the tube's voltage.
EQ: C_total = τ / (2 Z0),    L_total = τ Z0 / 2,    Vload = Vcharge / 2
H2: Worked Example 8.3
WEP: A magnetron needs 1 µs pulses at 50 kV and 50 A, which is 1 kΩ, at 1 kHz. A 1:10 pulse transformer feeds it from a pulse-forming network. Find the primary pulse voltage, current, and impedance, the network's charge voltage, total capacitance, and inductance, the energy per pulse, and the average power.
WES: The 1:10 transformer reflects 1 kΩ as 1000/100 = 10 Ω, with a primary pulse of 5 kV and 500 A. The network must have Z0 = 10 Ω and is charged to 2 × 5 = 10 kV. Its capacitance is 10⁻⁶/(2 × 10) = 50 nF and its inductance 10⁻⁶ × 10/2 = 5 µH. The energy per pulse is ½ × 50 × 10⁻⁹ × 10⁸ = 2.5 J, which matches the 2.5 MW pulse power times 1 µs. At 1 kHz, the average power is 2.5 kW.
H2: 8.4 The pulse transformer's droop
P: During a pulse, the transformer's magnetizing current rises linearly and diverts current from the load, so the pulse top droops. For a load resistance R referred to the primary and magnetizing inductance Lm, the fractional droop is about Rτ/Lm. The leakage inductance and winding capacitance set the rise time and overshoot, so the designer must balance a large magnetizing inductance, which needs more turns, against small leakage and capacitance, which need fewer. The core must also be reset between pulses, so that its flux does not walk toward saturation.
EQ: droop ≈ R τ / Lm
H2: Worked Example 8.4
WEP: For the modulator of Example 8.3, what primary magnetizing inductance limits the droop to 5%? What is it referred to the secondary?
WES: Lm ≥ Rτ/0.05 = 10 × 10⁻⁶/0.05 = 200 µH on the primary. Referred to the secondary, through the square of the 1:10 ratio, it is 20 mH, which is the same as computing with the 1 kΩ load directly: 1000 × 10⁻⁶/0.05 = 20 mH.
