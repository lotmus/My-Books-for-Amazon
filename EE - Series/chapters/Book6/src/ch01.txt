PART: Part I. The Converters
P: Part I builds the converters and their parts: the efficiency arithmetic that runs through the book, the linear regulator, the buck, boost, and buck-boost with their control loops, the isolated flyback, forward, and LLC converters, the magnetic components, the connection to the grid through rectifiers and power-factor correction, and the insulation and stored energy of high voltage.
H1: Chapter 1. Efficiency and Waste
P: Power electronics is the engineering of converting electrical energy from the form in which it arrives to the form a load needs, with as little lost as possible. The loss matters more than the efficiency figure suggests, because the loss is what has to be cooled, what limits the size of the converter, and what the owner pays for twice, once as electricity and again as cooling. This chapter introduces the one formula that runs through the whole book, the waste of a converter in terms of its output and efficiency, and applies it to the place where power conversion now matters most visibly: the racks of an artificial-intelligence data center.
H2: 1.1 Waste, not efficiency
P: A converter delivering P to its load at efficiency η draws P/η from its source, and the difference becomes heat. Efficiency figures near 100% hide large differences in waste: going from 96% to 98% looks like a 2% improvement, but it halves the heat.
EQ: Pwaste = P (1/η − 1)
H2: Worked Example 1.1
WEP: An 80 kW AI rack is fed through a power stage at 96% or at 98% efficiency. Find the waste in each case, the difference per rack, and the difference for 1000 racks. Repeat for a rack averaging 45 kW.
WES: At 96%, the waste is 80 × (1/0.96 − 1) = 3.33 kW. At 98%, it is 80 × (1/0.98 − 1) = 1.63 kW. The difference is 1.70 kW per rack, and 1.70 MW across 1000 racks, the output of a small power plant, all of it heat that the cooling plant must also remove. At an average of 45 kW, the difference is 0.96 kW per rack. Running for a year, 1.70 MW is 14,900 MWh; at $0.10 per kWh, that is $1.49 million of electricity, before the cost of cooling it.
KEY: Key idea. Count the waste, not the efficiency. Two percentage points near the top of the scale can halve the heat, and in a data center that heat is paid for as electricity, as cooling, and as floor space.
H2: 1.2 The conversion chain
P: Power reaches a processor through several conversions in series, and their efficiencies multiply. In a typical 2026 AI rack, alternating current from the facility is rectified and converted to a 48 V or higher DC bus by power-supply shelves, an intermediate bus converter steps that down to about 12 V or directly to a lower voltage on the board, and voltage regulators beside the processor supply its core at under one volt. Each stage has a different job and a different efficiency, and the last stage, which handles the highest current at the lowest voltage, is usually the least efficient. Book 7 describes the package and the 875 A that a 700 W processor draws at 0.8 V; this book designs the converters that deliver it.
H2: Worked Example 1.2
WEP: A rack's chain consists of a 97.5% power-supply shelf, a 97% intermediate bus converter, and 90% point-of-load regulators. Find the overall efficiency, the input power for 80 kW delivered to the processors, and the total waste.
WES: The overall efficiency is 0.975 × 0.97 × 0.90 = 0.851. Delivering 80 kW needs 80/0.851 = 94.0 kW at the input, and 14.0 kW is waste. Of that, the final stage alone loses 80 × (1/0.90 − 1) = 8.9 kW. Improving the regulators from 90% to 92% would save more than eliminating the bus converter's loss entirely, which is why so much engineering in 2026 goes into vertical power delivery and higher-voltage distribution to the board.
H2: 1.3 The scale of the problem
P: The data center industry's planning figures make the stakes clear. JLL's 2026 Global Data Center Outlook projects nearly 100 GW of new capacity worldwide between 2026 and 2030, roughly doubling the global total, with shell-and-core construction costs averaging about $11.3 million per megawatt in 2026 and AI fit-out adding up to $25 million per megawatt on top. Rack densities for AI training are 40 to more than 100 kW, requiring liquid cooling. At these scales, every percentage point of conversion efficiency is measured in power plants. These are planning numbers, dated to 2026, and they will change; the waste formula will not.
H2: 1.4 Efficiency is a curve, not a number
P: A data sheet's headline efficiency is measured at one load, usually the best one. Real converters spend much of their lives elsewhere. At light load the fixed losses, gate drive, controller bias, magnetizing current, and the switching loss that does not shrink with current, are a large share of a small output, and efficiency collapses; at full load the conduction losses, which grow with current squared, take over. The curve therefore rises steeply, peaks somewhere between 30 and 60 % of rated load, and sags gently toward full load. The 80 PLUS Titanium standard for 230 V server supplies writes that shape into a requirement: at least 90 % at 10 % load, 94 % at 20 %, 96 % at 50 %, and 91 % at 100 %. A designer chooses where to put the peak by sizing the switches, and puts it where the product will actually run: near half load for a redundant server supply, whose two units share the load, and near light load for a phone charger that spends most of its life trickle-charging a full battery.
H2: 1.5 Efficiencies multiply
P: Power reaches a load through a chain of conversions, and the efficiency of the chain is the product of the stage efficiencies. Each stage also passes on the losses of the stages after it, because the upstream stage must deliver the downstream loss too. A chain of four stages at 98.5, 97.5, 96, and 90 % is only 83 % efficient overall, and the last stage, the least efficient, is also the one whose losses are amplified by every stage before it. This is why the most valuable efficiency improvement is usually at the point of load, and why removing a stage entirely, as the 48 V rack of Chapter 14 does, beats polishing any single one.
EQ: η_chain = η₁ × η₂ × … × ηₙ
H2: Worked Example 1.3
WEP: Power reaches a processor through a transformer at 98.5 %, a rectifier and power-factor stage at 97.5 %, an isolated DC-DC stage at 96 %, and a point-of-load regulator at 90 %. Find the overall efficiency and the input power and waste for 1 kW delivered to the processor.
WES: The product is 0.985 × 0.975 × 0.96 × 0.90 = 0.830. The input is 1000/0.830 = 1205 W, and the waste, by the book's formula, is 1000 × (1/0.830 − 1) = 205 W. Improving the last stage from 90 % to 93 % alone raises the chain to 0.857 and cuts the waste to 167 W, a saving of 38 W, twice the 18 W that a perfect, lossless transformer would bring.
H2: Worked Example 1.4
WEP: A 3 kW Titanium-rated server supply runs at 50 % load, where it is 96 % efficient. Its replacement is a Platinum unit at 94 %. Find the waste of each, and the yearly energy difference for a supply that runs continuously.
WES: At 1.5 kW out, the Titanium unit wastes 1500 × (1/0.96 − 1) = 62.5 W and the Platinum unit 1500 × (1/0.94 − 1) = 95.7 W, a difference of 33.2 W. Over 8760 hours that is 291 kWh per supply per year, before the cooling energy needed to remove it. A hall of 10 000 such supplies saves 2.9 GWh a year.
H2: 1.6 What a wasted watt costs
P: A watt of loss costs money twice. The utility charges for the energy, and the facility spends more energy removing the heat, which power usage effectiveness, Chapter 14, captures: at a PUE of 1.3, every watt lost inside a server costs 1.3 W at the meter. Over the five-year life of a server, a watt wasted continuously is 43.8 kWh, or 57 kWh at the meter. That is why server buyers evaluate supplies by total cost of ownership rather than price, and why a percentage point of efficiency on a 3 kW supply, 30 W, justifies a considerable premium.
H2: Worked Example 1.5
WEP: Find the five-year cost of 1 W wasted continuously in a data center with a PUE of 1.3, at $0.12 per kWh. What is a 30 W saving per supply worth across 10 000 supplies?
WES: 1 W × 8766 h × 5 = 43.8 kWh, times 1.3 is 57.0 kWh, costing $6.84. Thirty watts per supply is worth $205 over five years, and $2.05 million across 10 000 supplies, before counting the cooling and electrical capacity that the lower loss frees for more servers.
