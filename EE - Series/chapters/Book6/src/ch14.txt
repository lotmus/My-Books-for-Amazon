H1: Chapter 14. The Data-Center Power Chain
P: Chapter 1 used the data center to show that a percent of efficiency is worth megawatts. This chapter follows the power from the utility to the processor and counts the losses at each stage. It is facility power, shelf B of this book: transformers, uninterruptible power supplies, power distribution, rack power supplies, and the board-level converters that end at Book 7's 875 A at 0.8 V. The chain's overall efficiency is the product of its stages, so a small loss at each adds up to a large one in total.
H2: 14.1 The stages
P: A large data center takes power at medium voltage, tens of kilovolts, and steps it down in transformers that are about 99% efficient. A double-conversion UPS rectifies the AC to charge its batteries and inverts it back, about 96% to 97% efficient; in eco mode, bypassing the conversion when the grid is healthy, it reaches about 99%. Power distribution units and cables lose a fraction of a percent. In the rack, power supplies convert AC to a DC bus, 48 V in the Open Compute Project's rack architecture, at about 96% for the best grades. On the board, an intermediate bus converter may step 48 V to 12 V at about 97.5%, and the multiphase voltage regulators of Chapter 10 bring it to the processor's core voltage at about 90%.
EQ: ηchain = Π ηi,   waste = Pload (1/ηchain − 1)
H2: Worked Example 14.1
WEP: Take stage efficiencies of 99% for the transformer, 97% for the UPS, 99.5% for distribution, 96% for the rack supply, 97.5% for the bus converter, and 90% for the regulator. Find the chain efficiency and the waste per kilowatt delivered to the processors, and per 10 MW of IT load. Repeat with the UPS in eco mode at 99%.
WES: The product is 0.99 × 0.97 × 0.995 × 0.96 × 0.975 × 0.90 = 0.805. Per kilowatt delivered, the waste is 1000 × (1/0.805 − 1) = 242 W. For 10 MW delivered, it is 2.42 MW. With eco mode the chain rises to 0.822 and the waste falls to 2.17 MW, a saving of about 250 kW from one setting.
KEY: Key idea. A chain's efficiency is the product of its stages, so every stage counts. The last one, the processor's regulator, is usually the least efficient and carries the most current.
H2: 14.2 Why the rack bus voltage is rising
P: AI racks now draw over 100 kW. At a 48 V bus that is thousands of amperes in the rack's busbar, and conduction loss grows with the square of the current. Raising the distribution voltage cuts the current and the loss proportionally to its square. That is why the industry has moved from 12 V to 48 V racks, and why several vendors and the Open Compute community announced in 2025 plans for distribution at around 800 V DC to future racks, converting down close to the load.
H2: Worked Example 14.2
WEP: A 120 kW rack is fed through a busbar of 0.1 mΩ. Find the current and the busbar loss at 48 V and at 800 V.
WES: At 48 V the current is 120,000/48 = 2500 A, and the loss is 2500² × 0.0001 = 625 W. At 800 V the current is 150 A, and the loss is 150² × 0.0001 = 2.25 W, a factor of (800/48)² = 278 lower. The higher voltage brings its own costs: insulation, arc-flash protection, and touch-safety rules, and a high-ratio converter near the board.
H2: 14.3 Power usage effectiveness
P: The facility also spends power on cooling, lighting, and its own losses. Power usage effectiveness, PUE, is the facility's total power divided by the IT equipment's power; 1.0 would mean no overhead. Older enterprise data centers ran at 1.8 to 2.0; large modern sites report 1.1 to 1.2, and liquid-cooled AI halls aim lower. Conversion losses before the IT equipment, in the transformer and UPS, count as overhead in PUE, but losses inside the server, in its supply and regulators, count as IT power, so a PUE figure hides the board-level losses this chapter has counted.
EQ: PUE = Pfacility / PIT
H2: 14.4 Scale
P: JLL's 2026 outlook projects about 100 GW of new data-center capacity worldwide between 2026 and 2030, at construction costs around $11.3 million per megawatt for the building and up to about $25 million per megawatt including AI fit-out. At that scale, the 2.4 MW of conversion waste per 10 MW in Example 14.1 becomes tens of gigawatts across the industry, which is why each half-percent of converter efficiency in this book has a direct cost in power plants and cooling.
