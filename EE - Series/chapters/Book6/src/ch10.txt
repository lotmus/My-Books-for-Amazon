H1: Chapter 10. Compensating the Loop
P: Chapter 4 explained why a converter needs feedback and why the crossover frequency sets how fast it recovers from a load step. This chapter designs the compensator that sets that crossover. It follows the approach of Basso's Designing Control Loops for Linear and Switching Power Supplies: model the power stage's control-to-output response, choose a crossover and a phase margin, and place the compensator's poles and zeros to reach them. Feedback theory itself is Book 2's.
H2: 10.1 The power stage seen by the loop
P: In voltage-mode control the duty cycle drives the output through the LC filter, which contributes a double pole at f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)) and a phase drop of 180° above it; the output capacitor's equivalent series resistance adds a zero. In peak current-mode control an inner loop sets the inductor current directly, so the inductor drops out of the outer loop and the plant becomes a single pole formed by the load resistance and the output capacitance, plus the ESR zero, with a gain of R divided by the current-sense gain Ri. Current mode is simpler to compensate and limits current cycle by cycle, which is why most modern converters use it.
EQ: voltage mode: f0 = 1/(2π√(LC));   current mode: Gvc(s) ≈ (R/Ri)/(1 + s/ωp),  fp = 1/(2πRC)
H2: 10.2 Choosing the crossover and the margin
P: The crossover frequency fc is where the loop gain falls to 1. A higher fc gives a faster response, but the loop must cross well below the switching frequency, typically at a tenth or a fifth of it, because the converter is a sampled system, and below any right-half-plane zero, as Chapter 4 showed for the boost. The phase margin, how far the loop's phase is from −180° at fc, sets how much the response rings: 45° is the usual minimum, and 60° to 75° gives a well-damped step.
H2: 10.3 Type II and Type III compensators
P: A Type II compensator, built around an error amplifier with one capacitor-resistor network, provides an integrator for zero steady-state error, one zero, and one high-frequency pole. Placing the zero at or below the plant's pole cancels its phase drop, and placing the pole near the ESR zero or at half the switching frequency attenuates switching noise. It provides up to 90° of phase boost and suits current-mode control. A Type III compensator adds a second zero and pole, providing the extra phase boost needed to cross over above the LC double pole in voltage mode.
EQ: Type II: C(s) = (ωI/s)(1 + s/ωz)/(1 + s/ωp2);   with ωz = ωp, PM ≈ 90° − arctan(fc/fp2)
H2: Worked Example 10.1
WEP: The 1 V, 20 A buck of Chapter 9 uses peak current mode with a current-sense gain of 0.01 V/A, 1 mF of output capacitance, and a divider ratio of 0.6 to a 0.6 V reference. Its load is 0.05 Ω. Design a Type II compensator for a 50 kHz crossover, with its zero cancelling the plant pole and its high-frequency pole at 250 kHz. Find the plant pole, the compensator gain needed at crossover, the phase margin, and the output dip for a 10 A load step.
WES: The plant pole is at 1/(2π × 0.05 × 10⁻³) = 3.18 kHz. The DC gain R/Ri is 5, so at 50 kHz the plant gives 5/√(1 + (50/3.18)²) = 0.318, or −10.0 dB. With the 0.6 divider, the compensator must supply 1/(0.318 × 0.6) = 5.25, or 14.4 dB, at 50 kHz. With the zero cancelling the plant pole, the loop is an integrator times the 250 kHz pole, so the phase margin is 90° − arctan(50/250) = 78.7°. The load-step dip, with the formula of Chapter 4, is about 10/(2π × 50 kHz × 1 mF) = 31.8 mV.
KEY: Key idea. Compensation is pole-zero placement: model the plant, choose a crossover well below the switching frequency, and place the compensator's zeros and poles to leave adequate phase margin. Current mode turns the LC double pole into a single pole and makes this much easier.
H2: 10.4 Measuring the loop
P: The model is only a starting point; parasitic ESR, capacitor derating with voltage, and layout change the real loop. A network analyzer injects a small signal across a resistor inserted in the feedback path and measures the gain and phase around the loop directly, giving the true crossover and margin. Load-step testing confirms the result in the time domain: a well-damped loop shows one small overshoot; a marginal one rings. Both measurements belong to the design's verification, alongside the EMC tests of Book 7.
H2: Worked Example 10.2
WEP: A voltage-mode version of the same buck uses a 0.47 µH inductor. Where is the LC double pole, and why does a 50 kHz crossover need a Type III compensator?
WES: f0 = 1/(2π√(0.47 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁻³)) = 7.34 kHz. Above it the plant's phase falls toward −180°, so at 50 kHz the plant alone has almost no phase left. A Type II compensator, whose integrator already costs 90°, cannot restore it; the Type III's two zeros, placed near f0, supply the extra boost needed for a 45° to 60° margin.
