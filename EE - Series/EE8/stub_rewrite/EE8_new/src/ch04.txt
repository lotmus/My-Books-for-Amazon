H1: Chapter 4. The Boost, the Buck-Boost, and the Control Loop
P: The buck can only step down. Rearranging its switch, inductor, and diode gives the boost, which steps up, and the buck-boost, which can do either. They power LED strings from batteries, the 400 V DC link of a power-factor corrector, and every phone that keeps its supply constant as its battery discharges. This chapter derives their voltage ratios, shows the feature that makes the boost harder to control, the right-half-plane zero, and outlines how a converter's feedback loop is designed and how fast it can respond to a load step.
H2: 4.1 The boost converter
P: In a boost, the inductor is connected from the input to a switch to ground. When the switch is on, the inductor charges from the input; when it is off, the inductor's current flows through a diode or synchronous switch to the output, adding the inductor's voltage to the input. Volt-second balance over a cycle gives the ratio below. The input current is continuous, which suits batteries and power-factor correction, while the output current comes in pulses, so the output capacitor carries a large ripple current. The current through the switch and inductor is the output current divided by 1 − D, which grows quickly as the ratio rises.
EQ: Vout = Vin / (1 − D),    IL = Iout / (1 − D)
H2: Worked Example 4.1
WEP: A boost converts a 3.7 V lithium cell to 5 V at 2 A, switching at 1 MHz with a 1 µH inductor and 92% efficiency. Find the duty cycle, the average inductor current, the inductor ripple, and the battery current.
WES: D = 1 − 3.7/5 = 0.26. The average inductor current, which equals the input current in an ideal boost, is 2/(1 − 0.26) = 2.70 A. The ripple is VinD/(fswL) = 3.7 × 0.26/(10⁶ × 10⁻⁶) = 0.96 A. Including losses, the battery supplies 5 × 2/(3.7 × 0.92) = 2.94 A. A boost with a ratio of 4 or more draws large currents and suffers high conduction losses, and transformer-based converters, Chapter 5, become better choices.
H2: 4.2 The buck-boost family
P: The inverting buck-boost places the inductor to ground between the switch and diode, and produces Vout = −DVin/(1 − D), which is larger or smaller than the input in magnitude depending on D. The four-switch non-inverting buck-boost uses a buck stage followed by a boost stage sharing one inductor, and operates as a buck, a boost, or a mixture as the input passes through the output voltage. It is the standard choice for a 3.3 V rail from a single lithium cell, whose voltage falls from about 4.2 V to 3.0 V during discharge. The SEPIC and Ćuk converters achieve the same with coupled or additional inductors and a coupling capacitor.
EQ: Vout = −Vin D / (1 − D)   (inverting buck-boost)
H2: 4.3 Closing the loop
P: A converter's output must stay constant as its input and load change, so it is controlled by feedback. In voltage-mode control, an error amplifier compares the output with a reference and sets the duty cycle directly; the power stage then behaves as a second-order LC filter with a sharp resonance, and needs a type III compensator to give enough phase margin. In peak current-mode control, an inner loop switches off each cycle when the inductor current reaches a level set by the outer voltage loop. The inner loop turns the inductor into a controlled current source, removing one pole and simplifying compensation, and it limits current on every cycle. Its inner loop can oscillate at half the switching frequency when D exceeds 0.5, unless a compensating ramp is added. The usual practice puts the crossover frequency of the voltage loop at a tenth to a fifth of the switching frequency.
H2: 4.4 The right-half-plane zero
P: A boost has a feature the buck lacks. When the duty cycle increases to raise the output, the switch stays on longer, so less current reaches the output during that cycle and the output first dips before rising. That behavior is a right-half-plane zero in the control-to-output transfer function, at a frequency that moves with load and duty cycle. It adds phase lag like a pole while raising the gain like a zero, and no compensator can cancel it. The loop crossover must be kept well below it, typically at a fifth or less, which limits how fast a boost can respond.
EQ: fRHPZ = (1 − D)² R / (2π L)
H2: Worked Example 4.2
WEP: Find the right-half-plane zero of the boost of Example 4.1, whose load is 5 V/2 A = 2.5 Ω. What is the highest practical crossover frequency?
WES: fRHPZ = 0.74² × 2.5/(2π × 10⁻⁶) = 218 kHz. With crossover kept below a fifth of that, about 44 kHz, the boost responds much more slowly than a buck switching at the same 1 MHz could, whose crossover might be 100 to 200 kHz.
KEY: Key idea. A converter's response speed is set by its loop crossover, and its output capacitance must hold the voltage until the loop catches up. The boost's right-half-plane zero sets a ceiling on that crossover that no compensator can raise.
H2: 4.5 How large a load step can the output survive?
P: When the load current steps by ΔI, the output capacitor supplies the difference until the loop responds, about a quarter-period of the crossover frequency later. A useful estimate of the output deviation for a well-damped loop is ΔV ≈ ΔI/(2πfcC).
H2: Worked Example 4.3
WEP: A processor regulator must hold its output within 40 mV during a 50 A load step. Its loop crossover is 100 kHz. Find the output capacitance needed.
WES: C ≈ ΔI/(2πfcΔV) = 50/(2π × 10⁵ × 0.04) = 1990 µF, about 2 mF. Raising the crossover to 200 kHz would halve the capacitance. That is why processor regulators use many interleaved phases with high crossover frequencies, and why a modern board is covered with hundreds of small ceramic capacitors beside the processor socket.
