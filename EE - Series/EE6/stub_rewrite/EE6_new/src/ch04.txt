H1: Chapter 4. Linearity, Back-Off, and the Doherty Amplifier
P: Chapter 3's efficiencies are peak values, reached only when the amplifier is driven to its full output. The signals of Book 5 are not like that. An OFDM signal has a peak-to-average power ratio of 8 to 11 dB, so a linear amplifier that must pass its peaks spends most of its time at a fraction of its rated power, where its efficiency is poor. This chapter measures the linearity an amplifier needs, computes how much efficiency back-off costs, and shows how the Doherty amplifier, digital predistortion, and envelope tracking recover it. The Doherty is the default architecture of cellular base stations in 2026.
H2: 4.1 Compression and intermodulation
P: As drive rises, a real amplifier's gain begins to fall. The 1 dB compression point, P1dB, is the output power at which the gain is 1 dB below its small-signal value. With two closely spaced tones at the input, the amplifier's curvature generates third-order intermodulation products at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1, which fall inside or next to the wanted band and cannot be filtered out. Their power rises 3 dB for every 1 dB of input. Extrapolating the fundamental and third-order lines to their intersection defines the output third-order intercept, OIP3, a figure of merit that is typically about 10 dB above P1dB for a well-behaved amplifier.
EQ: OIP3 = Pout,tone + ΔIM3 / 2,  where ΔIM3 is the carrier-to-IM3 ratio in dB
H2: Worked Example 4.1
WEP: With two tones of 30 dBm each at the output, the third-order products are 40 dB below each tone. Find OIP3, and the IM3 ratio after backing the drive off by 3 dB.
WES: OIP3 = 30 + 40/2 = 50 dBm. At 27 dBm per tone, the tones have fallen 3 dB and the products 9 dB, so the ratio becomes 40 + 6 = 46 dBc. Each decibel of back-off buys two decibels of intermodulation ratio, which is why the first remedy for a failing spectral mask, a subject for Book 7's chamber, is often less drive, at a cost in efficiency.
H2: 4.2 What back-off costs
P: A class A amplifier draws constant DC power, so its efficiency falls in proportion to output power: at 8 dB below peak it is 50% × 10^(−0.8) = 7.9%. A class B amplifier's DC current falls with the signal amplitude, so its efficiency falls only with the square root of output power: η = (π/4)√(Pout/Pmax), giving 55.6% at 3 dB back-off, 39.4% at 6 dB, and 31.3% at 8 dB. For a 100 W-peak class B stage carrying a signal with 8 dB of PAPR, the average output is 15.8 W and the DC input 50.7 W.
H2: 4.3 The Doherty amplifier
P: William Doherty's 1936 design uses two amplifiers. The main, or carrier, amplifier runs in class AB or B and handles the signal up to a set back-off; the auxiliary, or peaking, amplifier runs in class C and turns on only for the peaks. A quarter-wave line between them acts as an impedance inverter. At low power the main amplifier sees twice its optimum load, so it saturates and reaches peak efficiency at half its full voltage swing, which is 6 dB below the combined peak. Above that, the peaking amplifier's current pulls the main amplifier's load back down toward Ropt, a mechanism called load modulation, and both reach full power together. In the ideal symmetric Doherty the efficiency is 78.5% both at peak and at 6 dB back-off, dips only to about 70% between, and falls linearly with amplitude below 6 dB.
EQ: Below 6 dB back-off:  η = (π/4) (v / 0.5),  v = normalized output amplitude
H2: Worked Example 4.2
WEP: A 100 W-peak amplifier carries a signal with 8 dB of PAPR. Compare the efficiency and DC power at the average output for class B and for an ideal symmetric Doherty.
WES: At 8 dB back-off the normalized amplitude is v = 10^(−8/20) = 0.398. Class B gives (π/4)(0.398) = 31.3%; the Doherty gives (π/4)(0.398/0.5) = 62.5%. For the 15.8 W average output, the class B stage draws 50.7 W and the Doherty 25.3 W, halving the DC power and cutting the dissipation from 34.8 W to 9.5 W. Real Doherty amplifiers reach 45 to 55% average efficiency on such signals, below the ideal because the peaking device turns on gradually and the inverter has loss and limited bandwidth. Asymmetric versions, with a larger peaking device, place the second efficiency peak at 8 to 10 dB to match the signal.
KEY: Key idea. A linear amplifier's average efficiency is set by the signal's peak-to-average ratio. The Doherty restores efficiency at back-off by modulating the main amplifier's load, and digital predistortion then restores the linearity the Doherty gives up.
H2: 4.4 Predistortion and envelope tracking
P: A Doherty amplifier is efficient but not linear: its gain and phase change with drive, the AM-to-AM and AM-to-PM distortion, and they depend on the recent history of the signal through heating and charge trapping, which are memory effects. Digital predistortion measures the amplifier's output through a feedback receiver, fits a model, often a memory polynomial, and pre-distorts the baseband signal with the inverse, so that the cascade is linear. It typically improves the adjacent-channel leakage ratio by 15 to 20 dB. Envelope tracking takes a different route: it varies the supply voltage with the signal's envelope, so the amplifier always runs close to saturation. It is widely used in phones, where a fast DC-DC converter can follow the envelope of a 100 MHz signal, and less in base stations, where Doherty is simpler at high power.
