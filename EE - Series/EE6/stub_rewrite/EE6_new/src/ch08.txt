H1: Chapter 8. Radar
P: Radar is the application in which this book's two halves meet most directly. A transmitter sends a pulse of hundreds of watts to megawatts, a target reflects a tiny fraction of it, and a receiver with the lowest possible noise must detect the echo. Because the signal travels out and back, the received power falls as the fourth power of range, not the square, and that one fact shapes everything else: the high peak powers, the long pulses compressed after reception, and the integration of many echoes. This chapter derives the radar equation, computes range, resolution, and Doppler, and finishes with the 77 GHz FMCW radar of a modern car. Antenna gain is taken from Book 4; if the last stage is a tube, its pulse modulator is Book 8's.
H2: 8.1 The radar equation
P: A transmitter of power Pt with antenna gain G produces a power density of PtG/(4πR²) at range R. A target of radar cross-section σ intercepts and reradiates σ times that density, which spreads again over 4πR² on the way back, and the receiving antenna, of effective area Gλ²/(4π), collects part of it. Multiplying gives the monostatic radar equation, with one antenna for both directions.
EQ: Pr = Pt G² λ² σ / [(4π)³ R⁴]
H2: Worked Example 8.1
WEP: A 10 GHz radar transmits 1 kW peak through a 30 dBi antenna at a 1 m² target 10 km away. Its receiver has a 1 MHz bandwidth and a 3 dB noise figure, and detection needs 13 dB of SNR. Find the received power and the maximum range.
WES: λ = 3.00 cm. Pr = 10³ × 10⁶ × (0.0300)² × 1/[(4π)³ × 10¹⁶] = 4.53 × 10⁻¹⁴ W, or −103.4 dBm. The noise floor is −174 + 60 + 3 = −111 dBm, so the required power is −98 dBm. The echo at 10 km is 5.4 dB too weak; since Pr ∝ R⁻⁴, the maximum range is 10 km × 10^(−5.4/40) = 7.31 km. Doubling the transmitter power would extend it only by 2^(1/4) = 1.19, to 8.69 km.
KEY: Key idea. Radar's echo falls as 1/R⁴. Doubling the range takes sixteen times the power, so radar designers buy range with antenna gain, pulse energy, and integration rather than with peak watts alone.
H2: 8.2 Pulses, energy, and resolution
P: Detection depends on the energy of the pulse, not its peak power: a matched filter's output SNR is the received pulse energy divided by kTF, as in Book 5. The range resolution, the smallest separation at which two targets appear distinct, is ΔR = c/(2B), where B is the pulse bandwidth; for a simple pulse of length τ, B ≈ 1/τ. A long pulse has more energy but poor resolution. Pulse compression removes the conflict: the transmitter sends a long pulse with a wide bandwidth, usually a linear frequency chirp, and the receiver's matched filter compresses it into a short one. The compression ratio is the time-bandwidth product Bτ.
EQ: ΔR = c / (2B),    Runamb = c × PRI / 2,    duty = τ / PRI
H2: Worked Example 8.2
WEP: The radar of Example 8.1 uses a 1 µs pulse at a pulse repetition interval of 1 ms. Find its range resolution, unambiguous range, duty cycle, and average power. It is changed to a 10 µs pulse chirped over 10 MHz at the same peak power. Find the new resolution, the compression ratio, and the new maximum range.
WES: With 1 µs, B ≈ 1 MHz and ΔR = 150 m. The unambiguous range is c × 1 ms/2 = 150 km, the duty cycle 0.1%, and the average power 1 W. The chirped pulse has ΔR = c/(2 × 10 MHz) = 15 m and Bτ = 100, or 20 dB of compression. Its energy is ten times larger, so its SNR rises by 10 dB, and the maximum range becomes 7.31 × 10^(10/40) = 13.0 km, while the resolution improves tenfold. The average power rises to 10 W at 1% duty cycle, which the transmitter's thermal design of Chapter 5 must accept.
H2: 8.3 Doppler
P: A target moving with radial velocity v shifts the echo by fd = 2v/λ, twice the one-way shift because both paths shorten. Pulse-Doppler radars measure that shift across a train of pulses to separate moving targets from stationary clutter. At 10 GHz, a target closing at 100 m/s shifts the echo by 6.67 kHz.
EQ: fd = 2v / λ
H2: 8.4 The 77 GHz automotive radar
P: Automotive radars use the 76 to 81 GHz band and a frequency-modulated continuous wave instead of pulses. The transmitter sweeps linearly over a bandwidth B in a time Tc, a slope S = B/Tc. The echo from a target at range R arrives 2R/c later, so mixing it with the current transmit signal produces a beat frequency fb = 2RS/c, which an FFT turns into a range profile. The resolution is again c/(2B): a 4 GHz sweep resolves 3.75 cm, enough to separate a pedestrian from a nearby car. A sequence of chirps gives the Doppler, and an array of receive antennas, Book 4's subject, gives the angle. The whole radar is a single CMOS or SiGe chip with a few milliwatts per transmit channel; the R⁴ law is overcome by the short ranges and by integrating hundreds of chirps.
H2: Worked Example 8.3
WEP: A 77 GHz FMCW radar sweeps 1 GHz in 50 µs. Find the slope, the beat frequency for a target at 50 m, the range resolution, and the Doppler shift for a closing speed of 30 m/s.
WES: S = 10⁹/(50 × 10⁻⁶) = 2 × 10¹³ Hz/s. fb = 2 × 50 × 2 × 10¹³/2.998 × 10⁸ = 6.67 MHz, within reach of an inexpensive converter. ΔR = c/(2 × 1 GHz) = 15.0 cm. λ = 3.89 mm, so fd = 2 × 30/0.00389 = 15.4 kHz.
