H1: Chapter 9. The Low-Noise Amplifier
P: Chapter 6 showed that the first stage of a receiver sets its noise figure. This chapter designs that stage. A low-noise amplifier must add as little noise as possible, provide enough gain to hide the stages after it, remain stable, survive the strong signals and transmitter leakage around it, and match the antenna well enough not to reflect the signal away. Those goals conflict, because the source impedance that minimizes a transistor's noise is not the one that maximizes its gain or its input match. The LNA designer's craft is resolving that conflict. Stability from S-parameters, which every amplifier must satisfy, is treated in Book 4; this chapter assumes a stable device.
H2: 9.1 The noise parameters
P: A transistor's noise figure depends on the impedance of the source driving it, which the input matching network sets. Four noise parameters describe the dependence: the minimum noise factor Fmin, the optimum source reflection coefficient Γopt at which it occurs, and the noise resistance Rn, which says how fast the noise rises as the source moves away from Γopt. A device with small Rn is forgiving; one with large Rn must be matched precisely. Manufacturers give the four parameters at each frequency, and they are measured with a tuner that sweeps the source impedance.
EQ: F = Fmin + (4Rn/Z0) |Γs − Γopt|² / [(1 − |Γs|²) |1 + Γopt|²]
H2: Worked Example 9.1
WEP: A transistor has NFmin = 0.5 dB, Rn = 8 Ω, and Γopt = 0.5∠60° in a 50 Ω system. Find its noise figure with a 50 Ω source, Γs = 0, and with Γs = 0.3∠60° and 0.5∠30°.
WES: With Γs = 0, F = 1.122 + (32/50) × 0.25/(1 × |1 + 0.5∠60°|²) = 1.122 + 0.64 × 0.25/1.75 = 1.213, or 0.84 dB. At Γs = Γopt the figure is the minimum, 0.50 dB. With Γs = 0.3∠60°, a partial step toward the optimum, it is 0.56 dB, and with 0.5∠30°, the right magnitude at the wrong angle, 0.62 dB. Driving the device straight from 50 Ω would cost 0.34 dB, which in a satellite ground receiver, where the antenna's noise temperature is only tens of kelvin, is a large fraction of the system noise.
H2: 9.2 Noise match versus gain match
P: The source impedance for minimum noise is generally not the conjugate of the device's input impedance, so a noise-matched LNA reflects part of the incoming power and has a poor input return loss. Two techniques reconcile them. Inductive source degeneration, a small inductance in the emitter or source, adds a real part to the input impedance without adding noise, moving the input match toward Γopt's conjugate; it is the standard narrowband CMOS and HEMT LNA. Balanced amplifiers place two identical noise-matched stages between 90° hybrids, so that their reflections cancel at the input port and appear instead in the hybrid's load, giving both low noise and good match at the cost of twice the devices.
KEY: Key idea. A transistor has one source impedance for lowest noise and another for best match. The LNA's input network chooses between them, and degeneration or a balanced pair lets it have both.
H2: 9.3 Gain and the cascade
P: The LNA's gain should be high enough that later stages hardly affect the system noise figure, but not so high that it overloads them or lowers the cascade's intercept point, as Chapter 6 showed. A lower-noise device with less gain may give a worse system than a noisier device with more gain. Any loss before the LNA, in a connector, a filter, or a switch, adds decibel for decibel to the noise figure, so in the most sensitive systems the LNA is mounted directly at the antenna, and sometimes cooled.
H2: Worked Example 9.2
WEP: An LNA is followed by a stage with a 6 dB noise figure. Compare device A, with 0.8 dB noise figure and 10 dB gain, and device B, with 0.6 dB and 6 dB gain. Then find the system noise figure if a 0.3 dB loss precedes device A.
WES: For A, F = 1.202 + (3.981 − 1)/10 = 1.500, a system noise figure of 1.76 dB. For B, F = 1.148 + 2.981/3.981 = 1.897, or 2.78 dB. The lower-noise device gives the worse receiver by a full decibel. With 0.3 dB of loss before device A, the system figure becomes 2.06 dB, and the loss has cost as much as the whole difference between the two devices' own noise figures.
H2: 9.4 Linearity and protection
P: An LNA in a phone or base station sits beside its own transmitter, and in a radar beside a kilowatt pulse. It must tolerate out-of-band blockers without compressing, survive the leakage that the duplexer or switch lets through, Chapter 15's subject, and recover quickly after an overload. Limiters, diodes that clip large signals before the LNA, protect radar receivers, but they add loss and therefore noise. GaN LNAs, which tolerate several watts of input, are increasingly used where survivability matters more than the last tenth of a decibel.
