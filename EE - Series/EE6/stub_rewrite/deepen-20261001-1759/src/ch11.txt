H1: Chapter 11. The Link Budget
P: A link budget adds up every gain and loss between the transmitter's output and the receiver's detector and compares the result with what the demodulator needs. It ties together almost every topic in this book and Book 5: power amplifier output, feeder loss, antenna gain, path loss, receiver noise, and the SNR or Eb/N0 that a modulation needs. Because everything is in decibels, the budget is a column of additions, and the bottom line, the margin, says how much fading or degradation the link can tolerate. This chapter builds a terrestrial budget in dBm and a satellite budget in the G/T form.
H2: 11.1 The terrestrial budget
P: The received power is the transmitter power, minus the feeder losses, plus both antenna gains, minus the path loss. In free space the path loss is the Friis term, which grows 20 dB per decade of distance and 20 dB per decade of frequency. The fade margin is the received power minus the receiver's sensitivity, which Book 4 defined as the noise floor plus the noise figure plus the required SNR. Real paths add atmospheric absorption, rain at millimeter waves, obstruction of the Fresnel zone, and multipath fading, so a microwave link is usually designed for a margin of 20 to 40 dB depending on the availability required.
EQ: Pr = Pt − Lt + Gt − Lpath + Gr − Lr,   Lpath = 20 log10(4πd f / c)
H2: Worked Example 11.1
WEP: A 5.8 GHz point-to-point link spans 10 km. The transmitter delivers 27 dBm, each feeder loses 1 dB, each antenna has 25 dBi, and the receiver sensitivity is −80 dBm. Find the path loss, the received power, and the fade margin.
WES: Path loss = 20 log10(4π × 10⁴ × 5.8 × 10⁹ / 2.998 × 10⁸) = 127.7 dB. Received power = 27 − 1 + 25 − 127.7 + 25 − 1 = −52.7 dBm. The fade margin is −52.7 − (−80) = 27.3 dB, comfortable for a clear line-of-sight path at this frequency. Doubling the distance to 20 km would add 6.0 dB of path loss and leave 21.3 dB.
KEY: Key idea. A link budget is a sum in decibels: transmit power plus antenna gains minus losses gives the received power, and the margin over the receiver's sensitivity is what protects the link against fading.
H2: 11.2 The satellite budget and G/T
P: Satellite links use a different bookkeeping. The transmitter's power and antenna are combined into the effective isotropic radiated power, EIRP, in dBW. The receiver's antenna gain and system noise temperature are combined into a figure of merit, G/T, in dB/K, because a larger antenna and a quieter receiver are interchangeable ways of improving the link. The carrier-to-noise-density ratio then follows from Boltzmann's constant, −228.6 dBW/K/Hz, and dividing by the noise bandwidth gives C/N. The system temperature is the antenna temperature, the sky noise the antenna sees, plus the receiver's equivalent temperature Te = 290(F − 1).
EQ: C/N0 = EIRP − Lpath + G/T − k  (dBHz),   C/N = C/N0 − 10 log10 B
H2: Worked Example 11.2
WEP: A Ku-band satellite at 12 GHz is 38,000 km from the ground station. Its EIRP is 52 dBW; the ground terminal's G/T is 15 dB/K. Find C/N0 and the C/N in a 30 MHz channel, clear sky and with a 3 dB rain fade.
WES: Path loss = 20 log10(4π × 3.8 × 10⁷ × 1.2 × 10¹⁰ / 2.998 × 10⁸) = 205.6 dB. C/N0 = 52 − 205.6 + 15 + 228.6 = 90.0 dBHz. In 30 MHz, 10 log10(3 × 10⁷) = 74.8 dB, so C/N = 15.2 dB in clear sky and 12.2 dB in the rain fade (strictly, rain also raises the sky noise and so lowers G/T a little more). Both exceed the roughly 6 to 10 dB that common satellite modulation and coding formats need.
H2: Worked Example 11.3
WEP: A ground antenna has 40 dBi of gain and sees an antenna temperature of 50 K. Its LNA has a 0.8 dB noise figure. Find the system temperature and G/T.
WES: Te = 290(10^0.08 − 1) = 58.7 K, so Tsys = 50 + 58.7 = 108.7 K, or 20.4 dBK. G/T = 40 − 20.4 = 19.6 dB/K. If the system temperature were 150 K, G/T would fall to 18.2 dB/K; the quieter LNA is worth about 1.4 dB, the same as a 17% larger dish diameter.
H2: 11.3 Margins and availability
P: The margin a link needs depends on how often it may fail. Rain fading at frequencies above about 10 GHz is described statistically: a link designed for 99.99% availability, about 53 minutes of outage per year, needs far more rain margin than one designed for 99.9%. Multipath fading on terrestrial links is countered by diversity, two antennas spaced vertically or two frequencies, rather than by power alone. Adaptive modulation, as in Book 5, trades throughput for margin, dropping to a robust format during a fade instead of losing the link.
