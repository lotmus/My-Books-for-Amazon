H1: Chapter 15. Transceiver Architectures and Isolation
P: A transceiver puts a transmitter that delivers watts next to a receiver that must detect signals near −100 dBm, often sharing one antenna. The ratio between them can exceed 120 dB, and keeping the transmitter from deafening its own receiver is a central problem of transceiver design. This chapter compares the ways of sharing the antenna, time division, frequency division, and full duplex, and works out the isolation each requires.
H2: 15.1 Time division and frequency division
P: In time-division duplex, TDD, the transmitter and receiver take turns on the same frequency, and a switch connects the antenna to one or the other. The receiver never has to listen while the transmitter is on, so the isolation required is modest, but the switch must change state quickly and guard times must separate the bursts. Wi-Fi, most 5G mid-band and millimeter-wave systems, and many radars use TDD. In frequency-division duplex, FDD, the transmitter and receiver work at the same time on different frequencies, separated by a duplex gap, and a duplexer of two filters, the subject of Chapter 5, separates them. FDD needs the duplexer to provide high isolation in two places: at the transmit frequency, to keep the carrier from overloading the receiver, and at the receive frequency, to keep the transmitter's noise out of the receive band.
H2: 15.2 Transmitter noise in the receive band
P: A power amplifier's output contains not only the carrier but broadband noise, and part of that noise falls in the receive band. The receiver cannot filter it, because it is at the wanted frequency, so the duplexer must attenuate it before it reaches the LNA. If the leaked noise equals the receiver's own noise floor, the floor rises by 3 dB, a 3 dB loss of sensitivity; designs usually keep the leak about 10 dB below the floor, which costs about half a decibel.
EQ: Nfloor = −174 + NF dBm/Hz,   desense = 10 log10(1 + 10^((Nleak − Nfloor)/10))
H2: Worked Example 15.1
WEP: An FDD receiver has a 3 dB noise figure. The transmitter's noise in the receive band is −135 dBm/Hz at the antenna port. What duplexer isolation at the receive frequency keeps the leak 9 dB below the receiver floor, and what is the desense?
WES: The receiver floor is −174 + 3 = −171 dBm/Hz. The leak must be −180 dBm/Hz, so the isolation must be −135 − (−180) = 45 dB. The desense is 10 log10(1 + 10^(−0.9)) = 0.51 dB. With only 40 dB of isolation, the leak would be −175 dBm/Hz, 4 dB below the floor, and the desense would grow to 1.46 dB.
KEY: Key idea. In FDD, transmitter noise that lands in the receive band cannot be filtered at the receiver, so the duplexer's isolation at the receive frequency sets how much sensitivity the transmitter costs.
H2: 15.3 The transmit carrier as a blocker
P: The transmitter's carrier, attenuated by the duplexer's isolation at the transmit frequency, arrives at the LNA as a strong blocker just outside the receive band. It can compress the LNA, generate intermodulation with other signals, and, mixed with the oscillator's phase noise as in Chapter 6's reciprocal mixing, raise the noise floor. Its own modulation can also leak through second-order nonlinearity in a direct-conversion receiver, creating a baseband interferer. The receiver's linearity must be specified against this known blocker.
H2: Worked Example 15.2
WEP: A handset transmits 25 dBm. The duplexer provides 50 dB of isolation at the transmit frequency. What carrier power reaches the LNA, and what does that imply?
WES: 25 − 50 = −25 dBm at the LNA input. The LNA's input compression point must be well above −25 dBm, typically by 10 dB or more, and its IIP3 must keep intermodulation between this carrier and other blockers below the noise floor. A receiver that was designed for weak signals alone would be overloaded by its own transmitter.
H2: 15.4 Full duplex on one frequency
P: Transmitting and receiving at once on the same frequency, full duplex, would double spectral efficiency, but the receiver must then remove its own transmitted signal almost completely. The cancellation is done in stages: antenna separation or a circulator gives a few tens of decibels, an analog canceller that subtracts a tapped copy of the transmit signal gives more, and a digital canceller that models the residual channel and nonlinearities removes the rest. Each stage must track changes in the antenna and environment. Full duplex is used today in some cable systems and in-band relays, and it remains a research topic for cellular radios.
H2: Worked Example 15.3
WEP: A full-duplex radio transmits 25 dBm and receives in a 20 MHz channel with a 5 dB noise figure. How much self-interference cancellation brings its own signal down to the noise floor?
WES: The noise floor is −174 + 10 log10(2 × 10⁷) + 5 = −96 dBm. The cancellation must be 25 − (−96) = 121 dB, all in the same band as the wanted signal, which is why it must combine passive isolation, analog cancellation, and digital cancellation, each contributing a few tens of decibels.
