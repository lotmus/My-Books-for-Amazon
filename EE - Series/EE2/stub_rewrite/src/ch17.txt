H1: Chapter 17 — Feedback Control: Plant, Sensor, and Controller
P: Chapter 11 wrapped an amplifier in a loop and found that the loop made the result depend on a precise resistor instead of an imprecise gain. A control system uses exactly the same idea on something that is not an amplifier at all: a motor, a heater, a joint of a robot arm. The forward path now contains a physical machine whose behavior is slow, uncertain, and sometimes nonlinear, and the feedback path contains a sensor. This chapter introduces the vocabulary and works one plant from start to finish, so that Chapters 18 and 19 can shape and then digitize the controller.
H2: 17.1 The Blocks and Their Names
P: The thing being controlled is the plant, P(s). The quantity you want, a speed or a temperature or an angle, is the reference r. A sensor H measures the actual output y. The controller C(s) takes the error e = r − Hy and computes a command u for an actuator, such as a motor driver or a heater switch. Disturbances d, such as a load torque or a draft of cold air, push on the plant from outside. With a perfect sensor, H = 1, the closed-loop relations take the same form as Chapter 11:
EQ: Y/R = C P / (1 + C P),    Y/D = P / (1 + C P)
P: The first expression says the output follows the reference when the loop gain C P is large. The second says the loop divides the effect of a disturbance by the same 1 + C P. Tracking and disturbance rejection are two faces of one quantity.

H2: 17.2 A First-Order Plant
P: Many plants, over the range a controller cares about, behave like a single time constant. A small DC motor driven by a voltage, viewed from voltage to shaft speed, is one: apply a step of voltage and the speed rises exponentially toward a final value. Two numbers describe it, a DC gain K and a time constant τ:
EQ: P(s) = K / (τ s + 1)
P: For this chapter, declare a motor with K = 2 (rad/s)/V and τ = 0.1 s, driven from a supply that can provide at most ±12 V. Open-loop, 5 V gives a final speed of 10 rad/s, reached to 63% after 0.1 s and to within 2% after about 0.4 s. Open-loop control works only as well as K is known; if the load or the temperature changes K by 20%, the speed changes by 20%.
H2: 17.3 Proportional Control
P: The simplest controller multiplies the error by a gain: u = K_p e. Substituting into the closed-loop expression gives another first-order system, with a new DC gain and a new time constant:
EQ: Y/R = K_p K / (τ s + 1 + K_p K):  DC gain K_p K/(1 + K_p K),  τ_cl = τ/(1 + K_p K)
P: The product K_p K is the loop gain at DC. The steady-state error, the fraction of the reference the output never reaches, is 1/(1 + K_p K).
WE: Worked Example 17.1
PROB: For the declared motor, find the steady-state error and the closed-loop time constant for K_p = 1, 5, and 20 V per rad/s. For a 10 rad/s step, find the initial drive voltage each gain demands.
SOL: For K_p = 1, the loop gain is 2, the steady-state error is 1/3, or 33%, and τ_cl = 0.1/3 ≈ 33 ms. For K_p = 5, the loop gain is 10, the error is 1/11 ≈ 9.1%, and τ_cl ≈ 9.1 ms. For K_p = 20, the loop gain is 40, the error is 1/41 ≈ 2.4%, and τ_cl ≈ 2.4 ms. At the instant of the step the error is the full 10 rad/s, so the command is K_p × 10: 10 V, 50 V, and 200 V. Only the first is inside the ±12 V the driver can supply. The other two saturate the driver, and the response is slower than the linear formula predicts until the error has shrunk enough for the command to come back inside the rails.
KEY: Proportional gain shortens the time constant and shrinks the steady-state error by the same factor, 1 + K_p K, but never removes the error. || Large gains also demand large initial commands, so actuator saturation, not the formula, often sets the real response time.
H2: 17.4 Integral Action Removes the Error
P: The steady error remains because a proportional controller needs a nonzero error to produce the nonzero command that holds the motor at speed. An integrator removes that need. If the controller adds a term proportional to the accumulated error, u = K_p e + K_i ∫e dt, then the only way for the output to be steady is for the error to be zero; any remaining error keeps the integral growing. A loop with one integrator in it is called a type-1 system, and it tracks a constant reference with zero steady-state error regardless of the exact value of K.
P: The cost is phase. An integrator contributes 90 degrees of lag at every frequency, and the loop can ring or oscillate if that lag is not managed. Chapter 18 shows how a PI controller places its zero so that the integrator's lag disappears at the frequencies that matter for stability.
H2: 17.5 Disturbances and the Load
P: Suppose a constant load torque slows the motor by an amount equivalent to a 2 V reduction in drive. Open-loop, the speed drops by K × 2 = 4 rad/s. Under proportional control with K_p = 5, the loop divides that by 1 + K_p K = 11, and the speed drops by about 0.36 rad/s. With an integrator, the speed returns exactly to the reference once the integral has built up the extra 2 V of command. This is often the stronger argument for feedback: a controller that tracks a reference well is useful, but a controller that holds the reference while the world pushes on the plant is what a machine needs.
H2: 17.6 How Fast Must the Loop Be?
P: A loop can correct only disturbances slower than its own bandwidth. As a first estimate, the closed-loop bandwidth in radians per second is 1/τ_cl; for K_p = 5 above, that is 110 rad/s, or about 17.5 Hz. The sensor must measure faster than this, and if the controller is digital, as almost every modern controller is, it must sample considerably faster. Chapter 19 makes that precise. Chapter 10 has already declared the joint tracker of this series, a 200 Hz loop with a 5 ms period; a common rule of thumb places such a loop's closed-loop bandwidth between about a tenth and a thirtieth of its sample rate, so roughly 7 Hz to 20 Hz.
MIS: A higher-bandwidth controller is not automatically a better one. It amplifies sensor noise further, demands more from the actuator, and approaches the frequencies where the plant model, with its single time constant, stops describing the real machine: shaft resonances, gear backlash, and sensor delay all live up there. A controller should be fast enough to reject the disturbances that matter and no faster.
H2: 17.7 Limits and the Safety Layer
P: Every real actuator saturates, every real sensor can fail, and every model is wrong somewhere. A control loop therefore sits inside limits it cannot override: current limits in the driver, position limits on the joint, and in a robot an independent safety controller that can stop motion regardless of what the loop commands. Chapter 10 stated the rule for this series and it bears repeating here: when the safety layer and the controller disagree, the safety layer wins.

SUM: A control loop applies Chapter 11's feedback to a physical plant: the output follows the reference as C P/(1 + C P), and disturbances are divided by 1 + C P. A first-order plant K/(τs + 1) under proportional control becomes faster and more accurate by the factor 1 + K_p K, but keeps a steady-state error; integral action removes it at the cost of phase lag. Actuator saturation, sensor noise, unmodeled dynamics, and the safety layer bound how aggressive a controller may be.
GLOS: Plant || The physical system a controller acts on, such as a motor, heater, or joint.
GLOS: Steady-state error || The difference between reference and output that remains after transients have died out.
GLOS: Type-1 system || A control loop containing one integrator, which tracks a constant reference with zero steady-state error.
GLOS: Disturbance || An external input that pushes the plant's output away from the reference.
FORM: Closed-loop tracking || Y/R = CP/(1 + CP)
FORM: First-order plant, P control || τ_cl = τ/(1 + K_p K); e_ss = 1/(1 + K_p K)
