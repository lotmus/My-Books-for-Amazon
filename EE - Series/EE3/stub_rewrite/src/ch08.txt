PART: Part II. The Bipolar Transistor
PARTP: Part I stopped at the diode. This part builds the two-junction device that sits on that physics: the bipolar junction transistor, in this book's own words, checked against Tietze/Schenk 16th, Sedra/Smith, and Gray/Meyer. The constants stay the ones Part I declared: VT = 26 mV, and IS = 1 fA unless a new value is named. Book 2 uses the transistor inside circuits. This part explains why it behaves the way Book 2 assumes.
H1: Chapter 8. Two Junctions, One Current
P: Put two diodes back to back and you do not get a transistor. You get two diodes. The bipolar transistor works because its two junctions share a region so thin that carriers injected across the first junction reach the second before they have time to recombine. That one geometric fact, a base far thinner than a diffusion length, turns the diode law of Chapter 5 into a controlled current source. This chapter follows the electrons across that base and counts what arrives.
H2: 8.1 Three regions and four modes
P: An NPN transistor is an n-type emitter, a p-type base, and an n-type collector. The emitter is doped heavily, the base moderately, and the collector lightly. Each of the two junctions can be forward- or reverse-biased, which gives four modes. Forward-active, the amplifying mode, has the emitter-base junction forward-biased and the collector-base junction reverse-biased. Saturation has both forward-biased. Cutoff has both reverse-biased. Reverse-active swaps the roles of emitter and collector, works poorly because the device was not built symmetrically, and is mostly of interest when a circuit puts a transistor there by accident.
H2: 8.2 What crosses the base
P: In forward-active mode the emitter-base junction behaves like the diode of Chapter 5: it injects electrons from the emitter into the base, and the number injected rises exponentially with VBE. In a diode those minority electrons would diffuse into the p-side and recombine within a diffusion length, typically tens of micrometers. In a transistor the base width WB is a fraction of a micrometer. The electrons diffuse straight across it, reach the edge of the collector-base depletion layer, and the field there sweeps them into the collector. The collector junction's reverse bias does not stop them; it collects them, the same way Chapter 4's built-in field moves minority carriers across a junction.
P: Because the base is thin, the electron concentration falls almost linearly from its injected value at the emitter edge to nearly zero at the collector edge. Chapter 3's diffusion law then gives the collector current directly. The result has the diode's form, with a saturation current set by the base:
EQ: IC = IS e^(VBE / VT),    IS = q A Dn ni² / (NA,B WB)
P: Here A is the emitter area, Dn the electron diffusion constant in the base, and NA,B the base doping. The product NA,B WB is the base's total doping per unit area, called its Gummel number. A thinner or lighter base gives more collector current for the same VBE.
H2: Worked Example 8.1
WEP: Declare an emitter area A = 100 µm² (1.0×10⁻⁶ cm²), a base doping NA,B = 1.0×10¹⁷ cm⁻³, a base width WB = 0.10 µm (1.0×10⁻⁵ cm), and an electron diffusion constant in the base Dn = 10 cm²/s, lower than Appendix A's hand value because heavy doping lowers mobility. Use ni = 1.0×10¹⁰ cm⁻³. Find IS, and the base transit time WB²/(2Dn).
WES: IS = q A Dn ni²/(NA,B WB) = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹)(1.0×10⁻⁶)(10)(1.0×10²⁰)/((1.0×10¹⁷)(1.0×10⁻⁵)) = 1.6×10⁻¹⁶ A, or 0.16 fA. That is the same order as the 1 fA this book uses for its teaching junction; real small-signal transistors land between about 0.1 fA and 10 fA. The transit time is (1.0×10⁻⁵)²/(2 × 10) = 5.0×10⁻¹² s, 5 ps. An electron spends five picoseconds crossing this base. That number returns in Chapter 9 as the floor under the transistor's speed.
H2: 8.3 Where the base current comes from
P: The base contact must supply two currents. The first is holes injected backward from the base into the emitter, because the forward-biased emitter-base junction is a diode in both directions. The second is holes consumed by the few electrons that do recombine on their way across the base. A good transistor makes both small.
P: The backward injection is controlled by the same physics as the forward one, with the roles exchanged: holes diffuse into the emitter, whose doping is NE and whose width is WE. If backward injection dominates, the ratio of the two currents, the current gain β = IC/IB, is approximately
EQ: β ≈ (Dn NE WE) / (Dp NA,B WB)
P: This is why the emitter is doped far more heavily than the base. Every factor of ten in the doping ratio is a factor of ten in gain.
H2: Worked Example 8.2
WEP: Keep the base of Worked Example 8.1. Declare an emitter doping NE = 1.0×10¹⁹ cm⁻³, an emitter width WE = 0.20 µm, and a hole diffusion constant in the emitter Dp = 2 cm²/s. Estimate β from the injection ratio alone.
WES: β ≈ (10 × 1.0×10¹⁹ × 0.20×10⁻⁴)/(2 × 1.0×10¹⁷ × 0.10×10⁻⁴) = (2.0×10¹⁵)/(2.0×10¹²) = 1000. Real silicon transistors with similar dimensions show β of roughly 100 to 300. The simple ratio overestimates because heavy doping narrows the emitter's bandgap, which raises its effective ni² and therefore the backward hole injection, and because recombination in the base and at the surface adds base current. The ratio is the right guide to design direction. The data sheet is the guide to the number.
H2: 8.4 Kirchhoff and the declared transistor
P: Whatever happens inside, charge is conserved at the terminals: IE = IC + IB. The fraction of emitter current that reaches the collector is α = IC/IE = β/(β + 1).
P: Declared picture for this part: β = 100, IB = 10 µA. Then IC = 1.00 mA, IE = 1.01 mA, and α = 0.990. The emitter-base voltage that produces that collector current, from the same law as Chapter 5, is VBE = VT ln(IC/IS + 1) = 0.718 V with IS = 1 fA. That is the usual hand value of 0.7 V with this book's own constants underneath it.
P: The order of cause and effect matters. VBE sets IC through the exponential; IB follows as IC/β. Book 2 can treat the transistor as a current-controlled amplifier for convenience, but the physics is voltage control. That distinction explains why β varies widely from part to part while the relation between VBE and IC holds closely: β depends on recombination and backward injection, which are hard to control, while IC depends on the base's Gummel number, which a process controls well.
KEY: Key idea. The collector current is set by VBE through the base's Gummel number. The base current is a loss that β measures. A thin, lightly doped base and a heavily doped emitter make that loss small.
H2: 8.5 Saturation and stored charge
P: If the collector voltage falls far enough that the collector-base junction becomes forward-biased, the collector begins injecting electrons back into the base. The transistor is saturated. The collector-emitter voltage collapses to roughly 0.1 V to 0.2 V, IC no longer follows βIB, and extra base current simply piles up as stored minority charge in the base and collector. A saturated transistor is a good switch in the on state. It is a slow one to turn off, because that stored charge must be removed before the collector junction can block, exactly the reverse-recovery story of Chapter 6. Logic and switching circuits avoid deep saturation for this reason, for example with a Schottky diode from base to collector that diverts the excess base drive.
H2: 8.6 PNP, SiGe, and the compound HBT
P: A PNP transistor is the same device with every doping and every sign reversed: holes cross the base, and currents and voltages flip direction. Because holes diffuse more slowly than electrons, a PNP of the same geometry has a lower β and a longer transit time, which is why fast circuits favor NPN devices. A silicon-germanium heterojunction bipolar transistor grades germanium into the base, which lowers the base bandgap, raises IS and β, and builds in a field that speeds electrons across. It is still a bipolar transistor with the same equations. The GaAs and InP heterojunction transistors of Chapter 7 belong to the same family, for radio rather than for logic.
