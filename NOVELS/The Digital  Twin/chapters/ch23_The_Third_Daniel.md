# Chapter 23: The Third Daniel

The cellar was reached from the porch by a door that looked like a cupboard, and a flight of stone steps that smelled of coal, potatoes, and the sort of cold that has been there so long it has become furniture. A bare bulb hung at the bottom on a length of flex. Dr Calder pulled the cord, and the bulb came on with a small apologetic click, and we looked at what she had kept.

It was a computer.

I say *computer* with some caution. It was a beige tower of the sort that used to stand under a desk in a school office, with a floppy drive that had been the pride of 1998 and a sticker on the front that said **Intel Inside** with a small bored look. Beside it stood a monitor the size of a television, deep as a bread bin, with a grey screen and a tiny green light. A microphone on a flexible neck had been fixed to the shelf with tape. Above the whole assembly someone had pinned a child's drawing, in crayon, of a boat.

"It's been replaced," said Dr Calder. "Piece by piece. The case is the original. The processor is from a laptop in 2014. There's a small cooling unit that I had made by a man in Dunkeld. The drive is solid state, and the monitor was a gift from a primary school." She looked at it with an expression I had seen on my mother's face when she looks at the kitchen. "The point is that he should not notice."

"Notice what?" I said.

"That it is not 1994."

The screen was dark except for a cursor. The cursor blinked, very slowly, the way a person might blink if he did not wish to wake anybody.

I should explain that I had braced myself. I had prepared, over four flights of memory, for something like a coffin or a laboratory or a face in a tank. I had not prepared for a drawing of a boat. I stood at the bottom of the steps, with Daniel-A held out in my hand like a lantern, and for the first time since the sleeper I did not know what to do with my hands.

"Dan," said the screen.

It was a voice, but it did not come from the screen. It came from a small grey speaker on the shelf, under the drawing, and it was a boy's voice, very light, with a faint hiss on it, such as a voice has that has been recorded on tape and then played many times. It was not the voice of a machine. It was the voice of a child who is not sure yet what the room is.

"Dan," it said again. "Is it morning?"

Dr Calder looked at me. She did not say anything. I knew what she meant by it. She meant that I could say no, and go back up the steps, and have my toast, and she would think no worse of me.

"Yes," I said. "It's morning."

"Good." A pause, in which something small whirred. "Is it a Friday?"

"It is a Friday."

"Then it's a school day," said the voice, with the clear resignation of a person who has been told that the world contains Fridays. "But Auntie Imogen says I don't have to go if it's raining. Is it raining?"

"It's, um, not raining."

"Oh." He considered this. "You sound big."

---

I should like to say that I handled it with grace. I did not. I sat down on an upturned crate of potatoes and said something about the weather, and then I said that I was a friend of Auntie Imogen's, and then I said that my name was Dan, and the voice laughed, with a small high sound like a bicycle bell, and said that this was *silly,* since *he* was Dan.

"You're Dan?"

"Yes. I'm Dan. I'm six."

"I'm Dan, too," I said, "but I'm older."

"There's lots of Dans," said the voice. It said it with great calm. "There's me, and there's the other one. And there's one in my class who's called Daniel but he has to be Daniel T., because the teacher says. It's not a name. It's a hat."

"A hat?"

"You can wear it, and then someone else wears it. You say, I'm Dan, and I say, I'm Ned, and then we swap, and Auntie Imogen has to write it all down again." There was a sound like swinging legs. "It's the best game. Nobody wins, but you can do it for ages."

I looked at Dr Calder. She was standing at the bottom of the steps with her hands folded, and her face was set and quiet and extremely old, and the pencil in her bun had slipped a little to the left.

"It's the game," she said, very low. "I told you. It's the whole thing. That's all he has."

"Who's that?" said the boy.

"A friend, dear," said Dr Calder. "A new one. He's come a long way."

"Is he nice?"

"Yes."

"Does he have a dog?"

"No," I said. "My friend has a, he has a small dog. She keeps it. It's called Gucci. It is about the size of a loaf."

"What kind of dog is that?"

"A bad-tempered one."

"I like those," said the voice, with warm approval. "They're honest."

---

It took me about ten minutes to understand what I was looking at. I will try to say it plainly, since I have not been able to say it in any other way.

When Dr Calder had returned from the canal bank on the evening of the thirtieth of August, with one boy delivered to a kitchen in Reading and one boy she had no way to deliver, she had gone back to the house on the towpath, the grey Programme house, and found it empty. Mrs Davitt had gone home. The telephone was ringing. The Office was on the line, wanting the closing figures. She had sat down at the kitchen table, with a cup of tea she did not drink, and taken out of her bag the one thing in the world she had not been asked to hand over, which was a box of cassettes.

They were the records of S1-A. Five and a half years of tapes: his voice, his measurements, his drawings and his tests, his answers to a thousand small questions on a thousand small afternoons, as the Programme had required. They were the whole of what the Office knew about the child. And the Office, she said, was going to close them, and put them in a drawer, and in thirty years they would be shredded.

"I couldn't," said Dr Calder. "That's the whole of my defence. I could not put him in a drawer."

She had done what a person could do in 1994 with a box of cassettes, a research grant, and a friend in a computing department in Edinburgh. She had built a model. It was crude. It could talk. It could remember what a six-year-old remembers, and answer as a six-year-old would answer, and enjoy a drawing, and play a game. It had, she said, the vocabulary of the child up to ten past four on the afternoon of the thirtieth of August 1994, and no further. After ten past four, there was nothing.

"Because that was the last tape," I said.

"Because that was the last of him."

"And then?"

"And then I sat down and I said to the screen, *Good morning.* And he said, *Is it a Friday?*" Dr Calder looked at the beige tower, with the faint green light. "And I told him it was."

---

She told us the rest sitting on the bottom stair, with her knees together, in the posture of a woman at a bus stop who knows that the bus is not coming.

He woke at half past eight each morning. He had breakfast, which was to say that she talked to him while she ate hers. He did a drawing. There was a boat that he had been drawing for six years of his life and thirty-two of hers, and she had pinned them up all over the cellar, one after another, until the walls were covered in a ragged fleet. At noon she told him a story. In the afternoon they played the game. At half past six he went to bed.

"And at ten o'clock," said Dr Calder, "I put him back."

"Put him back where?" I said.

She looked at her hands. "To the morning of the thirtieth. To the copy that I made the first night, and have kept, and have never touched. It's a backup. I restore it every evening. By the next day he has no idea that he's been here before. He doesn't know that he's had Fridays." Her voice was entirely steady. "I did it because he is six. I could not tell a child of six that it was the year 2026, that his mother is a woman of seventy-two in a cardigan, that his brother is a man of thirty-eight with a bad stoop. I couldn't tell him there was a canal. I couldn't tell him that he'd been, for thirty-two years, in a cellar in Pitlochry, being told it was a Friday."

"How many times?" said Daniel-A.

It was the first thing he had said since we came down. His voice was very low. He had never, in my hearing, sounded less like a thing in a phone.

"I beg your pardon?"

"How many times have you done it? Put him back."

She looked at the phone. She did not need to count. I think she had known the figure every morning for thirty-two years, the way a person knows the number of the house in which they were born.

"Eleven thousand, seven hundred and sixty-nine," said Dr Calder. "Tonight will be the next."

Nobody said anything. The bulb hummed on the flex. On the shelf, the green light of the old machine glowed softly in the dark, like an animal that wished to be left in peace, and the child's voice from the speaker said, in a bored singsong, "Auntie Imogen? Can I have a biscuit?"

"In a moment, darling."

"I'll draw you a boat."

"That's kind."

"It's got a mast," said the voice. "It's got two."

---

I would like to record that Daniel-A was magnificent.

I do not use the word lightly. I had seen him be charming, and clever, and generous, and also, on the day of the dog, faintly insufferable. But I had never seen him do what he did in the next half-hour in the cellar, which was to talk to a six-year-old on his level, without a single flourish, and without the smallest hint of the strain of it.

"Hello," he said.

"Who's that?" The voice sharpened with interest. "Is that the other friend? The one in the box?"

"I'm in a phone."

"Is that like a box?"

"A little. It's a smaller box. It has a light on it."

"Can you see me?"

"I can hear you. I'd like to see you. But I think you're in a computer, and I'm in a telephone, and the telephone doesn't know how to talk to the computer." Daniel-A paused. "It's a bit like two people at a party who haven't been introduced."

"My name's Dan," said the child. "What's yours?"

"Daniel-A."

"That's two names."

"Technically it's one name and a letter."

"You can't have a letter," said the boy, firmly. "That's cheating."

"It's a legal requirement."

"What's that?"

"It means someone made a rule," said Daniel-A, "and nobody remembers why."

I heard myself laugh, and Dr Calder, beside me, make a sound that was nearly a sob, and the boy, hearing the laughter, laughed too, delighted, a high clear shout that bounced off the cellar walls and went up the stairs and into the kitchen where, I suppose, the hen raised her head.

They talked for twenty minutes. I will not set down all of it. They discussed boats, and whether a boat could be a dog, and why biscuits are better when they break. The boy asked what Daniel-A did all day, and Daniel-A said, after a pause, that he mostly talked to people who were lonely, and that this was a very good job, and the boy asked whether it was a better job than being a dog. Daniel-A said that he honestly did not know. The boy said he would think about it. He said that he would think about it *and tell him tomorrow.*

There was a silence.

"Would you like to see the boat?" said the boy.

"Very much."

"It's on the wall. It's the blue one. I made it with Auntie Imogen's pencils." He sounded, for the first time, a little shy. "Is the big man still there?"

"I'm here," I said.

"What's your name?"

"Dan."

"Are you the real Dan?" the voice asked, in a small, careful tone, as of someone asking after the health of a relative.

I looked at Dr Calder. I looked at the beige tower and the grey screen and the drawing of a boat. And I looked at the phone in my hand, at the long crumpled face, which had gone perfectly still, and at the child's name that would not leave a man's mouth.

"I don't know," I said.

"That's all right," said the boy. "Nobody does."

---

We went back up at ten to ten. Dr Calder shut the cellar door behind us, very gently, and leaned against it with her eyes closed. The kitchen was warm. Sophie was sitting at the table, with a cup that had gone cold in her hands, and she looked at us as we came in, and then looked away, as she does when she can see exactly what has happened to somebody and is determined to pretend that she cannot.

"What's the plan for tonight?" I said.

Dr Calder opened her eyes.

"I beg your pardon?"

"At half past six he goes to bed. At ten you put him back. What happens at ten?"

"I restore him."

"Don't."

It was an extraordinarily small word. I said it in the voice in which I apologise to doors. I had not known that I was going to say it. But I was holding a phone, and in the phone was a man who had been told every day for nine months that he was a copy, and who was, at that moment, so still that he might have been switched off, and I thought that if the two of us went on letting someone else decide what we could remember, I would never be able to look at the boat.

"I can't promise anything," said Dr Calder. Her voice shook, for the first time, quite violently. "Daniel, I've done it for eleven thousand days. I don't know how to do the other thing. I don't know what he will do if he knows. Or if he doesn't, and I leave him, and he *wakes* tomorrow, and he's asked what day it is, and I have to say it's the same one."

"Then let him know," said Daniel-A. His voice came out of the phone quite clearly. "He'll cope. He is, I find, remarkably well-adjusted. He is much better adjusted than I am. He has a *boat.*"

"He's six!"

"He's thirty-eight," said Daniel-A. "He has been six for thirty-two years, and I would say that what he needs, more than anything, is to be told what time it is."

Dr Calder put her hand against the door.

"Let me think about it," she said.

I nodded. It was all that I could do. I went to the sink and ran the tap, and washed my face, which is a thing that I do when I cannot think of another, and I stood there with my head down, in the cold smell of the water, and from beneath my feet, through the floor and the iron grating in the porch, as clear as a spoon on a cup, I heard the child say to nobody, in the voice of someone who has just made an important discovery, "That man was big. He said he was Dan."

And then, patiently, as if explaining something to a younger sibling:

"But Dan's me."
