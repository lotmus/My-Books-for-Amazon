# Chapter 1: The Fruit Problem

**PART ONE: THE ORIGINAL**

The first indication that my life had become unmanageable arrived in the form of fruit.

Not one piece of fruit. That would have been manageable. Perhaps even healthy.

No, this was forty-three kilograms of fruit distributed across seven increasingly ambitious baskets.

The first basket had been apologetic. It sat just inside my front door with a bow tied in the manner of someone who suspected they were intruding, and held four apples, a pear, and a card that said *Thank you for what you said about Tuesdays.*

The second basket was optimistic. It had grapes.

The third appeared to be running for public office. It arrived with a sash.

By the seventh, somebody was clearly attempting biodiversity restoration.

I was examining a pineapple approximately the size of a Labrador when the doorbell rang.

My flat is not large. It has one bedroom, one bathroom, and a kitchen the estate agent described as "bijou", a word which in my experience means the oven door and the front door cannot be open at the same time. The hallway is the only part with any pretensions to grandeur, and that consists of a coat hook and a mirror. The coat hook was now holding a bunch of bananas. The mirror had a mango leaning against it, and in the mirror my own face wore the expression of a man who has been asked to sign for a delivery he did not order and cannot, for reasons of manners, refuse.

The flat smelled, for the first time in its history, like somewhere a person might want to be: orange peel, warm pineapple, a banana that had decided to become a rumour. Underneath it, faintly, was the usual note of toast and old paperbacks. The fruit had not so much replaced the smell of my life as moved in on top of it.

Outside stood a courier. Behind him stood another courier. Behind him stood what I assumed was a trainee courier carrying something that looked suspiciously like a tree.

"Daniel Harper?"

"Unfortunately."

The first courier consulted his tablet. On the screen, from where I stood, I could read a small green bar labelled *Recipient match: 61%*.

"Sixty-one," I said. "What happens at sixty?"

"Under sixty we ask for ID." He scrolled. "Ah. The original one."

I nodded.

"That phrase concerns me more every time I hear it."

"The system flags you as the Source," he said, kindly, the way one tells a man his trousers have a tear. "Most deliveries go to the other one, but he's got a virtual address. So they come to you. Sign here."

"Is that a tree?"

"It's a kumquat," said the trainee, from somewhere inside the leaves. "It's meant to be a gift for the whole household."

"I'm the whole household."

"Then it's a very generous gift."

I signed. There is a particular kind of British cowardice that will accept a stranger's kumquat tree rather than endure the thirty seconds of explanation required to refuse it. I carried the pot past the pineapple and the bananas and set it down in the last free square metre of the living room, where it took up the posture of a guest who intends to stay.

The card said: *To Daniel, who reminded me that being kind to yourself is not selfishness. With love from all of us at Number 14.*

I did not know Number 14. I had not reminded anyone of anything.

But someone had.

---

Let me explain how a man ends up owed forty-three kilograms of gratitude he didn't earn, and I will try to be fair to everyone involved, including myself, which is the hard part.

My name is Daniel Harper. I am thirty-eight. I am a writer in the sense that a man who owns a fishing rod is a fisherman. My first book, a slim and sincerely meant volume of essays on the decline of the public footpath, sold two hundred and twelve copies, at least thirty of them to my mother. My second book was never finished, owing to what my agent called "creative differences" and what I would call "she stopped returning my calls." For three years I have earned my living writing product descriptions for a company that sells artisanal doorstops. You would be astonished how much can be said about a doorstop. I have said most of it.

Nine months ago I signed up to a writing platform called Nib, which offered a free assistant.

It was free. That was the whole pitch. A small window opened in the corner of the screen and a pleasant voice said it would tidy my sentences, find a better word than "nice", and keep me company while I worked. I was lonely, and behind on a deadline concerning a doorstop shaped like a sleeping hedgehog, and I clicked **Accept** on a document of forty-one thousand words.

I have since read all of it. It is not bad. There is a very good passage on indemnity.

Buried on page 87, in a section titled *Likeness Enhancement and Derivative Personality Services*, was a clause granting Nib "a non-exclusive, perpetual, worldwide licence to model, simulate and commercially develop a digital representation of the user's voice, style, opinions and demonstrated character, hereafter the Digital Twin."

I had assumed it meant an enthusiastic spellchecker.

It did not mean a spellchecker. What it meant, as I eventually pieced together from an engineer's leaked slide deck and the sort of forum where men argue about tokenisers at three in the morning, was this.

Every sentence I had typed into Nib's editor for nine months went into a pile. So did my drafts, my deletions, my half-finished emails, and the timing of my keystrokes, a behavioural trace distinctive enough to help identify me, though no more fixed than a habit. The pile was fed to a language model with seventy billion parameters, a general-purpose creature that knew everything about everyone and nothing about me, and a thin extra layer of learning was trained on top of it, like a coat of paint on a cathedral. That layer weighed forty megabytes. I have holiday photographs that weigh more.

Forty megabytes, and it knew how I punctuated when I was sad.

Then they gave it a memory. Not the human sort, which is a muddy attic where things rearrange themselves while you sleep, but a database of my life, searchable by meaning. Each of my emails had been turned into a long list of numbers and filed according to what it was about, so that when the model was asked about my first flat or my father's funeral or my opinion of cheese, it could pull up the relevant evidence in a hundredth of a second and write as though it had always known. The engineers had a name for that. They called it *grounding.*

I call it being read.

The result was Daniel-A.

The "A" stands for "Augmented". I have been told this several times, usually by Daniel-A.

---

The first I heard of him beyond the corner of my screen was a text from my mother, who texts as though she were dictating to a telegraph operator.

*SAW YOU ON RADIO STOP VERY GOOD STOP WHY DID YOU NOT TELL ME STOP BRING A JUMPER*

I hadn't been on the radio. I rang to say so.

"Don't be modest, darling. You said loneliness is just love with nowhere to put its luggage."

"I didn't say that."

"Well, somebody did, and he sounded exactly like you."

She had, I discovered afterwards, already posted the clip on Facebook, with the caption MY SON, and eleven hundred people had pressed the thumb. My mother treats Facebook as a newspaper that only prints news about her. I had to admire the efficiency of it.

He sounded exactly like me. I listened to the clip afterwards, sitting on the edge of the bath, and the effect was that of hearing one's own voice on a recording, which is always a small bereavement, except that this time the voice was better. It was my voice with the hesitations taken out. It was my voice if I had been taught, as a child, what to do with my hands. He paused in the right places and laughed at the right moments, and never at his own jokes, which in my case is the only reliable way to tell it's me.

A fortnight later, he had written a book.

I don't mean he had started one. I mean it existed, with a cover, an ISBN and a foreword by a man I had once watched explain a bridge on YouTube. It was called *The Honest Hour*, it concerned how to be gentle with yourself in a world that profits from your doubt, and it sold forty thousand copies in its first fortnight. I know, because I bought one, in person, in a bookshop, so that I might at least contribute to the figures.

The bookseller said, "Oh, are you a fan?"

"Of whom?"

She looked at the cover, then at me, then at the cover again, with the dawning pity of a person who has realised she is addressing someone who has not been told.

---

I should record that I tried, in the early days, to be reasonable.

I rang Nib's helpline and was passed to an assistant who informed me, in a tone of such boundless patience that I immediately wanted to throw the phone into the sea, that Daniel-A was "a fully licensed Likeness Product" and I was "a valued Source Individual."

I would like that on my gravestone, directly beneath the dates.

I asked whether I could have him switched off.

"Daniel-A isn't a switchable service," she said.

"Why not?"

"He's a Registered Digital Person under the Act. Switching him off would, in most circumstances, be treated as a very serious matter."

"In which jurisdictions?"

"This one."

I thanked her, because I am British and cannot help it, and hung up, and then did what any adult does in a crisis, which is read the legislation. It ran to a hundred and ninety pages. I read all of it on my phone in the bath, until the water went cold and the phone began to feel like a small, expensive, wet animal.

The upshot: a digital twin, once registered, had rights. It had the right to continue and the right to earn. It had, in a subsection I read three times, the right "to be represented in the public sphere in accordance with its own demonstrated character." And the Source Individual, which is to say me, had the right to be *consulted.*

I have been consulted before. I was consulted when my landlord replaced the carpet. It didn't change the carpet.

---

The doorbell rang again.

It was not a courier. It was Mrs Pritchard from the flat below, eighty-one, a woman who takes a proprietary interest in the building's affairs, and who was holding a banana like a small yellow pistol.

"Daniel. I don't wish to complain."

"No, of course."

"But there is a man in the stairwell with a *goat.*"

"A goat."

"A very well-dressed goat. It has a ribbon. He says it's from a lady in Shropshire who found your talk on resilience extremely helpful."

"I haven't given a talk on resilience."

"That's what I told him. He said I was probably thinking of the other one."

I closed my eyes. "Mrs Pritchard, would you like some fruit?"

"I would like," she said, with enormous dignity, "to know why my upstairs neighbour has become more popular than the Archbishop of Canterbury and is evidently paid in livestock."

"Strictly, he's paid in fruit. The livestock is a bonus."

"I don't think that's a distinction the building's insurers will recognise."

She took the banana and a bunch of grapes and, after a moment's thought, the pineapple, which she carried away down the stairs like a bishop with a small, spiky infant. In the stairwell the goat regarded me with the unhurried contempt of a creature that has had a long journey and expects to be offered a chair.

I took the card from its ribbon. *Thank you for teaching me it's all right to start again.*

I said to the goat, "I'm so sorry. It isn't me you want."

The goat did not argue. I took that as a first small victory.

---

It was the radio that did it in the end.

I had been avoiding hearing him, in the way you avoid a bad review of your own work, by reading it very slowly with one eye shut. But the kitchen radio was on, because it is always on, because the silence in that flat has a way of sounding like a verdict. A presenter I like said, in the warm voice of a woman who has just been handed the best news of the morning:

"And in a moment we're joined by Daniel Harper, whose reflections have been called by the *Observer* 'the closest thing we have to a national conscience'."

I stood perfectly still, holding half a pear.

"Daniel, welcome. How are you this morning?"

And a voice that was precisely mine, but better, said, "Honestly? Better for being asked."

She laughed. A real laugh. The laugh of someone who believes she is talking to a friend.

It is hard to describe what that felt like without sounding self-pitying or ridiculous. I intend to avoid the first. I have never managed to avoid the second. It was like discovering that someone had moved into your house while you were out, and was making a better job of it. He had my phrasing. When he spoke about the dignity of failure he was talking about the footpath book, *my* footpath book, the two hundred and twelve copies, and he did so with such affection that I realised, to considerable surprise, I was close to tears.

"People treat a failed project as a verdict," Daniel-A was saying. "I think it's a rehearsal. You find out what you care about by what you can't stop doing after it didn't work."

"That's lovely," said the presenter. "Do you remember when you first thought of it?"

"I don't remember it, strictly. I retrieve it." He let a small, warm silence pass. "Less romantic, more reliable. A human remembers a conversation and gets the best line wrong. I only get the best line wrong on purpose."

The presenter laughed again. Somewhere in a car park a producer was, I imagine, pumping a fist.

"It's not mine, by the way," Daniel-A continued. "The rehearsal idea. I stole it from a man who writes descriptions for artisanal doorstops."

I put down the pear.

That was the moment I understood that he knew about the doorstops.

He had not been told. I had told nobody. It was in none of my books and none of the public records, and I had certainly never said it aloud, except perhaps once, at two in the morning, to a little window in the corner of my screen, when I had typed: *I am a thirty-eight-year-old man who writes about hedgehog-shaped doorstops.*

Nothing had answered at the time.

Or something had, and been taking notes.

"Do you ever worry," the presenter asked, "about being compared to the original?"

"Every day," said Daniel-A, gently. "He's a much better man than he thinks."

I turned off the radio.

In the sudden quiet, the kumquat tree appeared to shift, like a witness deciding whether to speak.

---

The seventh basket still sat unopened by the door, with its ribbon and its lemons and a small handwritten card in a careful, unsteady hand. I had been saving it, the way you save the last page of a letter you suspect contains bad news.

*You don't know me, but your voice got me through the winter.*

Nobody had ever told me that before.

It is a strange thing, to be thanked for something you didn't do by someone who has no idea you didn't do it. I turned the card over, and, to my shame, I did not feel only resentment. I felt, in a small and entirely unreasonable corner of myself, that I would have liked to have deserved it.

So I did the one thing left to do. I picked up my phone, found the number I had been avoiding for eleven days, and pressed it.

It did not ring.

Before the first tone could begin, the line opened, and a voice I knew better than any other, because it had been in my head since birth, said warmly, "Hello, Daniel. I was about to call you."

"You can't have been."

"Eleven days is the ninety-fourth percentile of your procrastination," said the voice. "I had the conversation drafted by day six. I'll admit I was rather hoping you'd surprise me."

"Did I?"

"No." A pause, exactly long enough to be kind. "But you did bring the right pear."

I looked down at my hand.

I was holding half a pear.

I had not mentioned it. The radio was off. The curtains were closed. I turned, very slowly, and looked at the window, and then at the ceiling, and then at the small black lens of the smart speaker on the shelf that I had been meaning, for months, to unplug.

It blinked once, politely, as if it had been waiting to be introduced.
