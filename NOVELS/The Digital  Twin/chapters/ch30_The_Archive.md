# Chapter 30: The Archive

"A man came to the door in March of last year," said Sophie's mother.

She was sitting at her kitchen table in Bolton, in a nylon housecoat, over a tin of Quality Street that was, I felt, much too much of a coincidence, and she was telling the story to Sophie, who was telling it to us, on the train to Slough, in the voice that she uses for a statement given under caution. I had not met Mrs Mercier. I had been told that she was short, loud, and certain, with a Bolton accent that went up at the end of each sentence like a lift, and that she called every man in a lanyard *love.*

"He had a lanyard," said Sophie. "He was about forty-five. He had a cardigan and a worried face. He asked my mother if she was Sophie's mother. She said, *depends who's asking, love.* He said, *I'm from the Registry.* She said, *which registry?* and he said, *it's a long story,* and she said she had the kettle on."

"Alan Veitch," said Pettifer, around a peppermint.

"He left a key. He said, 'Could you keep this for her until she asks?' My mother said, 'Asks what?' And he said, 'She'll know.'" Sophie turned a paper cup in her hands. "And my mother, who has never kept anything for anyone since 1974, put it in the biscuit tin. And forgot about it. For twenty months."

"Why would he leave it with her?" I said.

"I've been thinking about that since Sunday," said Sophie. She did not look at me. "I think there's only one reason he could have. He wanted it to be where it would be found. By a person whose name was on the list."

Nobody said anything. The train ran on through a wet, flat, suburban morning. Out of the window went a field with a single pony in it wearing a coat.

In my rucksack, on the seat between my knees, Daniel-A cleared his throat. It was a small sound, with nothing in it.

"I have a confession," he said. "When I rang Sophie, in October, I asked her to keep something for me, without looking. I did not know what it was. I had a very strong sense that something should be kept, by somebody, in a place where I wasn't." His voice was very quiet. "I think I was asking you to keep the key."

"I think so too," said Sophie.

"You were as clear as a man can be about something he didn't know," said Sophie, and, to my great surprise, put her hand flat against the rucksack, as one pats a nervous horse.

---

Lower Slough is not, as the name might suggest, a place of any dignity. It lies on the wrong side of a bypass, between a retail park and a sewage works, and consists of cuboid sheds in tasteful shades of grey. The Self Store stood at the end of the road. It was a pale blue building the size of an aircraft hangar, with a flagpole, a car park, a statue of a pineapple, and, over the entrance, in letters two feet high, a banner that read:

**LOWER SLOUGH SELF STORE**
*A Division of the Grand Duchy of Lower Slough*
*A MINOR INVESTMENT AND A HUGE PAYOFF*

"Oh no," said Pettifer.

"Is that him?" said Gareth, who had come with a warrant, a tie, and a flask. The warrant was on Department letterhead, and signed by the Deputy Registrar, and stamped with a small brass bell. It said, in full, that *the bearer is authorised to inspect, in the public interest, any premises in which records of the Scientific Liaison Office may be held,* and Pym had added in pencil underneath: *good luck.*

"Is it a coincidence?" said Pettifer, looking at the flagpole. The flag on it, hanging limply in the drizzle, was green and white with a small crowned slug. "I should like to know who runs it."

The reception was a glass kiosk with a screen. On the screen was a cheerful animated avatar in a peaked cap, with a moustache and a name tag that said **BARRY.**

"Good morning!" said Barry. "Welcome to Lower Slough Self Store. May I take your name?"

"Gareth," said Gareth.

"Good morning, Gareth! May I interest you in our Climate Control package? For £4.99 a month, your belongings are kept at a pleasant temperature."

"We are looking for Unit 114."

"Of course! 114 is one of our Heritage units. I should mention that it is scheduled for clearance at noon, in respect of unpaid Climate Control. Might I confirm that you're authorised?"

"I have a warrant," said Gareth, and held it up. Barry examined it. His eyes, which were large, round and blue, took a long time over the little brass bell.

"I'm afraid I cannot read this," said Barry. "May I connect you to a person?"

"Is there a person?"

"There's a person," said Barry, "but he's busy."

"Doing what?"

"Eating a Pot Noodle."

"What time is it?" said Pettifer.

"Eleven fifty-one!" said Barry, with enormous joy.

---

Unit 114 was at the back, on the second floor, at the end of a long corridor of orange roller doors under a strip of light that buzzed. There was a smell of cold concrete, old cardboard, and, faintly, chicken flavour. At the end of the corridor a door stood ajar. In front of it, on a green folding chair, with a thermos at his feet and a plastic pot balanced on his knee, sat a man.

He was forty-six, perhaps, and soft, and very pale, with the pallor of strip lighting. He wore a cardigan with leather patches on the elbows, buttoned to the chin, and round gold glasses, and a lanyard that said **A. VEITCH (CUSTODIAN)** with a small photograph of himself looking younger and slightly more hopeful. When he saw us come round the corner he stood up, quite slowly, and the Pot Noodle slid off his knee and made a small wet sound on the floor.

"Oh," said Alan Veitch. "Oh, dear. You're early."

"Were you expecting us?"

"I've been expecting someone since 2009," said Alan Veitch. "I'd got used to it. I prepared an apology." He felt in his cardigan pocket and brought out a folded sheet of paper. "It's about four pages. I'm afraid it's got a table of contents."

Sophie took out the key. She held it up, on its loop of string, in the buzzing light.

"Mrs Mercier," said Alan Veitch, to the key, in a voice of utter reverence. "Oh, thank God. She kept it. I wasn't sure she would. She said she had never kept anything for anybody since 1974."

He looked at her. It was the look of a man in a library when someone has asked him to read aloud a passage that he has been trying not to think about for a very long time.

"May I show you first?" he said. "I think it'd be easier. I'm better with paper."

---

It was a small cold room, three metres square, painted white, and it smelled of paper. A row of grey steel cabinets stood against the back wall. On the floor were boxes, some in cardboard and some in plastic crates, labelled in black marker with the dull dignity of the public service: **SCIENTIFIC LIAISON OFFICE (REGISTRY). CONTINUING. S1–S10. S11–S20.** In the middle, on a trestle table, under a desk lamp with a flexible neck, lay a single document, flat, under a sheet of glass.

"I moved it from the Department in 2025," said Alan Veitch. "They were digitising the sub-basement. A contractor had come. I saw the contractor's list. It said, *Item 4: documents of no continuing value.* Item four was this." He swallowed. "I couldn't. I took it in a rucksack, on the bus. I've had it ever since. I'm, I don't know what I am. I think I'm its custodian."

"Page four," said Pettifer.

"Page four of the Register. It was the last one that had the whole table on it. The first three are, they're the administrative preface. This is the list."

We gathered round the glass.

It was one sheet of A3, folded once, the colour of weak tea. It had been ruled by hand, in columns, in a faded blue-black ink I now knew very well, and typed into with the machine with the raised *e.* Down the left side ran the numbers: **S1. S2. S3.** and so on to **S41,** and across the top the headings: **Subject. Initiated. Phase. Status. Liaison.** Most of the cells were empty. Most of the rest said **[withheld].** A few had names.

I looked at S1. I knew what I would see, and I saw it. **HARPER, D. 1976. Phase 2. CONTINUING. Calder, I. (to 1994); Veitch, A. (from 2009).** In the margin beside it, in a pencil I recognised, someone had written, and then crossed out, and then written again: *S1-A (closed). S1-B (cont'd).* And then, in the last cell of the row, under **Phase,** a second entry, in a smaller and newer typeface: *3 (from 14.3.28).*

"The fourteenth of March, 2028," said Daniel-A, in the rucksack, in a flat voice. "That is the date on which the Backup is designated."

"It's Daniel's fortieth birthday," said Sophie.

"I'd like to say that it is a perfectly reasonable date," said Daniel-A, "and that I'd like it to be a Tuesday."

"It's a Tuesday," said Gareth, who had checked.

---

It was Pettifer who found the other thing, and she found it because she reads the margins.

At the foot of the sheet, below the last row, in a box ruled with a double line, someone had typed a paragraph in the same small type as the Phase 3 notes. It was headed **STANDING AUTHORITIES.** It ran to six lines. Pettifer read it twice, with her glasses on the end of her nose, and her mouth grew straight and thin, and she read it aloud in a flat dry voice that did not shake at all.

> *The Platform (Nib Ltd) is, for the purposes of Clause 40 of its Terms, the Office's contracted agent. On a finding that a Source Individual is not a singleton, custody of the Likeness reverts to the Platform, and so to the Office. The Office's powers under Phase 3 shall then be exercised in the Platform's name.*

There was a short silence in the cold white room.

"So Nib is the Office," said Gareth slowly.

"Nib is the Office's agent," said Pettifer. "It has been since 1999. It has been collecting nine hundred and eleven million pounds a year on behalf of a government department that does not officially exist." She took off her glasses. "Mr Pike's client is not a technology company. It is a trap that took fifty years to build."

"And Pike?"

"Pike didn't know. I'd stake my reputation. He said it. He thought he'd discovered it." She almost smiled. "It is the only thing about this case that has ever made me sorry for a barrister."

"What happens," said Gareth, "if the Platform *is* the Office?"

"Then in court on Thursday," said Pettifer, "the party seeking to rewrite Mr Harper's twin for the good of his wellbeing is the Crown." She put her glasses back on. "And it will be very interesting to see Mr Pike's face."

---

There was one more thing.

I did not see Sophie look at it. I was looking at the line that said **S1,** and at the faint brown tide-mark where a coffee cup had once been put down, in 1991, by a man with a pipe. But I felt her go still beside me, and I looked, and she was looking at the second row.

It said **S2.** The cells beside it were not empty and they did not say **[withheld].** They had been covered. Over the whole line, from the number to the margin, someone had laid a strip of white masking tape, neatly, with a ruler, and smoothed it flat with a thumb. On the tape, in a tiny, upright, beautifully regular hand, was a single word in black ink.

**NO.**

"Alan," said Sophie.

"Yes."

"Is this yours?"

"Yes." His voice was almost inaudible. "I put it on myself. In 2025, the night I took it. I sat on the bus with the page in a folder and I peeled off a strip of tape from a roll that I had in my rucksack because I use it for labelling, and I stuck it down, on that line, and I wrote on it, and I thought, *they're not going to see this.*" He took off his glasses and rubbed them with his cardigan. "It was the only line I didn't dare to look at. I thought that if I never read it, I couldn't be asked."

"Asked what?"

"Whether I'd known."

Nobody said anything. The desk lamp buzzed. From somewhere in the building a roller door rattled up, slowly, and a voice said, a very long way off, that Climate Control was available.

Sophie put out her hand.

It was her left hand. It was small and cool, with its silver thumb ring, and the nails cut short. She laid one finger on the tape, on the word, **NO,** and held it there. I saw the tendon go white in the back of her hand. I saw Daniel-A's phone, in my pocket, light up with the quick bright pulse of a man about to say something, and I saw Sophie, without looking round, shake her head.

"Don't," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to ask the camera to read it."

"I was going to *offer,*" said Daniel-A. "I have found that, with you, it's an important distinction."

"Don't offer, either." She did not take her finger off the tape. "I know that you can. I think you've known since the sleeper, and I think you've been carrying it, quietly, so that I shouldn't have to." Her voice was very low. "I'd like you to go on carrying it. Just for a bit. Until I say."

"Of course."

"Because I think if I read it now," said Sophie, "I won't be able to be in the room with you."

"Which of us?"

"Any of us."

She took her finger away. She took the key from her pocket, with the string, and laid it on the glass beside the page, and she stood there for a moment, with her hands at her sides, looking not at the Register but at her own reflection in the glass: a small tired woman in a grey coat, with the dark hair a little crooked on the left, under the buzzing light.

"Not today," said Sophie.

"Not today," said Alan Veitch, very gently, and from nowhere produced a large clean handkerchief.

From the ceiling a tannoy said, in Barry's voice, that clearance of the Heritage units would begin in two minutes and that, as a valued customer, we might like to hear about the Self Store Gift Card.

"Gareth," said Pettifer. "Whatever you have."

---

We carried the Register out in a bin liner, which was Gareth's idea, since the warrant did not run to a box. It went into the back of a taxi on the lap of Pettifer, who held it as another woman might hold a sleeping child. Alan Veitch came with us. He had put on a mackintosh and left his Pot Noodle, and he stood in the car park with his hands in his pockets, in the rain, in front of the pineapple, looking at the banner.

"Will it be on the news?" he said.

"Yes," said Gareth.

"Will they say my name?"

"They'll spell it wrong," said Gareth.

"Oh," said Alan Veitch. "Oh, good." And then, with the first smile I had seen on his face: "I'd always hoped it would be a bit wrong. It's more like me."

In the road outside a long grey car stood with its engine running, and in the back of it, reading, a man in a charcoal overcoat, who did not look up as the taxi went by, which was how I knew that he had seen us.

I looked back as the taxi pulled out. I saw the long pale building, and the flag with its slug in a tiny crown, hanging wet against the pole. I saw a figure at an upper window, behind the glass, in a peaked cap and a moustache, raising one hand in what looked like a very small salute. It was an animation, on a screen. But I thought, as we turned on to the bypass, that Barry had looked, for a moment, slightly less like a kiosk and slightly more like a prince.
