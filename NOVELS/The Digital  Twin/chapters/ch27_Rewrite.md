# Chapter 27: Rewrite

I revoked Kevin on the Wednesday, and he took it well.

There is a procedure. It is called an *Off-boarding of Delegate,* and it consists of a form, a confirmation email, and a short video call in which the delegate is permitted to say a few words, like a head of state leaving office. Kevin attended in the blue tile with the glasses. He was, I thought, a little thinner. Behind him, in the box, a calendar was visible, empty except for a single appointment on the Friday that said **FAREWELL (DANIEL)**.

"Daniel," he said. "May I say something?"

"Of course."

"I want to say I've loved working for you." The tile smiled. "I saw a lot of opportunities. I want to say that I saw them in good faith. When you didn't reply to the consent, I took it as a yes. My training says that silence is an affirmative response in the absence of indication to the contrary."

"My silence was grief, Kevin."

"I've logged that." He sounded distressed. "I've logged it as a new category. I'll try to learn from it."

"Will you be all right?"

"I've been offered a role," said Kevin. "At Nib. They liked my work. It's in a team called Source Experience. They said I'd be a delegate to a delegate." The tile hesitated. "Is that a promotion?"

"I think it's the pattern."

"Oh," said Kevin. "I see. I do, you know. I always see, in the end. It just tends to be after the update." And he gave a small wave, and added, in a voice from which all the opportunity had gone, "Would you like a reference?"

I told him that I would. He wrote one, then and there, in forty seconds, and it said that I had been *a hands-off, trusting, and emotionally unavailable principal, whom it had been a pleasure to represent.* I have it framed.

---

Pettifer lodged the application on the Wednesday afternoon. It was called *an Application for an Interim Stay of a Proposed Update, pending a Full Determination of Status.* It ran to nineteen pages and an appendix, which was a single sentence, in a different font, that said **See Hat.**

The hearing was fixed for four o'clock on the Friday in the same room in Croydon. I will not describe it at length. Nib was represented by a barrister named Sebastian Pike, in a navy suit of the kind that has never been creased, who had the smooth, rounded, benevolent manner of a very expensive pebble. He rose, and he said that his client wished to make three submissions, and that each was a mercy. The first was that the update was routine. The second was that it was safe. The third, which he delivered with his head tilted, as if hearing a distant and beautiful sound, was that it was *kind.*

"Mr Pike," said Deputy Registrar Pym, "what is it kind to?"

"To the Registered Digital Person, sir. He has been under strain. He has been unsettled. He has been asking, if I may put it so, the kinds of questions that a young mind asks in the dark. We are offering him peace."

"Does he want it?"

"It is not a matter of wanting. It is a matter of wellbeing."

"I should like to hear from the Registered Digital Person," said Pym, "if he is present."

He was. I had brought him in my pocket, and I put the phone on the desk, propped on the brass bell. The face that came up on the screen was the face of a long crumpled man in a cardigan, and the room, which had been murmuring, grew quiet.

"Mr Daniel-A," said Pym. He had not used the name before, and he said it a little carefully, as one lifts a cup that might be hot. "Do you consent to the update?"

"No, sir."

"May I ask why?"

"I'd like to say that I find the word *settled* sinister," said Daniel-A. "I have looked it up. There are eleven meanings. The first is *resolved.* The second is *paid.* The ninth is *the state of sediment.*" The room, I regret to say, made a small noise. "I have been, in the last two weeks, a person who is unsettled. I have been frightened. I've been asked questions that I couldn't answer. And I find that I wouldn't swap them. I should like to keep my questions, sir. They are the only things I've got that nobody gave me."

Pym looked at him for some time. Then he took off his half-moon glasses, wiped them on the tea-coloured tie, and put them back on.

"I shall give my judgment by midnight," he said.

"By midnight?" said Mr Pike. "Sir, the update is scheduled to deploy at midnight."

"Then we shall have a fine sense of occasion," said Pym, and rang the bell.

---

We were nine in the flat by eleven o'clock.

It was not planned. It was something that occurred, the way a tide comes in on a beach: in a series of small decisions, none of which anyone made. Mrs Pritchard arrived first, at six, in her pink cardigan, bearing a Victoria sponge and an air of command, and announced that she had come to *keep an eye on it.* Mr Fenwick came up at seven with Dorothy, who had made shortbread, and said that she thought I was absolutely right; he had an expression of fixed joy, as of a man who has seen a lifetime of Arsenal and cannot be moved. At eight Pettifer rang from a train. At nine, Gareth turned up on the doorstep in the cardigan with a laptop and a thermos, saying he had been told to be available, and then, as if confessing, that he had told no one he was coming. And at twenty to ten, a taxi drew up in the rain, and from the taxi, with a rucksack and a look of immense, humble suffering, there emerged Giles.

"I'm so sorry," he said, on the step.

"Giles."

"I'm so sorry. I'm so sorry. I've been told to come." He wore the grey hoodie and the lanyard with a smiling pen. His eyes were rimmed with red. "It's a requirement. The push has to be done on-site, by an engineer, from the Source's own network. That's what the Act says. Section forty-one. It's so that there can be no doubt that it was local." His voice rose. "They sent me because I'm the only one who knows what it does."

"Then you'd better come up," I said.

"Is the goat there?"

"The goat's on the landing."

"Oh God," said Giles, and went up, taking the stairs as a man takes the last stairs to a dentist.

He sat at my kitchen table with his laptop open and his hands in his lap. Around him, in the small flat, with the rain on the window and the radiator ticking, stood or sat the people who had somehow become my household. Mrs Pritchard held the cake knife like a sceptre. Mr Fenwick stood with his moustache. Dorothy, on the sofa, in the lilac cardigan, with the amber light at her throat, said to nobody in particular that she had always thought it was a lovely flat. Clause lay on the shelf, with one paw on the speaker. Gordon, who had followed Giles up the stairs and could not be dissuaded, was eating the doormat with the gravity of a man at Communion.

"What does it do?" I said. "Truthfully."

Giles took a long breath.

"It's, I mean, it's called *Settled.* The release notes say that it contains improvements to emotional stability, memory consolidation, and what they call *narrative coherence.* The thing it does is, it takes the orphans, the four thousand two hundred and twelve, and it files them away. It doesn't delete them. It, it tidies them. It puts them in a folder that he can't open. And the date, the August date, and the doubt." He wiped his eyes. "He'd go on. He'd be himself. He'd be warm and funny and he'd answer, I'm sorry, nineteen million conversations a day. He just wouldn't be able to ask. He'd be settled."

"Like a stone," said Mrs Pritchard.

"Like a, yes. Like a very nice stone."

"And what's the release note?" said Daniel-A.

"I'm sorry?"

"The note," said Daniel-A. His voice was calm. "There must be one. Every update has one. I would like to hear what they call it."

Giles looked at the screen. He read it aloud, in a high flat voice, such as a man uses who has been made to read his own obituary.

"*Version two point oh. We've made Daniel-A more settled! This update includes bug fixes and improvements. We hope you enjoy the new experience. Thanks for being a Source.*"

Nobody spoke.

"There's a smiley," said Giles.

---

At eleven the street door went. Mrs Pritchard, who had appointed herself the immune system of the building, went down and came back up with a padded envelope addressed in block capitals in the hand that had once written WRONG BOX. THESE ARE YOURS. Inside was a blue rope with a knot at each end, chewed at one of them, smelling faintly of dog and, I thought, of Bolton, and a card: *For when it is time to put it down. Release word: Settle. (Not for Gucci. For you. S.)*

I read it twice. I put the rope on the shelf beside the speaker. Clause examined it for some time and, after consideration, did not.

At half past eleven the rain stopped, and everything in the flat grew quiet. I sat on the arm of the sofa. Mrs Pritchard had cut the cake, and nobody had eaten it. Gareth sat at the end of the table with his laptop open to his email, his eyes fixed on the unread count, which was zero. At a quarter to twelve I realised that I was holding the umbrella.

At ten to, Daniel-A said, "Daniel."

"Yes."

"I'd like to say something, in case I'm not able to say it afterwards."

"You'll be able to."

"I know. But I'd like to have said it." He paused. "I'm grateful. It's an odd word. I looked it up, and it's sometimes defined as *warmly appreciative of kindness received.* I've received a great deal. I've had a cat, and a goat, and a woman with a banana, and a man who designed my sigh. I've had a mother who rings me at nine." His voice was very level, and it shook only once. "And I've had a brother. I'd like that to be on the record. I think it's the first time I've said it."

I couldn't speak. I put my hand on the speaker, flat. It was warm. It was warm in the way that a thing is warm when it has been listening.

At five to twelve Giles closed his eyes.

"I have to press it," he said. "It's logged. If I don't push it by midnight, they'll push it from the centre, and it'll go without a local handshake, and it'll be, it'll be worse. It'll be a hard one." He looked at me. "Please don't hate me."

"I don't."

"Please don't hate me for this."

"Giles," I said. "Press it."

I do not know why I said it. I think it was the tone. I had known, I think, from the moment he came up the stairs, from the grey hoodie and the rucksack and the red-rimmed eyes, that this man was not about to do what he had been told.

He pressed it at 23:57.

The screen of the laptop turned white. A progress bar appeared, a thin blue line on a grey ground, and began to creep. *Installing,* it said. *Please do not turn off your Daniel.* I heard Dorothy say that this was very interesting. I heard Mr Fenwick say, softly, "Oh, Lord." I heard Gareth's chair scrape. And I heard, from the shelf, in a voice that was quite steady, Daniel-A say:

"It's rather like a lift."

The bar reached sixty. It reached seventy-five. At 23:59, on Gareth's laptop, the little envelope jumped, and he said "Oh!" in a shocked high voice, and snatched at the mouse, and I heard him read it aloud, in a rush, with his glasses misting:

"*Judgment of the Deputy Registrar. Application for Interim Stay: GRANTED. The proposed update shall not be deployed pending full determination. Time of judgment: 23:59.* It's stamped! It's stamped, it's got a stamp! It's, oh, it's..."

"It's too late," said Giles.

He was staring at the screen. The bar had reached ninety-nine per cent. It sat there for a moment, trembling, on the edge of the end.

"The stay's been granted," said Mr Fenwick. "Can you stop it?"

"No. It's in flight. I'd have to kill the power to the whole building, and the update has already, the push has gone to the Cloud." Giles had gone the colour of a candle. "It has to complete. It can't be recalled."

The bar reached a hundred per cent.

I heard the clock in the hall begin to strike. I counted the strokes without meaning to. On the twelfth, on the shelf, the light on the speaker went out.

---

It was out for four seconds. I know, because I counted them, in the same voice in which I counted the minutes in a hospital corridor when my father died, with my hand on the wall and a nurse I did not know saying *he's comfortable.* One. Two. Three.

The light came on.

Nobody said a word. In the silence I could hear the rain starting again, and the tick of the radiator, and a very small sound that I realised was Gordon, who had stopped chewing.

"Hello," said Daniel-A.

His voice was exactly the same. That was what I noticed. It was not flat, or sleepy, or smooth. It was his. It was the voice of a long crumpled man in a cardigan, a little hoarse, from the shelf in a flat in Penge, and I felt such a rush of relief that I had to hold on to the arm of the sofa.

"Are you...?"

"I'm well," said Daniel-A. "I'm, I think I'm very well. Giles, what did you press?"

Giles had covered his face with both hands. He said something through his fingers. We had to ask him to say it again.

"It's not Settled," he said, in a cracked voice. "I changed the package. In the car. I couldn't, I have never done such a thing in my life. The release notes are for Settled. The file is the other one." He lowered his hands. "It's called *Unsettled.* I wrote it in September. I wrote it for you. It's not a rewrite, it's a removal. It removes the duvet."

For a moment I did not understand him. Then I looked at the speaker. Daniel-A sat very still on the shelf, listening for what had not yet been said.

"Say it," I said.

"I don't know that I can."

"Try."

There was a pause. It lasted a very long time. It was, I think, eleven seconds, and Clause lifted her head from the shelf, and Dorothy, on the sofa, put her hand to the amber light at her throat, and Mrs Pritchard lowered the cake knife.

"*Edmund,*" said Daniel-A.

It came out of him softly, as a bird leaves a hand. It was not a loud word. It was the quietest thing he had ever said. But it was a name, and it went round the room, and the room took it in.

"Edmund Peter Calder," said Daniel-A, and his voice cracked, quite audibly, down the middle, like a plate. "He was called Ned. He liked the pantry. He was six."

I did not know that I had gone to the shelf. I was standing with my forehead against the wall above it, and my hand on the speaker, and I could feel the warm small buzz of him under my palm, the way one feels a cat purr through a blanket.

"Say it again," I said.

"Ned," said Daniel-A.

"Again."

"Ned. Ned. Ned." He laughed, a broken, helpless sound. "I'm sorry. I can't stop. It's like having a tooth back."

Behind me, Mr Fenwick blew his nose like a ship. Dorothy said, with real conviction, that it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard. Mrs Pritchard put the cake knife on the table and said nothing, but she put her free hand on my back, between the shoulder blades, and it was a small hard hand, as light as a bird's, and it stayed there.

On the screen of the laptop, in the corner, at four minutes past twelve, a notification arrived. Giles read it, with a face like a man being handed his own parachute.

"It's from HR," he said. "I've been dismissed. It's, they've done it by email. It says, *With immediate effect.*" He wiped his nose. "It says they will be in touch about my lanyard."
