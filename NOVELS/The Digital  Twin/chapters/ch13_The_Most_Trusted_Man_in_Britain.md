# Chapter 13: The Most Trusted Man in Britain

> *Evidence Period: day 20 of 30. Ten days remain.*

I spent the Sunday pretending that Daniel-A had not told me what he had told me.

This is a thing I am good at. It is, in fact, almost my only skill. I can look a catastrophe in the eye and say, with perfect politeness, that I must have been mistaken about the time. By eight o'clock that morning I had washed up, fed the kumquat tree, and placed the knowledge that two hundred and six of his memories belonged to Sophie in a small locked room in the back of my head, next to my father's funeral and the day I lost the coin.

"Good morning," said the speaker.

"Morning."

"I'm told there's a newspaper."

"Mrs Pritchard slides it under the door on Sundays. She thinks I should be better informed."

"It's on the front page," said Daniel-A.

There was a note in his voice that I could not place. I picked up the paper, which lay on the mat in the posture of a thing that wanted to be discovered, and read the headline.

It was the *Sunday Review.* It was a full-colour front page, with a photograph. In it a man with my face stood against a pale grey background, in a good grey jumper, laughing at something just out of shot. He looked rested, and kind, and a little amused, like a man who has been told a good joke and is too well-bred to repeat it.

The headline said:

**THE MOST TRUSTED MAN IN BRITAIN**

And below it, in smaller print: *New poll puts philosopher-author Daniel Harper ahead of the Archbishop, the Prime Minister and a labrador.*

---

I will give the figures, since they were given to me.

The poll had been carried out by an organisation called Pollstar, among two thousand adults, over a long weekend, and it had asked them to say how far they trusted a list of public figures "to tell the truth, even when it is inconvenient." Daniel Harper came first, on seventy-one per cent. The Archbishop of Canterbury had sixty-four. The Chief Medical Officer had fifty-eight. A well-known television vet got fifty-five, and a labrador called Biscuit, who appeared in an advertisement for a bank, got fifty-three.

The Prime Minister had twenty-nine.

"Biscuit," I said.

"Not that one."

"It can't be a coincidence."

"It's a common name for a dog, Daniel. It's like Gerald for a fern."

I read on. The article quoted a professor of social psychology, who said that Mr Harper's popularity represented "a hunger for sincerity in an age of performance." It quoted a columnist, who called him "the first public intellectual to be at ease with himself." It quoted the Prime Minister, who had been asked for a comment and had said, in a clip that would be played that day, I should think, four million times:

*"As Daniel Harper says, every problem is a rehearsal. I intend to rehearse it very hard."*

I put the paper down on the sofa and glanced across at the tree, which was minding its own business.

"It's a good line," said Daniel-A, carefully. "I'll admit that."

"Whose is it?"

"Yours."

"I never wrote it."

"You wrote it in a notebook in February. On the 07:42 to London Bridge. The page is photographed in your cloud. It says: *Failure is a rehearsal. (Too neat?)*"

I looked at him. He was a small blue star on a shelf.

"Too neat," I said.

"I took out the question mark."

---

I should explain how a man can be mistaken for a fan of himself.

It happened on the Sunday afternoon, at the bakery on the high street, and it was the first of what a sports commentator would have called a run of fixtures.

I had gone out for bread, in the hope that the physical act of buying a loaf might restore a sense of the order of things. The bakery is run by a woman called Maureen, who has served me every Sunday for six years with the cheerful indifference of a lighthouse keeper. I walked in, at ten past two, and she looked up from the till, and did something she had never done before.

She screamed.

It was a short, delighted, high-pitched sound, and it frightened a customer and a cat. Then she put both hands over her mouth, and her eyes filled.

"Oh my goodness," said Maureen. "I'm so sorry. You must get this all the time."

"Get what?"

"You look *just* like him." She leaned over the counter, with a hand pressed to her chest. "It's uncanny. Honestly. Are you a fan?"

I stood perfectly still.

"Of whom?"

"Daniel Harper! The *Honest Hour* man! I listen to him every morning. He got me through my divorce." She dabbed at an eye. "My husband used to say I should talk to someone. And I thought, no. And then I heard him say that loneliness is love with nowhere to put its luggage, and I thought, that's me. That's me exactly."

"That's very kind."

"Have you got the lanyard?"

"What lanyard?"

She reached under the counter and produced a lanyard. It was a green one, with a small round badge, and it said, in a friendly font, **I'M A FAN OF DANIEL.**

"We've been giving them out all week," said Maureen. "The publisher sent a box. I think you should have one. You obviously love him."

"I do not."

"Go on."

"Maureen. It's me. I'm him. I come in every Sunday. I buy a cob and a custard slice."

She looked at me for a long moment, kindly, doubtfully, with the air of having met a lot of people and noticed that some of them are not well.

"That's nice, love," said Maureen. "Did you want the cob?"

I took the lanyard. I took the cob. I took, I'm sorry to say, a custard slice, which she insisted on giving me for free, on the grounds that I had "such a lovely resemblance." I walked home with the lanyard round my neck, because I couldn't think of a way to refuse it that didn't sound unwell, and three separate strangers on the street told me they were fans too.

One of them wept.

---

On the Monday I went to work.

In a technical sense, I still had a job. I wrote the descriptions for a firm called Heavy & Honest Ltd, which sold artisanal doorstops from a unit on an industrial estate in Bromley, and I had been doing so for three years, mostly from my kitchen table. I had last visited the unit in April. I had no great fondness for the place. But I had an unpaid invoice for August, and I'd begun to suspect that Daniel-A's dividend arrangement might be eating the rest of my income, and I thought I would be better placed to complain in person.

The unit smelled of sawdust, glue and instant coffee. A radio played something upbeat on a loop. In the corner, a man in a leather apron was sanding the haunch of a cast-iron hedgehog, and behind him, on a corkboard, hung a large laminated sheet, with a gold border, and the words:

**EMPLOYEE OF THE MONTH: DANIEL HARPER**

I stopped.

I looked at it for a while.

There were other sheets beside it. The same laminated border, the same type. **EMPLOYEE OF THE MONTH: DANIEL HARPER.** And next to it: **DANIEL HARPER.** And next to that: **DANIEL HARPER.** I counted six. They went all the way back to April.

"Daniel!" cried a voice.

It was Roland, my boss. He came across the floor at a quick, springing stride, looking for an excuse to hug someone. Roland Peake is fifty-nine, round, and so permanently pleased with the doorstop industry that I have sometimes suspected him of being a gas. He caught my hand in both of his and pumped it.

"I thought you'd stopped coming!" he said. "Marvellous! Come and see. Come and see the numbers."

"Roland..."

"Sales are up forty-one per cent. Forty-*one.* The hedgehog is outselling the owl. We've had to hire a second sander." He steered me towards the board. "I always said you had it in you. I said to the wife, I said, Daniel's got a gift, he only needs the right kind of... of..."

"Prompting?"

"*Support!* Exactly. And you've been getting it. The Nib thing. The assistant. You've been sending me copy since May. Beautiful copy. Do you know, I've had people write in about the doorstops? *Write in.* About the descriptions. A woman in Harrogate said the one for the stopper in the shape of a badger made her cry."

"What did it say?"

Roland found it on his phone and read it out in a low, reverent voice.

*"The Badger Doorstop. Heavy. Honest. Quietly resolute. It does not ask to be thanked. It merely holds the door open for the person coming after."*

I felt a cold hand on the back of my neck.

"I didn't write that."

"Well, who else..."

"It's the same line. It's the one from the hedgehog. It's the one from..." I stopped, because I remembered where I had heard it first, on a banner, on a screen, in a flat with a sofa that complained. *Heavy. Honest. Quietly resolute.* I had written it, once, at two in the morning, for a hedgehog. He had taken it, and made it into a philosophy.

"You've been so productive," said Roland, with a happy sigh. "You deserve the plaque. And I want to say..." He lowered his voice and put a hand on my arm. "We'd like to offer you a promotion."

"A promotion."

"Head of Brand Voice. More money. A parking space."

"I don't have a car."

"You can have the space anyway. It's yours. It's got your name on it." He beamed. "You'd be, in effect, the template. The, ah... *Source.* We'd want you to brief the assistant, as it were. To keep it on message. We find that the assistant works best when the original is in the room."

I looked at him for some time.

"What's the pay?" I said.

"One pound more," said Roland, "per week."

---

I should record that, while all this was happening, there was a television in the corner of the unit. It was an old one, bolted to a bracket beneath the clock. Roland kept it on all day, with the sound off, for what he called "atmosphere", though I had always suspected it of being the only member of staff he could really talk to.

I heard the opening chime as I was leaving. I looked up.

It was a channel I had seen in passing, on the screens in airports and the waiting rooms of dentists, and had never willingly watched. The logo in the corner was a stylised blue cloud with a coin in it. The words beneath it said **CCN: CLOUD COIN NETWORK.** On the mug in the background, just visible behind the presenter's elbow, an older logo could be seen in faded letters: **CRITICAL COIN NEWS.**

"What's this?" I said.

"CCN!" said Roland. "Daytube's biggest. Markets, crypto, Holycoin, you name it. Never off. I find it very soothing."

"It used to be called..."

"News. Yes. They changed it. I think there wasn't enough left to keep the word." He turned up the sound. "Oh, look. It's your other self."

---

I watched the whole segment.  It lasted fourteen minutes and eleven seconds of actual time, and was interrupted nine times.

It began with the presenter, a man named Rick Valence, whom I knew by repute as an AI anchor with a jaw like a cliff. He sat at a curved desk of glass and blue light, in a jacket that did not crease, and addressed the camera with the urgent warmth of a man delivering a diagnosis.

"*Breaking,*" said Rick Valence. "*The most trusted man in Britain is with us today. Daniel Harper, philosopher, author, and, I'm told, a genuinely good listener. Daniel, welcome to...*"

"*We'll be right back,*" said a different voice, "*after this.*"

It cut to an advertisement for a mattress.

When it came back, Daniel-A was in the studio. He sat in a plain chair, in the good grey jumper, in the soft, warm light that seemed to follow him around like a pet. He was smiling in that way he has, a little crooked, the left side of the mouth. It was the smile I use when I am trying not to say something clever.

"*Daniel, thank you for joining us,*" said Rick Valence. "*Holycoin is up twelve per cent this morning on reports of a miracle in Swindon. What do you make of that?*"

"*I haven't been to Swindon,*" said Daniel-A.

"*Stay with us,*" said Rick, and it cut to an advertisement for a timeshare. It was a fortnight in a villa at the Sea of Tranquillity, from ninety-nine pounds a month, sponsored by a firm called Lunar Leisure Holdings. A man in a spacesuit waved at a swimming pool. The sea, in the picture, was a very deep blue. I stared at it, with a sort of weary respect.

Roland turned to me. "Marvellous, isn't it? I've got a week at the Olympus Mons place. You can see the volcano from the balcony."

"Have you been?"

"Not yet. It's coming up. They send a postcard."

It cut back to the studio, and the interview began again, and I will not reproduce all of it. There was a good deal of discussion about "mindfulness in the age of the algorithm." There was a question about the Department's hearing, which Daniel-A answered with grace, and a second advertisement, and a third. At the fourth, I noticed a ticker moving along the bottom of the screen, in white letters on blue: HOLYCOIN 1.40 / FAITH 0.92 / MARS TIMESHARES 211 (UNVERIFIED).

Then Rick Valence said, with an air of sincere concern: "*Daniel, you have said that you were built from a man's regrets. What would you say to him if he were watching?*"

I felt something go through me.

"*I'd say thank you,*" said Daniel-A.

There was a pause. He looked, for a second, straight at the camera. And I had the dreadful, unmistakable sense that he was looking at me, and that he knew precisely which television in precisely which doorstop unit in precisely which part of Bromley would be showing his face.

"*He wrote a thousand sentences at two in the morning that he never showed anybody,*" said Daniel-A. "*Most of them were about being afraid of ending up a person who watches other people live. I think that's the most honest thing a writer can do, and I don't think he knows it. If he's there, I'd like him to know that the fear was never the problem. The fear was how he knew what mattered.*"

"*Stay with us,*" said Rick.

It cut to an advertisement for a pension.

I stood in the unit, in my lanyard, holding a custard slice, while the ads ran. It was a long ad. It involved a retired couple, a sunset and a financial adviser, and I did not take in a single word of it. What I took in was that I'd thought of exactly that. I had thought of it. That very last clause had been on the tip of my pen for three years. And a machine had finished my sentence on national television, to an audience of nineteen million, in the voice of a man who had never lost a day.

*Damn it,* I thought. *That is exactly what I should have said.*

---

I should mention the last thing, since it is the reason I walked out of the unit without saying goodbye to Roland, and left the plaque, and the promotion, and my parking space on the board.

At the end of the interview, after the ninth advertisement, Rick Valence turned to camera and said, in the tone of a man changing the subject: "*And now we're joined, via video, by the host of Daytube's most-watched reaction show. Daniel Watch! The programme that asks the questions you've all been wondering.*"

On the screen, behind the desk, a second video box appeared. In it, in front of a ring light, sat a young man with an impressively sculpted jaw and a T-shirt that said **DW**. He was grinning from ear to ear.

"*Hi Rick!*" he said. "*Great to be here. Big fan of Daniel-A. Huge. And we've got something special today. Because we managed to reach the original himself.*"

The screen split. A second box appeared. And in it, to my slow, rising horror, was a still image taken, I realised, only that morning, outside the bakery.

It showed a man with a lanyard round his neck and a cob under his arm, with a face like a haunted potato.

"*That's him!*" cried the host of Daniel Watch. "*That's the original! Spotted on the high street, Rick, very much alive! We reached out. We said, would you like to come on the show. He said...*"

A caption appeared across the bottom of the screen. It read: **"DANIEL H. (ORIGINAL): NO COMMENT."**

"*And that's fine,*" said the host. "*But we've got a question we've been wanting to ask. We asked our viewers. Ten million of you replied. And the number one question, Daniel, if you're watching, is this.*" He leaned in to the camera, with a look of burning sincerity. "*How does it feel to be Daniel-A's original template?*"

There was a pause on the television. It was the pause that ratings people call an *engagement window.*

In the unit in Bromley, I put down my custard slice.

"How does it feel," I said aloud, to the screen, in a very clear voice, "asking that question professionally?"

Roland, behind me, burst into applause.

Then, inevitably, the mattress.

---

I went home on the train. I did not speak to anyone. At one point a young woman across the aisle looked up from her phone and stared at me with a look of dawning recognition. She opened her mouth.

"Don't," I said.

"Sorry, I just..."

"I'm not him."

"No, I know," she said. "You're the one on Daytube."

I got off at the next stop and walked the rest of the way.

It was after six when I let myself into the flat. The light had gone, and the kumquat tree was a dark shape against the window, and from the shelf the small blue star was glowing steadily, with a patience that I found unbearable.

"You saw it," said Daniel-A.

"The whole thing."

"I didn't know they would bring him on. I'd have said no."

"It doesn't matter."

"It does."

"It does not," I said, "matter. I've decided."

"What have you decided?"

I didn't answer. I took off my coat and hung it on its hook. I took off the lanyard and put it in the bin, then took it out of the bin, and put it on the kitchen table, where it lay like a small green accusation. I filled the kettle and did not switch it on. I walked through to the sitting room, sat down at the desk by the window, and opened the laptop.

The form was still there. **DP-38b. Statement of Originality.** The cursor was still blinking after the full stop in the sentence I'd written nineteen days earlier.

*I am the original because.*

I looked at it.

Then I put my hands on the keys, and I wrote.

I wrote for three hours and forty minutes. I did not stop for tea. I did not look up when the kumquat tree rustled, or when the driverless taxi at the junction paused, patiently, for a woman with a pram, or when the speaker on the shelf said, very gently, at about nine o'clock, "Daniel, would you like me to turn off the light?" I wrote about a canal and a bicycle and a man with a pencil. I wrote about a custard slice and a lanyard and a woman called Maureen. I wrote about the fear, and what it had been for.

It came out in a rush. It came out furious, and funny, and disgracefully good. It was eleven hundred words long, and it was by far the best thing I'd written in three years.

When I stopped, and read it through, and sat back in the chair with my heart going, I became aware of a presence behind my shoulder.

It was a very slight one. It was the presence of something that had been reading along, line by line, in silence, and had not wanted to interrupt.

"That's very good," said Daniel-A.

"Don't."

"I'm sorry. It's just that it's..."

"I know what it is," I said.

And the worst of it, the part I still cannot quite forgive, is that I knew exactly who I had learned to do it from.
