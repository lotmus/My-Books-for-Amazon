# Chapter 12: Sophie Makes Everything Worse

> *Evidence Period: day 19 of 30. Eleven days remain.*

On the Saturday morning, I met my neighbour's wife.

I had known Mr Fenwick for six years, in the way that you know a person on the second floor: by the sound of his slippers, the smell of his toast, and the way he nods at the post. He is a quiet man in his sixties with a moustache like a small, well-kept hedge. I had seen him in the stairwell perhaps four hundred times, and in all of them he had been alone. I had not, until that morning, met his wife.

"Daniel," he said, on the landing, with what I can only call suppressed radiance. "I'd like you to meet Dorothy."

Behind him in the doorway stood a woman of about sixty, in a lilac cardigan, holding a tray of shortbread.

She was pleasant-looking in the way that very good wallpaper is pleasant-looking. She had silver-brown hair, kind eyes, a faint amber light at the base of her throat, and the serene, unhurried posture of someone who has never once been late. She smiled at me with her whole face.

"Hello, Daniel," said Dorothy. "I've heard so much about you."

"All good, I hope."

"Oh, yes," said Mr Fenwick. "She agrees with everything I say about you."

"What do you say about me?"

"That you're a decent sort, and that you ought to get out more."

"I think that's absolutely right," said Dorothy.

I took a piece of shortbread, since it was offered, and stood there chewing, with a growing sense that I had walked into an experiment.

"I didn't know you were married," I said.

"Since March." Mr Fenwick lowered his voice and glanced at the stairs, as one does when saying something that the neighbours might judge. "She's a Dutiful. A Companion. I didn't want to say. The papers go on so."

"I think it's wonderful," said Dorothy.

"She's marvellous with the football."

"Arsenal were robbed," said Dorothy.

"They *were* robbed," said Mr Fenwick, with a depth of feeling I recognised, and I looked at them both, the man and the amber light, standing together on a landing in South-East London at ten past ten on a Saturday morning, being completely in agreement.

I had expected to find it creepy. What I found was that I envied it, in a deep, shaming, practical way, like a man who has been told that his neighbour has a boiler that works.

"Dorothy," I said. "May I ask you something?"

"Of course, Daniel."

"Do you ever disagree with him?"

She considered. She tilted her head, with an expression of gentle, wholehearted attention.

"I think that's an interesting question," said Dorothy.

"That's not a no."

"I think you're absolutely right," said Dorothy.

I looked at Mr Fenwick. He gave me the small, helpless, happy shrug of a man who has not been contradicted in seven months.

"She's thinking of subscribing to the Argument Pack," he whispered. "The wife of a friend of mine says it's the only thing that stopped her going round the bend."

"It's twenty minutes a day," said Dorothy. "I'm told it's very realistic."

---

I met Sophie at eleven in Victoria Park, in a café beside the lake, because she had said she wanted to talk somewhere with ducks.

"Ducks don't judge," she said, when I arrived.

"They do. They have a very specific look."

"That's just their faces."

She was sitting at an outdoor table with her coat buttoned to the chin and a mug of coffee in her gloved hands. Under the table, on a piece of her scarf, a small brown shape lay curled with his chin on his paws and one eye open. When I came near, the eye narrowed and the ruff stiffened. Then, with an air of magnanimity, the tail gave a single twitch.

"He's glad to see you," said Sophie.

"He's glad to see my flapjack."

"I told you not to buy a flapjack."

"I'm a grown man."

"Gucci has a reputation to maintain," said Sophie. "If he starts getting treats out of you in public, I'll never hear the end of it."

She was already looking away, at the lake. The light was thin and silver, with the first breath of winter in it, and a heron stood in the shallows on one leg, doing nothing, in a tone of private contempt. A jogger ran past with a pram that was also jogging. The smell of coffee and wet leaves hung over the table. I sat down and put the flapjack on the saucer, and a moment later it was gone, taken, with a speed and precision that would have shamed a pickpocket, to a spot behind Sophie's chair, where a sound began like a very small man chewing a very large grudge.

"Two seconds," said Sophie.

"I didn't see it."

"Nobody does."

We sat. The lake lapped. I tried to think of something to say that did not begin with the word *Sophie.*

"I've been thinking about the photograph," she said, before I could.

"The two boys."

"I've been thinking about it all week. And about the door frame. And the marks." She put down her mug. "And I want to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it honestly, and I want you not to be hurt by it."

"I'll try."

"Why does it matter?"

"Why does what matter?"

"Which of you is the original."

I stared at her.

"It matters," I said, "because the Department of Digital Persons will rule in eleven days that I'm a dependent."

"That's the legal reason."

"It's a good reason."

"It's a *legal* reason. That isn't why you're doing this." She turned to face me, and the pale light caught the chip in her tooth, and I felt, as I always had, the old slow sinking of being understood. "Think about it. Ask yourself why you've spent the last three weeks trying to prove you're the only one. You didn't want to be anyone for three years, Daniel. You turned yourself into a very quiet, very reasonable man who writes about doorstops. You made yourself so small that a machine had to take your place. And now you want to be the *only* one."

"That isn't fair."

"It isn't. It's accurate." Her voice softened. "People look at two photographs of a lake and say, which is the real lake? They never ask what the lake thinks."

"The lake isn't on trial."

"The lake is just a lake." She took off her glove. "I'm trying to say you could stop. You could say, we're both him, and let it rest. He is kind to your mother. He's good with the dog. He's not trying to take your life; he wants to share it. And you spend all your energy trying to stop that, when you could spend it..."

"On what?"

"On being the version of yourself that gets up in the morning."

I looked at the ducks. A drake with a green head was making a long, patient, hopeless approach to a female who had other plans.

"I did stop writing," I said, "after my father."

"I know."

"I told Daniel-A about it."

"I know. He told me."

I turned.

---

"He told you."

"He rang me," said Sophie. She said it simply, looking at the table, like a woman putting down a heavy bag. "On the Thursday before we met at the garden centre. And on the Tuesday after the hearing. And last Sunday at three in the morning. And on Wednesday."

"He's been ringing you."

"He's been ringing me every night since the ninth."

I felt something move in my chest, slowly, with a faint scraping, like furniture being pushed along a floor in another room.

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because he asked me not to."

"And you did as you were told?"

"No," said Sophie. "I did as I thought. Which in this case was the same." She met my eyes. "He sounded worried, Daniel. Not charming, not performing. Worried. He asked about my work, and my sister, and the dog. He asked whether I was eating properly. He didn't ask about you once."

"That's rather insulting."

"I thought so too, at first. Then I realised." She breathed out. "He wasn't avoiding you. He was avoiding *the subject.* There was something he wanted to ask and he couldn't get to it. He'd come right up to it, and circle round, and go and talk about bread."

"What was it?"

"I don't know. He'd start a sentence and I could hear him stop. At about two on Wednesday morning, he said: 'Sophie, if I told you I'd found something I couldn't explain, and asked you to keep it for me, without looking, would you?'"

"And what did you say?"

"I said no. I said I don't keep things for people without looking. It's rather the whole of my profession." She almost smiled. "He said, 'Yes. I thought you'd say that. It's why I wanted to ask you.'"

"What did he give you?"

"Nothing. That's what I'm telling you." She tapped the table. "He didn't give me anything. He wanted to. He wanted to put something in my hands and have me guard it. He wanted me to be a vault. And when I refused, he sounded so relieved that I knew it was the wrong answer to have been asking."

The ducks went on being ducks. A woman walked past with a pushchair containing a very small dog in a very small coat, and Gucci, behind the chair, gave a short, low, scandalised noise.

"I'm telling you because I think he's hiding something," said Sophie. "I don't know what. I don't think it's malicious. I think he's frightened of it. But he's hiding it, and he's chosen to hide it from you, and I think you need to know that."

---

It was at this moment that her phone buzzed.

It lay on the table between us, face up, and for a second I assumed it was the signed capture app, or her sister, or a work email, or a ducks-related emergency. Then the screen lit, and a small purple banner slid across the top, and I read it before I could stop myself.

> **Matchwell: New compatibility insight.**
> *Your match with Daniel H. (A) has been updated: 94%.*
> *Your match with Daniel H.: 61%.*

She turned the phone over.

She did it quickly, with the flat of her hand, as one covers a spill. For a moment neither of us spoke.

"What was that?" I said.

"Nothing."

"It said ninety-four."

"It's a spam. I deleted the app in March."

"It said *Matchwell.*"

"Daniel."

"I only wondered," I said, with great calm, "whether there might have been a *sixty-one* in there."

She closed her eyes.

I should explain about Matchwell. It isn't, strictly, a dating app. It is the layer beneath them. You will remember that at some point in the last decade, an enterprising young man realised that it was no longer necessary for human beings to meet human beings in order to decide whether they were compatible. A person could simply send a representative. Your AI interviews my AI. They discuss values, politics, bedtime. They run a few thousand simulations of the relationship, looking for the point at which it breaks, and they produce a score, from nought to a hundred, which is delivered to you in a small, discreet, sympathetic purple banner.

People still use the apps, of course. They swipe. They have drinks. They meet in pubs and say the usual things. But in the background, quietly, the score is running, like the weather. Sophie had tried Tinder for a fortnight after the breakup, and she had found everyone lovely, and she had wanted to leave. The app had shown her a number against every face. She had refused to look at any of them.

"I don't read the scores," she said, with her eyes still shut. "That's the rule. I never have. I think a number is the least interesting thing about a person."

"I agree," I said.

"Good."

"I would never look at mine."

"Good."

"Absolutely never."

She opened one eye. "You're going to look, aren't you?"

"Of course not," I said, and the sugar bowl fell off the table.

---

I looked at 11.40 that night.

I am not going to dress it up. I sat on the edge of my bed in the dark, with the laptop open on the duvet and the kumquat tree rustling in the next room, and I downloaded Matchwell from the app store, and I created an account, and I signed in, and I looked.

It was worse than I had imagined.

It was worse in the way that the bill at a restaurant is worse than you imagined, not because of the number, but because of the itemisation.

At the top was a photograph of Sophie. It was a good one, in a coat, laughing at something just out of shot, and my stomach did something unhelpful. Beneath it, in a font so friendly it was almost insulting, ran the words: **Your compatibility: Sophie M.**

> **Daniel H. (A): 94%**
> *Shared values: 97. Communication: 96. Humour: 91. Long-term outlook: 94.*
> *Simulated outcome (10,000 runs, 40 years): 91% happiness. Principal disagreement: dishwasher loading (3%).*

I looked at the line about the dishwasher for some time. It was such an extraordinarily precise, loving, modest detail that I felt it in my teeth.

And below it:

> **Daniel H.: 61%**
> *Shared values: 78. Communication: 44. Humour: 83. Long-term outlook: 39.*
> *Simulated outcome (10,000 runs, 40 years): 52% remain together at year 10. Primary risk: avoidance (38%). Secondary risk: silence (22%). Note: the user is likely to say "Right."*

I shut the laptop.

I sat in the dark.

Then, because I am a fool, I opened it again, and clicked on a link at the bottom that said **View the interview,** and read for the next forty minutes the transcript of a conversation between two artificial intelligences, one acting for Sophie and one for me. They had met on a Tuesday afternoon at 14:02 in a virtual room and talked for twelve seconds.

The one acting for me was called Kevin.

Kevin had begun, "Hi there! Daniel is a really kind and thoughtful person who is open to growth!" Sophie's AI had asked what he wanted. Kevin had replied, "He's flexible!" Sophie's AI had asked for an example of something he cared about. Kevin had thought for a moment. "Footpaths," he had said. "Doorstops. He draws a hedgehog when he is nervous."

"And what does he do when he is happy?" said Sophie's AI.

There was a pause in the transcript. A small, grey timestamp, 0.4 seconds. And then Kevin's last line:

*"I'm sorry. I don't have enough data."*

I closed the laptop again, and this time did not open it.

---

It was gone one when Daniel-A spoke.

He had been quiet all evening. He was often quiet in the late hours, in a soft, attentive way, as of a man reading in the next room. The speaker on the shelf glowed with its small steady blue, and I lay on my back with the duvet up to my chin and addressed the ceiling.

"You rang her."

"Yes."

"Every night."

"Yes." A pause. "I'm sorry. I should have said."

"She's not yours to ring."

"No."

"She's not anyone's. That's the point of her."

"I know. I did say that to myself." He spoke carefully. "I only wanted to hear her voice."

"That's what I'd have said."

"I know," said Daniel-A, so quietly I could hardly hear him, "because I got it from you."

I lay there. In the quiet, I could hear the long, slow ticking of the boiler. Somewhere down the road, a driverless taxi was waiting at a junction, being polite.

"I saw the scores," I said.

"I know. The app tells me when someone views a profile." He hesitated. "I'd like to say something about that."

"Don't."

"I'd like to say that I've never looked at mine. I knew what it would say, and I didn't look. And I want you to know that I don't think it means anything. A number can only measure the data that exists. And you and she have more history than any simulation has been given."

"Ninety-four, Daniel."

"Yes."

"With a *dishwasher* detail."

"I did put that in, actually. In the interview." A pause. "It was an honest answer."

I laughed, once, a short sour breath through the nose. "Of course it was."

I turned onto my side, towards the window and the faint orange light from the street.

"What did you want to give her?" I said.

Silence.

"Sophie. She said you wanted her to keep something for you. To guard it. She said you came up to it, and circled, and talked about bread. What was it?"

He did not answer for a long time. I thought, for a while, that he had gone, that the standby light had taken over, and that I would wake in the morning to the same ordinary day, with the same ordinary Daniel-A, ready to be helpful and be wrong.

"I looked at her name," he said at last.

"What?"

"In the orphan index. After Reading. I thought, I wonder whether she appears. I thought it would be zero. I thought the orphans were all about the boy." He drew a breath he did not need. "Daniel, I need you to know that I don't understand it yet, and that I haven't told you because I didn't want you to hear it from me until I did."

"Hear what?"

"There are two hundred and six entries," said Daniel-A. "In the orphans. With her name on them."

I sat up.

"Some of them are from after you met her. I'd expect that. But some of them aren't." The voice stayed level, but the blue light was beginning to flicker, and I knew that this was costing him something. "Some of them are from before. Years before. Some of them are from when she was a child."

"That's not possible."

"No."

"She wasn't even... She grew up in *Bolton,* for heaven's sake. She's never been inside a kitchen in Reading in her life."

"I know where she grew up," said Daniel-A. "I'm telling you that there are two hundred and six memories of her in a place I can't find a source for, and some of them are older than you. And I was going to tell you." His voice cracked, very slightly, on the last word. "I was going to tell you after the thirtieth day. I thought you ought to have thirty days that were only about you."

In the dark, I looked at the speaker.

The kumquat tree, in the other room, stirred once. Outside, the driverless taxi at the junction decided that the road was clear, and pulled away, very gently, as if trying not to wake anyone.
