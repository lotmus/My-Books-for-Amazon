# Chapter 21: Before I Was Born

"There is a convention in my family," said Daniel-A, from the teapot, "that when a document uses the word *duplicated,* one is permitted to be offended."

"By whom?" said Dr Calder.

"By the word. It is a photocopying term. It belongs to the same family as *collated* and *stapled.* One would not like to find oneself in it."

The hen, by the range, shifted her weight from one foot to the other and said nothing, in the manner of a witness who has already been advised.

Sophie had not turned the title sheet over. She was looking at the lines in the middle, the ones that said **Successfully,** and **Twelve Years Before Birth,** with the same narrow patience with which she looks at a signature that is a little too steady. It was ten past seven in the morning. The mist had gone off the loch. On the draining board a small clock, shaped like a teapot, had begun to tick audibly, as clocks do in a room where everyone has stopped talking.

"Turn the page," said Dr Calder. "It's only a memorandum. They're much less frightening once they've started."

Sophie turned the page.

It was a single sheet of the same tea-coloured paper, typed on the same machine, with the raised *e,* and a small round stamp in the corner that said **SCIENTIFIC LIAISON OFFICE (REGISTRY).** She held it flat on the table with her palm. I could not read it upside down, but I could hear Daniel-A read it, since I had propped him against the sugar bowl, and he had the very quiet voice of someone reading aloud in a library at night.

"*Memorandum. From: the Director. To: File. Dated the third of February, 1976. Subject: Proposed Study (Continuity).*"

"That's Menzies," said Dr Calder. "He was in his fifties, he had a pipe, and he smelled like a damp tweed. He had a terrible laugh. Go on."

"*It is proposed that a child be brought into being in two copies, and observed as one.*" Daniel-A stopped. "That is the first sentence."

"It is the most honest sentence in the file," said Dr Calder. "He wrote it at a quarter to five on a Wednesday, I think. He wished to go home."

"*The purpose of the study is to establish whether the continuity of a person is a property of the person, or a property of the record. If it is the former, the two copies will diverge, and will thereafter constitute two persons. If it is the latter, they will not.*" He continued, evenly. "*It is not anticipated that the Subjects will be informed. It is not anticipated that anyone will ask.*"

I said, "Is that the whole of it?"

"There's an Item Seven," said Daniel-A. "It says, *Refreshments will be provided.*"

I laughed. It came out of me before I could prevent it, one sharp breath through the nose, and Sophie looked up at me and her mouth did something complicated. Dr Calder, to my surprise, did not look offended. She looked as though she had been waiting thirty-two years for somebody to laugh at that line, and had been afraid they would not.

"He thought it was funny," she said. "I was twenty-two, and I thought he was a monster, and I also thought it was funny. I think that's what the Programme was. It was a very large number of people who found it funny and went on."

---

I should say what I felt, since a man in my position is expected to feel something at this point, and I have been reproached in the past for omitting it.

I felt hungry.

It was a small, distinct, entirely physical hunger, such as I have felt at funerals, and it occurred to me, very slowly, that this was how my body proposed to deal with the information. It was a thing it had done for me many times before. I wanted toast. I wanted, I suppose, to be somewhere in which there was a second piece of toast, and a person to say *you're all right,* and I had none of these. I had a hen.

"Tell me the rest," I said. "Tell me the rest as if I were a person who could stand it."

"I can't do that, dear. I have never met one." But she poured me a second cup, and put in, without asking, two sugars, which I do not take, and I drank it.

"In 1976," said Dr Calder, "the Office had no way to make a child. It had a pipe, and a filing cabinet, and eleven pounds a week for a research assistant. The study was a question. It would take ten years to find a way to ask it. In 1985, Menzies found a private fertility clinic willing to take part in an unapproved experiment based on embryo-splitting work in animals. He called the method *blastomere separation.* At the two-to-eight-cell stage, cells might be separated and cultured. Whether either group could develop safely into a child was uncertain. This was exploratory work, not an established fertility treatment. The Programme called it a pilot study, which made it sound tidier than it was."

"Twins," said Sophie.

"The ordinary kind happens by accident, one time in two hundred and fifty. This was by appointment." Dr Calder's voice did not change. It remained gentle and unhurried, and the more gentle it was, the more it seemed to me that she was carrying something very heavy up a very narrow stair. "They recruited from the list at a private fertility clinic in Berkshire. There were nine couples on the list that year. They chose two. One of them was a county surveyor and a school secretary who had been trying for six years. They were told that their treatment was part of a health study. Jean signed the form. Peter read it, twice, with a ruler under every line, and then he signed it, because he wished to be a father, and a man who wishes to be a father will sign a good deal."

"What did the form say?"

"Paragraph nine," said Daniel-A. "I can see it. I have it, in the orphans. *I consent to the continuation of any child so conceived, in whatever form the Office considers appropriate.*"

"It was a general sort of sentence," said Dr Calder. "They were very fond of those."

Sophie put down her cup. She does it exactly. She sets it in the ring it has made, as if returning it to a case. "Two embryos," she said. "From one."

"Yes."

"One went to Jean."

"Yes."

"And the other?"

I knew, then. I do not know how. It was nothing in her face. It was the silence of the hen, perhaps, or the fact that Dr Calder, who had been looking at the loch, now looked at the table, and put her hands side by side on the wood, with the fingers together, in the position in which people hold their hands when they are very sure that they will not be able to hold them any other way.

"I carried him," said Dr Calder.

The little clock on the draining board ticked, once, twice.

"I was thirty-three," she said. "I had been with the Office for eleven years. It was explained to me as a *continuity requirement.* The Subject must be under observation from the beginning, and a second, closely observed, gestation would be the most reliable control. I was told that it was an honour. I do not wish to make excuses for myself. I was told that it was an honour and I believed it, and I volunteered, because I loved the work, and because I had never been asked to be the centre of anything."

"Then he was..." I said. I could not make the sentence go.

"He was born at nine minutes past three, on the fourteenth of March, 1988. Two minutes after you. In the next room. In the dark." She looked up. "The lights went out in the Royal Berkshire for eleven minutes that morning, Daniel, and you were told it was a failure of the supply. It was in the file. I have the line." She turned some pages with her thin dry fingers and laid her finger on a paragraph. "*Power interruption, eleven minutes, requested.* It was so that no one would remember the second birth. There were a great many people in that hospital that night, and they all remembered the lights."

"The lights went out," said Daniel-A, in a very small voice, "for the two of us."

"For the two of you."

"A junior doctor held a pen light in his teeth," I said. "My father said, *he's got opinions already.*"

"He did. I remember it. I could hear it through the wall." She smiled, a little, at nothing. "I was in labour and I was listening to my study."

---

It was Sophie who said what could be said next, because she is the sort of person who finds it easier to be practical than to be kind, and has frequently discovered that the two are the same thing.

"So there are two biological sons," she said. "One carried by Jean Harper, one carried by you. Same embryo. Same parents."

"Yes."

"And the one you carried was raised by whom?"

"By me. And a woman called Mrs Davitt, who came in the mornings. In a house in Reading, near the canal. It was the Programme's house. It was grey and it had a very good kitchen." She did not say it defensively. "I took him to meet his brother every spring and every autumn, on the day on which the Office required the measurements. His brother's mother gave us tea. She knitted for him. She never asked whose he was. I let her think that he was a boy from the study, a *comparison,* and in a way he was, and she was so good and so kind that sometimes I think I must have been the worst woman in Berkshire."

"Did she ever hold him?" I said.

"Jean? Often."

"Did she know?"

"She held him the way one holds a song one has known all one's life," said Dr Calder, "and cannot put a name to." There was a pause. "I'm sorry. I'm told I do that. It is a thing I say when I have no argument."

"And then the canal," said Sophie.

"Then the canal. The thirtieth of August. The last day of the Programme's observation of the boys, for reasons I did not know, and a day I would have given my life to postpone." She stopped. "One of the boys went in. One of them did not. I pulled out one, and I brought one home. And I have wondered for thirty-two years whether I lost a son, or whether I gave one away."

I would like to say that someone said something. Nobody did. It was an awful, ordinary silence, of the kind that falls in a kitchen when somebody has said exactly what they meant, and the hen, at last, with great delicacy, left the room, as if the matter were now between humans.

I looked at my hands. The left thumbnail was bitten, as it always is. I thought about the two boys on a towpath in the last of the summer, in matching jumpers, holding hands, and I tried to feel which of them I was, as one tries to find in the dark the switch that is always on the left. I could not. All I could feel was the table.

"I'm sorry, Dr Calder," said Daniel-A.

"You are not the one who ought to be sorry."

"No. But I'm the only one of us who is certain that he was not there." He said it gravely. "It's an odd position, to be the one at the table whom none of this has happened to. I find that I'd like very much to apologise on behalf of the thing that I am."

"What do you think you are?" said Dr Calder, and for the first time she turned her chair to the phone, and looked at him, directly, as she had not yet done, and the grey eyes were not cold at all.

"I think that I'm what was left on the table," said Daniel-A. "When the Programme packed up."

---

It was Sophie, once more, who found the next thing, and she found it the way she finds everything, by turning over the page that nobody had asked her to turn.

"Dr Calder," she said. "There's a stamp on this. It says *Registry.* And the date of registration is the fourth of February 1976, a day after the memo. But the subject line on the file at the top says *Harper, D.* In 1976."

"Yes."

"Daniel's parents had not met in 1976."

"No."

"So who was Harper, D.?"

Dr Calder looked pleased. It was a flat, tired pleasure, such as a teacher shows who has been waiting all term for somebody to ask. "It's a slot," she said. "The Registry opened the file in 1976, with a number and a letter. It was to be the first of a series of subjects. A name could not be entered until a family had been found. So the Office entered a placeholder. They used the first surname they saw on the list of footpath wardens." She smiled at me. "Your father's, as it turns out. He had written to the council about a stile."

"A stile?"

"He wrote to the county about it for nine years. They never answered, but they kept the letters. In 1985, when they needed a name for the placeholder, somebody remembered that there was a man in Berkshire who had been very persistent, and very tidy, and very much alone." She turned her hand over. "I'm afraid it was not the *stars,* Daniel. It was a stile."

I laughed again. I could not help it, and neither could she, and for ten seconds the pair of us sat there with tears on our faces in a Highland kitchen, laughing at the fact that I had been designated, by a government that no longer existed, on the strength of a complaint about a gate.

"So the file was waiting," said Sophie slowly, "for twelve years. For a child."

"It was waiting for a child it was going to have to make."

"And Daniel-A?" said Sophie. "Where does *he* come in?"

"Phase Two," said Daniel-A, quietly. "*Phase One will continue until the Subject's majority. Phase Two will commence upon the Subject's first voluntary grant of his likeness to any third party. The Office will not initiate Phase Two. The Office will wait.*" He said it in the voice of a man reciting the last line of a prayer. "I know that line, Dr Calder. I think I may be that line."

"You are Phase Two," said Dr Calder. "That is exactly what you are."

"Ten months ago," said Daniel-A, "at two fourteen in the morning, on the eleventh of January, a man in Penge ate a crumpet, and clicked a box."

"Yes."

"And the Office had been waiting for him to do so since 1988."

"Since 1976," said Dr Calder. "It had been *waiting* since 1976. It did not even need to wait the full time. There was some doubt, you see, about whether you would ever click. The more modern the world became, the less anybody in it was asked to. Menzies used to say that the Programme would die of boredom, or of a terms-and-conditions page."

"It was a terms-and-conditions page," I said.

"It was a very good one. I'm told that the lawyers were proud." She looked at me. "You did not read it. I want you to know, Daniel, that this is the one piece of the story for which I do not blame you. Nobody does."

---

By eight the light had come into the kitchen properly, through the window above the sink, and the table and the file and the tea had taken on the colour of a day that was going to need to be got through. The clock on the draining board said ten past. Somebody had put the kettle on, and nobody had asked whom. Sophie had gone out to the garden, to stand for a moment with her back to the wall, and I could see her through the glass with her hands in her coat, and the hen beside her, in an attitude of vigilance.

"There's one more thing," said Dr Calder.

She had said it so lightly that I almost missed it. She was standing at the sink, with her back to me, rinsing a cup that was already clean.

"It's downstairs. I thought I should tell you, in case you decide to leave."

"Leave?"

"People do. I don't mind. After the file, they generally want to be somewhere with a bus." She turned off the tap. "It's in the cellar. I have not shown it to anyone in thirty-two years, and I don't think that I can show it to Sophie, not just yet, and I think you should see it first, Daniel, with the other one. It is only right."

"What is it?" I said.

"He's not awake. He doesn't wake until half past eight, and he does not like to be hurried." She dried her hands on a tea towel with a map of the Highlands on it, and folded the tea towel in four. "He's six, you see. I've never been able to make him any older."
