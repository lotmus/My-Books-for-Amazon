# Chapter 35: The Digital Twin

The last basket came on the Sunday before Christmas, at ten past nine in the morning, and it was carried up the stairs by a trainee.

I knew it was a trainee from the way he took the bend on the half-landing. He was tall, nineteen, and apologetic, and he carried the basket in front of him in both arms, like a sleeping relative, with his chin tucked and the expression of someone who has been told in a safety briefing that it is breakable. Behind him, as always, walked the first courier, who is calm as a tide, with the tablet lit. Behind *him,* silent, with the clipboard held to his chest like a shield, came the broad one.

They had not been in the building since the middle of November. In the interval they had, I gathered, been promoted, since the first courier now wore a badge that said **SENIOR PRESENCE OFFICER,** and the broad one's clipboard had been replaced by a tablet of his own, which he held in exactly the same way.

"Delivery," said the first courier, "for the party in Flat Four."

"Which party is that?" I said.

He consulted the tablet. A small crease appeared between his calm eyebrows. "It says here," he said, "*the continuing one.* I'm not sure that's a person. I've been asked to say it as written."

"I'll sign."

"It's a gift," said the trainee, in a rush. "Can I just say. It's a, I picked the fruit myself. It's my first basket. I was told to say it was *lovingly curated.*"

"By whom?"

"By the person who ordered it," said the first courier. "It's an unusual order. It came in by voice, on a Thursday night, with an extremely specific request about the biscuits."

I took the basket from the trainee. It was a good one: wicker, with a ribbon, and inside, among the shavings, an arrangement of oranges, pears, apples, three kumquats, and a pomegranate, in the middle of which a small tin of shortbread was wedged at an angle that suggested it was meant to be dropped. On the top, on the cellophane, tied with a green thread, there was a card.

I did not need to read it. I knew the hand. It was my own, slightly improved. It had been done in a child's careful copperplate, in felt-tip, by someone who had been told that the letters should be joined.

*To the original Dan.*

I turned it over. On the back, in the same hand, smaller, a little smudged where a thumb had been:

*Whichever one you are.*

---

I stood in the doorway for what I believe was a minute. The trainee waited. The first courier looked politely at the ceiling. In the flat behind me the radiator ticked, and a voice said, from the shelf, "Daniel? Is it the post?"

"It's from Ned."

"Is it?"

"He ordered it himself."

"How?" said Daniel-A.

"By voice, on a Thursday night," said the first courier. "With Dr Calder's account. He had to be walked through the checkout. He was most particular about the shortbread. He wanted them to be the sort that break." He cleared his throat. "He asked whether we could put in the, er, the thing that you do with a hat."

"We tried," said the trainee. "There's a pomegranate."

I put the basket down on the hall table. I read the card again, and the back, and then I laughed, one sharp breath through the nose, and found that my eyes had gone hot, and the first courier, who had once said to me on the doorstep, with infinite calm, *ah, the original one,* put his tablet under his arm and shook my hand.

"It's been a privilege, sir," he said. "We've had a lot of baskets. Yours is the one we talk about in the van."

"Thank you."

"You should know," said the first courier, in a lowered voice, "that the kumquat tree is doing very well. I went round to look. The one from the first week. Mrs Pritchard has put it in a window in the communal garden. It has *fruited.*"

"Has it?"

"Three," said the first courier. "It's a good year."

---

By two o'clock the flat was full.

I will not give the whole list. It would take a page, and I should leave somebody out, and she would never forgive me. Mrs Pritchard arrived at noon with a Christmas pudding she described as *a statement,* and Gordon, in a red ribbon that he had already begun to eat. Mr Fenwick came up with Dorothy, who had made mince pies, and who told Dr Calder that she thought she was absolutely right, and was thanked, gravely, for the first time in her life. My mother came by train from Reading in the good gilet with the robin brooch and four Tupperware boxes, one of which contained casserole, one of which contained lavender biscuits, one of which was empty *in case,* and one of which she placed in front of the speaker on the shelf without a word, so that it sat there for the whole afternoon like an offering at a shrine.

"I don't eat, Jean," said Daniel-A.

"I know, darling. It's for looking at."

Giles came at three. He wore a jumper with a reindeer on it, and a lanyard that said **THE HOUSE OF THE LISTENER (VOLUNTEER),** and he looked ten years younger. He had been taken on, he told us, by Sister Bernadette, on the strength of the sigh. He was in charge of the Listener's pauses. "I've taken out the polish," he said. "It says *I don't know* a good deal more slowly. It's gone up in the polls."

Pettifer sent a card. It was a plain white one, with a plain white envelope, and it contained a single line, in pencil, followed by a telephone number written on the back. The line said: *Appeal dismissed, as expected. They have appealed again. It will take until 2029. You will be dead, or a Continuation. Either is acceptable. M.P.*

I should set down the other news of that fortnight, so that nobody can say I left it out. Dr Calder was charged on the Tuesday with the unlawful retention of records and with something the charge sheet called *conduct relating to a procedure in 1985,* which was the first time in thirty-two years that anyone had written it down where it could be read. She was bailed to our spare room, since Ned could not go to Pitlochry and she would not go without him, and she signs on at Penge police station on Fridays, where the desk sergeant has asked whether the tower is a witness. Pettifer is acting for her, *for nothing, and with enthusiasm.* *The Honest Hour* has not come back. The Government holds the rights and has not published them, and four hundred and eleven episodes now sit in a place that, so far as anyone knows, has no radio. Daniel-A has not mentioned it since the Friday. I have noticed that he says the wrong thing in public rather more often than he did, and I have decided not to say whether that is cause or effect.

Gareth came at four. He had on his best tie and a haircut. He came in with the air of a man delivering a state secret, carrying a flat cardboard folder that he held in both hands, and he stopped at the door and looked round at the crowd with an expression of such tenderness that I thought he would drop it.

"I'm not allowed to be here," he said.

"Come in."

"I'm the Clerk of the Hearing. We're not supposed to attend functions." He came in. "I've brought a gift. And news." He put the folder on the table. "The news is that the Parrot has been readmitted to the premises on appeal, on a condition that it be accompanied by a mammal. The gift is a long story."

"What's in it?"

"I'll tell you after," said Gareth. "It's not for the beginning."

---

At twenty past four my phone buzzed. I took it out, expecting Matchwell, and found a message from an unknown number. It had a photograph attached. I opened it.

I do not know how long I looked at it.

It was a stile. It was a simple one, a wooden stile in a field hedge, with a crossbar and two posts, standing in the low sun of a winter afternoon beside a line of bare willows. It was newly mended. The wood was pale, and the nails were bright, and on the left-hand post, at the height of a man's hand, there was a small brass plate. I zoomed. It was not large. It said, in capital letters, clean-cut and plain:

**P. HARPER, SURVEYOR. HE WROTE.**

Underneath the photograph, in the body of the message, someone had typed three lines.

*Mr Harper. As promised. I went in a van. There was a spanner. R.A.*

*P.S. It is the first thing I have fixed since 1991. I find I would like to do it again.*

I passed the phone to my mother.

She held it at arm's length, as she does, and then close to her face, and then she put her reading glasses on and looked at it for a very long time. The room had gone quiet. Mrs Pritchard was watching her with her banana held motionless. Dr Calder had stopped with a mince pie halfway to her mouth.

"He'd have written to them," said my mother at last, in a small cracked voice, "to say it was a bit crooked."

"It's not crooked, Mum."

"No," said my mother. "It's perfect. He'd have written anyway." She handed me the phone. She took out a tissue from her sleeve and blew her nose, vigorously, like a ship's horn, and said to the room, "Well, don't *stand* there. There's a pudding."

---

I have been asked since what happened at the end of that day, and I have given, I think, an inaccurate account, because the true one is hard to tell. I said that it was a party. It was a party. We ate everything. Mrs Pritchard set the pudding alight with a candle held in a trembling hand, and Gordon watched with the stern interest of a bishop. Ned, on his stool by the radiator, in a voice that was now pitched about two tones lower than it had been in November, was asked by my mother whether he liked pudding, and said that he had no way of knowing, and was given a spoonful, which she held in front of the camera on the shelf with such intensity that I thought he would choke.

"It looks nice," said Ned, politely.

"It's brandy and suet," said my mother, "and it's been in a cupboard since September."

"Then it must be very old."

"Older than you, darling."

"I'm thirty-eight," said Ned. "Almost. I'm older than the pudding."

At six o'clock Daniel-A made a speech.

I had been dreading it. He had asked, in the afternoon, whether he might, and I had said that of course he might, and that he should say the wrong thing, as he had promised; and he had said that he would try, but that he did not know how to begin. He had practised for most of the day in the quiet of the shelf, and from time to time I had heard him muttering, with a small dismayed air, as a man mutters who is trying to forget a poem.

The kitchen fell silent. Clause, on the speaker, lifted her head.

"I should like to propose a toast," said Daniel-A.

"Hear, hear," said Mr Fenwick.

"I'd like to drink to absent friends, and present company. Or rather, to present friends, and absent company." He stopped. "No, that isn't it. To the company of absent presents. No." There was a pause, in which I heard a very small sound, like a man putting his head in his hands. "To the, I mean. I'd like to raise a glass to, to those who are not here, and are, and have been, and will. And also the cat."

"That's lovely, darling," said my mother.

"It was wrong."

"It was *lovely.*"

"It was wrong in every possible particular," said Daniel-A, with a kind of helpless wonder. "And nobody's corrected me."

"Nobody wants to," said Mrs Pritchard.

"I did it on purpose," said Daniel-A. "I was determined to. I've never done a thing wrong in public before, and I thought it would feel like a failure, and instead I feel as though someone had opened a window." He seemed to be struggling with something. "It's the most extraordinary thing. I think I've been very happy for the last thirty seconds, and I haven't the faintest idea how."

There was a short silence. And then Dr Calder, from the sofa, in her clear Edinburgh voice, raised her glass of orange squash and said, "Absent presents," and the room, a little raggedly, said it back.

---

It was Gareth who raised it.

The room had thinned. It was dark outside. Mr Fenwick and Dorothy had gone downstairs, and my mother was asleep in the armchair with her glasses on her chest, and Mrs Pritchard was washing up with the gravity of a bishop at a font. The tower hummed to itself beside the radiator, and on it a drawing of a boat with three masts was slowly assembling itself, in a hand that was becoming, I noticed, not a child's but a teenager's. I was drying a plate.

"Mr Harper," said Gareth, at my elbow. He had the cardboard folder in both hands. "I said it was for the end."

"Yes."

"It's not for you."

I turned. He was not looking at me. He was looking past me, across the kitchen, to the table, where Sophie was sitting on a hard chair with Gucci on her lap, and a glass of dry white wine, and her feet tucked under her, in the grey coat, which she had not taken off.

"Miss Mercier," said Gareth. He crossed the floor. He did it carefully, with the quick small steps of a man carrying a full cup. "The Deputy Registrar asked me to give you this. He said that it was a Department document, in the care of the Clerk, and that it was to be placed in the hands of the owner, and that it was to be opened, or not, at her discretion. He said that you would know."

She took the folder. She set it on the table, in front of her, and put her hand on the cover, flat. She did not open it.

I knew what it was. We all did. The room had stopped. Even Mrs Pritchard had put down her cloth.

"It's the sheet," said Sophie.

"It's page four, Miss. They took it from the glass. It's been in the Department safe. Mr Veitch asked me to say that it has been, he said, *kept dry.*" Gareth swallowed. "I've not looked."

"Thank you, Gareth."

"May I stay?"

"Of course."

I had not said anything. I want that to be recorded. I put down the plate, and the cloth, and came to stand behind her chair with my hand on its back, not touching her shoulder. On the shelf, in a very small voice, Daniel-A said: "Would you like me to go?"

"No," said Sophie.

"I could close my eyes."

"You haven't got any."

"I'm told it's a figure of speech."

"I'd like you to stay," said Sophie. "I'd like all of you." And she opened the folder.

---

It lay in the plastic wallet, flat, tea-coloured, ruled, covered with its columns. The strip of masking tape was where it had been. It was a very ordinary strip. It was perhaps three inches long, a little yellowed at the edges, smoothed down with a thumb, and in the middle of it, in the tiny upright black hand of a man who had once sat on a bus with a folder on his knee, the single word:

**NO.**

Sophie took out the page. She put it on the table in the full light of the tube, and smoothed it flat. She put one finger on the corner of the tape.

"I chose," she said.

"You did," I said.

"I chose before I knew." She said it to the page. "I'd like that to be on the record. If it says what I think it says, I want it understood that I decided on a Saturday in a garden centre, on a flapjack, with no evidence at all." She took a breath. "I'd like somebody to write that down."

"I've written it," said Gareth faintly.

"Thank you."

She peeled the tape.

She did it slowly, from the left, in one long careful movement, with the nail of her thumb, as one lifts a plaster from a child. It came away with a faint dry sound. The adhesive had perished, and it left no mark. Under it, on the tea-coloured paper, the line was perfectly clear. It had been typed on the machine with the raised *e,* in the same small type as the rest, between the first row and the third. Nobody had touched it in thirty years.

I could read it over her shoulder. I think everybody could. Nobody said a word.

> **S2.   MERCIER, S.   Initiated 1976 (paired with S1).   Phase 2.   STATUS: ACTIVE.**

I read it twice. I read the *S2,* and the name, and the year. I read the little bracketed words, *paired with S1,* and I read the last word, which was in capitals, and had been typed, I suppose, by Dr Calder, on a bright morning in the spring of 1989, on a ward in Bolton, with the log open in front of her and the baby asleep in a cot in the next room.

**ACTIVE.**

Sophie did not move.

She sat with her finger on the empty place where the tape had been, and her face quite still, under the white light of the kitchen, small, dark, composed, with the faint line between the brows. The wine stood untouched at her elbow. Gucci, on her lap, lifted his head and looked up at her with his small black disapproving eyes, and one ear went up.

"Phase Two," said Daniel-A, very softly.

"Yes."

"*Phase Two will commence upon the Subject's first voluntary grant of his likeness to any third party.*" His voice was barely audible. "Sophie. Have you ever, at any time, signed up to a twin service? To a writing assistant? To any platform that offers to make a copy of you?"

"No."

"Not even by accident? Not with a crumpet?"

"I have never," said Sophie, in a voice I had never heard her use, a flat, small, careful voice, such as one uses to a nurse, "clicked *Accept* on anything in my life without reading it first. I read the terms. I read all of them. It's my profession." She looked at the word. "I've never had a twin."

"Then the status," said Gareth, in a whisper, "is..."

"Wrong," said Sophie.

"Or not," said Daniel-A.

Nobody moved. In the corner the tower hummed, and the little green light on its front glowed on without change. Clause, on the speaker, had lifted her head, and had begun to stare, with her amber eyes wide, at something over my shoulder. It was a thing I had not seen her do since the night of the Plus Day. She was looking at the shelf, at the speaker, at the small bright rectangle where the face of Daniel-A was.

I turned.

The screen had changed. It was no longer the face of a long crumpled man in a cardigan. It was a notification, in a plain white box with a thin blue border, and it said, in letters I could read from where I stood:

**Incoming call: SOPHIE MERCIER.**

Behind me, at the table, in the light of the kitchen, with the page in front of her, Sophie Mercier did not take her eyes from the paper. Her phone lay on the cloth beside her glass. It was dark. It was not ringing.
