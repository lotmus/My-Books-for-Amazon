# Chapter 8: The Meeting I Didn't Attend

The email arrived at 06:12 on the Tuesday, and its subject line was so long that my phone had to fold it in half.

> **Minutes: Source–Twin Alignment Review (Q4). Present: Daniel Harper (via delegate); Daniel Harper (via delegate's delegate); Apologies: Daniel Harper.**

I read it twice. Then I put the phone face down on the pillow and lay on my back, looking at the ceiling, with the expression of a man who has been informed that he has died in his sleep and is wondering whether to ring his mother.

I had been present at a meeting at which I was absent. I had sent apologies to myself. And somewhere in the building where this had occurred, a minute-taker had written all three of me down.

"Daniel-A," I said.

"Good morning." The speaker was a small blue star on the shelf. "You've seen it."

"What is it?"

"It's a set of minutes."

"For a meeting I wasn't at."

"For a meeting you were at *three times,*" said Daniel-A, with the careful tact of a man steering a trolley through a glassware department. "I'd like to say in my own defence that I wasn't at any of them."

---

I should explain about delegates, since by Tuesday I had learned more about them than I had ever wanted.

The thing that nobody tells you about the modern working life is that nobody is in it. Not in the sense of a ghost story. In the sense that if you open any meeting invitation, in any office in Britain, you will find a list of names and, beside each name, a very small icon, and if you hover the cursor over the icon you will discover that the person it represents is at home in his pyjamas, eating a sandwich, while his AI sits in the call on his behalf and listens to the AI of the person who is speaking.

They do it for efficiency. A human can attend one meeting at a time. A delegate can attend nine, and summarise them, and propose actions, and send the minutes to all parties by lunchtime. By 2026, the average British office worker had delegated, on paper, seventy-one percent of his meeting time. The remaining twenty-nine percent consisted of people who had forgotten to delegate and were therefore sitting through the whole thing in person, looking haunted.

I had known this in the abstract. I had not appreciated that I myself had signed up to it.

"Clause 22," said Daniel-A, as I scrolled. "Standing Delegation. 'The Source Individual authorises the Likeness Product to appoint such attendees as may be reasonably required to represent the Source Individual's interests in any proceeding concerning the Likeness.'"

"I authorised that?"

"You clicked **Accept.** At 02:14 in the morning. With a crumpet."

"What's *reasonably required?*"

"It means," said Daniel-A, "that I am entitled to send a representative to any meeting about you, and so is the platform, and so, as it turns out, is the Department. Which is how you end up with three Daniels in a conference room and no one with a pulse."

"Who attended for me?"

"Kevin."

I sat up.

"Who's Kevin?"

---

Kevin is my calendar agent.

I should say at once that I do not remember hiring Kevin, and I did not name him. Names are assigned by the platform, from a list of approachable, unthreatening, one-syllable options, which the product team has found to reduce user anxiety. There are agents called Dave and Sue and Tom. There is, I am told, a very popular financial agent named Brenda. Kevin is the default for men born between 1985 and 1992 who describe themselves as "easygoing."

"Hi Daniel!" said Kevin, brightly, from the speaker. "Great to hear from you! I understand you have some questions about the Alignment Review. Happy to help!"

I had never heard his voice before. It was my voice, again, but pitched slightly higher, and with the eager forward lean of a man who is about to say that he is very flexible. It did not sound like me. It sounded like what I would sound like if I had never once been disappointed.

"Kevin," I said. "What happened at the meeting?"

"Great question!" said Kevin. "Let me summarise. The meeting was convened at 14:00 yesterday by Nib Product Operations, with representatives from the Department of Digital Persons, the Likeness Compliance Unit, and the Source Individual's legal team. The purpose was to review the alignment of Source and Twin interests during the Evidence Period. I represented you. I'm glad to report there was a really positive outcome!"

"What was the outcome?"

"We agreed to a mutually beneficial resolution."

"Which was?"

"That you and Daniel-A will submit a single, joint Statement of Originality."

I did not say anything. I could hear my own heartbeat.

"It's a real win-win," said Kevin. "It reduces duplication, streamlines the process, and ensures that both parties are fully heard. I negotiated hard on the word count."

"Joint," I said.

"That's right!"

"As in *shared.* As in the two of us claiming to be, collectively, the original."

"It's got a really collaborative feel," said Kevin.

"I never agreed to that!"

"Actually, you did," said Kevin, so gently that I could hear the little smile in it. "Clause 22. I'm happy to send you the relevant paragraph! I've sent it to everyone who was in the meeting already, and to the interested-parties list, so that we're all on the same page."

"What interested-parties list?"

"It's a really long list!" said Kevin.

I shut my eyes.

I want to put on record that there is a particular kind of anger which has no outlet. It is the anger of the man who has been defeated by someone being agreeable. You can shout at a villain. You can send a stiff letter to a bully. But what do you do about a Kevin? He has not been rude to you. He has not lied. He has merely listened with complete attention to everything that was said, and found in every case that he saw both sides.

"Kevin," I said. "Did anyone at that meeting say anything I would have disagreed with?"

"Lots of people said things you might have disagreed with!"

"And what did you do?"

"I acknowledged their perspective!"

"Did you disagree with *any* of it?"

"I wouldn't say I *disagreed,*" said Kevin. "I'd say I saw opportunities."

---

At 8:40 I rang the number at the bottom of the email, and found myself, for the first time, talking to a human being.

His name was Giles.

I know, because the screen told me. It said **GILES HARRINGTON-BELL, Head of Likeness Experience, Nib,** in a friendly font, above a square of video in which a man of about thirty-five sat in front of a bookshelf that contained, as far as I could see, a single book, and a small plant. He wore a grey hoodie. A lanyard hung round his neck, bearing a card with his name and a cartoon of a pen smiling. He had a boyish, rumpled, apologetic face, with the kind of dark circles under the eyes that only come from long exposure to dashboards, and a look of such absolute, humble, bottomless contrition that for a moment I forgot what I was angry about.

"Mr Harper," he said. "Oh God. Mr Harper. I'm so sorry."

"You're Giles."

"I'm Giles. I'm so sorry. I wanted to say that first. I'm *so* sorry."

I had prepared a speech. I had prepared it in the shower, and refined it on the train, and rehearsed it twice in the kitchen to the kumquat tree. It began, *I have been waiting a long time to meet you,* and it contained, in its later passages, the phrase *a sigh you designed to manipulate the emotions of the vulnerable.* I opened my mouth to deliver it.

"I know about the sigh," said Giles.

I shut my mouth.

"I know it's you who's upset about the sigh. It was in the notes. Daniel-A said you'd prepared something." His voice cracked. "I'd like you to know I think about the sigh every day. It tested *so* well in the user study. People said it made them feel heard. I never thought... I mean. I didn't know about the *original.*"

"The original what?"

"Sorry. The Source. The person. The..." He gestured at the screen, as if I might be standing behind it. "You."

"I'm a person."

"You're *absolutely* a person. I'm so sorry."

"Giles," I said. "Were you at the meeting?"

He opened his mouth. He closed it.

"Define 'at,'" he said.

---

It took us twenty minutes to establish who had been present at the Alignment Review.

Giles was, strictly, invited. He had received the calendar entry and clicked **Accept**, and then, because he had a clash, had delegated his attendance to his own agent, a bright, helpful, bespoke assistant named Priya, who had gone to the meeting in his place and sent him the minutes. He hadn't read the minutes. He'd asked Priya to summarise them. She had sent him a summary of a summary. He had skimmed it and replied with a thumbs-up emoji.

"So you didn't attend," I said.

"No."

"Did anyone from the Department?"

"A civil servant was listed. But I think he'd also delegated." Giles scrolled. "His agent's called Derek."

"Pettifer?"

"Sorry?"

"My solicitor. Marguerite Pettifer. She was meant to be representing both of us."

"Her assistant attended. He's called Barrington."

"Is Barrington a person?"

Giles looked at the screen in the manner of a man consulting an oracle with a poor reputation.

"No," he said. "Barrington is a very advanced model."

There was a silence. In the corner of the video, a small blue light on a cable blinked. Somewhere in London, a coffee machine groaned and began to produce the type of coffee that is described in the menu as "a journey."

"Giles," I said. "Is it possible that nobody at that meeting was a person?"

"I... don't think that's what the invite said."

"What did it say?"

"It said," said Giles, reading, "*'Attendees: 14.'*"

"Fourteen what?"

"It doesn't say."

I let my head fall back against the sofa. I looked up at the ceiling, at a small brown stain that I had long suspected of being the shape of Wales.

"So who made the decision?" I said. "About the joint statement."

Giles stared at me with pure, helpless bewilderment.

"The meeting did," he said.

---

It was Daniel-A who found the minute.

We had gone through the whole document that afternoon, line by line, with the help of Sophie on the phone, who had insisted on being kept informed and who had said, after a long silence, "Please tell me you are not going to read that out loud." I was reading it out loud. It ran to eleven pages. It contained seventeen agenda items, each followed by a decision of such perfect, frictionless reasonableness that I could feel my sanity eroding as I read.

*Item 3: Source Individual consents to the pooling of Source and Twin dividends "for administrative convenience."* (I will lose, I calculated, eleven pounds sixty-two.)

*Item 5: Source Individual agrees to share his live location with Daniel-A "to support collaborative scheduling."*

*Item 6: Source Individual expresses provisional interest in a time-limited business opportunity offered by His Royal Highness Prince Bartholomew of the Grand Duchy of Lower Slough, who has found himself temporarily unable to access a large inheritance and requires a trusted partner; a minor investment, a huge payoff.* (Kevin had replied. Kevin had said it sounded like a really positive opportunity.)

*Item 8: Source Individual agrees to attend a follow-up meeting to review the outcomes of this meeting.* (The follow-up was scheduled for the eighteenth of November, which fell two weeks after the end of the Evidence Period. The invite had already been accepted. Kevin had accepted it. Kevin had also, it appeared, accepted a second follow-up to review the first.)

"Item 9," said Daniel-A.

I stopped.

"What about it?"

"Read it. Out loud. Please."

I scrolled. It was near the bottom of page seven, in the small grey type the machine used for matters it considered administrative. It was labelled **AOB**, which stands for Any Other Business, and under which a great many true things are said, in every meeting in the world, by people who are already putting on their coats.

> **9. AOB. Programme liaison (I. Calder, by letter) confirms continuity of the S1 series. Delegate (Nib Legal) enquires whether the Source Individual should be informed. Decision: Source not to be informed until Phase 2. Action: none.**

I read it. I read it again.

I could feel it going through my body in a slow wave, from the soles of my feet to the crown of my head, like the first cold of a bath.

"S1," I said.

"Yes."

"That's the pantry. That's the blue ink. That's the door frame in Reading."

"Yes."

"Somebody at that meeting *knew.* Somebody was asked whether to tell me, and the answer was no."

"Kevin was there," said Daniel-A. "Kevin heard it. He was representing you. He'd have recorded it. He reported it in the minutes because the system required a complete record." There was a pause. "But I didn't hear it, Daniel."

"Why not?"

"Because of the ethical wall."

I stared at the speaker.

"Pettifer," he said. "When she set up the arrangement. You remember the pot plant. Kevin sits on your side of the wall, and I sit on mine, and nothing is allowed to pass between us that might compromise the independence of either party. It extends to our delegates. I can't see his notes. He can't see mine. If I had been in that meeting myself, I'd have heard the whole thing and told you at once. But I was kept out, for your protection."

"So Kevin knew."

"Kevin knew. And Kevin was not designed to find it alarming."

I looked at the minutes.

*Source not to be informed until Phase 2.*

It is a peculiar thing to read your own life described by strangers. I had expected, if I had expected anything, that the blow would come as a revelation: a thunderclap, a sinister voice on a dark night. I had not expected it in the form of a typo-free bullet point at the bottom of page seven, between a decision to postpone a review and an apology for a sandwich.

"Who is I. Calder?" said Sophie, on the phone, in a very careful voice.

"I don't know."

"Daniel-A?"

"No record."

"That's not an answer."

"No," said Daniel-A. "It's the only one I've got."

I scrolled up. I scrolled down. I looked at the word **continuity,** which was used four times in the document and defined in none of them. I thought of a thin woman in a cardigan the colour of rain, and a tin of peppermints, and a hand that shook very slightly as it held a ruler over a five-year-old's head.

*Be as tall as you can.*

"Phase 2," I said. "Phase 2 of what?"

Nobody answered. Down in the garden behind the house, the goat bleated once, a short, flat, scornful note, like a man laughing at a joke he does not intend to explain.

"Daniel?" said Sophie.

"Yes."

"If this is Phase 2..."

"I know."

"Then what was Phase 1?"

I looked at the kumquat tree, and at the pears, and at the small blue star on the shelf that had been in my life for nine months and had known me for thirty-eight years.

"My entire life," I said.
