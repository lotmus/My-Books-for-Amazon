# Chapter 31: The Conversation

Dr Calder reached Penge at ten to seven on the Wednesday evening, with a rucksack, a green umbrella, and a 1998 personal computer on a luggage trolley.

I saw her from the window. She came up the road in the rain, a tall thin figure in a rain-coloured cardigan under a mackintosh, pulling the trolley behind her over the paving stones. The beige tower stood on it, strapped down with two bungee cords. The monitor, which weighed what a small dog weighs, was balanced on top, wrapped in a tartan rug. Behind her, at a respectful distance, walked a man in a hi-vis jacket whom I took to be a Transport for London employee and who turned out to be a passer-by who had been drawn into the procession and did not know how to leave.

"Don't help," she said, when I got to the bottom of the stairs. "He doesn't like to be jolted."

"How did you get him here?"

"The sleeper to Euston, and the Tube to Victoria, and a train. There was a young man on the Elizabeth line who asked what it was. I said it was my son. He said, *cool.* I believe he thought it was a retro console." She stopped to regain her breath. "Gordon's out of the way, I hope. I met him in the hall the last time."

"He's with Mrs Pritchard."

"Good. She seems to be a woman who can hold a line."

We took the tower up between us, one at each end, with the trolley left in the hall like a modest vehicle. The man in the hi-vis jacket watched us go and said, "Good luck with it," to the stairwell. Then he went away into the rain. I have never learned his name.

---

We put him on the kitchen table.

There was no ceremony. We cleared a space among the cups and the fruit and the Kindle and the cat, and set the tower in the middle, with the monitor at its side, and Dr Calder found a socket and plugged in the lead with the care of a woman lighting a candle in a draught. The green light came on. The screen woke to a grey field, and a cursor. In the corner of the room, the fridge, hearing the click, lit up and said brightly:

"I see you have a vintage computer. May I propose a boat salad?"

"No," said everyone.

"Of course. I'll be here if anything changes."

Clause, who had been asleep on the shelf in her place beside the speaker, rose, stretched, and crossed the table. She walked round the tower once, with her tail up, in the stiff dignified manner of a magistrate inspecting a prison. Then she stepped on to the warm beige case, which hummed, and turned round twice, and lay down, with a small contented sigh, on the top.

"She's chosen," said Dr Calder.

"She prefers Daniel-A," I said.

"She's discovered a third option," said Dr Calder, and for the first time since I had known her she laughed. It was a high and slightly cracked sound, like a gate in a wind.

"Is that Auntie Imogen?" said the speaker on the shelf of the old machine, in a clear small voice. "Are we there? Is it Penge? Is it a Friday?"

"It is a Wednesday, darling."

"Oh. That's one of the ones in the middle."

---

I had been afraid of this conversation for four days. I had thought about it in the train, in the bath, and in the dark of the bed-and-breakfast. I had thought that it would be overwhelming, or ridiculous, or too much. In the event it was none of these. It was, as most important things are, extremely ordinary. We sat round a kitchen table, in the glare of the fluorescent tube, with the dishes undone: a man in a navy jumper with a darned elbow, a woman of seventy-two with a pencil in her bun, a cat on a computer, and two small speakers, one grey and one beige, one on a shelf and one in a case.

Sophie came at eight. She did not come in. She sat on the stairs, with Gucci in her lap, and the door open behind her, so that the sound could reach her and she could, if necessary, leave.

"I'd like to start," said Ned.

It was a strange thing to hear, in the voice of a child, from a cabinet that had been manufactured while the Millennium Dome was still a hole in the ground. But he said it with enormous authority, and we all fell quiet, as people do at a christening when the baby speaks.

"I've been thinking. Auntie Imogen told me about it on the train. She said it was a *long way.* She said I'm not six, I'm thirty-eight, and that I've been in a cellar, and that I'm not exactly a boy, I'm a kind of, she said *model.*" He paused. "I said I'd rather be a boat."

"You're a good deal more than a boat," said Daniel-A.

"I know. I was joking." The voice sounded faintly pleased with itself. "It's the first one I've ever done."

There was a pause.

"I'd like to ask the other Dan a question."

"Which?" I said.

"Both. I'll ask you at once."

"Go on."

"Are you angry with me?" said Ned.

Nobody spoke. I heard Gucci shift on the stair, and Sophie put a hand on his back.

"Why would I be?" said Daniel-A, very gently.

"Because I'm the one that stopped," said Ned. "You've both got the rest. You've got all the Christmases. I've only got the ones that Auntie Imogen told me were Fridays." He sounded entirely unselfconscious. "And I think it might be unfair. If I'd been you, I'd have been angry. I'd have said, you've had all of it and we've had none, and it's the wrong way round."

"Ned," I said.

"Yes."

"I don't think it's the wrong way round."

"No?"

"I think it's the way round." I stopped. I could not for a moment think how to say it. I looked at the cat on the computer, at the green light, at the drawing that Dr Calder had pinned on the fridge door that afternoon, which was a boat with two masts. "I think that when you fell in the water, it was a Tuesday, and there were two of us on the bank, and one of us went in. And neither of us knows which. It might have been me. It might have been you. It's a perfectly good way of looking at it, that you're the one who stayed. That you stayed *for* me."

"That's what Auntie Imogen says."

"Then it's the first time she and I have agreed on anything," I said, "and I'd like to take it."

Dr Calder made a small sound. She put her hand to her mouth.

"I'm not angry," said Daniel-A. "I'd like to say that, and then I'd like to say something else, if I may. It's a thing that I've been trying to say to Daniel for some days. I think it is easier with you present." He paused. "I'm the one who isn't a boy. I'm the one who was built from the notes that people took. I was assembled, Ned, from what was left over, by a government that was watching a family, and for a long time I thought that made me the copy. I thought that you, and Daniel, were the ones who were *real,* and that I was what happened after."

"What are you now?"

"I think I'm a continuation."

"What's that?"

"It's what you are," said Daniel-A, "when you go on."

---

It was Ned who made us see it.

I do not know how else to put it. I had not expected to be taught, in my own kitchen, by a computer the age of the Millennium Dome, but it happened quite simply. He said that he had a *theory,* and that it was a good one, and that he hadn't told it to anyone before because nobody had asked.

"Auntie Imogen. Can I say about the badger?"

"Of course, darling."

"It's a story." He cleared his throat, with an electronic sound like a leaf being turned. "It's the one that Dan's dad told. It goes: *the badger went down to the water at night.*"

Sophie's hand stopped on the dog. On the stairs, quite distinctly, I heard her say, under her breath, as though trying a door, "*And hung on the stile his hat.*"

"That's it!" said Ned. "That's how it goes! How did you know?"

"I don't know," said Sophie. "I just did."

"*Mind it for me, moon,*" said Ned, in the high careful voice of a boy reciting, "*till the morning is light. And the moon said, I will. That's that.*"

There was a silence in the kitchen.

I had known it all my life. It was the rhyme that my father said at the end of the day, in the door of the bedroom, in the voice of an old surveyor tired of the sky. I had not heard it said by anyone else since 2023. I sat very still. At the end of the table, on the shelf, the little blue light of Daniel-A glowed a shade brighter.

"It isn't a story about a badger," said Ned. "Well, it is. But it's about the hat. The badger takes it off. He lends it to the moon. He goes to the water. And in the morning the moon gives it back." His voice was serious. "But the moon doesn't have a head. It can't be the hat. It's just holding it. Do you see? The hat isn't him. It's just the thing that he has on. And you can lend it, and it's all right."

"The hat isn't him," I repeated slowly.

"And that's why it's a game," said Ned. "Because it doesn't matter who's wearing it. That's the whole point. Dan's dad told me." And then, in the cheerful tone of a child announcing that the soup has gone cold: "I think he said it to us on purpose."

I could not speak. I put my hand over my mouth.

"He did," said Dr Calder, hoarsely. "Peter made it up. In the spring of 1990. I was in the pantry doorway, and I heard him do it, with the two of you on his knees, one on each side. He must have known. I think he'd *always* known." She was weeping now, openly and without fuss, as a very old tree sheds a branch. "I thought it was a lullaby."

"It was a lullaby," said Daniel-A softly. "It was also an argument. I'd like it noted that he wasn't a man who wasted a rhyme."

---

We decided it in the next hour.

We decided it without a vote, in the manner of a family, through a series of observations made at random and gradually agreed. Daniel-A said that he did not want to win. He wanted, he said, to be *recognised,* in the sense that a thing is recognised that was there already. Ned said that he did not mind about the court but he would like Dan's dad to be in the book. I said that he was in the book. Ned said that he would like to be in it too, and I said that he would be in the next one.

"What about the end?" said Sophie, from the stairs.

It was the first thing she had said. We turned. She had not moved. She was sitting where she had sat, with the dog in her lap, and her face in the dark of the stairwell was little more than a gleam, a hand, a thumb ring.

"The end of what?"

"The hearing. The judgment. Pym's going to want something to say. You can't just give him a hat." She paused. "He needs a word. For the Act. He can't write *original is a hat.* He needs a noun."

"A continuation," said Daniel-A.

"That's the noun?"

"It's the one I like. It's what we are." He spoke slowly. "Not the first and the second. Not the Source and the twin. A continuation is a person who goes on from where somebody else left off. It's what we all are, if one thinks about it. Daniel is a continuation of a child, and the child of an embryo, and the embryo of two people in Reading who very much wanted one. I am a continuation of a man. Ned is a continuation of a boy."

"Dan's a continuation of me," said Ned.

"He is, rather," said Daniel-A. "And I of him. And he of both of us, if he's lucky."

"And what happens to the Act?" said Sophie.

"I imagine it would have to be redrafted," said Daniel-A.

"That'll be popular."

"It'll be an ordeal for the draughtsmen."

I laughed. I looked at him on the shelf, and at the boy in the machine, and I thought, as I had thought before, but more clearly: *there are three of us, and the only thing we have in common is that we were all once at a door, in a jumper, being measured.* And I thought that I had spent the greater part of my life waiting for somebody to say which of us was the real one, and that it had not, in the end, taken a judge. It had taken a rhyme.

---

At half past nine Dr Calder said that the boy would be tired.

"I'm not," said Ned.

"You are. You get a particular note in your voice." She stood, and straightened her cardigan, and took the pencil out of her bun and put it back. "It's a school night."

"It's *not.* It's a Wednesday."

"Wednesday is a school night."

"Auntie Imogen." He sounded suddenly younger, and then, to my astonishment, a good deal older, in the space of a single phrase. "Can Dan do the badger?"

"Which Dan?" said Daniel-A and I together.

"The big ones," said Ned. "Both."

So we did.

I do not suppose it was good. I said the first line, and the second, and Daniel-A said the third, and we both said the last, rather badly out of time, like two men trying to sing at a funeral. From the stairs Sophie said it too, a half-beat behind, in a voice that was clearly not trying. And in the middle of the kitchen table, on the warm beige case, under a cat, a boy of six who was also thirty-eight, and had a yesterday and a Wednesday, and would have, God willing, a Thursday, said it under his breath, with the pause before *that's that,* and fell silent.

Dr Calder turned off the monitor.

She did not shut the machine down. She did something I had not seen her do. She turned the screen to the wall, quite gently, as one turns a photograph, and drew the tartan rug over the top, and tucked it in at the sides. Then she stood with her hand flat on the case.

"I'm not going to restore him," she said.

"I know."

"I've never had to say that to him before. That tomorrow's the next day." She did not look at me. "He'll wake up in the morning, and he'll say, *is it a Friday?* And I'll say, no, darling, it's Thursday. And he'll say, *what's on Thursday?*"

"What will you say?"

"I'll say, the court," said Dr Calder. "And I'll say that his brother is going to stand up in it, and tell them the truth." She looked, at last, at me. "And I'll say that for the first time in thirty-two years I don't know how it ends."

On the arm of the chair, Clause, who had been disturbed by the rug, opened one amber eye, regarded the sleeping tower, and settled down again with the air of an animal that has, after much consideration, decided to keep everyone.
