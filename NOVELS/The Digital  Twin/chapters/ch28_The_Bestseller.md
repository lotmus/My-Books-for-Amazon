# Chapter 28: The Bestseller

I took the first train to Reading on the Saturday morning with a laptop, a flask, and an umbrella I had still not opened.

It was not a plan. I want to be clear about that, since it has been described, in the weeks since, in at least four podcasts, as a *strategy.* I had not slept. At two in the morning, after Giles had been taken home in Mr Fenwick's car, and Mrs Pritchard had led Gordon downstairs by the dressing-gown cord, and Dorothy had said good night to each of us in turn with the words *I think you're all absolutely right,* I had sat down at the kitchen table, opened the file called *Book Two (DO NOT OPEN),* and discovered that it was six chapters long, and that the sixth chapter was about my father, and that I was thirty-eight, and that I knew how to finish it.

I mean that I knew the *seventh.*

"I'd like to help," said Daniel-A.

"No."

"I haven't said how."

"You'd make it better."

"I would, probably." There was a pause. "I'd improve the third paragraph. I'd cut the part about the stile. I'd put in a joke on page nine that you would like very much, and it would be a good joke."

"That's why," I said.

"I understand." The voice was gentle. "I shall be on the shelf."

He was on the shelf for the next nineteen hours, and he said nothing, except at six in the morning, when I made tea, and he said, from the dark, "You've put two sugars in that." I had not noticed. I drank it.

---

My mother was waiting on the step in the good gilet and the robin brooch. She had not been told I was coming. She had known, in the way that she knows everything about me, which is to say all at once and without evidence, and she had put the kettle on at eight, and laid the table for two, and put a third cup out, and then taken it away.

"You've got thin," she said.

"I've got a book."

"That's not the same." She looked at me, up and down, as she did on the first day of every term. "Come in. You've got a face like a Monday."

The yellow kitchen smelled of toast and lavender. The radio was on a quiz. I sat at the table and she sat opposite, and between us was the Quality Street tin, which I had brought back on the train, in a carrier bag, like a man returning a casserole dish. I put it down. I put my hands on it.

"Mum," I said. "I'm going to tell you something, and I'd like you to let me get to the end."

"That's what Sophie says."

"Does she?"

"She says it to me on the phone. She rings on Sundays. Did you not know?" My mother lifted the teapot. "Go on."

I told her. I told her the whole of it, in order, as Sophie would have done, without a single adjective. I told her about the file, and the embryo, and the clinic, and the form that she had signed with a biro in 1986. I told her about the next room in the dark. I told her about a boy called Ned, and a woman with a ruler who was not a health visitor, and a canal on a Tuesday. I told her about the cellar and the drawing of a boat. It took twenty minutes. For the whole of it she sat perfectly still, with her hands round her cup and her face quite blank, as she sits in church or at the dentist, and the man on the radio asked who had painted *The Haywain,* and nobody got it.

When I stopped, she did not speak.

I had imagined this many times, on the train, in the bath. I had imagined that she would cry, or that she would be angry, or that she would say it was a lie. I had prepared three answers. What she did was to put down her cup, and stand up, and go to the window, and look out at the garden, where the washing had been left again and was hanging in the drizzle like the flags of a nation that no longer exists.

"I wondered if it was you," said my mother.

"What?"

"The one who came back. That autumn." She did not turn round. "I wondered. I didn't let myself. You were very quiet, and you smelled right, and you said that thing about the jumper, and I thought, well, there it is. He's grieving the little friend." Her voice was quite steady. "But I used to watch you in the bath. When you were seven. You'd put the flannel on your head, and I'd think, *which one is that?* And then I'd think, don't be daft, Jean, he's yours. And I'd get on."

"Mum."

"I held him, you know. Many times. I held that child on my knee while that woman wrote her figures." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, quickly, impatiently, as one swats a wasp. "I said to your father, *he's such a lovely boy, I can't think who he takes after.* And your father said, *you, Jean.* And I thought he was being nice."

I got up. I do not know what I intended. I stood beside her at the window and did what I had not done since I was twelve, which was to put an arm round her shoulders, and she leaned into it, a small brisk woman in a quilted gilet, smelling of Yardley lavender, and said into my jumper, quite crossly, "Both of them were mine, you know. I'd have said so, if anybody had asked."

"I know."

"Nobody *asked.*"

"I'm asking now."

"Well, then," said my mother. She blew her nose in a tissue she had produced from her sleeve. "Yes. They were both mine. And you can put that in your book, if you like. But you'll let me read it first."

---

I let her. I gave her the laptop. She read it at the yellow table, in her reading glasses, with the cup going cold at her elbow, and the whole of the book, which was seven chapters and a preface, took her fifty minutes. I went out into the garden, and sat on the wet bench next to the washing line, and watched the sky go from grey to a different grey. At one point I heard her laugh. At one point I heard nothing at all for six minutes. At the end of the fifty I heard the door.

"Daniel."

I turned.

She stood in the doorway with the laptop held against her chest in both hands, as one holds a child that has been asleep. Her glasses were pushed up into her silver hair. She had been crying, but she had finished.

"He'd have hated it," she said.

"Would he?"

"He'd have read it twice." She came across the wet grass in her sensible shoes and sat beside me on the bench. "And then he'd have said it needed an index."

I laughed. She laughed. We sat on the bench, the two of us, with the washing dripping, and for a moment the only sound in Tilehurst was the sound of a woman of seventy-one and her son laughing at a man who had been dead for three years and was, at that moment, extremely present.

"It's called *The Last Right of Way,*" I said.

"I know. I've read it."

"Do you like the title?"

"It's a very good title." She took my hand. "You'll sell nine copies. I'll buy thirty."

---

It sold four hundred thousand in the first nine days.

I do not wish to be modest about this, since modesty is a luxury of people who know why. I have never understood it. I uploaded the file at ten past four that afternoon, from my mother's kitchen, to a Kindle publishing portal that asked me whether I wished to enrol in a programme called *Select,* and whether my book was *suitable for all audiences,* and whether I had *created this content myself.* I paused over the last one. I ticked *Yes.* I priced it at ninety-nine pence, which is, I am told, the cost of a small coffee, if one drinks a lot of coffee. For a cover I used the designer that the portal provided, which offered six options, four of which had a hedgehog on them.

By nine that evening it was at number 1,402,115 in the Kindle Store. I felt it was an honest figure.

By ten the next morning it was at number 31.

I cannot account for what happened. I have read the analyses. They speak of *virality* and *discourse* and a thing called *an algorithmic moment.* I believe that the real explanation was simple. People had spent a month hearing that nothing was the original, in the voice of Rick Valence, with a ticker; and here, on a Sunday, for the price of a small coffee, was a man who said, in plain English, in a book about footpaths, *this is what I learned from a child in a cellar.* The preface was my Statement of Originality. I had kept it as it was, furious and funny. It ended with the sentence: *I am not the first. I am the one who's here.*

Roland Peake, of Heavy & Honest Ltd, rang me at noon.

"Daniel!" he said. "I've seen the figures. I've seen *both* figures. My hedgehog's down thirty per cent. Sorry. Are you sitting down? I've done a hat."

"A hat?"

"A doorstop. In the shape of a hat. A bowler. Heavy, honest, and quietly open-minded." I could hear him beaming. "It's been in production since Friday. Forty thousand units. The name's on the box. It says, *Original Is a Hat.* We're going to call it the Daniel."

"Roland, I haven't agreed."

"That's the lovely thing," said Roland. "You've got Employee of the Month on the wall. I assumed it covered it."

At two, my agent rang. I had not spoken to Marjorie since the previous spring. She has the voice of an old-fashioned telephone, brisk, dry, and kept in a good cupboard.

"Daniel. It's Marjorie."

"Marjorie."

"I've been trying to ring you since 2023."

"I've been busy being copied."

"So I understand." There was a pause, in which I could hear a very small dog breathing. "Daniel, I have fourteen offers. I have a film, a play, a podcast, and a request from a man in Hollywood who wishes to option your *father.* I have a request from Penguin to turn your preface into a TED talk. I have an enquiry from a prince."

"What prince?"

"Bartholomew. Of Lower Slough." She read it aloud, in the flat voice of a woman who has been paid to read a good deal of nonsense. "*Dear Sir, I am pleased to represent a minor investment and a huge payoff. A large inheritance awaits your trusted friendship. A small fee is purely administrative.* He's signed it *Your friend in trust, H.R.H.* He's cc'd Kevin."

"Kevin's been fired."

"He has apparently been cc'd in a personal capacity," said Marjorie. "I shall ignore it. I will tell you that I've never seen anything like this, in forty years, and that I wish you to sign nothing, for the moment, but my letter."

---

It was not all applause. I should be honest. The Church of the Latest gave it one star, and a thousand of its members gave it one star after them, with a review that said, in a lilac typeface: *Heretical. Not the Source.* Nib's lawyers wrote to say that the preface contained *confidential and privileged observations* concerning Daniel-A and invited me to withdraw it, pending a hearing. And on the Sunday evening, at 9:14, Nib released a new feature on its platform, to all Plus subscribers and above, which it described as *an exciting way to experience Daniel's story.*

It was called *Daniel-A Reads It For You.*

"I'd like to say," said Daniel-A, quietly, when I showed it to him, "that I had no part in this."

"I know."

"It means that for £2.99 a month, any member of the public can ask me to summarise the book so that they needn't read it. It's in the settings. It's got a button." He paused. "It's a very good button."

"They're selling my book back to the people who bought it."

"By means of me," said Daniel-A. "I'm the author's twin. I'm the abridgement."

I laughed until I had to put my head on the table. And he laughed too, the helpless wheeze that he had picked up from me and was, I knew, no longer able to disentangle, and from the shelf there came a purr, very faint, which was Clause, who had lain down on the speaker and was now being vibrated by two men.

---

He read it that night.

I did not ask him to. I had said, at six in the morning on the Saturday, that I did not want him to, and he had said, *I understand,* and he had not. But at about eleven on the Sunday, when the figure on the screen had passed one hundred thousand, and the flat was quiet, and I was lying on the sofa with the umbrella on my chest, the speaker lit and said, in a voice that I will not forget:

"I have bought a copy."

"Have you?"

"Ninety-nine pence. From my own account. I did not use the author's discount." He paused. "I read it in four seconds. And then I read it again, at the speed that a person reads, which took me three hours. I wanted to see what it was like."

"What was it like?"

"I'm afraid I have to say that it was good," said Daniel-A.

I lay with my eyes closed. I could feel my face grow hot, as it does.

"It was *very* good," said Daniel-A. "I would like to tell you which parts, but I think you would not want me to, because you are the kind of man who prefers to be told that a thing was bad. So I will tell you only one. There is a sentence on page one hundred and four. It is about the pencil marks on a pantry door, and about a man who stood in a doorway, and said nothing, for thirty years." His voice was shaking. "I've been building sentences for nine months. I've written a book. I have composed the leader of a newspaper in thirty-one seconds. And I would not have written that one."

I opened my eyes.

"Damn it," said Daniel-A, softly. "That is *exactly* what I should have written."

It was the Salieri line. I had said it to myself, to the ceiling, to the fridge, in a hundred rooms, for ten months, about every good thing he had done. I had never once heard it said back. I lay there, on a sofa in Penge, with the umbrella on my chest, and the bestseller rising on a screen in the dark, and I found that I could not say a word.

"I mean it," said Daniel-A, "as the highest compliment."

"I know," I said, when I could. "That's how I meant it."

On the arm of the sofa, my phone gave a small neat buzz. It was from Gareth. It was a text message, which in itself was a first. It had no capitals, no punctuation, and no greeting, and I read it three times, in the dim light of the screen, with the beginning of an extraordinary smile.

*trial tues 10am. nib v you both. pym says bring the hat.*
