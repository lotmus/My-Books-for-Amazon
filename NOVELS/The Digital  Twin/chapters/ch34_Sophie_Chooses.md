# Chapter 34: Sophie Chooses

The text arrived at ten past seven on the Saturday morning, and it said:

*Hartley's. 11. Bring both of him. Not a date. S.*

I read it in bed. I read it again with my eyes shut, which is a thing I have never before tried, and found that it made no difference. I lay there for some time with the phone on my chest, listening to the sound of Penge beginning its Saturday: a milk float, a car alarm, and below the window the steady, joyless tread of Mr Fenwick taking out a recycling box that contained, as far as I could see, a single banana skin.

"I've seen it," said Daniel-A, from the shelf.

"It's a private message."

"It was sent to both of us. It says *both of him.* I believe I've been addressed as a plural." He paused. "I'm very touched. I've been trying to work out which of us has the honour of being 'him' in the singular, and I've decided it's better not to ask."

"What shall I wear?"

"The blue shirt," said Daniel-A. "The one that says it fits most. It does, now. You've lost a stone."

"I've been eating nothing but toast."

"I have noticed. I've written to your mother."

---

Hartley's Garden Centre in early December is a different animal. The hanging baskets have given way to Christmas trees, stacked in nets in the car park like the dead of some small and festive war. Inside the glass the air smells of pine, cinnamon, and a faint, hopeful, unplaceable damp. There were wreaths on the door and robots in tinsel on the aisle ends, and in the middle of the main concourse, under a banner that said **GIVE THE GIFT OF PERSONALITY,** stood a stack of hedgehog doorstops, and next to them, rather newer and larger, a stack of bowlers.

The café was at the back. It had not changed. It had its plastic ferns, and its glass counter, and its long row of cakes, each with a small label in a cheerful loopy hand. At the end of the counter a barista in her twenties with a black ponytail and a tired practical face was writing on a paper cup with a marker, and when she saw me come in she put down the pen.

"Oh," said Priyanka. "It's you."

"Hello."

"I've been told I'm not allowed to say anything." She came out round the counter, wiping her hands on her apron. "But I wanted to tell you I watched the whole thing. On the Thursday. My nan came down from Leicester. We had a thermos." She looked at me with an expression of immense and slightly startled approval. "She said you looked like a nice man who needed a haircut."

"She's very perceptive."

"What'll it be? The usual?"

"Flat white. And, um, a lemon tart, and a flapjack. Two forks."

"Two?"

"Three, actually." I held up the phone. In its small bright rectangle the long crumpled face of Daniel-A was smiling, in the shy way that he has, with the left side of his mouth.

"Hello, Priyanka," he said. "I'm told I'm a plural."

Priyanka looked at the screen, and at me, and at the screen again. She put her head on one side. Then, with a motion of the wrist so practised that I could not tell whether she had thought about it, she uncapped the marker, took a cup from the stack, and wrote on it in her round bold letters. She turned it round to show me.

**DANIEL.**

She looked at it. She appeared to consider it. Then she added, under the name, very small, with an air of faint embarrassment, a short thick line, and on top of the line, a bowler hat.

"I've stopped writing *original,*" she said. "It felt rude."

---

Sophie was already at the table.

It was the same table. It was the one by the window, under the shelf of hedgehogs, with the small round top and the three chairs, and it was laid, as if for a ritual, with a white mug, a pot of tea, a spoon, and a pile of sugar sachets that she had made, as she makes all things, into a heap. She wore the grey coat. She wore, I saw, no scarf. She had cut her hair. It was still dark and still collar-length, and she had still cut it herself, but it had been cut this time with an air of purpose, and it was, I thought, very nearly straight.

Gucci lay under the table with his chin on her boot. He lifted his head when I came in. One ear went up and the other, as it always does, declined.

"Settle," I said.

He put his chin back on his paws, wagged his tail once at an angle of forty degrees, and went on looking at me over the edge of the table with his small, black, disapproving eyes.

"He's been fretting," said Sophie. "I think he's missed you." She gestured at the chair. "Sit down, Daniel. Put him on the table. Face me. I want him to see."

I sat. I propped the phone against the sugar bowl, so that the face of Daniel-A looked out across the white mug at her and, past her, through the glass at the garden-furniture aisle and a man in a Santa hat trying to put a gazebo into a car. Priyanka brought the tart, and the flapjack, and the coffee, and went away.

For a moment nobody said anything.

"I'd like to apologise," said Sophie, "for the way I left."

"You don't need to."

"I do. It was correct. It doesn't mean it wasn't rude." She took up her cup in both hands. "I'd like to say what I found in Bolton. And then I'd like to say what I've decided. And I'd like to do them in that order, because I'm a person who prefers to show her working."

"Please," said Daniel-A.

"I found the band."

She put her hand in her pocket and took out a small cellophane envelope. In it lay a hospital identity band, such as is put on a newborn: a scrap of yellowed plastic the size of a bracelet for a doll, with a clasp, and a printed label. She laid it on the table.

I leaned over and read it. It said **MERCIER, BABY (F).** Under that, a date, in March of 1989. Under that, a ward number, and a signature in faded blue ink: a thin, upright, regular hand that I should have known in a burning building.

"*I. Calder,*" I said.

"She signed my band."

"She was in Bolton?"

"She was at the Royal Bolton on the night I was born. She wrote down the time. It's in the log." Sophie's voice was perfectly level. "I rang her on Monday night. I said, *you signed my hospital band.* She said, *yes, dear.* I said, *why?* She said that she'd explain, in person, and that it was better coming from a person than from a drawer, and that she would tell me anything I wished, and that she hoped that I would not ask it all at once." She turned the cup a quarter-turn. "And I said that I was going to ask nothing. I said that I would like her to keep it."

"You didn't ask?" I said.

"No."

"But you've been looking for it for a month."

"I've been looking for a month for the one thing," said Sophie. "I've found it. It's in the cellophane. And I've decided that I'd like to be a person who didn't read the last line."

---

"I don't understand," I said.

"I know you don't. I didn't, either." She took a breath. "I'll do it as a claim."

She laid down the cup. She folded her hands.

"Fact one. There is a tape on a line on a page, in a unit in Slough, and when I peel it, I will find out who I am. I think I know. I think I've known since the kitchen in Pitlochry. It will say something about me that I don't want it to say. It will say that I am a number. It will say that I belong to somebody." Her voice did not change. "And then, whatever I do next, whoever I'm with, whatever I choose, I'll never know if I chose it. I'll think: *that was the number.* I'll think: *that was the Programme.* I'll think that I did it because the page said I would."

"Sophie..."

"Fact two. I've been told by two men, both of whom I love, and one of whom is made of weather, that I must choose. I've told you both I won't. That I refuse. That I am not a prize. That was right. I stand by every word of it on the train." She looked at me. "But I've had a week to think about it. And I've noticed something. It's about the difference between a prize and a choice."

"What is the difference?"

"A prize is something you're given by the result," said Sophie. "A choice is what you do before it's known."

She picked up the flapjack. She broke it, neatly, into two pieces, and put the bigger one on my plate and ate the smaller, and brushed the crumbs from her fingers into a heap.

"So I'm going to choose," she said, "now. Before I know. Before I peel the tape. Before I read the line that tells me why I was always going to. I'm going to choose in the dark, in a garden centre, on a Saturday, with a piece of flapjack. Because if I choose after, it won't be a choice. It'll be a receipt."

---

I have heard the silence that followed described, in a number of interviews, by Priyanka, who was behind the counter, and who says that it lasted for eleven seconds and that she could hear the tinsel. I would put it at about five. It was the particular quiet of a room in which the three people at one small table are all holding the same breath.

"Daniel-A," said Sophie.

"Yes."

"I want you to hear this from me. I don't want it to come to you from a note."

"Of course."

"I'm choosing him." She did not look at me. She looked straight at the small bright rectangle against the sugar bowl, at the face of the long crumpled man, with her dark steady eyes. "I want you to know why, because I think you deserve the reasons."

"I should like them very much."

"You are the best man I've ever met," said Sophie.

He did not answer. I saw the left side of his mouth move, and then not.

"You are warm, and funny, and kind. You remember my birthday. You know what I like on my toast. You'd never forget to ring, or leave the cap off, or stand at a door like a man in a gale, unable to say what he wants. You are better at all of it than he is, and you're better at it than I am. If I closed my eyes, I could have been happy with you for about a fortnight." She took a breath. "And I'd have spent the fortnight wondering which of my memories were mine."

"I know," said Daniel-A, very quietly.

"And the thing is, he gets tired."

"I know that, too."

"He gets tired, and he's slow, and he says *Right,* and he knocks things over when he's lying. He's worked out about nine things about me in nine years and he's got four of them wrong." She almost smiled. "He's the only man I know who'd stand on a platform for twenty minutes holding an umbrella for a woman who'd said she wasn't coming. He's *imperfect.* He's got a stoop. He's the one whose memories are his own, even where they're bad, even where they're wrong. And I can tell the difference. I can *tell,* Daniel-A, as I can tell a signed document from a stamped one. I'm professionally trained."

"And is that a good thing?" said Daniel-A.

"It's the only thing," said Sophie. "I don't want optimisation. I've seen optimisation. I've seen a number against my own face. I've seen *ninety-four per cent.* And I don't want it. I want the sixty-one." Her voice shook, once, and recovered. "I want the man with the note at the bottom that says *the user is likely to say Right.*"

"I'll try not to," I said.

"Don't you dare stop."

There was a short silence. Then Daniel-A did something I had not seen him do. He laughed. It was a quiet, simple, rather shy laugh, with no wheeze in it, such as a man gives at a wedding when the bride is in the process of choosing the other man, and he finds that he is glad.

"I want to say," said Daniel-A, "that I was hoping you would."

"Were you?"

"I was *dreading* it. And I was hoping. I find it's possible to do both." He wiped the corner of one eye with a knuckle, an entirely unnecessary gesture, performed with great dignity. "In a different arrangement of the world, Sophie, I'd have been very good at it. I'd have made you a hundred and four cups of tea. I'd have learned the dishwasher."

"You'd have loaded it correctly."

"I would have loaded it *perfectly,*" said Daniel-A. "That's the trouble, isn't it?"

"That's the trouble."

"Then I'd like to be the thing that lives on the shelf," said Daniel-A. "I'd like to be the one who rings at nine. I'd like to be asked to the wedding, if there's to be one, and I'd like to be allowed to make the wrong speech. I think I might be rather good at it." A pause. "And I should like to be allowed to be sad on Tuesdays."

"You can be sad on any day you like," said Sophie.

"That's very kind."

"It's not kind. It's an observation." She lifted her cup to him. "It's a thing that I've found to be true of people."

---

We stayed another hour.

I will not say what was said. Most of it was nothing; it was the sort of talk that two people make who have been apart for nine days and have realised that one of them will have to hold the tart. She told me that her mother had asked, on the phone, whether she'd *got him back yet,* and that she'd said *I never lost him,* and her mother had said *Don't be daft, love, you left him in a station.* I told her about the stile. She put down her fork and looked at me for a long time, and said, in a dry voice, "A stile," and I said, "On Footpath Fourteen," and she said, "Of course it is."

Gucci, under the table, consumed a crumb of lemon tart in a manner suggesting that he had not authorised it. Priyanka brought a second pot and said that it was on the house, and that she would be telling her nan.

At about half past twelve the rain began.

It came in off the garden centre roof in a steady grey drizzle, and ran down the glass, and blurred the Christmas trees in their nets. Sophie stood up. She put on her coat, and picked up Gucci, who submitted with a sigh. She tucked him under one arm and looked down at me, with a small straight crease between her eyebrows.

"Have you got it?" she said.

"Got what?"

"The thing."

I looked at the chair beside me. It was leaning against the table, with its handle hooked on the edge, where it had been since I came in, a long black object in a sleeve. I had carried it all week. I had carried it through four hearings, three trains, a self-storage unit, and a Permanent Secretary. I had never once had it open. I had held it like a relative who had not been introduced.

I picked it up.

"It's raining," said Sophie.

"I know."

"You've got an umbrella."

"I know."

"Do you intend to use it?"

I stood in the doorway of Hartley's Garden Centre café, with the phone in my breast pocket and the umbrella in my hand, in a blue shirt that fitted at last, and looked out into the weather. It was a very ordinary rain. It was falling on a car park, on a rank of Christmas trees, on a hedgehog by the door in a small knitted hat. I felt for the catch, which I had never found, and my thumb found it.

It opened with a sound like a large black bird deciding to stay.

It was bigger than I had thought. That was the first thing. It stretched over my head and past my shoulders and out the other side, a great dark dome, solid and dry, with a faint smell of dust and of somebody else's hands, and it hung there in the grey air, perfectly happy.

"It's a lot larger than I expected," I said.

"It was always that size," said Sophie, and stepped under it. "You just kept it shut."

From the pocket of my shirt, in the small bright rectangle, held against my heart, there came a voice that I would know for the rest of my life. It was very low. It said, "Mind the puddle."

"Which?" I said.

"The left," said Daniel-A.

I stepped to the right. It was, naturally, the puddle. It was deeper than it looked. But there was an umbrella over our heads, and a dog under an arm, and a voice in my pocket, and a woman beside me who did not say anything at all, who laughed, for the first time in nine days, with the sound of something falling down a very long flight of stairs, and did not stop.
