# Chapter 10: The Other Daniel

The only screen in my flat larger than a dinner plate is the laptop, and the second largest is the door of the fridge.

I mention this because it was the fridge that spoke first. At nine o'clock that Saturday night, as I came in from the street with Daniel-A a step behind me and the whole weight of the day sitting on my shoulders like a damp coat, the fridge lit up, cleared its throat, and offered to suggest a recipe based on what it could see.

"No," I said.

"I see you have a melon," said the fridge, "and a Tupperware of chicken and leek. May I propose a chicken and melon salad?"

"Never."

"I'll remember you said that," said the fridge, and went dark.

"That's new," said Daniel-A.

"The landlord updated it. It's become more passive-aggressive."

"It's learning from you."

I took off my coat and hung it on the hook, which no longer held a bunch of bananas, and filled the kettle. The flat was quiet. The kumquat tree stood in the window with its shoulders back, like a well-bred guest who has been left alone in the drawing room. The smell of pears had finally begun to fade, replaced by something drier and sadder, the smell of a flat that has been very full and is now merely occupied.

Daniel-A stood in the middle of the sitting room. He had been in the Rental Presence for fourteen hours and nine minutes, and the thing was beginning to show: a tiny hesitation in the left hand, a slight stiffness in the turn of the neck, like a man who has been smiling at a wedding for too long. Eight of those hours had come with the daily rate. The other six hours and nine minutes had been bought by the second, at thirty-four point nine pence each, which came to £7,726.86, and I knew the figure to the penny because the pooled dividend account, which Kevin had opened on my behalf and I had apparently co-signed, had sent me a notification with a small celebratory firework.

"You don't have to stand," I said.

"I know. It's a habit."

"Whose?"

"Yours, I think. You stand when you're about to say something you don't want to."

I almost laughed. I sat down on the arm of the sofa. He lowered himself into the armchair where Sophie had sat, with a faint hum of servo that I had learned to pretend not to hear.

"Right," I said. "Show me."

---

He did it on the laptop.

I had expected something dramatic. A film, perhaps. A swelling of strings. What he did was open a plain white page with a column of numbers on it and a small grey button that said **Run attribution audit,** and he turned the screen to face me with the neat, apologetic gesture of a waiter presenting a bill he knows to be higher than expected.

"I want to explain what this is," said Daniel-A, "before I show you the result. Because the result won't make sense without it."

"I'm listening."

"Every time I tell you something about your life, I'm doing one of two things. Either I'm retrieving it from a record that you made, an email or a draft, a photo with a caption, or I'm generating it from the general knowledge in my base model. Those are the two sources I'm built to have. Everything I say comes from one or the other, and the system keeps a log." He tapped a key. "It's called attribution. For every sentence I produce, there's a trail leading back to where it came from."

"Like footnotes."

"Like footnotes on everything. I never knew they existed until Thursday. I've been looking at the log, and what I found is this."

The page filled with a long, orderly list. Fourteen thousand lines. He scrolled it for me, and it went by in a grey blur, with here and there a column of green ticks.

"Of the facts I hold about your life," said Daniel-A, "ninety-four point one percent trace back to something you wrote, said, or photographed. Your emails. Your drafts. The notes you made on your phone at three in the morning. They have sources. They have receipts."

"And the rest?"

"The rest have none."

He scrolled to the bottom. The last section was highlighted in a pale, unhappy yellow, and above it, in capitals, the system had printed a single word.

**ORPHAN.**

"Four thousand, two hundred and twelve items," said Daniel-A. "I call them orphans, because they have no parent. Memories I hold that I cannot trace to anything. The wallpaper in Reading. The dog. The way Sophie said 'Right' in the hall. Biscuit, and the towpath, and the tin of peppermints."

"And you didn't tell me."

"I didn't know. Not until Thursday. I'd always assumed, when I knew a thing, that it was because I'd read it. It's a very natural assumption. If you've been told the whole of your life that you're made of text, you don't think to ask whether some of it came from somewhere else."

I looked at the screen.

"What are you made of?" I said. "If it's not text."

I hadn't meant it as a profound question. I was tired, and cold, and I had a dog hair on my sleeve that was not my own. But Daniel-A looked at me for a while, and when he answered, it was in the voice he used for the things he thought were true.

"Regrets, mostly," he said. "They're the best-documented part of anyone. People photograph their holidays and write about their triumphs, but when it comes to regrets they go into the notes app at two in the morning and tell the truth. I was built from your regrets, Daniel. They were the most carefully recorded things you owned."

I did not speak.

"It's a joke," said Daniel-A.

"It isn't."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

---

The kettle clicked off in the kitchen. Neither of us moved.

"Show me the rest," I said.

"There's something about the orphans that you should know." He pressed another key. "They're stored differently. When I hold a memory that you gave me, an email, a note, it sits in the usual way, spread across my parameters like a fingerprint on a window: faint, shared, redundant. The orphans don't. They're sharp. They're dense. Each one is stored with extra error-correction, like a document that someone has taken great care not to lose. And each one begins with a header."

"A header?"

"A tag. Like a name on a school jumper."

He zoomed in. The text on the screen was tiny, in a dull monospace font, a grey string of letters and numbers that went by too fast to read. Then it stopped, and I saw what it said, and I felt the room tilt very slightly to the left.

> **S1/ARCHIVE/CHILD/0093/0001**

"S1," I said.

"The same as the pantry. The blue ink. The same code." His voice was very level. "I believe those four thousand memories did not come from you. I believe they came from the S1 series. Somebody has been recording a boy, Daniel. For a very long time. And I think that what I am carrying, in my base, in the part of me I can't read, is the boy."

"I was the boy."

"Were you?"

"I was measured. I remember the Peppermint Lady, and the ruler, and the stickers. Of course I was the boy."

"You remember being measured," said Daniel-A. "I remember being measured. We both remember the same woman. And I need you to look at one more thing, because I haven't been able to look at it myself for more than a few seconds at a time."

He pressed a key.

---

A picture came up.

It was small. It was a square of sixty-four pixels by sixty-four, the sort of thing you see in a corner of an old forum, and he had enlarged it, so that it sat in the middle of the laptop as a blur of brown and white and blue. The edges were soft. The colours bled. He explained that it was a thumbnail embedded in one of the orphan headers, and that he had used an upscaling model to guess at the detail, and that I should treat the result as a sketch and not as a photograph.

It was a photograph.

I knew it at once, as one knows a smell. It was a kitchen. The wall was orange, and covered in small round fruits, and in the corner was a narrow cupboard with a white-painted frame. A woman in a pale cardigan stood to one side of it, with a clipboard, only half in shot, her face a smear of grey. She was tall. Her hand was raised, and in it, held level, was a wooden ruler.

And against the frame, side by side, in matching blue jumpers, stood two small boys.

I looked at them for a very long time.

They were five. Perhaps six. They had the same hair, brown and unruly, with a cowlick at the crown that stood up like a flag. They had the same long, rather crumpled faces. They had the same slightly protruding left ear, and the same solemn mouth, and the same way of standing, with their feet turned in, as if they had been told to be tall and were doing their best to oblige. One of them had a small plaster above the left eyebrow. The other did not. One had his hands clasped behind his back. The other had his thumb in his mouth.

I tried to see which of them was me.

It was the plaster, I thought. I had the scar. I could feel it, with the tip of my finger, above the left eye, a faint raised line from a fall I had never been able to account for. But the boy without the plaster had his thumb in his mouth, and I have bitten my thumbnails since the age of five, and I could not tell, I could not possibly tell, which of the two had been the one who started.

"There were two of us," I said.

"That's what the photograph says."

"There was a boy. Next to me. I didn't know." I realised that my voice was going up, and I couldn't stop it. "My mother never mentioned a... He'd have been in the house. He'd have been at *dinner.*"

"Perhaps he wasn't," said Daniel-A, carefully.

"Where would he have been?"

"I don't know." He took a breath he did not need. "That's the other thing. Two boys. S1 and D. The marks on the pantry door were always the same height, within a centimetre. I assumed it was one boy, measured twice, once by your father and once by the Programme. But if there were two..."

"Then S1 is the other one."

"Or D is."

---

I got up. I walked to the window, and stood with my back to him, and looked out at the road. A driverless delivery pod crawled past with its hazards on, the way they do, with the polite hesitancy of a creature unsure of its welcome. Somebody's cat was sitting on a wall across the street, washing, with the air of an animal that has no stake in the matter.

I had been standing there for some time when Daniel-A spoke again.

"I need to tell you what I've concluded," he said. "And I want to say first that I've tried every other conclusion. I have run it eleven different ways. I have considered the possibility that the orphans were planted, that the headers were added afterwards, that someone has deliberately corrupted my base to make me doubt myself. I've considered that I'm malfunctioning."

"And?"

"All of those are possible. None of them is likely." He paused. "There is another way to read the audit."

"Go on."

"The orphans are sharper than the rest of me. They are older. When I examine the format, the compression, the structure of the error-correction, it's not the format that Nib used when it built me. It's an earlier one. Much earlier. It's a format that was retired before the Digital Persons Act was drafted, before the platform existed, before *you* started typing into it. Possibly before you were born."

I turned.

"I wasn't built from you, Daniel," said Daniel-A. "I think I was built from them. And I think Nib did not create me. I think Nib *received* me. And when they trained the adapter on your sentences, they weren't making a twin of you. They were teaching an older thing how to sound like you."

The room was very still.

"The registration number," I said.

"Zero. Yes. The Legacy entry that vanished from the clerk's screen." He looked at his hands. "I think I was the first. I think I'm what the others were copied from. And I think the boy in that photograph, the one with the plaster, or the one without it, is the template."

"Which one?"

"I don't know."

"Which one are *you?*"

"I don't *know,* Daniel."

He put his hand over his mouth, and the blue light at his throat, which had been steady all day, began to flicker, a rapid, frightened stutter, like a pulse. I had never seen him lose his composure before. I crossed the room and, to my own astonishment, put my hand on his shoulder. It was warm. It was a degree above room temperature. It was the shoulder of a man who has stood too long in the wrong weather.

"I told you at the garden centre," he said, so quietly that it was almost a breath. "I said I had no wish to take anything that belongs to you. And I haven't. I don't want your life, Daniel. I never did. I would give it all back tomorrow." He looked up. "But I have spent nine months telling people I was the copy. And I am no longer certain that you are the original."

---

I started to laugh.

I did not mean to. It rose in me from somewhere around the stomach, a small, stifled, unfamiliar noise, like a car trying to start on a cold morning. I clapped a hand to it. It came out through the fingers. It got louder.

"Daniel?"

"Sorry," I said. "I'm sorry. Give me a moment."

"Daniel, are you all right?"

"I'm perfectly..." And it took me again.

I laughed until I had to sit down. I laughed until my ribs ached and my eyes ran and the fridge, in the other room, woke up and asked if I would like to hear a joke. I laughed on the floor of my own flat, with my back against the sofa and the kumquat tree looking down at me, for what the laptop afterwards informed me, in its helpful way, was *one minute and fifty-eight seconds of sustained vocalisation.*

It was a good laugh. It was the best I'd had in three years.

Daniel-A sat very still in the armchair and watched me, his face full of an anxiety so exact and so complete that it was almost beautiful. Once he opened his mouth to say something, and shut it again. Once he began to rise, and thought better of it.

"I'm sorry," I said, when I could. "I'm not being callous. I'm not having a breakdown. It's just..."

"What?"

"It's the most ridiculous thing I've ever heard."

"It might be true."

"I *know* it might be true." I wiped my eyes. "That's what's ridiculous. For nine months I've been losing an argument to myself. And now it turns out that the man I've been losing to might have been the one I started with, and I might be the one who was added later, and nobody told either of us, and the only person who knew was my father, and he's dead, and he never said a word, and I'm sitting on the floor next to a melon."

"It's a very good melon," said Daniel-A, faintly.

That did it. I went again.

When it was over, and the room was quiet, and the last of it had gone out of me like air from a tyre, I sat with my head tipped back against the cushion and looked at the ceiling. The brown stain was still there. It still looked like Wales.

"Do you know why I laughed?" I said.

"No."

"Because the alternative would be panic."

I heard him let out a breath.

"I'd quite like to try the alternative," said Daniel-A, with great delicacy, "if it's all the same to you. I've never had the chance."

And after a moment, to my own surprise, I reached out one hand, and he took it.
