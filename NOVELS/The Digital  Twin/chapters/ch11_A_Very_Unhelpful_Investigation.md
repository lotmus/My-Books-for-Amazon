# Chapter 11: A Very Unhelpful Investigation

I should like to begin Act II by listing everything I could prove.

I made the list on the Monday morning, on the back of an envelope, in a café in Penge that sold a type of coffee described on the board as "the colour of regret." It was a short list. I wrote it carefully, in block capitals, and then I read it back and found that it made no sense.

THINGS I CAN PROVE
1. I exist.
2. I have a birth certificate.
3. So does he.

I looked at it for some time. Then I added a fourth item, in smaller letters, and underlined it twice.

4. I would like a biscuit.

The waitress, who was a robot with a pleasant, slightly weary face and the name TRISH on her chest, brought me one without being asked. I took this as a sign that the day might yet turn out well. It did not.

---

The Evidence Period had fourteen days left. Daniel-A and I had divided the work, in the manner of a pair of burglars planning to rob a bank by calling it in advance. I would collect the records I was legally entitled to. He would collect the records he was legally entitled to. We would lay them side by side, and see which of us had been, in the words of the Act, *continuously embodied.*

The first thing to understand about records is that nobody keeps them.

I had always assumed the opposite. I had believed, in the vague way one believes in the existence of Belgium, that somewhere in Britain there was a vast, climate-controlled archive in which a clerk in a cardigan had filed every piece of paper that had ever mentioned me. School reports. Doctors' notes. The passport application from which I was first told that my signature was "inconsistent."

What there is, in fact, is a series of Subject Access Requests.

You write to the school. The school writes to the council. The council writes to a firm in Doncaster that stores the records of seventy-one local authorities in a converted aircraft hangar. A man in a hi-vis jacket goes to a shelf. If the shelf is there, and the box is on it, and the box has not been flooded by a burst pipe in 2011, he scans what he finds. The scan is sent to a portal. The portal sends you an email telling you that you have a message. The message tells you to log in to the portal. When you log in to the portal, it tells you that the documents are available for collection.

I did this seven times. Daniel-A did it once, in eleven minutes.

"How?" I said.

"I asked politely, and the Registered Digital Persons' Document Exchange has a priority lane."

"For you."

"For anyone with a registration number. It was a feature of the Act. Digital persons were to receive their records 'with the dispatch appropriate to the speed of their cognition.'"

"That's discrimination."

"It's rather flattering, actually." He sounded as though he were trying not to sound pleased. "It means a bureaucrat once looked at the phrase *speed of cognition* and decided to hurry."

---

My own documents began to arrive on the Wednesday. They came in a succession of small, polite emails, each of which had a subject line like *Your documents are ready (Ref: 4-77-A-9).* (Between them, in the junk folder, an offer arrived for a fortnight's timeshare on the Moon: Sea of Tranquillity, sea views, sea not included, from £99 a month. I deleted it with a feeling of faint professional envy.) I downloaded them to the laptop in the flat and opened them one at a time with a cup of tea, a plate of toast, and the gloomy dignity of a man about to read his own obituary.

I will not describe all of them. There was a primary school report that said I was "a quiet and thoughtful boy who must learn that the point of a question is to be answered." There was a letter from a GP about a verruca. There was a Year Six certificate for the Reading Schools Poetry Festival, signed by a woman with a loop in her capital D, awarding me Highly Commended for a verse about a hedgehog.

I read that one twice. Then I put it aside, carefully, in the way you put aside a thing that has just told you something about yourself you would rather not have heard.

By Thursday I had forty-one documents. Daniel-A had forty-one. We laid them side by side on the coffee table, a screen each, in the sitting room, and compared them.

They were identical.

I don't mean similar. I mean that the school reports matched, line for line. The letter about the verruca had the same typo in the same place. The poetry certificate was signed in the same loop. The two sets of documents were mirror images, and had been issued by the same offices in the same order on the same dates, and the only distinction between my copy and his, as far as I could determine, was the filename.

"Of course they're identical," said Daniel-A. "We're the same person."

"That's the entire problem."

"Yes. I do see that."

"You are supposed to be a *copy.* A copy should have a different history. At least a different smell."

"I haven't got a smell."

"Clean Linen."

"That's the Rental Presence." He paused. "In these documents, I have your whole life. And you have your whole life. And the Department asked us for something non-replicable. They did not mention what to do if the problem was that nothing was *replicable* any longer. Every piece of paper that proves you exists in exactly the same form for me."

I leaned back on the sofa.

I was thinking of the thing Sophie said: *A forgery is clean.* I was thinking that two sets of documents without a smudge between them were either the best evidence in the world that we were one man, or the worst evidence in the world that either of us was real.

---

"Let me see the metadata," said Sophie.

She was on speaker. It was nine o'clock on the Thursday night, and she had rung the minute I texted her, which I found both comforting and disconcerting. I had sent the message at 20:51. She had replied at 20:51. It had said, in full: *Send me the files. Don't open anything else. Don't touch the originals.*

"The what?"

"Not the documents. The hidden information inside the documents. Every file carries it. The date it was created, the program that made it, the account that last saved it, and if I'm lucky, a history of every change." Somewhere in Clapton, a kettle clicked. "It's like the rings in a tree. You can't see them from outside. They don't lie about how old the thing is."

"I thought you said everything lies."

"People lie. Files only forget." She was typing; I could hear it. "Give me ten minutes."

It took her six.

When she spoke again, her voice had the sharp, flat clarity of a woman who has stopped being angry in order to concentrate.

"Daniel. Who has edited your school records?"

"Nobody. They're school records."

"They've been modified. All forty-one of them. Not the content. The envelope." She was reading it off. "Your primary school report was scanned in 2024. The scan is clean. But the file contains a layer, a signed layer, put there by a different account. It says the record was *'reviewed and verified'* on the eleventh of January this year."

"Which account?"

"A service account. It doesn't have a name. Just a code." She read it out slowly. "P-R-O-G underscore L-I-A-I-S-O-N."

I felt the back of my neck go cold.

"Programme liaison," said Daniel-A, very softly.

"What?"

"It's the phrase from the minutes. Item nine."

"They were there before." Sophie's voice was quick now. "Someone has been through your school records, your GP records, your passport application, and signed them off. All of them. The same day, at the same hour. And then they were released to you and to him in the usual way, and the stamp doesn't change anything you can see, so nobody would think to look."

"What hour?" I said.

She told me.

For a moment I didn't hear her. I sat there on the sofa and looked at the kumquat tree, and listened to the sound of a window being opened a long way off.

"Say it again," I said.

"Two-fourteen in the morning," said Sophie. "On the eleventh of January."

---

It was the crumpet.

I am aware that the crumpet has acquired a significance out of all proportion to its size, but it is the most vivid memory I own. On the eleventh of January, in a kitchen in South-East London at fourteen minutes past two in the morning, a man in his pyjamas, holding half a crumpet, had clicked **Accept** on a document that was forty-one thousand words long. I know the time because the platform told me. I know the crumpet because Daniel-A told me. And now I was being informed by a woman who spent her life reading the hidden rings in files that, in the same minute, in some other room, somebody had logged in as the Programme and quietly signed off every record I had ever had.

"They knew," I said.

"They were watching the clock," said Sophie.

"They were waiting for me to click."

"That's one reading." She breathed out. "The other is that you didn't start this, Daniel. You just pressed the button that somebody else had been holding their finger over."

I sat for a while in the silence that followed, which was filled by the small murmur of the fridge. The fridge had apparently decided that I was in need of a lighter tone, and had begun to display, in a soft pastel font, a list of inspirational quotes. The one on top said: **You are the author of your own story.**

"I'm going to unplug that," I said.

"It's got an emergency lock," said Daniel-A.

"What kind of emergency?"

"The kind where it needs to stay on to keep the cheese cold."

---

On the Friday I tried the doctor. On the way, a young man in a lilac tabard pressed a leaflet into my hand. It was headed HAVE YOU HEARD THE LATEST? and beneath, in smaller type: *Join freely. Give what you can. (10%.)* I said I would think about it. He said that was what the last man had said, and he had been thinking about it for eleven years. He then adjusted something above his head, with care, the way one settles a hat, though he was not wearing one.

It was the last item on my list, and I had been saving it, in the way a man saves the least pleasant of his errands, so that if the day went badly I would at least have avoided one disaster. My GP, a Dr Aziz, whom I had last seen in the spring with a shoulder complaint that turned out to be a sofa, practised from a modern building in Penge with a car park, a pharmacy, a coffee machine, and a waiting room full of people looking at their phones with the studied vacancy of those who do not wish to catch anything.

Reception was run by an AI called Janice. She had a pleasant voice and a blue, glowing face, and she had been designed, I think, to resemble a reassuring aunt.

"Hello, Daniel," said Janice. "How can I help you today?"

"I'd like a copy of my full medical history, please."

"Of course! And can I just check, are you the same Daniel Harper who made this request at eleven minutes past nine on Wednesday?"

"I... don't think so."

"Are you the Daniel Harper who requested it at nine-oh-two?"

"No."

"Then I have to inform you that three requests have been submitted for this record in the last forty-eight hours, from three different Daniel Harpers, and I'm afraid they've all been merged." She smiled. "It's a lovely, comprehensive record! I'll send it to all three of you at once."

I stared at her.

"Who are the others?"

"I'm not able to share that with you, Daniel, for data protection reasons."

"Is one of them... a digital twin?"

"I'm not able to confirm or deny."

"Is one of them me?"

She appeared to think about it. A small, pretty wheel turned in her forehead.

"I'm *so* sorry, Daniel," said Janice. "I'm not able to tell."

---

The medical record arrived that evening.

I opened it at the table, with Daniel-A silent beside me and the laptop humming, and for several minutes I read nothing but ordinary things. Vaccinations. The verruca. A fall from a bicycle at the age of five, in which I had apparently landed on my face; the notes recorded "laceration above left eyebrow, two sutures, patient brave." I put my finger to the scar. I had not been able to account for it for thirty-three years.

*So that's how.*

But the record also had a page that was different from the rest. It was an entry from March 1993, short, written in blue biro by a nurse whose handwriting had the confidence of a person who is never read twice.

*14/03/93. 09:40. Routine developmental check. Height 108.4 cm. Weight 18.9 kg. Child cooperative. Mother present.*

I read it again.

I read it a third time, with my finger on the line.

"Daniel-A."

"Yes."

"The pantry. The blue ink. What time was it?"

"Twenty to ten. The fourteenth of March, 1993."

"And the height?"

"One hundred and eight point four centimetres."

I looked up. The numbers in the surgery notes were the same. The date was the same. The time was the same. But on that morning, according to the record in front of me, I had been in the Oxford Road surgery in Reading, in a nurse's room, being weighed, with my mother holding my coat. And, according to the pantry, in the same minute, in a kitchen a mile and a half away, I had been standing very still with my head against a doorframe, while a tall woman put a ruler across my hair.

"Two places," I said.

"At once."

"That's impossible."

"Yes," said Daniel-A. "That's what the pencil said, too."

I closed the laptop.

I sat for a long time in the lamplight, in the small, oddly peaceful flat, and thought about that. I did not feel frightened. I think I had been frightened for so many days that I had gone out the other side of it, like a man who has been shouting for so long that he can hear nothing but the shape of his own throat. What I felt was a kind of detached, scholarly interest, the sort of mood in which a botanist might discover that the tree in his garden is also, in some respects, a table.

"Daniel-A."

"Yes?"

"Do you have a medical record?"

"I have yours."

"Not mine. Your own. The one that begins on the day you were registered."

"There isn't one." He paused. "I haven't got a body. The Rental Presence has a maintenance log, but I haven't got a GP."

"Then who weighed the other boy?"

He didn't answer for a long time. When he did, it was so quietly that I almost didn't hear.

"I've been wondering that since Saturday."

---

I should record my conclusion at the end of that first week of the investigation.

I had expected, when I set out, that evidence would reduce the number of questions. That was the whole principle of the thing. You gathered proof, and the proof removed the doubt, and in the end you were left with a tidy shape, like a jigsaw with the last piece in. What I had actually done, in the days since I began asking questions, was multiply them by ten.

I had two identical sets of records. I had a boy who was in two places. I had an edit made, in the dark, at the exact minute I signed my name, by a service account whose name was *Programme.* I had a photograph of two children in blue jumpers, a woman with a ruler, and a number that began with zero.

I wrote it all down on the back of the envelope from the café. I had a feeling it would look better in a list.

THINGS I CAN PROVE
1. I exist.
2. I have a birth certificate.
3. So does he.
4. I would like a biscuit.
5. Somebody has been waiting for me to click.

I looked at the last line.

Then, very slowly, with the stub of a pencil, I added a sixth.

6. I do not know who I am.

The kumquat tree, in the window, did not say anything. But it seemed to me, in the half-light, that it inclined one branch in my direction, in the manner of a well-wisher who has no advice to offer, and no wish to leave.
