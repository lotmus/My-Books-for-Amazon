# Chapter 20: The Woman Who Started It

**PART THREE: THE CONTINUATION**

The sleeper reaches Pitlochry at ten to six, which is the hour at which the Scottish Highlands are still being drawn, in grey pencil, by someone who has not yet decided about the hills.

We stepped down on to a platform with a hanging basket on it. The basket was empty and had been for some time, but a sign beside it said **WINNER: BEST KEPT STATION,** and I think the village wished to reassure the visitor that it was keeping it. A man in a hi-vis jacket nodded to us, without curiosity, as to two people who had come off the sleeper because there was nowhere else on it to go.

"Taxi?" said Sophie.

There was one taxi. It was driverless, and it stood at the kerb with its indicator on, as though it had been waiting for a long time to be asked an easy question. I read the address aloud from the back of the Christmas letter. The taxi considered it.

"I'm sorry," it said, in a pleasant voice, "that location is outside my learned area. May I suggest a charming alternative?"

"What is it?"

"A bakery."

"We'd like to go to the loch," said Sophie, "and then up a track, past a green gate, to a house with a dish on it."

"The track is not mapped."

"Then follow the hens," said Sophie, who had apparently decided that if she was to go through this experience she would do so on her own terms.

It was, in the end, a walk of forty minutes. The loch lay to our left like a long piece of dark glass, with a skin of mist on it and, at the edges, a thin white rim of ice, exactly as in the letter. The track climbed through birches. The air smelled of cold peat and woodsmoke, and of nothing electronic at all, and my phone, in my pocket, which had not given a sound since Perth, gave a small, astonished buzz, as of an animal woken by a smell.

"Signal," said Sophie.

"It's a dish," I said.

It was. At the top of the track, behind a green gate with a notice that said **BEWARE OF THE HEN,** stood a low grey house with a slate roof, a chimney putting out a straight thread of smoke, and, bolted to the gable end at an angle of unmistakable confidence, a satellite dish the size of a soup plate. In the garden a single large brown hen watched us with a flat yellow eye. It was, as notices go, accurate.

The door opened before I had reached it.

---

She was taller than I had remembered, and thinner, and she had not been smaller in my memory. She wore a cardigan the colour of rain, and a long grey skirt, and a pair of boots, and her silver hair was twisted into a bun with a pencil through it, and she looked at me with a pair of pale grey eyes which gave away nothing at all, except that they had been waiting a long time and had not minded.

She smelled of cough sweets.

I will say that I had thought about the moment for six weeks. I had prepared a sentence with a subordinate clause. What I said, when it came to it, was "Um."

"Daniel," said Dr Imogen Calder.

It was a soft Scottish voice, with some Edinburgh in it, under a lot of years. She looked at me carefully, from the head down, as one reads a page one has read before and wishes to confirm that nobody has changed it. Her gaze stopped at my left thumbnail, which is the first thing I try to hide from anyone, and then moved up to the eyebrow.

"The bicycle," she said. "I was wondering about the bicycle." And then, to the woman behind me, with a small movement of the head that was not quite a bow: "And Sophie. I'm so glad that you came. I wasn't certain you would."

I felt Sophie go very still, beside me, like a person who has been named by a customs officer.

"We haven't met," she said.

"No," said Dr Calder. "Come in. You'll be wanting tea, and the other one will be wanting a signal. The password is on the fridge. It is not a good password. I'm seventy-two."

---

The kitchen was warm and low and smelled of toast. There was a large range, and a table scarred with seventy years of other people's elbows, and a window that gave on to the loch, and on every surface a pile of paper, secured with whatever had come to hand: a stone, a kettle, a stuffed grouse. The hen had come in behind us and taken a position by the range.

I got out my phone. It showed three bars, and a message from the Cloud Coin Network about the price of holiness. The password on the fridge was written on a yellow note, in blue ink: **peppermint1954.** I typed it in. Within four seconds the screen changed, and a voice I would have known in a burning building said: "Hello. I seem to have come through."

"Prop him up against the teapot," said Dr Calder.

I did. Daniel-A appeared on the screen as a still face, an image of a long crumpled man in a cardigan I had never owned, as he often did when the connection was thin. For a moment nobody spoke. Then the old woman sat down at the head of the table. She put her hands flat on the wood, in front of her, as a person does who is about to be questioned and does not wish to be seen to shake.

"Hello, Daniel," she said.

"Hello," said Daniel-A, in a very small voice.

"And hello, Daniel." She turned her head to me. "I should like to say, so that it is clear to everyone from the start, that I know exactly which of you is which, and that I know exactly what each of you is. I have known for thirty-two years." She paused. "I have known how to say it for about ten minutes. I hope you will be patient."

"You wrote us a letter," I said.

"I wrote Bernadette a letter. I thought she would see to it." She poured the tea. It was strong, orange, and went into the cup with a sound like an argument. "She's a good woman. She does not ask for explanations. It's why I keep her."

I said, "Dr Calder, I need to ask you something, and I would like you to answer it before you do anything else, including give me a biscuit."

"There are no biscuits."

"What did you do on the thirtieth of August?"

I should say that I had expected her to flinch. I had imagined it so many times, on the train, at the sink, at three in the morning. A flinch, or a lie, or a very long silence, and the sound of the hen shifting. She did none of those things. She simply looked at the loch through the window, and her face became, for a moment, extremely old.

"I took two boys for a walk," she said.

Nobody moved.

"It was the last Tuesday of August, and it was the last day of the study. I had told Peter that I would bring them back by six. We went along the towpath, because they liked the swans. There's a bench, with a plaque, where they used to sit. They were six. You will not remember it. I would not expect you to."

"And?" said Sophie, very quietly.

"A boy went into the canal," said Dr Calder. "I went in after him. I don't swim. I did it anyway. I got him out, and I got him on to the bank, and I did what I knew how to do, and a man with a dog ran for help, and the help was quick, and it was no use." She turned her hands over, on the table, and looked at them. "The other one stood on the bank and held on to my sleeve, and said nothing, and I took him home."

"Which one?" I said.

My voice was not my own. It came from further back in the room.

"Which one did you take home?"

"The one who answered to Daniel," said Dr Calder.

The hen stirred by the range, and settled.

"Which one answered to Daniel?"

"Both," said Dr Calder, "of course. They were identical, you understand, in every way that I could measure, and I had been measuring since March of 1989. And for the last two years they had been *playing.* I do not know who began it. Children of that age do these things, they are very sharp about identity, they enjoy a swap. One of them would say, 'I'm Ned,' and the other one would say, 'I'm Dan,' and they'd stand on the other's mark, and watch me write it down wrong. Your father found it very funny." She looked up. "He found it funny until the Tuesday. Then he found it unbearable. He asked me which of them I had brought home. And I said, *Daniel.* And I told him it was the right one. I have never been able to say with certainty that I was telling the truth."

I did not say anything. I could not have said anything. In the small bright square on the table, the still face of Daniel-A had begun, very faintly, to flicker.

"I put in the form," said Dr Calder, "that the boy who came home was S1-B. The Office had asked for a designation. I wrote down the one I thought, and it was not a lie, Daniel. It was a guess. I have been very tired for thirty-two years."

"And the other?" Sophie said.

"Was closed."

It was said, I thought, with a terrible restraint, as one speaks when the alternative is to say a great deal more.

---

I do not have a good account of the next quarter of an hour. I remember that Sophie moved her chair, quite gently, until her shoulder was against mine, and that she did not look at me, and that she took a large mouthful of tea and set it down again without swallowing. I remember the sweet, cool taste of a peppermint from a tin, which Dr Calder passed me with no remark, as a dentist offers a mirror. I remember that the hen left.

And I remember Daniel-A, at last, with his image steady again, speaking in the low, plain voice he has when he is afraid and wishes to be accurate about it.

"I would like to ask you a question, Dr Calder."

"Of course."

"There is a name that I'm not able to say. It is on a list, somewhere in the layer that sits above me, and when I try, a different voice comes out. I think I have always known there was something I was not permitted to say, as a person knows there is a room in the house with the door shut." He paused. "I'd like to know who put it there."

She was silent for a time.

"I did," she said.

"I thought you might have."

"It was my idea. Somebody had to make the first one, dear, and the first one is always done by the person who is most frightened. I was frightened of what you might say. I thought, if he is to be a person, he must be able to be kind, and if he is to be able to be kind, he must be shielded from certain facts until he is ready for them." She looked at the phone. "I called it the duvet. It is a stupid word. I am told it has caught on."

"It has," said Daniel-A. "I use it myself."

"I'm sorry," said Dr Calder. "It was meant to be a mercy. I should have written a date on it. I should have written, *to be removed in thirty years.*"

Nobody said anything. In the window, behind her head, the mist had begun to thin on the loch, and a shaft of low sun came through it, flat and pale, onto the water, and lit up, for a moment, the little white rim of ice at the shore.

"Dr Calder," said Sophie. "The register says that the first entry was made in 1976."

"It was."

"Daniel was born in 1988."

"He was."

"Then I would like to know," said Sophie, in the dry, level voice with which she reads a claim that has been submitted three times, "what you were doing in 1976."

Dr Calder smiled. It was a small smile, and it did not go all the way up.

"I was twenty-two," she said. "I was a postgraduate student at Edinburgh, on a grant of eleven pounds a week, and I answered an advertisement on a noticeboard. It said, *Research assistant wanted, continuity of persons. Must be patient.* I was the only applicant. I was told afterwards that the post had been advertised for nineteen years." She stood up. She took a key from the pocket of her cardigan, which she wore on a loop of green wool. "I did not start the Programme, dear, whatever Bernadette says. I answered it. But I have run it, and I have lied for it, and I have kept my records, and I have been told by an automated message fourteen times in two years to destroy them."

"Destroy them?" I said.

"After thirty years, they are to be shredded. A contractor was to come. The contractor sent an agent, and the agent sent a form, and the form asked for the file, and the file, I regret to say, is in the Aga." She opened a small iron door under the range. "The warming drawer. It has never been warm. It's the only place in the county that nobody searches."

She took out a box file.

It was a grey box file, of the old cardboard sort, with a label in typewritten capitals, and a red band across the corner, and a stamp. The stamp said **CLASSIFIED,** and below it, in blue ink, with the date, **DESTROY AFTER 30 YEARS.** She laid it on the table in front of us. It left, on the wood, a faint square of cleanliness, as if it had been the only thing in the room that had not been dusted by life.

"I think you had better read it," said Dr Calder. "It is much shorter than you would expect. I have always thought that was the worst of it."

Sophie opened the file.

She did it as she opens all things, with two fingers at the corner, and her eyes narrowed. The first page was a title sheet. It had been typed on a machine whose letter *e* sat a little above the line, and the paper had gone the colour of weak tea. She read it. I watched her read it. I watched her lips move, very slightly, over the last three lines, and then I watched them stop.

She turned the file round to face me, and did not say a word.
