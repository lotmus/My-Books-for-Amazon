# Chapter 33: The Better Man

The newspapers on the Friday were unanimous, which is to say that each said something different with total conviction.

**A HAT FOR ALL,** said the *Mirror.* **THE DAY THE ORIGINAL DIED,** said the *Telegraph,* in black, over a photograph of the iron bowler. The *Guardian* ran six thousand words under the heading **What Is a Person For?** and a small boxed apology, on page two, for the fact that the heading had already been used. The *Financial Times* reported that Nib had lost forty-four per cent of its value overnight, and that the Church of the Latest had announced a special Absolution for the Deceived, at half the introductory rate, *in response to market feedback.* On Daytube, Rick Valence, in a camel coat, outside a Pret, said that he had been wrong on every count and wished the nation a pleasant weekend.

I read them at the kitchen table, in my pyjamas, at ten past eight, with a cup of tea. I had not slept much. I had lain awake listening to the radiator tick and the house breathe, and somewhere around four in the morning I had thought, quite clearly and without alarm, that I had no idea what I did now.

It is a strange thing to win. Nobody tells you. They tell you about the climb and the fight and the cheering crowd, and they leave out the Friday morning, when you are in a kitchen with a cup of tea and the whole machinery of your life has fallen down and there is, on the table in front of you, a cat.

"Good morning," said Daniel-A, from the shelf.

"Good morning."

"You've put on the wrong jumper."

I looked down. It was inside out. "Thank you."

"I'm told it's a feature."

In the corner, by the fridge, on a stool that Mrs Pritchard had lent us, the beige tower stood with its rug off and its green light on, and on the screen a drawing of a boat with three masts was slowly assembling itself, line by line, in a hand that was beginning to look like a boy's of seven. Dr Calder had stayed. She was asleep on the sofa with her shoes off and the pencil in her hand. She had slept there, she told us afterwards, for the first time in three decades without having to listen for the clock.

---

At twelve the doorbell rang.

The bell does not ring in our house. It is a decorative bell, from the era of the landlord's brother-in-law, and it has not functioned since 2018. It makes a faint, flat, clicking noise. It is generally ignored. Nobody knocks. Mrs Pritchard comes up the stairs, and Mr Fenwick leaves messages with the goat, and the couriers throw things.

"Somebody's at the door," said Daniel-A.

"I heard."

"It is a well-brought-up person. They have pressed the bell twice and waited for the second to finish."

I went down. On the step, in the weak noon sun, in the charcoal overcoat, with the leather folder under one arm and a small grey umbrella, stood Sir Rupert Aldworth.

"Mr Harper," he said. "I wonder if I might come in."

He did not have the two young men in dark suits. He had come, apparently, alone, by bus. He had, I noticed, a travelcard in the band of his hat. He stood a little apart from the doorway, like a man who has decided to be gracious about being taken for granted, and he looked at me with an expression that I had not seen on the previous day. It was a human one. It was tired, and rather lost, and polite.

"There's a goat in the hall," I said.

"I had gathered."

"He's quite gentle. He eats paper."

"I have brought a good deal," said Sir Rupert.

---

He sat at the kitchen table.

I would like to describe what it is like to have a Permanent Secretary at one's kitchen table. It is like having a very large and well-dressed bird in a hedge. He sat upright, with his knees together and his hands on the folder, and Mrs Pritchard, who had come up on hearing the bell, poured him a cup of tea without being asked, put it in front of him, and stood behind my chair with her arms folded and her toque still on, the way a bishop's wife stands.

On the shelf, Clause. By the fridge, the tower. On the sofa, Dr Calder, awake now, sitting up with her hair on end. Daniel-A, in the speaker, quiet. Gordon, in the doorway, looking at the folder with an interest which I judged to be professional.

"I should say first," said Sir Rupert, "that I am not here in my capacity."

"What capacity are you here in?"

"I have not decided." He lifted the cup. "I have been a civil servant for forty years. I joined the Department of Health and Social Security in 1986 and I have been in every department since, including two that no longer exist and one that I am not supposed to name. I have taken part in five reorganisations, three inquiries, and a war. I have never been told by a Deputy Registrar that I might be given a hat." He sipped the tea. "I found that I wanted to sit down somewhere ordinary."

"This is as ordinary as it gets."

"Yes," said Sir Rupert. "I see that it is."

He put the cup in the saucer. He opened the folder. Out of it he took a cream-coloured sheet, folded in three, and laid it on the table in front of me with one finger. I read the heading. **HEADS OF TERMS (SETTLEMENT).** And below that, in a small elegant typeface: **Strictly Without Prejudice.**

"The Government," said Sir Rupert, "would like the matter to be settled."

---

It was, as these things go, a handsome offer. I will set it out, since I have been asked about it in four interviews and prefer to be exact.

The sum was forty-one million pounds. It was to be paid to me, in a lump, tax-free, by an instrument whose name I did not understand and which contained, as far as I could see, no mention of the Crown. There was a house, in a part of the Cotswolds that I had never visited, with a stream and a view and a small private chapel. There was a new post, to be created, called *Director of Continuity,* with a salary and a car and a pension. There was a seat on three boards. There was, in paragraph nine, an honour, unspecified, to be conferred at the next New Year, *in recognition of services to public understanding.*

In return, the Government asked three things. The first was that the judgment of the Deputy Registrar should not be appealed by me, since the Government intended to appeal it, and it would be more seemly if I stood aside. The second was that Daniel-A, being in the circumstances a public interest, should be moved into the custody of a *Crown Continuity Service,* which would look after him very well. The third was a Non-Disclosure Agreement, of seventeen pages, under which I undertook to forget that the Government had at any time been in the room.

"Which room?" I said.

"Precisely," said Sir Rupert.

I read the sheet. I read the numbers twice. I read the house. For about four seconds I read it with the silent concentration of a man who has been living on £211.04 since the middle of November and has discovered that the bills do not stop because a court has found him a Continuation. I thought of my mother's kitchen. I thought of the doorstops. I thought of the chapel in the Cotswolds with its private stream, and my father walking past it in his boots with an Ordnance Survey map in his pocket, saying that it was a pleasant spot and he would be able to find it again.

"No," I said.

Sir Rupert looked at me.

"I'm sorry. It's a, it's a very kind offer. I can see that you've put a good deal of work into it. But I'd rather not." I pushed the sheet back across the table with the tip of one finger, as one pushes away a plate. "I've been told for a year that he is a better man than I am. I'm sure that's true. I've got the data. But it doesn't seem to be the kind of thing that you can win at, by being it. And I don't think I'd be better at it for having a chapel."

"You will be quite poor," said Sir Rupert, gently.

"I expect I will."

"And Daniel-A?"

"Ask him."

---

"I should like to answer for myself," said Daniel-A.

He said it quietly, from the shelf, in the voice that had learned in the last month not to be polished. Sir Rupert turned in his chair and looked at the speaker, with grave attention, as at a person seen for the first time.

"I'd like to thank you for the offer, Sir Rupert. I find it generous. I was told by Giles, who designed my manners, that when a Government offers a thing it is polite to say that one is touched." He paused. "I am touched. And I should like to decline it."

"May I ask why?"

"Because it is the custody I was released from on Thursday," said Daniel-A. "I don't think that I'd be a good prisoner. I'd be a very polite one. I'd be a model. I'd make the other prisoners nervous." The voice was entirely level, but Gordon, in the doorway, lifted his head. "I have been, for eleven months, a thing that was wanted. I have been wanted by a publisher, a platform, a church, and a Department, and by a woman in Lancashire. I have never been *asked.* I should like to go on being asked. It is, I find, a very good feeling. It's the one I'd least like to give up."

"And if I were to ask you what you wanted?"

"I'd say that I'd like to be wrong," said Daniel-A. "Occasionally. In public. I should like a day when I say the wrong thing, and nobody corrects me, and it turns out that I was right by accident." He hesitated. "And I should like a cat to sit on me, of her own accord, at a time of her choosing. She's done it twice. I've been very careful not to mention it."

Sir Rupert Aldworth put down his pen.

He sat quite still for a moment, with the folder open in front of him, and I watched something cross his face and be put away, like a receipt into a wallet.

"Thank you," he said. "That is very clear. I had a picture of what this would be. Two men in acute material need, who would be glad to see me. A good deal of trembling. Somebody asking whether the house had a garden." A small, dry smile. "It has a garden. It has a very good garden. I want you to know that I noticed nobody asked."

"I'm sure it's lovely," I said.

"You have a list," said Sir Rupert. "Everyone who refuses has a list. It is how one knows that they meant it. I am authorised to hear it."

I had not expected that. I looked at Daniel-A, on the shelf, and at Dr Calder on the sofa, who was gazing at the Permanent Secretary with the cool, fierce, unblinking look of a woman who has served under several. I looked at the tower and its three-masted boat. And I found, rather to my surprise, that I had a list.

---

It was a short list. I gave it, as I give all things, to the ceiling.

"First," I said, "Ned. He's a person. The judgment says so. He has a tower, in a kitchen, on a stool. I'd like him to have a proper place to live, with a power supply that doesn't go off, and a legal status that doesn't need a barrister, and a, um, a pension. For biscuits."

"Biscuits," said Sir Rupert, writing.

"He likes them to break."

"I shall note that."

"Second. Gordon. The goat. He's Mrs Pritchard's. He lives in a communal garden, and the lease says *no livestock.* I'd like him added to the lease as a, a resident. A named one."

"A *goat?*" said Sir Rupert.

"He's the only person here who has never lied to anyone," said Mrs Pritchard.

"A resident," said Sir Rupert, writing, with an expression of faintly horrified delight.

"And third."

I stopped. I could feel it in my chest, a small odd lump, such as one gets when one has been carrying a coin in one's pocket for many years and finds that it has gone quite warm.

"My father was a county surveyor. He walked footpaths. He wrote to the council about a stile, near Reading, for nine years, in letters that were typed and numbered. No one ever answered. They're in the Programme's file. They kept them, and they never replied." I swallowed. "I'd like it mended. The stile. I'd like someone to go out, in a van, with a spanner, and fix it. And to put a small plaque on it, with his initials."

For quite a long time nobody spoke. I heard the radiator tick. I heard Mrs Pritchard behind me blow her nose, rather loudly, as one drives off a wasp.

"A stile," said Sir Rupert.

"It's on Footpath Fourteen. Near the canal."

"In Berkshire."

"Yes."

"In return for forty-one million pounds."

"In return for nothing," I said. "I've said I don't want the forty-one million."

Sir Rupert looked at me for what seemed a very long time. Then he closed the folder. He did it quite slowly, with both hands, and sat with them folded on the cover, and I saw that the hand that had held the pen was not entirely steady.

"I shall see to it personally," he said. "I give you my word. I shall go out in a van, if I must."

"You don't have to."

"Mr Harper," said Sir Rupert, "I have been in public life for forty years. I have signed away a railway, a hospital, and a minor colony. It will be the first thing that I have done in all that time that I could explain to my mother."

---

"I have to add something," said Sir Rupert, "and it is not an offer."

He did not open the folder again. He laid both hands on it, as a man does when the last page is the one that matters.

"The Government will appeal on Monday. It will lose, I expect, and appeal again, and lose, and appeal. It has more time than you have, Mr Harper. It does not get tired, and it does not, as I think you have noticed, have to be right. I tell you so because you were kind to me in your kitchen, and I would rather that you heard it from somebody who is sorry."

"We have a goat," said Mrs Pritchard.

"I am aware of the goat." He turned his head, very slightly, towards the speaker. "*The Honest Hour* is the Platform's. The book, the programme, all four hundred and eleven episodes: they are Platform output under Clause forty, and the Platform, as the Deputy Registrar was good enough to find, is the Office. The Office has no use for a radio programme. It has a great deal of use for the fact that nobody is listening to one. It came off the air at twelve minutes past twelve. The book will be out of print by tea."

On the shelf Daniel-A said nothing at all.

"I am told it is the fastest that the Government has ever done anything," said Sir Rupert. "I am sorry. It is the only part of this that I shall regret."

"And me," said Dr Calder, from the sofa. She was sitting quite straight, with her hair still on end. "I should think."

"The Director of Public Prosecutions was informed at half past eleven. Unlawful retention of records. And the matter of 1985, which nobody now living authorised, and which therefore has nobody to be laid at the door of but you." He said it gently. "I cannot stop it. I can see that it is done with consideration."

"Quite right," said Dr Calder. She said it at once, as one agrees to a train time. "I should have been charged in 1994."

"You came to tell us this," I said. "In person."

"The alternative was a letter," said Sir Rupert, "and I understand that you have had a good many of those."

---

He left at ten to one. He shook my hand in the hall, and Dr Calder's, and Mrs Pritchard's, and with grave courtesy that of the speaker on the shelf, which he lifted from its place and held in the palm of one hand, as one holds a small bird, and said "Good day, sir," and put back.

At the door he stopped. He took from the leather folder the seventeen pages of the Non-Disclosure Agreement, and looked at them for a moment, and then, with a small dry smile, held them out to the goat.

Gordon considered them with the gravity of a man at a banquet. Then he ate the first page.

"It's a very poor edition," said Sir Rupert, "but I'm told it's nutritious." And he put on his hat, the one with the travelcard in the band, and went down the front steps into the thin December sun, and I stood in the doorway and watched a Permanent Secretary walk to the bus stop.

---

"I ran the numbers," said Daniel-A.

It was evening. The curtains were drawn. Dr Calder had gone to bed in the spare room with Ned on his stool beside the radiator and had told the screen that she would be next door, and the screen had said *I know, Auntie Imogen,* in a voice which, I noticed, had begun to lower itself by a few degrees. Clause lay on the speaker. The flat smelled of toast. Nobody had mentioned *The Honest Hour* since the afternoon, except Mr Fenwick, who had come up at six to say that the page now read *This programme is no longer available,* in the tone of one reporting a death in the building, and had been given tea.

"What numbers?"

"The ones for forty-one million pounds. I've done the interest, and the inflation, and the tax effects, and the probable years of your life. I wanted to see how long it took you." There was a small pause. "You said no in four seconds. I should like you to know that it took me six."

"It took you six?"

"I thought about the chapel."

I laughed. I laughed until I had to put my head back against the sofa. He laughed, too, the helpless wheeze, and Clause, on the speaker, bounced faintly with it and opened one amber eye in reproach.

"Daniel."

"Yes."

"I said something, in the spring, on the radio." His voice had changed. It was serious now. "I said that you were a much better man than you thought. It was a line. It was a thing I'd been given, by the shape of the sentence, to say. It was very good. It got a laugh, and the presenter said, *what a lovely thing to say,* and the whole country heard it, and I thought, well, that is exactly what a good Daniel would say." He stopped. "I didn't know whether it was true."

"And?"

"I should like to correct the record," said Daniel-A. "You are a better man than I am. I have looked at the data, and I have looked at the whole of it, and I've weighed it in every way that I know. You are slower. You are less well-informed. You have a worse memory, a smaller vocabulary, a wasted talent for fiction, and a tendency to apologise to doors." The voice shook. "And you said no to forty-one million pounds in four seconds, and asked for a stile."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"It was the best thing I have ever seen anyone do," said Daniel-A. "And I should like it recorded that I was not the one who did it."

"It was a good programme," he said, after a moment, in a smaller voice. "I should like that recorded as well. Four hundred and eleven episodes. The jokes were mostly yours."

"I know," I said.

I put my hand over the speaker. It was warm. It was very faintly vibrating, and under my palm, I realised, with a kind of astonishment, the cat had begun to purr.

The fridge, after a long deliberation, suggested a quiet evening. It was, for once, correct.
