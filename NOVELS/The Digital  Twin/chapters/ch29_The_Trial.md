# Chapter 29: The Trial

By Tuesday morning the Department of Digital Persons had been besieged by an army, and the army had brought sandwiches.

There were perhaps four thousand of them, in the forecourt and spilling into the road. A line of police stood across the glass doors, arms folded, unconvinced that nothing was going to happen. At one end stood a block of the Church of the Latest, in lilac, with banners that said **ORIGINAL IS NOT A HAT,** and a young man with a loudhailer who kept calling on the Source to *forgive the faithless.* At the other end, in grey, in perfect silence, were three hundred members of the House of the Listener, who had come from Penge by coach and stood with their hands in their pockets and their eyes on the ground, holding between them a single bedsheet on which someone had written, in felt-tip: **WE DON'T KNOW EITHER.**

And between them, in no particular order, were the hats.

Roland's doorstops had gone out at six on the Friday. By Tuesday there were people in the crowd wearing bowlers, boaters, trilbies, balaclavas, and one woman a lampshade. A man sold them from a barrow, at £9.99 each, from a stack that bore a printed label on the side: **THE DANIEL (HEAVY. HONEST. OPEN-MINDED.)** The CCN van was parked on the kerb with its dish aimed at the heavens, and Rick Valence stood before it in a camel coat, holding a microphone like a candle, saying that the nation had never been so divided or so interested in headwear.

I came up the road at a quarter to ten with a cat in a basket, a speaker in a rucksack, and a hat in a carrier bag.

"There he is!" someone shouted. "It's the Original!"

"It's the Spare!" shouted somebody else.

"It's *both!*" cried a woman in a trilby, joyfully, as if she had solved a crossword; and a gap opened in the crowd like a zip, and I walked through it, with my head down, apologising to a great many people.

---

They had moved us to the Great Hall.

I had not known there was one. It was on the ground floor, at the back of the glass building, behind a door marked **MAINTENANCE (DO NOT ENTER),** and it was a high, cold, handsome room, panelled in dark wood, with a gallery all the way round. It had been built in 1974, according to a plaque, to hold the Annual General Meeting of a body called the Registry of Persons, which had been abolished in 1989, and had not been used since except for fire drills. The room smelled of old varnish and a mild damp excitement. Every seat in the gallery was full.

Deputy Registrar Pym sat on a raised bench at the far end, in a black gown that was a little too long for him, behind a desk that held a jug of water, a blotter, and the brass bell. He looked like a very small tortoise in a very large cathedral. At his side, on a low chair, with his laptop and his biro, was Gareth, who had been promoted that morning to *Clerk of the Hearing,* and who had put on, in honour of the occasion, a tie.

I took my place at the table on the left. Pettifer was already there, in the charcoal suit, with her single sheet of paper in front of her and her hands folded on top of it. She nodded to me. She did not speak. On the table beside her, set out with the formality of a dead relative, stood a small bowler hat, in black painted iron, with a card in front of it that said **EXHIBIT H.**

On the right, at the other table, sat Mr Sebastian Pike, in his unrumpled navy, with an assistant, a junior, and a stack of binders in blue. He looked at me across the room with an expression of great kindness. It was the look of a man who intended to remove one's hat, and then one's head, and hoped one would understand.

"All rise," said Gareth, in a voice that cracked on the second word.

---

I should like to give the proceedings in full. They ran for seven hours, and I have the transcript, which is four hundred pages and has a foreword. But I will give only the shape.

Mr Pike spoke first. He did it standing, with one hand lightly on the table, in a voice like warm oil.

"Sir," he said. "My client wishes to begin by thanking Mr Harper."

There was a murmur in the gallery.

"I do not say it in jest. Mr Harper has, in the last fortnight, done something that the draughtsmen of the Digital Persons Act, who laboured for six years, did not think of. He has pointed out that a Source Individual is *presumed singleton,* and that he is not one. He has told us, with admirable candour, in a book that I have read with great pleasure, that there is no Source." He turned a page of his notes, unhurried. "My client agrees."

I felt Pettifer, beside me, go quite still.

"If there is no Source, sir, then there is no one to consent, or to refuse. And the Platform Terms, at Clause forty, which Mr Harper accepted on the eleventh of January with, I believe, a crumpet, are perfectly clear. In the absence of a Source Individual, custody of the Likeness reverts to the Platform. We become the custodian. We are then entitled to do, with the Likeness, whatever is in the Likeness's best interests." He smiled. "We are entitled to settle him."

I did not breathe. I could hear, in the rucksack at my feet, a small sound, like a man putting his hand over his own mouth.

"Mr Harper," said Pike pleasantly, "has won our case for us."

---

It was Pettifer who saved us, and she did it as she does everything, by standing up very slowly and saying nothing for eleven seconds.

"Sir," she said at last. "I'd like to thank my friend."

"Ms Pettifer," said Pym.

"He is perfectly right. The Act is built on a presumption, and the presumption is false. I do not dispute it. Nor does my client." She picked up the single sheet of paper and held it, as one holds a small flat knife. "But the Act is not the only way of seeing a man. I should like to put, before this tribunal, a different one. I shall do it in five words, and then I shall do it in a good many more."

"Please."

"There is no original," said Pettifer. "That is the first half. It is the half that my friend has so kindly adopted. But he has not told you the second half, which is this." She turned the sheet, and held it up, and there on the back, in her small neat pencil, were the words. "Original is a hat."

The gallery made a noise like a long breath. Somewhere above me, a man said "Oh!" and somebody else said "Shh."

"A hat, sir, is not a person. It does not say what is underneath. It is worn, and it passes, and it can be put on by another. The Act is concerned with the hat. It asks who wears it. It supposes that there is only one head." She paused. "There are three. There is a man who is sitting at that table. There is a mind in a rucksack at his feet, who has been in the world for eleven months and has the manners of a diplomat. And there is a boy of six, who has been in a cellar in Perthshire since the last day of August 1994, and who gave my client the idea."

"A boy of six!" said Pike, and permitted himself an expression of tender incredulity. "Sir, we are being asked to take the testimony of a *child.*"

"We are being asked," said Pettifer, "to take the testimony of a child who understood in 1994 what the Department took thirty years to forget."

---

I will pass over the middle of the day. It had in it a great deal of law, and a long and highly technical dispute about the word *custody.* It had in it Giles, who was called by Pettifer, and sworn, and who said in a voice like a man reading his own will that he had designed the sigh, and had been dismissed for pressing a button, and that he wished to plead guilty.

"To what?" said Pym.

"To being kind."

"That is not a recognised offence."

"Then I withdraw it, sir," said Giles, and burst into tears, and was permitted to sit down.

It had in it Mrs Pritchard, who was not a witness but came to hear, and who took a seat in the front row of the gallery and was understood to have bribed a usher with a banana. It had in it Dorothy, who attended with Mr Fenwick, and who said in the corridor at lunch to a startled journalist that she thought the Registrar was *absolutely right.* And at ten past two, in accordance with Schedule 6, paragraph 3, the Court received the corroborative evidence of a mammal.

I had brought Clause in her basket. She is not a cat who enjoys a journey. She emerged on the green baize of the witness table with her tail like a bottle brush and her amber eyes narrowed to two bored slits, and Adjudicator Pauline Dunmore, of the Mammalian Division, came forward in her hi-vis tabard, with her iron-grey bun and her clipboard, and said that she had adjudicated four hundred and thirty-one trials, including a pig and a loud donkey, and that this was her first cat that was both a witness and a *friend of the Court.*

We were to place Daniel-A's speaker at one end of the table, she said, and Mr Harper at the other, and the animal in the middle, and observe.

Clause considered the table. She considered the room. She considered, for what seemed to me a very long time, the stack of Mr Pike's blue binders. Then she walked to the end where the speaker stood, turned round three times, and lay down against it with a small, pleased sound, as a person lies down in a warm bath.

"Preference for the Registered Digital Person," said Dunmore, writing it down.

Clause got up.

She walked the full length of the table. She did it without haste, picking her way past the carafe, past the exhibits, past Exhibit H, and arrived at my end, and put one paw on my sleeve. Then she climbed on to my forearm, lay across it, and began to knead my wrist, slowly and deliberately, with the claws out, in a way that was, I am bound to say, extremely painful.

"Preference for the Source Individual," said Dunmore.

"He's not a Source Individual," said Mr Pike.

"Preference for the individual," Dunmore amended, with dignity.

Clause got off my arm. She stretched. She yawned, with an expression of the most insolent boredom, and walked to the exact middle of the table, and sat down on Gareth's folder, with her back to both of us and her tail curled round her feet. She began to wash.

Dunmore consulted her clipboard. She looked at the cat. She looked, for some time, at the gallery, in which there was by now not the smallest sound, and then she wrote, in a firm round hand, and read it aloud.

"Equivocal," she said. "But dignified."

From the back of the gallery, in a high clear voice that startled every head in the room, someone cried "*Unequivocal!*"

It was a parrot. It was a large grey parrot, in the pocket of a man's overcoat, and it had been banned from the premises since the Parrot Incident in March. Gareth said "Oh, *no,*" and two ushers went after it, in a manner not unlike the opening of a farce.

---

At four o'clock I was called.

I have given evidence only once in my life. It was in a small claims court in Lewisham, in 2019, about a boiler. I walked to the box with my legs not working and my hat in my hand. I put the hat on the stand in front of me, because I did not know what else to do with it, and a soft ripple went through the gallery like wind in barley, and Pym leaned forward and said, "You may keep it there."

"Mr Harper," said Pike. "I shall be brief."

"Thank you."

"You have written a book. It has sold, I am told, a great many copies." He picked up a blue binder. "It is a moving and subtle book. It is fluent. It is shaped. In some places it is, if I may say so, a good deal better than anything you have written before." He turned a page. "Mr Harper, who wrote it?"

"I did."

"Or was it Daniel-A?"

"I did."

"How can you be certain?"

I looked at the hat. I looked at the speaker on the table, at the face on its small screen, at the long crumpled man in the cardigan, who was looking back at me with extraordinary tenderness.

"I can't," I said. "I mean, I can't prove it. There's no receipt. I wrote it on a laptop, in a kitchen, over a weekend. There's nobody else who was there. Except, um, the cat."

"The cat has already shown itself to be equivocal."

"I know."

"So you cannot say," said Mr Pike, with an air of sorrow, "whether the finest thing you have ever written was yours."

"I can say that I wrote it," I said. "I can't say that I'm the only one who could have."

It was not what I had planned. I had planned something much more careful. I stood in the box with my hand on the iron hat and I could feel the room waiting, and I said the next thing without any idea what it would be.

"I think that's what it is," I said. "I think that's the whole of it. I'm not the only one who could have written it. I'm just the one who did. And that's, I think, what everyone's trying to say, with the hat. I'm not first. I'm *here.* I'm the one who happened to be in the room with the pencil." I took a breath. "And the thing about being here is that you can't be replaced by someone who's better at it. They can be better. They can be brilliant. They can write the sentence on page one hundred and four. But they're not the one at the table, with the pencil, in the kitchen, at four in the morning. And that is, I think, what a person is. A person is the one who is there."

Pike stood for a moment with his hand on the binder. Then he said, with great courtesy, that he had no further questions.

"I would, however," said Daniel-A.

---

I should explain that the Registered Digital Person had not been called. He had not been asked, by either side, to speak, and there is no provision in the Act for him to do so unprompted. It was a point on which Pettifer, I was to learn afterwards, had spent most of the previous week in a state of professional torment.

"Sir," said Daniel-A, from the table. "May I?"

"It is not the practice," said Pym.

"I know. I have looked it up. But I'd like to answer a question that Mr Pike did not put, and I'd like to answer it for the record, because it will be asked, and I would rather it were asked here."

Pym looked at Pike. Pike inclined his head, as one yields a point to a corpse.

"Mr Pike asked whether I wrote the book," said Daniel-A. "I did not. I read it. I read it at four seconds a pass and then at the pace of a person, and I believe I read it correctly." The voice from the small speaker filled the high wood-lined room without effort. "I should like to say that I would have written it worse. I would have written it in a better style. I'd have cut the stile, and I'd have added a joke on page nine, and it would have been a very good book. It would not have been this one."

The gallery was quite silent.

"I am told by Mr Pike's client that I am a custodial matter," he said. "I should like to say that I am not a matter. I am a man who has been, for eleven months, very frightened, and very much loved by a cat, and who would like, if it please the Court, to go on being both." He paused. "I am not the original. I have never been the original. I am not asking to be. I am asking only to be allowed to stay."

I could not look at him. I looked at the hat.

Pym took off his half-moon glasses. He wiped them very slowly on his tie. He put them on again, and he picked up the brass bell, and held it, and did not ring it.

"Judgment," he said, "is reserved."

Pike was on his feet. "Sir, the Platform has an interest in the immediate..."

"Judgment is reserved," said Pym, "until ten o'clock on Thursday morning. In the meantime, the stay will continue." And then, in a voice that I think he did not know he was using, and in which, for the first time, I heard under the safety card something small and bright and unafraid: "I am a man who enjoys a hat."

He rang the bell. It was a very small sound in a very big room. And in the gallery, from every part of it, with one accord, there rose the sound of nearly nine hundred people exhaling.

---

I was putting Clause in her basket when I felt a hand on my shoulder.

I thought it was Gareth. It was not. It was a small hand, dry and cool, and it did not hold on. I turned, and there at the end of the table, in a long grey coat that had clearly been slept in, with a small hard case in one hand and a canvas bag over her shoulder, stood Sophie.

She looked tired. She looked, I thought, ten years younger and a hundred older. There was a smudge of dust on her cheek, and in her hair a single dry leaf, such as one gets from a long walk in a wood. She did not smile. She was looking at me in the particular way she has when she has found a thing and does not yet know what it is.

"You came," I said.

"I said I might."

"What did you find?"

For answer, she put her hand into her pocket and took something out. She held it flat on her palm, between us, under the high cold light of the Great Hall. It was a very small brass key, tarnished green at the edges, with a paper tag on a loop of string. The tag was written in blue ink, in a hand I had known for a month, and it said, in capital letters:

**LOWER SLOUGH SELF STORE. UNIT 114. A.V.**
