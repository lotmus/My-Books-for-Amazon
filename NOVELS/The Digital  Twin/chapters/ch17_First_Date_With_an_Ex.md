# Chapter 17: First Date With an Ex

*Not a date,* said the text. *Waterloo. Under the clock. 11. Bring your umbrella. S.*

I read it standing in the hall, in my socks, at ten past eight on the Saturday, while the kettle came to the boil in the kitchen behind me like a man clearing his throat.

I do not own an umbrella. I have never owned one. I own a coat with a hood, which has served me for eleven years on the theory that weather is a form of opinion. But I had understood the instruction at once, and so, I suspect, had she, because it was the thing she had not said. *Bring the thing we have in common.*

"I'd like to say something," said Daniel-A, from the shelf.

"Please don't."

"I'd like to say that I think you should go."

"I've said I'm going."

"I'd like to say that I think you should go *alone.*" There was a pause. The cat on the shelf opened an eye. "Without me in your ear. Without the phone. I'm very good at being quiet, Daniel, and I'll be quiet all day. But I think there are some things a man should do on his own account."

"You're being very decent about it."

"I'm told it's one of my features."

"Is it hard?"

He considered the question with the care he gave to the things he thought were true. "It's a little like being the sun," he said, "and being asked to go behind a cloud. It's no trouble at all. You only notice it afterwards."

I put the phone on the shelf, face down, beside him. Clause shifted slightly to make room.

"Have a lovely time," said Daniel-A. "Not that it's a date."

---

I was at Waterloo at twenty to eleven.

I don't wish to dwell on this. It is enough to say that a man of thirty-eight who arrives at a railway station twenty minutes early for an appointment he has repeatedly described, in the privacy of his own head, as *purely investigative,* and who then buys a new shirt from a stall on the concourse because the old one has been "looking a bit tired," has no standing to complain of being misjudged.

The shirt was blue. It was a decent blue. It had a label inside the neck that said *ONE SIZE FITS MOST,* and for the first half-hour it fitted none of me.

Waterloo is a cathedral of other people's lateness. Under the great clock, in the middle of the concourse, a river of commuters, tourists, schoolchildren and men with enormous suitcases flowed between the platforms, avoiding one another with the uncanny precision of a shoal of fish. A driverless luggage trolley was patiently following a woman who had forgotten it. A small robot with a mop was cleaning a stain that had been there since the nineties.

At 10:58 she came out of the Underground.

She wore the grey coat. Her hair was a little shorter on one side. She was carrying, in her left hand, a long black object wrapped in a bag, and she walked towards me through the crowd with her chin up and her eyes fixed on the clock, as if it could spare her the sight of me.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm exactly on time."

"You're two minutes early."

"I was *delayed,*" said Sophie, "by a man with a trolley."

She stopped. She looked at the shirt.

"That's new," she said.

"It's an old one."

"It says *one size fits most.*"

"It fits most of me," I said.

The corner of her mouth did the thing. She looked away quickly, at the departure board, and I took the opportunity to observe that she had put on, I thought, a little lipstick, in a shade that was slightly less than the one she wore for work.

I said nothing about it. I am a man of delicacy. I made a mental note of it, and filed it where I keep the things I intend to treasure and never mention.

"This is an investigation," said Sophie.

"Obviously."

"I'm checking a provenance."

"Of course."

"I want to establish whether a memory I have is a memory or something else, and the only way I know is to find a witness."

"Agreed."

"It isn't a date."

"No."

"Good." She unwrapped the bag. It was an umbrella, black, long, with a bent spoke and a brass tag on the handle, battered and faded and very slightly smug. "I took it when I left the flat. I think you'd stopped noticing it."

I looked at it. It had been standing in the hall of the Lowther Road flat for six years. It had been through two wars and a recycling collection. I had not noticed it for years, as one does not notice a coat hook, and I felt, looking at it now, the particular shame of a man who has taken a thing for granted that someone else has been carrying.

"I'd have given it back," I said.

"I know."

"I'd have posted it. In the same parcel as the books."

"Yes," said Sophie, "and it would have come back marked *wrong box.*"

---

The lost property office at Waterloo no longer exists, in the sense that the hatch where I met Sophie has been replaced by a glass kiosk with a screen on the front, and the screen says **FINDR.**

We stood in front of it. It lit up.

"*Hello,*" said the kiosk, in a bright, warm, androgynous voice. "*Have you lost something?*"

"Yes," I said.

"*Please describe it.*"

"Seven years."

There was a short, polite silence.

"*I'm sorry,*" said Findr. "*I don't recognise that item. Did you mean 'years' as in 'a quantity of time'? Would you like to try again?*"

"Leave it," said Sophie, and pulled me away by the sleeve.

Round the back of the kiosk, where the original office had been, there was a door. It was painted dark green and had a brass letterbox and a small handwritten notice, taped at an angle, that said **THE OFFICE. Fridays, 10-2. A PERSON IS AVAILABLE.** The capital letters had been added in a different pen. Under them, somebody had drawn a small, hopeful smiley face.

It was a Saturday. We tried it anyway.

The door opened at once.

"Ah," said a voice. "I thought I heard someone ask for seven years."

---

He was seventy-three, perhaps, and bald, and very small. He wore a brown cardigan with leather patches at the elbow and a lanyard that said **VOLUNTEER: R. DEWHURST**. He had round steel spectacles and the kindly, slightly vacant expression of a man who has spent his career in the vicinity of other people's umbrellas and has long ago made his peace with the human race.

"Come in, come in," he said. "I'm not strictly open. It's my day for the allotment. But I come in on Saturdays, in case anyone wants to talk to a person. Tea?"

"We don't want to be a bother."

"You are a bother. That's rather the point." He gestured. "Mind the box."

The room was narrow. It smelled of tea and old paper and wet wool. On shelves from floor to ceiling, in rows, in boxes, in numbered bags, lay the property of the lost: gloves and hats and prosthetic legs, a trombone, a stuffed owl, forty-one single shoes. A kettle stood on a filing cabinet. In the corner was a tin of travel sweets, and a green pencil in a mug.

I felt Sophie stop beside me.

It was a very small movement. It was the movement of someone who has put her foot on a stair she thought was there.

"We came about an umbrella," I said, after a moment.

"Ah." Mr Dewhurst brightened. "Always the umbrellas. Seven hundred a week, in a wet month. Black, mostly. They breed in the dark." He took the umbrella from Sophie, with the respect due to an old acquaintance, and turned it over in his hands. "Bent spoke. Brass tag. My word. *C. Herbert.*"

"Do you remember him?" said Sophie.

"Do I remember him." Mr Dewhurst began to laugh, a soft wheezing laugh, with his whole chest. "Mr Charles Herbert. A retired chartered accountant from Surbiton. He left that on the 10.14 from Guildford, a wet Tuesday in March, nine years ago. I remember it because he rang eleven times. I remember it because on the Friday he came in person, a very upset gentleman in a mackintosh, and I had to tell him that it had been claimed."

I looked at Sophie.

"*Claimed?*" she said.

"By a young man. And a young woman." He peered at us over the spectacles. "I told him it was a matter of a lost-property dispute. I said that two members of the public had presented themselves at my hatch, simultaneously, each asserting ownership, and that I had formed the view that neither of them was telling the truth."

"We were telling the truth," I said. "We thought it was ours."

"You were telling *a* truth." He chuckled. "It was a very wet day. Everyone had one. Anyway, Mr Herbert said it was theft. And I said, with the greatest respect, sir, it is romance."

There was a pause.

"I'm afraid," said Mr Dewhurst, "that I did say that. I'm not proud of it. I've never had a very good sense of the proprieties."

---

I would like to say that I took this well.

What I did was sit down on a box of walking sticks, which gave way, and I went down into it with helpless dignity. Sophie, beside me, did not move.

"So the umbrella," she said, very carefully, "wasn't ours."

"It was never yours."

"We stole it."

"You took it in good faith. It's a recognised category." Mr Dewhurst put the kettle on. "Mr Herbert came to terms. I gave him a very nice replacement with a duck-head handle. He wrote me a letter eighteen months later to say that he'd grown fond of it, and to ask if the young man had been looked after."

"He asked about *me?*" I said.

"He did. He said he hoped the umbrella had gone to a good home." He held up one finger. "And he asked about the young lady. Because you'll remember, young lady, that when you both came to my hatch, there was a point at which you stopped arguing."

Sophie said nothing. I could see the muscle moving in her jaw.

"I saw the whole thing," said Mr Dewhurst, gently. "I had my head down. I was doing the forms. But I looked up for a moment, and there you were, at the hatch, the pair of you, in the rain, the two of you with your hands on the handle. And you'd both seen the tag, you see. You'd both seen it was somebody else's. But the young man hadn't. He thought it was his. He was so *sure.* And the young lady had seen the initials, and she looked at him, and she looked at the umbrella, and then she looked at me."

"What did I do?" said Sophie, in a low voice.

"You let go."

There was a silence in the little room. The kettle began to tick.

"You let go," said Mr Dewhurst, "and you said to me, quite calmly, 'It's his.' And then you went out into the rain, with no umbrella, and you didn't look back. And I thought, I remember thinking, in all my years at this hatch I have not seen anyone give a thing away so well."

I couldn't look at her.

I looked at my hands, which were shaking, and at the umbrella on the desk, and at the green pencil in the mug.

---

We drank the tea. It was strong, and brown, and there was a biscuit with it that had the structure of a roof tile. Mr Dewhurst talked about his allotment, and his grandchildren, and the Great Wren Incident of 2019, when a bird had been found in a hat and had been taken home, and Sophie said very little, and ate her biscuit, and looked at the green pencil.

"That's an unusual pencil," she said, at last.

"Isn't it?" Mr Dewhurst glanced at it fondly. "It was Ron's. Ron Pickles. He had the hatch before me. He used to keep it behind his ear, along with a tin of those. Travel sweets." He nodded at the corner. "I took over the desk and I took over the tin. It would have seemed rude not to."

"When did he leave?"

"Oh, years ago. Two thousand and twelve. He retired the week of the Olympics. We gave him a barometer."

Sophie put down her cup.

"Two thousand and twelve," she said.

"That's right."

"Five years before we met."

"I'm sorry?" said Mr Dewhurst.

"Nothing," said Sophie. "I was just..." She stopped. For a second I saw her face change; it was the smallest change, a kind of closing, as of a shutter being lowered over a window, and then it was gone, and she smiled at him pleasantly. "I must be thinking of someone else."

"Easily done," said Mr Dewhurst.

I did not understand it at the time. I put it down, if I put it down to anything, to the heat of the little room, or to the strong tea, or to the general awkwardness of being told by a stranger that you had once been very nice. I noticed that she was holding her cup with both hands. I noticed that her tea, which she usually drank like a woman putting out a fire, had gone cold. And I noticed, with an odd sinking sensation that I could not name, that when we stood up to leave and Mr Dewhurst shook her hand, he said:

"Did you know Ron, young lady?"

And Sophie said, "No."

And her voice, for the first time since I had known her, did not sound entirely like her own.

---

We took the Tube to Walthamstow.

I do not know whose idea it was. I have gone over it a number of times, and I believe the suggestion came from neither of us, but from the Victoria line, which at Highbury is capable of taking decisions on behalf of its passengers. We sat side by side on the long seat, the umbrella across our knees like a sleeping child, and watched the dark pass in the window and our faces drift in it.

We did not speak much. It is a peculiar thing about silence with a person you have lived with: it is not empty. It is full, like a room in which furniture has been moved, and you walk round it in the dark and find, with a start, that you know where everything is.

At Walthamstow Central we got out. She said, "Orford Road," and I said, "Yes," and we walked there, past the market and the pound shops and the new flats with the balconies like trays, and nobody said anything about it at all.

The café was still there.

It had changed its name. Where it had been called **Brew & Co.** it was now **The Lemon Tree,** and the awning had been repainted, and the chalkboard outside offered a "flat white with oat milk and a hint of shame." But the window was the same. The tables, small and round, were the same. And behind the counter, polishing a cup, stood a woman of about sixty with iron-grey hair and an expression of calm authority, who looked up as we came in, and stopped.

"Well," she said. "Look what the cat brought."

"Hello, Eleni," said Sophie.

"I thought you'd moved to Clapton."

"I did."

"And him?" Eleni looked at me. "Is he all right? He used to come in here and look at the walls."

"I was thinking," I said, "about the wall."

"You were thinking about her," said Eleni, and went to put the kettle on.

---

We sat at the table in the window.

It was a quarter to three. The café was nearly empty, apart from a man at the back in headphones, writing furiously, and a very small child in a very large pushchair, who stared at us with the flat calm of a magistrate. The light came in sideways and gilded the dust.

Sophie ordered the lemon tart.

I want to be clear that I had not told her what to order. I had not, in fact, said a word. She simply looked at the menu, the same one, in its stained plastic folder, and said "Lemon tart, please," to Eleni, who nodded without writing it down.

"And for him?"

"A flapjack," said Sophie. "He doesn't know yet."

"I do know."

"You'd have said a lemon thing."

I put down the menu.

It came. We ate it. She cut the tart in two, with the side of her fork, and slid half onto my plate, without looking up, and I cut the flapjack in two, and slid half onto hers, and neither of us mentioned it. It was perfectly done. It was a ritual, I realised, that had never been discussed, the way you cannot remember learning to walk.

"Don't tell anyone I like flapjacks," said Sophie.

"I have a signed capture of you saying that."

"I have a reputation."

"I know," I said. "I've protected it for nine years."

She looked at me for a moment over the rim of the cup. Her eyes were very dark. There was nothing in them that I could have written down.

"I'm going to say something," she said.

"Right."

"Don't say *Right.*"

I hadn't meant to. I closed my mouth. We looked at one another across the little round table with the lemon tart between us, and I felt something go through me that I won't try to describe, because the language is not adequate and the word is not mine.

And then Sophie began to laugh.

It came up from nowhere. She put her hand over her mouth. She tried to speak and could not. She laughed until Eleni, behind the counter, looked round, and the man in the headphones glanced up, and the child in the pushchair stared at her with a new and respectful interest. She laughed until there were tears in the corners of her eyes, and when she wiped them away with the back of her wrist, she was smiling, the real one, the whole one, with the chip in her tooth.

"I'm sorry," she said. "It's the umbrella."

"What about it?"

"We *stole* it." She wheezed. "We're thieves. We're a pair of thieves. All this time. The foundation of the entire relationship was a retired accountant from Surbiton."

"He said it was romance."

"He did." She was off again. "He said, *with the greatest respect, sir, it is romance.*"

"I think it's the best thing anyone's ever said about us."

"It's the *only* thing anyone's ever said about us," said Sophie.

That did it. I went too.

We sat in the window of The Lemon Tree, on a Saturday afternoon in November, two people of nearly forty, laughing like idiots over a flapjack, while the child in the pushchair went on staring, and the light moved over the table, and the whole of that terrible season, the fruit and the forms and the clerks and the S1 and the zero and the redacted name, seemed to go still for a moment, and hold its breath, and let us be.

---

It was dusk when we left. The streetlights were coming on. We walked to the Tube without speaking, past the lit windows, and the umbrella swung from my hand like a pendulum.

At the barrier, she turned.

She stopped, and pressed the umbrella into my hand. "Your turn," she said. "It's been mine for three years."

"Daniel."

"Yes."

"I'm going to Pitlochry."

I stared at her.

"You can't."

"I can. I looked it up. There's a sleeper from Euston. I've booked two tickets." She said it in a flat, practical voice, as if reading from an insurance claim. "I've also booked a room. It's a single. With an extra bed."

"Sophie..."

"It's for the investigation." She did not look at me. "I need to know who wrote the fourth page. And I need to see that woman's face when she sees yours."

"And Daniel-A?"

"What about him?"

"Shall I tell him?"

She considered. "Tell him when we've gone," she said. "He'll want to come. He'll want to be with us. And I think that if we're going to do this, I'd like, just once, to do it with the one of you who gets tired."

I could not speak. The barrier beeped at us, politely, and she said, "Thursday week," and went through it.

"Thursday week?" I called after her.

"Not a date," she repeated, over her shoulder.

She vanished into the crowd. A moment later, my phone buzzed in my pocket. I took it out. It was a message from a number I had known for nine years and not rung for three.

*Bring the umbrella. S.*

I read it over and over, like a man memorising a phone number he will not dare to ring.

Then I went home on the Victoria line, and the umbrella and I sat together on the long seat, and watched our faces drift in the glass.

At Stockwell a man got on with a cello, looked at the umbrella, and decided against it.
