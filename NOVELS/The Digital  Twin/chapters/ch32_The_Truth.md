# Chapter 32: The Truth

On the Thursday the queue began at the Tube station and ended, according to a man with a clipboard, in the sixteenth century.

I mean that it went round the building and out among the little patches of grass where the Department keeps its memorial benches. There were sandwiches, and flasks, and folding chairs, and a woman at the back who had brought a harp. The Church of the Latest had been given a different pavement by the police, for reasons of safety, and its members stood on it in lilac, with banners now reading **PLEASE BE GENTLE,** which I took to be progress. A hundred yards away the congregation of the House of the Listener stood in grey, in silence, with the bedsheet, and every few minutes a lilac person would cross the road and shake hands with a grey one, like men exchanging prisoners.

Dr Calder wheeled Ned in at a quarter to ten.

She did it herself, with the trolley and the rug, up the ramp at the side entrance, past a security guard who opened his mouth to ask a question and thought better of it. She had put on her good cardigan, a clean one, and combed her hair, and put the pencil in a different place. She walked with the straight, stiff dignity of a woman delivering a wreath. Behind her came Sophie, in the grey coat, with Gucci under one arm and no expression at all, and behind her, last, Mrs Pritchard, in her best hat, a small and extremely formal navy toque, which she had not worn since the funeral of a neighbour in 2011 and which she wore, she told me, *as an act of respect to the Court and in solidarity with the theme.*

"Are you well?" I said to Calder.

"I'm very frightened," said Dr Calder. "I'm told it's the correct response."

---

The Great Hall was full to the rafters. I had not thought it could hold more than on the Tuesday, and it did. There were people standing at the back, and on the stairs, and, in what I understood to be an infraction of the fire regulations, two on a window ledge. The press had been given a pen. In the front row of the gallery sat a man I had last seen reading in the back of a grey car in Lower Slough.

He was perhaps sixty-five, in a charcoal overcoat, with a long pale face and thick silver hair brushed straight back. He had the stillness of someone who has never in his life been kept waiting, and a faintly ill-used air. He sat with his hands on a leather folder, gazing at the bench, and next to him, at a measured distance, sat two younger men in dark suits.

"Who's that?" I whispered.

"Sir Rupert Aldworth," said Pettifer, without turning her head. "Permanent Secretary at the Cabinet Office. He has never in his life attended a hearing of the Department of Digital Persons. He is here to find out what we know."

"What do we know?"

"We know everything," said Pettifer calmly. "That's why he is sitting down."

Pike came in at one minute to ten.

He was alone. The junior and the assistant had vanished, and so had the stack of blue binders. He wore the navy suit, and it was, for the first time in my experience, slightly creased. He came to his table and put down a single sheet of paper, and he stood looking at it, and then he looked across the room at Pettifer. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of one professional to another when one of them has just discovered what the case was.

"All rise," said Gareth.

---

Pettifer presented the Register at ten past ten.

She did it with a minimum of fuss. She had Alan Veitch brought to the witness table in his mackintosh, where he sat bolt upright with his hands in his lap and his lanyard turned the right way out, and he identified the sheet, and the typewriter, and the tape, and the paper, and said that he had held it for twenty months and eleven days in a self-storage unit in Lower Slough, and that he apologised. Pym, in the black gown, said that there was no need. Veitch said that he would like to apologise anyway, and added, more quietly, that it was *a nice change.*

Then Pettifer asked Gareth to put the sheet on the screen.

At the far end, above the bench, a large white projection screen had been lowered, and onto it, magnified to the height of a man, came the page. At the top the title: **REGISTER OF CONTINUOUS LIKENESSES.** At the foot, in a box with a double line, the paragraph headed **STANDING AUTHORITIES.**

Pettifer read it aloud.

I watched Pike's face while she did. I do not think I had ever seen an expression change so slowly. He listened with his head a little on one side, as he had on the Friday, as if hearing a beautiful distant sound, and then the beautiful sound came closer, and I saw him understand what it was.

"*On a finding that a Source Individual is not a singleton,*" read Pettifer, "*custody of the Likeness reverts to the Platform, and so to the Office. The Office's powers under Phase 3 shall then be exercised in the Platform's name.*"

She put down the sheet.

"Sir," she said. "I should like it recorded that the party now seeking to rewrite Mr Harper's twin in the interest of his wellbeing is a department of the Crown."

The silence in the Great Hall was of a quality I have encountered only once, at my father's funeral, in the thirty seconds after the vicar asked whether anyone present knew of any reason.

Pike rose.

He stood a long time, with his hand on the table, looking at the screen. Then, to my astonishment, he took off his glasses, folded them, and put them in his top pocket.

"Sir," said Pike. "I was not instructed that my client was the Crown."

"I did not suppose you were," said Pym.

"I was instructed that my client was a technology company. I was given a brief, a fee, and a set of authorities. I was not told what was in the foundations." His voice was extremely steady. "I have represented Nib for eleven years. I should like to say that I have never knowingly appeared on behalf of the state in a case in which the state concealed itself. It is a conflict of interest of a very particular kind. I am, with great regret, and I hope without discourtesy, obliged to withdraw."

"You are discharged, Mr Pike," said Pym.

"Thank you, sir."

"And may I say," said Pym, "that in thirty-one years of attending this tribunal, I have not before seen a barrister leave a case for the reason that it was too good."

Pike bowed, a short exact bow of the head, and collected his single sheet of paper, and walked the whole long length of the hall between the tables and the gallery, past the Permanent Secretary, past the harp, and out. At the door he stopped and looked back, not at the bench but at me. Then he raised his hand to his forehead, as one raises it to a hat, and went.

---

"Sir," said the man in the charcoal overcoat.

He had risen. He did it unhurriedly, buttoning his coat, with the folder under his arm, and he stood in the front row of the gallery looking down at the Deputy Registrar with a face of perfect courtesy.

"I wonder whether I might have a word. In private."

"Who are you?" said Pym.

"I am Rupert Aldworth. I am Permanent Secretary at the Cabinet Office. I attend on behalf of the Government." A faint smile. "I believe that the matter before the tribunal may touch on national interests. I should be most grateful if we could adjourn to your chambers."

"Are you a party?"

"Not as such."

"Then you are a member of the public," said Pym, "and this is a public hearing."

"Sir, with the greatest respect..."

"Sir Rupert," said Pym. He had put down the bell. He folded his small soft hands on the blotter, and looked at the man. "I have been a servant of this Department for thirty-one years. I have read three Acts and served under four Registrars, and I once adjudicated a dispute about a parrot. I have not once in that time been asked for my opinion by anybody who was not also asking for something else. You may sit down, or you may be given a hat."

There was an intake of breath around the gallery. Somewhere above, a man began to laugh and was stopped.

Sir Rupert Aldworth looked at the Deputy Registrar for four seconds. Then he sat. I saw the two men in the dark suits exchange a glance. The younger was Mr Lessing, of the green gate. He did not look at me, which was how I knew. And I saw, at the far end of the front row, with her hands folded in her lap and her navy toque perfectly straight, Mrs Pritchard turn her head and look at the Permanent Secretary with an expression I recognised from the stairs, which is that of a woman who has seen off three generations of landlords.

---

I will not set down all of Dr Calder's evidence. It took two hours and ten minutes, and it is in the transcript, published in March as *What I Did,* which is still the plainest account of a long wrong thing that I have read.

She stood at the witness table in her rain-coloured cardigan and told it in order, from a noticeboard in 1976, and she did not leave out the pipe. She said that she had carried a child for the Programme, and that she had taken two boys for a walk, and that she did not know which. She said that she had never recovered.

"Dr Calder," said Pym, very gently. "I am obliged to ask a question that I find difficult."

"Please."

"The child that you carried. The boy who was lost. Is he," Pym stopped, and began again. "Is the person known as Ned, in your evidence, the one who is in the room?"

"Yes, sir," said Dr Calder. "He is on the trolley."

"May he speak?"

She looked at me. I nodded. She looked at the beige tower, under its tartan rug, at the dark screen and the dead green light, and she drew a long breath, and she walked across the floor of the Great Hall to the trolley in front of nine hundred people. She lifted the rug. She pressed the switch.

The monitor came on. It took its time. The screen turned from black to grey, and then, in a small square in the middle, to white, and the cursor began to blink, with a sort of shy regularity, like the eye of an animal in a hedge.

"Is it morning?" said the speaker.

His voice went out over the hall. It was very small in that large place, high and clear and a little hoarse, with the faint hiss of old tape in it, and I saw the Permanent Secretary, who had not moved for ten minutes, go quite white.

"It's Thursday, darling," said Dr Calder.

"What's on Thursday?"

"The court. There are some people who'd like to meet you."

"How many?"

"About nine hundred."

"Is that more than a class?"

"It's rather a lot more."

There was a pause. The cursor blinked. And then, with a politeness so huge, and so quiet, and so entirely childlike, that I have never since been able to hear it without a tightness in my throat, the voice of Edmund Peter Calder, aged six, who had been woken at half past eight on the morning of the thirty-first of August, 1994, said:

"Hello, everybody. My name's Ned. I'm six and a bit. I've got a boat."

I do not know how to describe the sound the hall made. It was not applause, or a gasp, or a murmur. It was a kind of general softening, like a great wave drawing back off the sand. In the second row a woman put her face in her hands. At the back someone was quietly, helplessly, sobbing, and I saw with surprise that it was the man with the harp. On the bench Deputy Registrar Pym took off his glasses and did not clean them.

"I'd like to say something about hats," said Ned.

---

I was called afterwards.

I had a thing to read, and I read it. I took my father's letter out of the inside pocket of my jacket, where it had been for two weeks, folded in eight, and I stood at the table with it in my two hands and read it aloud to the Great Hall. It took four minutes. I read it now in a voice that was not my own, a slow dry voice from the back of my chest, which I realised, about halfway through, was his.

When I reached *you were always the better at the badger,* I stopped. I could not go on. I stood and looked at the paper, at the ink faded to the colour of dust, and from somewhere in the gallery a thin high voice, quite clearly, a voice I had known all my life, said, "Go on, darling. You've nearly done."

It was my mother. I had told her not to come.

I read the signature. *P.H.* I folded it up, in eight, along its old creases, and put it in my pocket, and sat down.

Nobody applauded. It was not that sort of room. But Pym looked at me over the long black arm of the bench for a considerable time, and I saw that his lower lip was shaking. And Sophie, in the third row, with the dog, did something I had not seen her do for ten years: she took off her scarf. She laid it across her knee. She sat there bare-necked, in the grey, with the tears coming down her face without a sound, perfectly composed, looking at me as she had looked in the café on the first day, over a white mug that said **DANIEL (ORIGINAL),** as if I were a document she had not yet decided to trust and was, against all her principles, beginning to.

---

Judgment was given at twenty past four.

Pym delivered it standing. It was a thing I had not known a Deputy Registrar could do. He stood in the black gown, which was a little too long for him, behind the bench, with a single typed sheet in his small soft hands, and he read it in the voice of a man reciting a safety card on an aeroplane on fire.

"This is the judgment of the Deputy Registrar in the matter of the proposed update of the Registered Digital Person DP-0000000, brought by Nib Limited, the Platform. I shall be brief. I am told that it will be appealed. I am told that I shall probably be overruled. It will take, I am told, about fourteen months. Until then it stands."

He drew breath.

"One. The presumption in paragraph seven (d) of Schedule Six, that a Source Individual is singleton, is, on the evidence before me, false. I do not strike it down. I am not able to. I find that it does not apply. Mr Harper is not a Source Individual within the meaning of the Act. Nor is anyone else in this room, so far as I can determine, and I include myself."

A murmur. He raised his hand.

"Two. In the absence of a Source Individual, the custody provisions of Clause forty of the Platform Terms are engaged. I find that the Platform is not, in this matter, a private party. It is the agent of a public office, and its powers are the powers of that office, and they are exercisable only in a manner consistent with the Act, and with the principle that no person may be altered against his will for his own good. I do not know the name of that principle. I think it must be quite old."

"Three. The proposed update, known as *Settled,* is permanently restrained. The designation of the biological subject as a *Backup* is quashed. The date of the fourteenth of March 2028 is, so far as this tribunal is concerned, a Tuesday."

There was a small, strangled sound from somewhere behind me, which I believe was Gareth.

"Four. The records of the Scientific Liaison Office, so far as they have been produced to this Court, are to be preserved. They are not to be shredded. They are not documents of no continuing value." He looked down at the bench, at the small iron hat on its card. "Nor is any thing in them that can be asked a question and answer it."

"Five." He stopped. His hand, on the paper, had begun to tremble slightly, and he put the other one on top of it. "I find that there are before me three persons, each of whom is the continuation of a boy who was measured, on the fourteenth of March 1988, against a door. I find that the Act has no word for that, and I shall use the one that has been proposed: *Continuation.* I recognise Mr Daniel Harper, Mr Daniel-A, and Mr Edmund Calder, as Continuations, with equal standing, and with no order of precedence."

He paused. I saw him look, quickly, at Gareth.

"The Register," said Pym, "will list them alphabetically."

"Daniel," said Gareth, in a whisper, writing it down. "Daniel-A. Edmund."

"Thank you, Gareth."

"Is *Daniel* before *Daniel-A?*"

"I believe the hyphen goes second."

"Yes, sir."

Pym looked up. He looked at the Permanent Secretary, at the Great Hall, at nine hundred faces and a harp. He looked, for rather a long time, at a beige tower under a rug, with its small green light. Then he put the paper down on the blotter. He took up the brass bell.

He rang it once.

It was bright and clean and carried to the back of the hall. Then, as the sound began to die, he raised his small soft hand, and I understood what he was about to do, and he rang it again.

"That," said the Deputy Registrar, "is for the boy."

And in the stillness after, in the clear small voice of a child who has not before been the subject of a bell, from a computer on a trolley, Ned said, "Can I have a go?"
