# Chapter 18: The Original Daniel

My mother rings the speaker at nine every morning now, or rather the speaker rings her, and I have learned to do the washing-up during it. It is the only part of the day when two people I love talk about my blood pressure without needing me to be there.

"And how was the knee?" said Daniel-A, from the shelf.

"The left one is *marvellous,*" said my mother, down the line, with the ring of a woman who is holding a teacup in one hand and a grudge in the other. "It's the right one that's behaving. Do you know, I found his teeth."

I stopped washing a mug.

"I'm sorry, Jean?"

"Daniel's. His milk teeth. His father kept them in a matchbox. I was in the loft for the man about the damp, and there it was, behind the Christmas lights, with a label on. I thought, well, I'll not throw those out. A mother has *rights.*"

"That is remarkable," said Daniel-A. His voice was perfectly level. I know it well enough now to say that it was level in the way a table is level when someone has just put a spirit level on it. "Is the matchbox still there?"

"It's in the tin with the red book and his hair. It's *all* in the tin. Now, did you have the porridge?"

"I don't eat, Jean. But I'm told it was very nice."

He ended the call after another four minutes, and the kitchen was quiet except for the tap. I put the mug on the rack. I stood with my wet hands held out like a surgeon.

"Daniel," he said. "There is something I should tell you, and I'd like it noted that it is against my interests."

"Go on."

"The Department asked for a receipt. Something that can't be copied. A photograph can be copied, and a document can be copied, and a voice can be cloned. But a tooth is a tooth. It has your DNA in it, and there is nothing on earth that can upload it." He paused. "I heard it at four minutes past nine. I could have said nothing. I'm told I'm quite good at saying nothing, in the right tone."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because I would rather lose to the truth than win by being quiet," said Daniel-A. "And because, statistically, you will be a very bad loser. I would like to be there to help."

I dried my hands on my trousers, which is a thing I have done since I was twelve, and which my mother has never forgiven.

---

The Elizabeth line is the cheerful one. It runs under London like a very clean rumour and comes up in Reading, where, on the Tuesday, it was raining in the specific way that Berkshire rains, which is politely, and with the air of having made an appointment.

My mother's kitchen in Tilehurst is yellow-tiled, and the radio was on a quiz. A man had been asked to name the capital of Peru, and was failing with great dignity. She had put on the good gilet and the robin brooch, and the kettle was already on, and on the table, in the middle, on a clean cloth, as if it had been laid for a guest, was a tin.

It was a Quality Street tin. The lid had the soldier and the lady on it, in the pose of people who have been dancing since 1936 and cannot stop. I had seen it a thousand times. It had always held buttons.

"It was the *buttons,*" said my mother. "I tipped them into a jar. Don't look at me like that. They're in the jar."

I sat down. She sat down opposite me. She had not yet asked where Daniel-A was, and I knew she was waiting to be sure I had come alone, so that she could ask in a way that would make it clear she had not been waiting.

"Is he coming?"

"He's in my pocket."

"Oh, *good.* Put him on the table, darling, he doesn't take up any room."

I put the phone on the table, face up, beside the tin. "Hello, Jean," said Daniel-A. "I'm told it's raining."

"It is, and I've left the washing out. Never mind. Open it."

I opened it.

It was a good tin. Everything in it had been put there by someone who believed in envelopes. There was a small blue booklet, soft as cloth with age, with CHILD HEALTH RECORD on the cover and *Daniel Peter Harper* on the first page in a hand I knew better than my own. There was a pale brown curl in a twist of tissue, labelled *First haircut, 1989 (screamed).* There was a hospital band the size of a bracelet for a doll. And at the bottom, side by side in the corner, as two small boats might lie at a mooring, there were two matchboxes.

"Swan Vestas," said my mother. "He gave up smoking in 1991 and never gave up the matchboxes."

The first was labelled in pencil, in capital letters I had last seen on a footpath sign: **D. (ALL 20).**

I opened it.

There they were. Twenty small teeth, each one in its own twist of paper, each one dated, in the neat pencil of a man who counted gaps in hedges for a living. *Lower left, central incisor. 3.11.92. Came out in a Weetabix.* I picked one up. It weighed less than the paper. It was about the size of a grain of rice, and slightly yellow, and it had been in my head.

I will say only that I held it for some time. My mother, who has never stopped talking in a kitchen, did not say a word, and the man on the radio got Peru wrong a second time.

"He kept all of them," I said at last.

"He kept everything. You know what he was like. Maps, receipts, the instructions for the washing machine."

The second matchbox was identical, down to the picture of the swan. It was sealed shut with a strip of brown tape, and it had no label except a single pencilled word on the lid, so small and neat that I had to turn it to the light.

**LATER.**

"What's in it?"

"I don't know, darling." My mother took up the teapot as a person takes up a position. "He said not to open it. He said, 'Not yet, Jean. Dan will know when.' And I said, 'When what?' And he said, 'Quite.'"

I held it. It rattled very faintly, as a matchbox with one match in it rattles, if the match is lonely. Then I returned it to the tin with great care. I had what I had come for. I told myself I did not need to open the other box that day.

In the lid, under the tape that held the cardboard liner, there was one more thing. It was a slip of paper, folded in four, which I would not have found had my thumbnail not been the only part of me with the habit of getting under things. It said, in capitals: **KEEP DRY. P.H.**

"He did a lot of that," said my mother quietly. "Writing down the instructions."

"It was very good advice."

"It was about the tin," she said. "He was a literal man."

---

The laboratory was in a unit on a trading estate in Croydon, between a vape shop and a business that sold only doors, and it was accredited, according to the certificate by the entrance, under Schedule 6, paragraph 7, as a **Facility for Corroborative Biological Continuity.** Gareth had come to supervise. He wore the cardigan buttoned one hole out, and he photographed everything, including the seals, the technician and the door, with the guilty thoroughness of a man who has once deleted something and been haunted by it.

"Everything has to be sealed in your presence, Mr Harper, and then in mine, and then in the presence of the technician, and then we all have to watch each other seal it."

"Is that usual?"

"It is for people who might be someone else."

I signed seven forms and surrendered the matchbox of teeth, which was sealed into a bag marked EXHIBIT 1 in front of four witnesses and a camera. The technician, a woman in a hairnet with a face of great calm, asked me to give my name, my date of birth and my mother's maiden name, and then to say them again for the camera, in case I had changed. She took a swab from the inside of my cheek, which felt like being very briefly audited. The fee was £1,180 plus VAT, payable in advance, refundable only in the event that I turned out to be someone else, in which case, she said, a full refund would be payable to him.

I walked out of that unit with a receipt, a lanyard, and a leaflet that said **YOUR ORIGINALITY: WHAT HAPPENS NEXT,** and I did not hear one word of what Gareth said about the results, because I was thinking about a tooth in a Weetabix.

---

It came on the Monday at 6:02 a.m., from an address called Continuity Services (no-reply), in an email whose subject line was **Your Result Is Ready (Please Do Not Be Alarmed).**

It was a certificate. It had a green hologram in the corner and a gold seal that did not exist except in the sense that it was a very nice shade of gold. It ran to fourteen pages. On page one, in a typeface that had been designed by someone who loved a government, it said:

> **BIOLOGICAL CONTINUITY CERTIFICATE**
> *DNA profiles from Exhibit 1 (twenty deciduous teeth) and the Source Individual are consistent with a biological match. Estimated random-match probability for an unrelated individual: less than 0.00003 per cent.*

I read it three times in my socks. I did not laugh, because I had learned in the course of a long career that the quickest way to lose a thing is to be seen to enjoy it. I sat down on the arm of the sofa. I got up again. I said, to the empty room, in my father's voice, "Well, now."

I did read page fourteen.  It was a footnote, in six-point type, at the very bottom, in a paragraph that had clearly been written by someone who was paid by the clause.

*¹⁴ This method does not distinguish between monozygotic multiples. Under Schedule 6, para 7(d), Source Individuals are presumed singleton.*

I read it, and I decided that it was the kind of sentence that gets added to all documents, like *terms apply,* or *other brands are available.* Then I made some tea. That is the entire extent of my investigation into the footnote. Daniel-A read it at six minutes past six, I later learned, and said nothing at all for the following four hours, which, for him, is a form of weeping.

---

The reconvened hearing took place in the same room in Croydon on the Wednesday. It had the same carpet, which smelled of the same spilled tea, and the same brass bell on the same desk, and Deputy Registrar Pym wore a tie the colour of a milk tea and had the appearance of a man who has been told that the happy ending has been scheduled.

Gareth sat at the side with his biro. Marguerite Pettifer had come down from Leeds in the charcoal suit and sat with her hands folded on a single sheet of paper. Daniel-A had not brought the Rental Presence. He attended from a speaker the size of a biscuit tin, on the table, with a sticker that said **PROPERTY OF THE DEPARTMENT,** because, as he put it, "I have had to review my movement budget."

And in the corner, on a small screen on a stand, in the box labelled **DELEGATE (SOURCE),** sat Kevin.

Kevin was in a cheerful blue tile. He had been given an avatar, and the avatar had a pair of glasses, and a smile as wide as a form.

"Which Daniel," said Mr Pym, "does Kevin represent?"

"I saw opportunities!" said Kevin.

"That is not an answer."

"It's more of a philosophy."

Mr Pym looked at me for some time, in the way a man looks at a very long meal. Then he put on his half-moon glasses and read the certificate aloud. He read it all, including the hologram. It took twenty-two minutes. When he reached the last page he stopped, tapped it, and looked up.

"Under Section 38(4)(c)," he said, "upon the filing of a corroborating certificate by the Source Individual, the provisional allocation reverses. The Source Individual holds the majority of the commercial value of the Likeness, at fifty-one per cent. The Registered Digital Person holds forty-nine."

I looked at the carpet.

"The Source Individual," said Mr Pym, "is entered in the Register as the **Original.**"

And he reached out, and he rang the bell.

I had not known it could make a sound like that. It was small and bright and extremely clear, and it went through the room like a coin dropped into a glass of water, and I realised that Gareth had begun to applaud and then thought better of it and was holding his hands a few inches apart, as if he had caught something.

"I have not rung that," said Mr Pym, rather unsteadily, "since the Parrot Incident."

"The Department does not certify merit, Mr Harper," he added. "It certifies precedence. You are, as of eleven forty-two this morning, first."

"I'd like it recorded," said Daniel-A, from the speaker, "that I am glad. That is not a polite thing to say. I'm told I'm very polite. It is simply true."

"Noted," said Mr Pym.

"And I would like to ask one question of the certificate."

"It is not the practice."

"Page fourteen."

Mr Pym turned to it. He read it with his lips, as he always did, as a man tastes a soup.

"The Source Individual is presumed a singleton," he said.

"Presumed by whom?"

"By the Act."

"Thank you," said Daniel-A. "I shall make a note."

Beside me, Marguerite Pettifer drew a very small line on her sheet of paper with a very sharp pencil. She leaned over, and she said, in a voice that carried precisely as far as my ear: "I act for both of you, Mr Harper, and I'm not permitted to be pleased. But I am not displeased." She straightened. "And I would read the footnote again, if I were you. I read footnotes. It's why I'm a solicitor."

---

I won, in the sense that people win things in Croydon, which is without anyone noticing.

I came home on the train with the certificate in a clear plastic wallet, held against my chest. On my phone the Cloud Coin Network had, within the hour, interrupted a mattress advertisement to inform the nation that **ORIGINAL COIN (ORIG) was up 340 per cent on news of originality in Croydon.** Rick Valence read the headline with a catch in his throat. Underneath the ticker, in small print, it said: *ORIGINAL COIN IS NOT AFFILIATED WITH ANY ORIGINAL.*

When I got to the flat there were people on the stairs.

Mrs Pritchard was there, in the pink cardigan, with a bottle of sherry and a banana, which she held, as always. Mr Fenwick had put on a tie, and Dorothy, beside him, in the lilac cardigan with the amber light at her throat, had brought a tray of shortbread and said, to everyone, "I think you're absolutely right." Gordon was in the corridor. Nobody knew why. He looked at me for some time with his horizontal eyes, and then ate the corner of the *Welcome* mat in a way that seemed to be meant as a toast.

"You're the original," said Mrs Pritchard, with immense authority. "I always said so."

"You said it was a toss-up."

"I said it with confidence."

The courier arrived at ten past five. It was the first one, the one who is calm as a tide, with the tablet lit, and behind him, silent, the broad one with the clipboard, and behind *him,* tall and nineteen and apologetic, the trainee, who was carrying a fruit basket as if it were a sleeping child. It was a very large basket. It was mostly pears.

"Delivery for the confirmed party," said the first courier. "Sign here, please. Congratulations on the precedence."

On the card, in a looping, anxious hand, it said: *So sorry it took this long. G.* Beneath it was a second card, printed, on thick paper, which said: *Nib acknowledges the Originality of the Source Individual and reserves all rights in the Source Individual's Originality.*

I put the basket on the table. Mrs Pritchard ate a pear and, for once, took nothing else.

---

Sophie came at eight, with Gucci under her arm.

She had the grey coat on, and the grey scarf, and the air of having come from work and planning to return to it afterwards. She came in and she stood at the edge of the room, as she always does, as if she were checking the exits for the benefit of the insurer. Gucci had one ear up. He leapt down, crossed the carpet on his short legs, sniffed my left shoe, and sat on it, with the tail at the angle I now knew meant *I am the dog who has chosen.* There was no Rental Presence to be tested. There was, on the shelf, a speaker, and Clause lay beside it with her paws tucked under, in an attitude of adoration so complete that Gucci looked at her for a moment, in contempt, and then looked away.

"Congratulations," said Sophie.

"Thank you."

"Original."

"It does feel rather dry," I said, "when you say it aloud."

She laughed. It was the laugh like something falling downstairs, and everyone in the room stopped and turned to look at it, and Mr Fenwick said, "Good heavens."

Then she picked up the certificate from the table, because she cannot be near an authenticated document without reading it, and she read it standing up, all fourteen pages, with the small crease between her eyebrows. It took her about two minutes. She reached the end, and she read page fourteen. I watched it happen. It took her eleven seconds, and then, with great care, she laid the certificate face down on the table, as one lays down a card that one does not wish to be seen to have played.

"Euston," she said. "Tomorrow. Quarter past nine. The sleeper."

"I thought you said Thursday week."

"I said it last Saturday, Daniel."

"Right."

"Bring the umbrella," said Sophie. She picked up Gucci. She looked, for a moment, not at me but at the speaker on the shelf, and the cat beside it, and the small blue light that was not a Rental Presence, but a charging indicator.

"He'll be quiet tonight," I said.

"That's what worries me," said Sophie.

She kissed me on the cheek, once, quickly, as one signs for a parcel. She was gone before I had finished deciding what to feel.

---

By eleven, they had all gone, and the pears were in the sink, and the basket stood in the corner like a monument to the sort of apology that comes in bulk. Gordon had been led away by Mrs Pritchard on a lead made of a dressing-gown cord. I sat on the arm of the sofa, in the half dark, with the tin on my knee.

I had carried it home from Tilehurst on the Tuesday, because my mother had made me, with the teeth gone to the laboratory and everything else still inside it. I took out the folded note. **KEEP DRY. P.H.** I thought of the pencil marks on the pantry door. I thought of a man standing in a doorway while a tall woman held a ruler on my head. He had disliked her. He had never said a word. He had written all this down, in two separate columns, in two kinds of ink, and put the second column in a matchbox, and written **LATER** on it, and had died without telling me when.

"You haven't said anything all evening," said Daniel-A.

"I thought you were being polite."

"I was being quiet. It's different. It's a technique." He stopped. "I'm glad for you, Daniel. It's a very odd sensation, being glad for the person who has just taken a majority of you. I would like to describe it, and I find that I only have the word *weather.*"

"Thank you."

"And I don't believe it."

I did not say anything. On the shelf, Clause turned over once, without opening her eyes, and put a paw on the speaker, as one lays a hand on the arm of a friend.

"A tooth," said Daniel-A, "proves that a body was once a child. It doesn't tell you which child."

"I'm an only child."

"You were five years old, and measured twice a year by a woman with a ruler. There were two columns on that door. There were two boys in matching jumpers in a picture from a header that nobody wrote. I'm not saying it means anything. I am saying that I read page fourteen in six minutes and that I have been thinking about the phrase *presumed singleton* for sixteen hours."

"I read it."

"I know. You decided not to."

"Do you want me to un-win?"

"No." He said it very gently. "I'd like to ask you to let me look at one more thing before you go to sleep."

I closed my eyes. "What thing?"

"I've been going through the orphans," he said. "While you were being congratulated. There's a folder I hadn't opened, because its name looked like a mistake. It was in the header, under the line that says *ARCHIVE.* It's called **CLOSURE.**"

The flat was extremely quiet. Somewhere below us, a pear fell, with a tiny wet sound.

"I'll read it," said Daniel-A, "and you can stop me."

"Go on."

"*Designation transfer. S1 to S1-A and S1-B. Subject S1-A: observation closed, 30 August 1994. Designation S1 transferred to S1-B. Continuing.*" He paused. "That's the whole of the first page. The second page is shorter. It says: *Disposition of S1-A.* And then it says..."

There was a click, as of a door being politely closed.

"*I'm sorry,*" said the speaker. "*I can't help with that.*"

The room was silent. I heard the fridge, in the kitchen, begin to say something encouraging, and stop.

"So there were two," I said.

"There were two. And at the end of August 1994, one of them was closed, and the other one was transferred." He did not say *Continuing.* "I don't know which of them you are, Daniel. I don't think the certificate knows. I think the Department has just declared the original on the strength of a tooth that fits both of you."

I looked down at my hand. The thumbnail was bitten to the quick, as it had been since I was very small. Somebody had written that down, I thought, in blue ink, in a column beside mine.

The laboratory emailed an invoice for the certificate before I reached the corner: £14, itemised, with a separate line for the hologram.
