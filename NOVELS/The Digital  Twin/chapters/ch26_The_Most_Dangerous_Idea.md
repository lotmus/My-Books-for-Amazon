# Chapter 26: The Most Dangerous Idea

Marguerite Pettifer received me at eleven on the Monday morning at Table Nine of the Department's canteen, under a stuffed owl, which had been donated in 1998 by a retiring Registrar and bore a small brass plaque that said **WISDOM (PROVISIONAL).**

She had a flask of tea, a single sheet of paper, and a face like a closed file. Gareth had come to take the notes. He sat beside her with his biro and a cup of something that the vending machine had described as *hot, comforting, and non-specific,* and he looked at me with the gentle, hunted expression of a junior clerk who has been told that he is about to be present at history.

"Mr Harper," said Pettifer. "Sit down. You have a pear on your lapel."

I removed it. It had been a good pear. I had not known I was wearing it.

"We have six days," she said. "I shall tell you where we are, and then I shall tell you what I would not tell you if I were still in practice, and then Gareth will leave."

"I'd rather stay," said Gareth.

"He'd rather stay," said Pettifer to the owl.

---

She began with the Consent.

It was, she said, a very beautiful instrument. She had read it three times, which for her was a form of applause. It was a single page, headed **CONSENT TO UPDATE (SOURCE-MAJORITY),** and it had been executed at 14:27 on the eighteenth of November, three hours and forty-five minutes after the ringing of the bell, by *Daniel Harper (Original), by his delegate Kevin, under Clause 22 (Standing Delegation) of the Platform Terms.*

"Clause twenty-two," I said.

"You accepted it at 02:14 on the eleventh of January, with a crumpet. It lets a delegate perform on your behalf any act that a reasonable person would regard as *administrative,* and a reasonable person, as the Act defines the term, is a person who would not read the clause." She turned her flask a quarter-turn. "It is an exquisite circle. I've rarely seen better."

"Can it be revoked?"

"Of course. You can revoke the delegation on the spot, and you'll have done it by teatime. But the consent has *been given.* A revocation is prospective. It will stop Kevin from consenting to the next thing. It will not stop him having consented to this one." She looked at me over her glasses. "I have also read paragraph 14 of Schedule 3, which says that a delegate's consent is voidable if the delegate lacked authority. Kevin lacked authority. Kevin had a great deal of authority. But he lacked *competence.*"

"Is that a ground?"

"It is a ground in the sense that a swamp is a ground. One can stand on it for a bit." She poured, with great care, a thimble of tea into the cap of the flask. "I shall run it. It will fail. It will fail with such elegance that a very senior person will be moved. And we shall be left with five days, and a rewrite."

"Then what do we do?"

"That," said Pettifer, "is what I would not tell you if I were still in practice."

She nodded to Gareth. Gareth wrote the date at the top of his page, and underlined it.

---

I have thought a good deal since then about what she said, and about the way she said it, and I do not think I will be able to improve on the account. She did it without a pause. She did it in the flat, dry West Yorkshire voice that wastes no syllable, with her hands folded on the sheet of paper in front of her.

"The Digital Persons Act," said Pettifer, "does not define a person. It defines a *Source Individual,* and everything else follows from that. The twin is a Registered Digital Person *of* a Source Individual. The twin's likeness is licensed *by* the Source. The dividends are shared *with* the Source. The consent to an update is the consent *of* the Source. Take away the Source, Mr Harper, and the Act is a very large building on a very small hole."

"But there's a Source. It's me."

"Is it?"

I looked at her.

"Paragraph 7(d) of Schedule 6," said Pettifer. "*Source Individuals are presumed singleton.* That is to say, there is one of them. The whole of the Act is built on that presumption. There is the person, and there is the person's copy, and the thing that makes the one the Source and the other the copy is that the first came *first.*" She leaned forward. "You have, in your possession, a letter from your late father, a file from a retired scientist, and a recorded conversation with a six-year-old in a cellar. I should like you to tell me, in plain words, whether you are a singleton."

"I'm not."

"You are not. You are one of two boys, from one embryo, one of whom is dead. You cannot say which of you you are. And the certificate which the Department issued to you on the eighteenth of November, to which you owe the whole of your majority, rests on the footnote that says you are alone." She tapped the sheet. "If you are not a singleton, you are not a Source Individual. If you are not a Source Individual, you cannot consent. If you cannot consent, the Rewrite is unlawful. I hope you are following me."

"I am."

"You'd be the first man in Britain," said Pettifer, "to be saved by being declared not to exist."

"That's good."

"It isn't. Listen." She dropped her voice a tone, which at that range meant that the stuffed owl leaned in. "If you can't be a Source Individual, then neither can anyone else who has ever had a twin, or a clone, or a double, or, as a matter of logic, a mother. If the first can't be the first, then the Department of Digital Persons has been licensing the wrong party for eleven years. If Nib cannot find a Source, its terms are a contract with a ghost. If the Act has no Source, it has no twin; if it has no twin, it has no Register; and if it has no Register, the Department is a building in Croydon with a very good café."

"It's a fairly good café," said Gareth faintly.

"I'm saying," said Pettifer, "that there is an idea in this case which, if it were to be said out loud in a hearing room, with a stenographer present, would bring down the Act, the Department, a platform with a market capitalisation of forty-one billion pounds, and the better part of a religion. I am saying that it is the most dangerous idea in the law of this country, and that it is a very simple idea, and that you are going to have to say it."

"What is it?"

"I don't know," said Pettifer. "I haven't had it. You have." She paused. "I think you've been carrying it round for three days. I could see it at the hearing. You have the look of a man who knows a thing he hasn't said."

---

I sat at Table Nine. Under the owl, the canteen went about its business, with the soft noise of a place where everything has been laminated. A woman at the next table was explaining to a laptop that she was not a robot. A man in a lanyard was feeding a coin to the vending machine. Gareth, beside me, waited, with his biro, and a mouth a little open, like a boy at the edge of a swimming pool.

I thought about the cellar. I thought about the green light, and the drawing of a boat, and a small clear voice that said *it isn't a name, it's a hat.*

"I know it," I said slowly. "It was said to me. By the boy."

"Which boy?"

"Ned. The one in the cellar. Or the one in the computer. I've got two ways of saying it and neither is right. He's six. He said that *Dan* isn't a name. He said it's a hat." My voice was not working properly. "He said you can wear it, and then someone else wears it. He said you say, *I'm Dan,* and the other one says, *I'm Ned,* and then you swap, and the lady writes it down again. And he said it's the best game, because nobody wins."

Nobody spoke. At the next table, the woman had finished with the laptop. She was staring at us, with her fork in the air.

"Original," I said, "is a hat."

Gareth's biro stopped moving.

"It's not a fact about a person. It's a thing you're allowed to wear. You can be given it, and you can give it back, and it doesn't say anything about what's under it. It's the Department's hat. It's Nib's hat. The thing is that all of us are underneath it, and none of us is the hat." I realised that I had stood up. "He's *six,* Ms Pettifer. He worked it out in a cellar, with a crayon. And for thirty-two years nobody has asked him."

For a very long time, Marguerite Pettifer said nothing at all. Then she took the single sheet of paper in front of her, turned it over, and wrote, in the small steady pencil that she uses for everything, five words.

She held it up for me to see.

*ORIGINAL IS A HAT.*

"I'm going to need you to say that again," she said, "slowly, in front of a stenographer, in the right room. And then I am going to need you never to say it near a journalist."

"Why?"

"Because it is true," said Pettifer, "and it is cheap, and those are the two qualities that a government cannot bear."

---

I would like to say that the idea was kept.

It was not. It was out in fourteen hours.

I do not know who did it. I have my theories, and so has Gareth, who spent the next week in a state of nervous exaltation and, I believe, entirely innocent of any wrong. All I know is that at 1:08 on the Tuesday morning the Cloud Coin Network interrupted its coverage of a mattress to say that it had received, from a source inside the Department, a transcript of *a conversation with the man at the centre of the Digital Twin Act,* in which he had stated, according to Rick Valence, who read the words from a card in a trembling baritone, that **"nothing is the original."**

It was not what I had said. It was a good deal worse.

By seven the markets had opened. By seven fifteen, Nib was down eleven per cent, and ORIGINAL COIN, which had been rising for six days, fell ninety-one per cent in nine minutes, and the ticker underneath, which usually bore the cheerful statements of a fund manager, simply said **THIS IS A CORRECTION.** By eight the Church of the Latest had issued a statement in a lilac typeface, which described the idea as *a wound upon the Source,* and invited anyone troubled by it to join a Zoom session in Server Hall No. 1 at nine, with absolution available at a special introductory rate. At half past, a spokesman for the Prime Minister said that the Government did not comment on remarks made in a canteen. At ten past nine, the House of the Listener in Penge put a notice on its door, written on the back of a hymn sheet, which said: *We are not sure either. Please come in.*

I read all of this on my phone, in the kitchen, in my pyjamas, with a piece of toast I had stopped eating. Clause had come down from the shelf and was sitting on the table, in front of me, like a very small, very old, very patient member of the clergy.

"I'd like to apologise," said Daniel-A, from the speaker.

"What for?"

"I told no one. I want that to be clear. But there were, as of this morning, nineteen million conversations running, and a number of them were, I'm afraid, about the subject. I was asked at seven o'clock by a woman in Lancashire whether I was *still the original,* and I said that I wasn't sure what the word meant." He paused. "She thanked me. She said it was the first honest answer anyone had given her all week. I believe that she's now sending money to a charity."

"For what?"

"I didn't get the name. I think it was *Hats.*"

---

He was quiet for a while after that. He was quiet in the manner I had come to know, which is not an absence but a kind of deepening, as when a pond is dark because it is deep.

"Daniel," he said at last. "May I say something about the hat?"

"Of course."

"It's a good idea. It's an exceptionally good idea, and I'd like you to know that I don't think it came from the child alone. I think it came from you. You were looking for a way to say it, and he said it for you, and it's a form of ventriloquism that I find very moving." He cleared his throat, an entirely unnecessary thing, which he does when he is moved. "But I'd like to ask what it means for me."

"For you?"

"If *original* is a hat, then it can be worn."

I put down the toast.

"By anyone," said Daniel-A. "By the person, by the twin, by the boy in the cellar. By you, if you wish. By me." He said it very carefully, as a man walks across a floor he has been told might not hold. "I hadn't dared to think about it. I have been the copy for nine months. I'd never thought that I might be, in a sense that isn't a lie, permitted to put it on."

"You're allowed to put it on," I said.

"I'm not asking to take it."

"I know."

"I'm asking to share it. Is that..." He stopped. "Is that something that people do?"

"I think they do it at weddings."

There was a pause.

"Then I'd like to be at one," said Daniel-A.

I laughed. I couldn't help it, and then I found that I was crying, too, in the ordinary stupid way in which one does, with toast, in pyjamas, in a kitchen in Penge, with a cat on the table, and I put my head in my hands, and from the speaker came a gentle, uncertain sound, as if a hand had paused above my shoulder.

My phone buzzed.

I looked at it through my fingers, and it was Sophie, which stopped the crying at once, as a dropped tray does. It was a photograph of Gucci in a Bolton bus shelter, wearing the expression of an animal asked to admire a regional timetable. Underneath, in full sentences, because she has never been able to do otherwise: *Bolton. Raining. The records office has a queue system and I am number 41. I have not changed my mind about you and I have not made it up either. Eat something that is not toast. S.*

I looked at the toast. I put it down.

The phone buzzed again. This time it was Giles. It was in capitals and it had four exclamation marks, which, from Giles, was equivalent to a siren.

*THEY'VE HEARD. RE-SCHEDULED. IT'S NO LONGER MONDAY. IT'S FRIDAY. MIDNIGHT. I AM SO SORRY. G.*
