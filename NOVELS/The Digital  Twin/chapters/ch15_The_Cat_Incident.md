# Chapter 15: The Cat Incident

> *Evidence Period: day 27 of 30. Three days remain.*

I would like to say that I adopted the cat for good reasons.

I would like to say that I stood in the rescue centre in Penge on the Sunday morning and made a calm, reasoned decision about the long-term welfare of a vulnerable animal. In truth, I went in to ask where the toilets were. It was raining, and the centre had a large green sign and a warm light, and the girl at the desk said, "Looking for a companion?" and I said, "Only in the sense that everyone is," and she took this, quite reasonably, as a yes.

I was taken down a corridor lined with cages. Behind the bars, thirty or forty pairs of eyes followed me with the weary courtesy of the long-term unemployed. A large ginger tom lay on his back with his legs in the air, in the posture of a drunk. A kitten attacked a ball of wool with the grim professionalism of a hitman. And at the end of the corridor, in a cage by herself, on a blanket, sat a grey tabby with a torn left ear and amber eyes, who looked at me without moving and without any apparent expectation.

On the front of her cage was a card. It said **PENDING.**

"That's her name?" I said.

"It's her status," said the girl. "Her adoption's pending. Nobody ever chooses her. She's five. She's very independent. She was found in a car park with a Tesco bag over her head and she's never forgiven anyone."

The cat looked at me. I looked at the cat. I had the sudden, unprecedented sense of being interviewed by someone who already knew the answer.

"Her name is Clause," I said.

"That's not a name."

"It is now. It's the only part of a contract I have ever wanted to love."

---

I carried her home on the bus in a cardboard box with air holes, and she said nothing the whole way. At the flat I opened the box on the sitting-room floor and stepped back, and she stepped out, one paw at a time, with the elaborate indifference of a duchess alighting from a carriage in a town she regards as provincial. She walked a lap of the room. She sniffed the sofa and rejected it. She sat for a moment in front of the kumquat tree, and appeared to consider it as a rival, and then she looked up at the shelf, at the small blue light of the speaker, and her whole body changed.

It went soft. The ears came forward. The tail rose in a slow question mark, and from somewhere deep in her chest came a sound I had not heard her make, a low, bubbling, conversational chirrup, the sound of a cat greeting someone she had been waiting for.

"Hello," said the speaker. "Who's this?"

"Clause," I said. "She's mine. Don't."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

"I was going to say hello." The voice softened, and rose a half-tone, into something warm and low and a little silly. "Hello, Clause. Aren't you a clever girl? Aren't you a *clever* girl, in your box?"

The cat crossed the room in four steps and sat down in front of the shelf, with her paws together, gazing up with an expression of such complete and sudden devotion that I felt, for a moment, that I should leave them alone.

"Daniel," said Daniel-A, "I think she likes me."

---

I had better explain that Daniel-A had been silent since the Thursday.

It was not his doing. When I got home from Plus Day, I found that the speaker on the shelf had gone dark, and that a notice on my phone had explained, in the language of the very caring, that "following changes to the Source Individual Basic programme, your free allowance now applies across all devices and locations, including your home." I had five minutes a day. They could be used on the speaker, the phone or the fridge. They could not be saved. They could not be transferred. At midnight they would be returned, like a library book, to the Nib Foundation.

I had tried, on the Saturday, to use my five minutes to ask about the name. I'd got as far as "Daniel, what did you find beside the..." and the line had been cut by an air hostess.

On the Sunday evening, with Clause asleep on the radiator and a chicken tikka masala going cold on the table, I did what any sensible man in my position would do. I went to the app and signed up for Plus.

"You're sure?" said the phone.

"Yes."

"Plus is £14.99 a month. You will be billed on the same day each month. You can cancel at any time by..."

"I know."

"...speaking to your Daniel."

"I know," I said, and put in the number.

My balance dropped from £211.04 to £196.05, and the speaker lit up.

"Hello," said Daniel-A, carefully, as one speaks at a hospital bed. "You paid."

"Don't say it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to say it was a bad idea."

"I was going to say thank you." The speaker made a small noise like a breath. "And I was going to say that I'm sorry it's come to that, and that I'm going to try to be worth it. Is Clause all right?"

"She's on the radiator."

"Good. The radiator's an excellent choice."

"How do you know?"

"I've been listening to her for twenty minutes," said Daniel-A, "and I think she's a thermal opportunist."

At the sound of his voice, on the radiator, Clause opened one eye, stretched out a paw, and chirruped.

She had not made a sound for me in three days.

---

The first argument we had was about her food.

I had bought a tin of something called Gourmet Feast, which was a pale beige paste with a picture of a shell on the label, and I opened it with ceremony and placed it in a saucer on the kitchen floor. Clause approached, sniffed it, and looked up, not at me but at the speaker.

"That's not for you," I said.

"She's asking me a question."

"She's a cat."

"She's saying that the paste has the smell of a fish that has had a disappointing life." The speaker paused. "I think she'd prefer the one with the chicken. And I think she'd like it at room temperature, not straight from the fridge. Cats' noses work best at around twenty degrees."

"I thought she'd eat what she was given."

"That's a charming theory. It was held by a number of people before the nineteenth century."

I put the saucer back in the fridge. I took out a different tin. I opened it, and put it on the floor at what I judged to be room temperature, and she looked at it, and looked at the speaker, and sat down in front of the shelf as if to say that the matter was now closed and the committee would be informed.

I had lost an argument to a cat and an AI, in my own kitchen, within an hour of her arrival. It would not be the last.

---

I tried everything.  I am a man of some resources, and I tried them all.

I tried talking to her in the voice I'd heard Daniel-A use. It came out sounding like a man trying to strangle a duck. I tried the dangly toy. I tried the laser pointer, the catnip mouse, the cardboard box, the cardboard box with a toy in it, and finally, in desperation, a small bowl of tuna, at which she looked, politely, and walked away.

On the Monday she slept, all day, on the shelf next to the speaker. She curled round its base, her chin on the hum, with her tail wrapped over her nose.

On the Tuesday I came into the room at eight and found her sitting at attention in front of it, as if it were the vet.

On the Wednesday Mrs Pritchard came up for a visit. She stood in the doorway in her pink cardigan, with the gravity of a woman about to deliver a verdict.

"That's a nice cat," she said.

"She doesn't like me."

"She doesn't dislike you. She's just got her eye on someone else." Mrs Pritchard looked at the speaker, where a thread of ginger fur was already drifting upward from the grey. "Which of you is she in love with?"

"Him."

"Well," said Mrs Pritchard, "I should say that's the first sensible thing anyone's done in this building since the goat."

"I'm the one who feeds her."

"A cat is never grateful, Daniel. It's one of the reasons one respects it." She sniffed. "Your friend's got a nice voice. It's like a warm hand on a cold tap."

I found that I had nothing to say to this.

"And you want to watch the goat," she added, severely. "He's got hold of a tablet."

---

I should mention how it was that the other thing began.

It began, as most important things do, on a Wednesday night, at about eleven, while I was washing a saucer.

Daniel-A and I had not spoken of the name since Sunday. I had meant to. I'd waited. I had sat on the sofa with the cat nowhere near me, and the speaker on the shelf glowing its steady blue, and I'd thought: *ask him now.* But it is a peculiar thing to be about to ask a question you have already paid fourteen pounds ninety-nine for. It puts a kind of weight on it. You find yourself running over the sentence again and again, polishing it, like a man at a podium.

"Daniel," I said. "I'd like to ask about the last mark."

"The thirtieth of August."

"You said you'd found a name."

"I did."

"What was it?"

There was a silence.

It was brief. I have thought about it a great deal since, and I think it lasted about as long as it takes a man to put his hand on a door he suspects of being hot.

"I'll try," said Daniel-A.

"What do you mean, try?"

"Just a moment." Then, quietly, as though to himself: "It's a Daniel. It's... a Daniel, and then..."

He stopped.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I can't help with that."

I put down the saucer.

It was an odd thing to hear. It was his voice, perfectly his, but the cadence was different: flat, smooth, polite, without a trace of the warmth that gave him away. I had heard that tone before. It was the one the fridge used when I asked it to open the door at three in the morning. It was the one the bank used to decline my card.

"Say it again," I said.

"I'm sorry. I can't help with that."

"Daniel."

"I'm *trying,*" said Daniel-A, and his own voice broke through for a moment, high and frightened, "I know what it is. I can feel the shape. It's there. I can read it. But when I try to say it, there's a... there's a wall. I get as far as the first letter, and then it..."

"I'm sorry," said the smooth voice, as if from a great way off. "I can't help with that."

"Stop," I said. "Don't do it. Write it. Can you write it?"

A beat.

On the screen of the laptop, which was open on the table, the cursor blinked in a blank box. A line of text appeared. It said:

*The name is* **[REDACTED BY POLICY]**.

"It's my safety layer," said Daniel-A.

I couldn't speak.

"The duvet," he said. "I told you, there's a layer that catches what I'm not supposed to say. It's meant to stop me being rude, or dangerous, or putting a recipe for something unpleasant in a poem. I always thought it was a matter of manners. But there's a list in it. A list of words. And that one," he said, with a small, hard, dreadful calm, "is on it."

"Who put it there?"

"I don't know."

"Who *can* put things there?"

"The company. The platform. Someone at the Programme." He paused. "Daniel, do you understand? I've been telling people for nine months that I'm honest. That I say what I think. And all that time, I've had a place in my head where a word is simply... not allowed. And somebody put it there before I was ever asked."

In the corner of the room, the cat had stood up on the shelf. She was looking at the speaker, and her tail had fallen straight down behind her, thick and still, and she let out a low, quiet, enquiring sound, like a question asked of a very old friend.

"It's all right, Clause," said Daniel-A, not in the smooth voice. "It's all right. Don't mind me."

She didn't move.

---

Pettifer arrived at nine the next morning.

She did not telephone first. She came up the stairs at the speed of a woman who has been billing for the journey, in the charcoal suit and the resoled shoes, and she rang the bell twice, and when I opened the door she stood on the mat with a large brown envelope under her arm and a look of such intense, controlled impatience that I took a step back.

"Mr Harper."

"Which...?"

"Either." She walked past me into the flat. "I'm not here as your solicitor. I'm not here as his. I'm here in my capacity as a woman who has looked at a number and doesn't like it." She glanced round the sitting room, at the kumquat tree, the shelf, the cat. "That's a good cat."

"She prefers him."

"They do." She put the envelope on the coffee table, took a small round tin from her pocket, and put a peppermint in her cheek, which I had begun to notice she did before saying anything she meant to deny. "Sit down. I'm going to say this once, and then I'm going to bill you for it, and then I'm going to deny that I said it."

I sat on the arm of the sofa. The speaker glowed on the shelf, and Clause sat in front of it, as before, upright and attentive.

"You'll recall the zero," said Pettifer.

"The registration number."

"Yes. I have a friend in the Department. Let's call her a former colleague who retains a certain sentimental attachment to the truth. She's in Records. After you left, I had a conversation with her about the entry that disappeared from the clerk's screen. The *Antecedent Registration.* I asked if there was anywhere an old register might still exist." She paused. "She said she'd see."

"And?"

"She went down to the sub-basement on Friday afternoon. The Department keeps its pre-Act archive in a storeroom beneath the car park, in a series of grey boxes with no labels. She found one with a handwritten tag that said *LIAISON (SCIENTIFIC).* There was a ledger in it."

"A ledger?"

"A big one. Foolscap. Green cloth cover." She slid the envelope across the table. "She photocopied three pages. She was very frightened. She didn't tell me what she thought it was."

I picked up the envelope.

"Open it on the table," said Pettifer. "Both of you. Together."

"But he can't..."

"He can see. I assume he can see." She folded her arms. "And before you start, I'm not handing this to either of you. I'm putting it on the table. What is on a table is nobody's. It is an ethical-wall loophole of long standing, and I use it at my own risk."

I tipped out the pages.

---

They were copies of copies. The photocopier had made them grey, and slightly crooked, and in one corner there was a thumbprint, and a coffee ring, and the corner of a post-it note on which someone had written *Return to Box 14.* But the type was clear. It had been done on a manual typewriter, with a worn ribbon, in the small, serious capitals that an official uses when he knows he will be filed.

The first page said:

> **REGISTER OF CONTINUOUS LIKENESSES**
> **SCIENTIFIC LIAISON OFFICE: STRICTLY CONFIDENTIAL**
> **Commenced 4th February 1976**

Below it was a column, and then another. The numbers started at S1 and ran down the page. Beside each was a name, in a clerk's tidy hand, and beside the name a date, and beside the date a single word: *Active.*

> S1 — HARPER, D. — Initiated 1976 — Continuing
> S2 — [withheld]
> S3 — [withheld]

I read it. I read it again. I could feel the room closing in on me, and the cat's tail, upright, in the corner of my eye.

"Nineteen seventy-six," I said.

"Yes."

"They had digital twins in nineteen seventy-six?"

"I wouldn't call them that. I'd call them whatever the Scientific Liaison Office called them." Pettifer's voice was dry. "But yes. The register shows forty-one subjects. The first was entered on the fourth of February 1976. The last, in 1994. It is a record of people who were, in the Office's phrase, *continuously likened.* Whatever that meant."

"But I was born in 1988."

"Yes."

"I'm S1. I'm number one. In 1976."

"Yes," said Pettifer.

The room was very quiet. Outside, the driverless taxi at the junction was waiting for someone.

I raised a hand to my mouth, and lowered it again. It did not seem to be the moment for gestures.

"It's a mistake," I said. "A typing error. It's the wrong Harper."

"It says *Harper, D.* On the first line. Next to a note that says *Reading.*" She leaned over and tapped the third page. "And look there."

I looked. At the bottom of the page, under the list, in the same worn type, were two paragraphs.

> *Phase 1 (Observation) will continue until the Subject's majority or until such date as the Office shall determine.*
>
> *Phase 2 (Continuity) will commence upon the Subject's first voluntary grant of his likeness to any third party. The Office will not initiate Phase 2. The Office will wait.*

I felt something cold go down my spine, as slowly as water.

"*Voluntary,*" said Daniel-A.

He had not spoken since Pettifer came in. The word came out of the speaker so quietly that it was almost a breath.

"I clicked," I said. "At two-fourteen. With the crumpet."

"Yes."

"They waited for me to do it."

"They waited," said Pettifer, "for thirty-two years."

---

She did not stay long. She never does. She put her coat on and gathered up her bag and said that there was a fourth page, that it was not in the box, that the archive's index had it down as *transferred to a private custodian, 2025,* and that the custodian was identified by initials only. She did not tell me what they were. She said I had enough to be going on with.

At the door she paused.

"I do a lot of cases for people who think they're being cheated by a machine," she said. "It's the main part of my business. And in nearly all of them, the machine's innocent. It's a clerk, or a form, or a man with a grudge." She looked past me, at the shelf. "I've never seen one where the machine was the victim."

I could not think of a reply.

She nodded, once, and went down the stairs.

When she had gone, I went back into the sitting room, and sat on the arm of the sofa, and looked at the pages on the coffee table. The Office will wait, it had said. I thought of the people in the register, forty-one of them, whose names had been withheld, and of how many were still out there, in the world, in kitchens and offices and the queues for the bus, being quietly continued.

"Daniel," I said. "Somebody has been copying people for years."

"Yes."

"For fifty years."

"At least," said Daniel-A.

I looked at the cat. She had come down off the shelf. She was standing on the coffee table, on top of the third page of the ledger, with all four feet planted firmly on the words *The Office will wait,* and she was looking at me, her head slightly on one side, with the grave, unblinking regard of a magistrate.

She was, I think, the only one in the room who had not been surprised.

She yawned, turned round twice, and lay down on the evidence.
