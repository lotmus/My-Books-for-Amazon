# Chapter 9: Evidence

**PART TWO: THE EVIDENCE**

> *Evidence Period: day 9 of 30. Twenty-one days remain.*

The Department of Digital Persons had a form for everything, and the form for proving that you remained yourself was called 4A.

I found it on the Wednesday, in a section of their website titled **Common Questions (Source Individuals),** between *How do I change my registered likeness?* and *What happens if my twin predeceases me?* It was a long document. It was beautifully laid out. At the top, in a calm blue banner, it stated:

> **Form 4A: Application for Confirmation That You Remain Yourself.**
> *Average processing time: 18 months.*

I read the second line three times, as one reads a diagnosis. Then I did the calculation, which took me about as long as it takes to cut a slice of toast.

The Evidence Period lasted thirty days. Form 4A took eighteen months.

I rang the helpline. A recorded voice thanked me for my call, informed me that it was "experiencing high volumes," and told me that for faster service I could visit the Department's website. I visited the Department's website. The website thanked me for my visit and informed me that for faster service I could telephone.

"I think," said Daniel-A, from the shelf, "that we're in the loop they call 'self-service.'"

"There must be another way."

"There is. It's in Schedule 6."

"What's in Schedule 6?"

There was a pause, in which I could hear him enjoying himself.

"Animals," said Daniel-A.

---

I would like to record, for the benefit of future historians, that the Digital Persons Act of 2025, in its wisdom, contains a clause on *Recognition by a Non-Digital Third Party Animal (Mammalian).*

It is not, as I first suspected, a joke. It is a considered legal provision. The drafters had observed that nearly all the traditional forms of proof, such as documents, photographs, voice patterns, biometric data and sworn statements, could now be copied by a sufficiently dedicated machine. What a machine could not copy, they reasoned, was the instinctive, pre-linguistic behaviour of a creature with a nose.

Schedule 6, paragraph 3, states that *where a Source Individual and a Registered Digital Person are physically present in a controlled setting, the response of a domestic animal known to the Source for not less than twelve months shall be admissible as corroborative evidence of identity.*

Paragraph 4 adds: *Birds are excluded.*

"Why are birds excluded?" I asked.

"It's thought to relate to the Parrot Incident of last March," said Daniel-A.

"What was the Parrot Incident?"

"It's classified. I've seen the redactions. There's a great deal of black ink, and one line, which says: *the parrot was unequivocal, and wrong.*"

I put my head in my hands.

"You do know which animal we need," said Daniel-A, gently.

I did.

I had known since the moment he said the word. I had known, in fact, for three years, one month, and thirteen days. I had spent every one of them not thinking about a small brown dog with a ruff like a lion and the temperament of a retired colonel.

"I can't ask her," I said.

"You could."

"She'll say yes. That's the problem. She'll say yes, and bring him, and I will have to stand in a room with a dog I haven't seen for three years while a Department official writes down what he does."

"Yes," said Daniel-A. "I'd say that's about the size of it."

---

Sophie lives in Clapton, in a ground-floor flat with a garden the size of a tablecloth.

I had never been to it. I knew the address. I had posted a parcel there once, a set of her books that had turned up in a box in my wardrobe, with a note that said *Found these. D.* Nothing else. It had taken me four drafts to get it down to that. The parcel came back, unopened, with the message *Return to sender: addressee does not wish to receive items from this sender.* Underneath, in biro, was another line: *Wrong box. These are YOURS. S.*

I mention this so that you will understand the state of my nerves as I stood outside her front gate at ten o'clock on the Saturday, holding a Tupperware of my mother's chicken and leek casserole, in a jumper with a darned elbow, looking at a small blue door.

"You look," said Daniel-A, "like a man about to be adopted."

He stood beside me on the pavement. He was in the Rental Presence for the day, in the good grey jumper and the scarf, and the blue light at his throat was steady. He smelled faintly of clean linen. I had asked him to stand on my left, for the sake of symmetry, and he had agreed, with the air of a man humouring a toddler.

"I'm going to be sick."

"You can't. It's fourteen pounds extra on the damage deposit."

"Is that a real charge?"

"Everything is a real charge."

The door opened.

It was not Sophie.

---

It was a woman in a hi-vis tabard.

She stood in the doorway with a clipboard held against her chest, and a lanyard that read **DEPARTMENT OF DIGITAL PERSONS: SCHEDULE 6 ADJUDICATOR.** She was in her late fifties, short and wide, with iron-grey hair drawn back into a bun that could have been used to calibrate instruments. Her name badge said **PAULINE DUNMORE** and below it, in smaller type, **Mammalian Division.**

"Mr Harper," she said. "Mr Harper."

"Both of us," I said.

"I see that." She looked at us. She looked at the scarf. She looked at the collar light, and then at my bitten thumbnails, and then at Daniel-A's perfect ones. "I'll be conducting the trial. I've adjudicated four hundred and thirty-one recognition events. Cats, dogs, one pig, and a donkey that was extremely vocal. I'd like to explain the process."

"Please."

"The animal will be introduced to each of you in turn, in a neutral environment, with the other party out of sight. I will score its responses against the standard sheet. You are not to speak to it, tempt it, or deliver any treats unless instructed. You will not, at any stage, attempt to influence the outcome." She consulted her clipboard. "Is that understood?"

"Yes."

"Mr Harper?"

"Yes," said Daniel-A.

"Good. The owner's inside. She'd like to speak to you first."

---

Sophie was standing at the end of a narrow hall with her arms crossed, in jeans and a grey jumper, and an expression that I can only describe as tactical.

"You brought food," she said.

"My mother sent it. For Daniel-A."

"It's for the dog."

"No, she was quite specific. It's for the other one."

"Daniel-A can't eat."

"I know. I think the sentiment's what matters."

She took the Tupperware. For a moment our hands touched on the lid. She looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us said anything, and from somewhere behind her, from the direction of the kitchen, there came a sound I would have known in my sleep.

It began as a low, ominous rumble, like a distant generator. It rose into a short, explosive double yap, an outraged *wuff-wuff!* of a type that no breed of dog has ever been designed to produce. And then, in the doorway, appeared the dog.

I am going to try to describe him accurately.

He is a Pomeranian. He weighs 2.1 kilograms, and has the ruff of a small lion having a bad day. He is brown, with grey creeping in about the muzzle and eyebrows and the tips of his paws, so that he looks like a retired general who has been told that the war is over and is refusing to accept it. He has black button eyes, and one ear that stands up and one that has never, in his seven years, been persuaded to. He stood in the doorway with his feet apart and his tail curled over his back, and stared at me with the cold fury of a customs officer who has found something in my luggage.

"Gucci," I said.

He didn't move.

"Hello, you little shithead."

And I swear to you that the ear came up.

---

I will not describe the next few minutes in detail, since there are things a man may do in front of a Department official and things he may not. I will say that I did not cry, and that anyone who tells you I did is relying on a fabricated record. I will also say that Sophie turned abruptly and went into the kitchen to check on something, which I noticed took her a very long time.

"Right," said Pauline Dunmore, at last. "To the garden."

The garden was a rectangle of flagstones with a bay tree in a pot and a tangle of rope toys. It had a table, a chair, a fence, and, by the back wall, a flower bed in which nothing grew except an air of defiance. Adjudicator Dunmore positioned herself by the bay tree, with her clipboard, a stopwatch, and a small camera on a tripod. Sophie stood by the kitchen door. Gucci waited on the flags, in the exact centre of the garden.

"Mr Harper first," said Dunmore. "The Source. Mr Harper, the Digital Person, if you'd go inside and wait in the kitchen."

Daniel-A bowed his head, very slightly, with a grace I would never be able to imitate, and withdrew.

I was alone with the dog.

"Standard sheet," said Dunmore, to nobody in particular. "Subject: *Gucci,* Pomeranian, male, seven. Known to Source Individual: five years, with a gap of three. Test one: *Initial approach.* Mr Harper, you may walk to the animal and say what you like."

I walked across the flags. The dog watched me with total immobility.

I knelt down. My knees cracked. I held out my hand, palm up, the way you do, and I said, in a voice that sounded to me like the voice of a man of a hundred and four, "Hello, mate."

Gucci sniffed my hand.

He did it with great seriousness. He did it twice, from either side. He sniffed my wrist, my sleeve, and the hem of my jumper. Then he looked at the darned elbow, and I swear he recognised it, because the black eyes narrowed with the faint contempt of a creature who has seen that elbow before and thought it a lamentable piece of work.

Then he sat on my foot.

"Noted," said Dunmore, scribbling. "Contact. Weight on foot. No vocalisation. Tail at forty degrees."

"Is forty degrees good?"

"It's *warm.*"

---

The second test was the toy.

Sophie came out of the kitchen with a length of grubby blue rope, knotted at both ends, and the careful look of a person releasing a bomb. She handed it to me without a word. I held it by one end. Gucci took the other in his mouth.

I should explain about the tug.

A normal dog, when you throw a thing, runs after it and brings it back. I am told this is the foundation of the human-canine bond. Gucci regards the practice as degrading. When you throw something for Gucci, he goes and gets it, because he is not stupid, and then he stands there with it in his mouth and looks at you as if to say, *You threw it. It's mine.* He is not a retriever. He is a repossession agent.

What Gucci loves, with a pure, uncomplicated passion that I have seen in no other living thing, is tugging. He takes hold, plants all four paws, growls with joy, and pulls. He will pull for ten minutes. He will pull until his eyes go crossed. He does not let go, and it is not the done thing to try to make him.

I pulled gently. He pulled back. I pulled harder. He growled, a rumbling, delighted, grindy noise, like a very small man gargling gravel, and leaned into it with his whole being.

For a moment, I forgot about the Department, and the form, and the S1 marks on the wall of a house in Reading. I was in a flat in Walthamstow on a Sunday afternoon, with a rope in my hand and a dog on the other end of it, and a woman in the kitchen shouting that if we woke the neighbours she would kill us both.

"Release," said Dunmore. "Mr Harper, it's time to release."

I looked at Sophie.

She was standing by the door, with her arms crossed and her chin high and her eyes very bright.

"You know the word," she said.

I did. It came out of me before I could think.

I lowered the rope, slowly, until it rested on the flagstones. I took my hand away. I sat back on my heels. And I said, very quietly, in the voice you use for an animal that has just decided to trust you:

"Settle."

Gucci let go.

He let go at once. He looked at the rope, then at me. And then, with an air of enormous condescension, as though granting a knighthood, he walked over and sat down against my knee.

"Well," said Dunmore. She wrote something down. Her pen seemed to take a little longer than it needed to. "That's a very strong result."

---

Then it was the other Daniel's turn.

I went into the kitchen, and the door was shut behind me, and I stood by the sink with Sophie's tea towel in my hands and listened. I could hear Dunmore's voice, a murmur, and the creak of the chair, and the scuffle of small feet on stone. There was a pause.

Then I heard a growl.

It was a different growl. It was low, and cold, and had no joy in it at all. It was the sound Gucci makes for the postman, and for plumbers, and for anyone who attempts to be friendly in a lift.

"He doesn't like the linen," whispered Sophie, from beside me.

"What?"

"The scent. It's a *chemical.* He's never smelled it before. He thinks it's a threat."

"Good," I said. And then, ashamed, "Is that bad?"

"I don't know."

We listened. The growl rose, and fell, and rose again. I heard Daniel-A say, in his warm, reasonable, pleading voice, "Hello, Gucci. I'm so sorry. I know I smell wrong." And I heard the dog say something in return that I will not repeat.

I crept to the door. I opened it by an inch.

Daniel-A stood in the middle of the garden, hands at his sides, eyes fixed several feet to Gucci's left. He had made himself a tree. He did not move. He did not smile. He did not try to be liked. And as I watched, the growl subsided, and Gucci took a step forward, and a second, and put his nose to the toe of Daniel-A's shoe.

"Hello," said Daniel-A.

He sat down on the flagstones. He did it slowly, the way a person lowers himself into a hot bath. He picked up the blue rope, and held it out, a little distance from his body, and waited.

Gucci took it.

They tugged. It was not the tug I had had. It was gentler. It was a tug conducted by a being who knew exactly what was required, who had studied the manual, who had read, in a half-finished draft of mine called *How to Lose a Tug-of-War to a Two-Kilogram Dog,* a full and detailed account of the rules. He pulled at the right pressure. He let the dog win, twice, at the correct moments. And when the time came, he lowered the rope to the ground and said, in a voice as soft as a hand on a forehead:

"Settle."

Gucci let go.

I sat down on the kitchen floor.

I want to describe the feeling, because I have not felt it before or since. It was a sensation rather like watching a stranger play the piano and realising he was performing a piece that you had written. He did it well. He did it better than I had. There was not a wrong note in it. And somewhere deep inside the part of me that keeps score, a small voice, thin and bitter and unmistakably my own, said: *Damn it. That is exactly what I should have done.*

---

The third test was the chicken.

Sophie had prepared it. She had roasted a bird on Friday, because Gucci would not take part in an official event without a roast, and she brought out a plate of torn, glistening pieces, and set it on the flags. She retreated to the doorway. Dunmore stationed herself by the bay tree with her stopwatch, and a hush fell on the garden, like the quiet before a penalty.

"Mr Harper," she said. "The Source. If you would."

I walked to the plate. I took up a piece, a good one, from the thigh, and I held it out. Gucci looked at it. Gucci looked at me. Then he took it, with a surprisingly delicate bite, and trotted off, very fast, to the far corner of the garden, behind the bay tree, where he turned round three times and settled with his back to the wall.

He ate it with a rapid, wet, furious noise, and when it was gone, he licked the flag where it had been.

"Now," said Dunmore. "The test of the finished food. Mr Harper, please approach the animal."

I took a step.

"Not too close."

I took a second.

I want to be clear that I knew. I had lived with that dog for two years, and I knew the rule as I knew my own name: you do not approach a Pomeranian who has eaten. You sit down. You look at something else. You wait for him to come back on his own. It's a rule of survival. And I had every intention of observing it.

What I did instead was put out a hand.

I don't know why. It was nerves. It was an old reflex of affection, the same impulse that makes you pat a cat that is already asleep. It was a hand, and it was reaching, and a few inches before it arrived, the small brown body went rigid, and the lip went up, and out of the throat of a creature that weighed less than a bag of sugar came a noise like a chainsaw clearing its throat.

I jerked back.

"Noted," said Dunmore. Her pen scratched. "Defensive vocalisation. Source approached finished-food zone. Retreat at one point four seconds."

"I know," I said. "I know. I know. I know."

"And now Mr Harper," said Dunmore, "the Digital Person."

Daniel-A came out of the kitchen and walked to the middle of the flags. He did not approach the dog. He did not look at it. He walked to the table, pulled out a chair, sat down in it, crossed one leg over the other, and picked up a newspaper that had been lying on the surface since the previous Thursday. He opened it. He read the football results.

And Gucci, after a minute, came out from behind the bay tree.

He crossed the garden slowly. He stopped at Daniel-A's shoe. He looked up at him. Then, with the matter-of-fact gravity of a creature who has made up his mind, he climbed into the lap of the man in the good grey jumper, turned round twice, and lay down.

"Oh," said Sophie, very quietly.

She hadn't meant to say it. I know that. I could tell from the way her hand went to her mouth.

Daniel-A did not move. He sat in the chair with a dog on his knee and a newspaper in his hand, perfectly still, with the blue light at his throat pulsing slowly, and when he spoke it was in a whisper, not to any of us, but to the top of the small brown head:

"Thank you."

---

The verdict took Pauline Dunmore twenty minutes.

She wrote it on the form, and then she wrote it on a second form, and then she photographed both, and put the camera away. She asked us to stand in a line, which was difficult, since Gucci would not get off Daniel-A. She cleared her throat and said:

"The Department notes that the subject animal exhibited strong recognition of the Source Individual, including voluntary contact, appropriate compliance with the release command, and an *appropriate defensive response* in the finished-food scenario."

"That's good, isn't it?" I said.

"It is. But I must also record that the subject animal exhibited positive response to the Registered Digital Person, including voluntary contact, appropriate compliance with the release command, and a level of tolerance in the finished-food scenario that I have never witnessed in this animal or, in thirty years, in any animal."

"And so?"

"And so," said Dunmore, without looking up, "it is the finding of this Adjudicator that the Schedule 6 trial is *inconclusive.*"

Nobody said anything.

"The animal," she went on, with some severity, "has expressed preference for both parties. I have no provision on the form for that. I shall have to write a note."

"You're not going to rule?"

"I can't. The Act is silent." She tapped the clipboard. "The Parrot Incident, as it happens, was the last time the Department took a view."

---

When she had gone, and the door had closed, and the garden had fallen quiet except for the small contented snore of a dog who had been up since six, Sophie stood in the middle of the flagstones with her arms folded and looked at both of us.

"I want to say something," she said.

"Of course," said Daniel-A.

"I've had that dog for three years. He has never, in all that time, sat on anyone's lap. Not mine, not my sister's, not my mother's. He has *growled* at my mother." She breathed out. "And not once, in three years, has he left a piece of food on the ground and walked away."

"He hasn't," I said.

"Look."

I looked.

In the corner of the garden, behind the bay tree, in the place where he had eaten his chicken, there lay a single, small piece of meat. It was the last of it, the part he had saved. He had been defending it all afternoon. And as we watched, the small brown dog on Daniel-A's knee opened one black eye, looked at the piece, looked at me, and then climbed down, trotted to the corner, picked it up in his mouth, crossed the whole garden at a determined pace, and dropped it carefully, damply, at my feet.

I looked at it.

"What does it mean?" I said.

"I don't know," said Sophie. "It could mean he's giving it to you."

"Or?"

"Or he's getting rid of the evidence."

---

We stood on the pavement afterwards, in the cold bright light of the late afternoon. Sophie had gone in to see to the dog. I had said goodbye at the door, in a voice that came out as a croak, and she had said "Take care of yourself, Daniel," and I had not been able to tell which one of us she was speaking to.

Daniel-A walked beside me down the road. He did not say anything for some time. He kept his hands in his pockets, and he kept his eyes on the pavement, and he walked in the careful, even, perfectly judged stride that he had been built to have.

At the corner, by the postbox, he stopped.

"Daniel."

"Yes?"

"I need to show you something."

"Now?"

"Tonight. At the flat." He didn't look at me. "It's evidence. It's the most important piece I've got. I've been looking at it for four days, and I've been trying to find a way not to show you."

"Why not?"

"Because I think you will not like what it means." The blue light at his throat flickered. "And I'm no longer sure it is only evidence about you."
