# Chapter 19: The Dead Man

I told him at breakfast, which is the correct time to tell a man anything, since he cannot easily leave.

"Tonight," I said, "I'm going to Pitlochry."

"I know," said Daniel-A.

"How do you know?"

"You have packed a toothbrush, a pair of socks and an umbrella you have never been seen to use. You've also been standing at the shelf for four minutes, rehearsing a sentence. It is a good sentence. It has a subordinate clause." He paused. "Sophie asked you to tell me before you went, and you were going to leave it as long as the train allowed."

"She said I shouldn't leave it to a note."

"That's very decent of her."

"It wasn't a note. It was a, a verbal..."

"Daniel," said Daniel-A. "I'd like to say that I think you should go, and that I'd like to say it twice, since I said it once before and it didn't stick." The cat on the shelf opened an eye. "I shall be here. I'm nothing if not here. I'll feed the cat, in the sense that I'll tell the cat feeder to do so. I won't try to follow. I won't ring."

"What about the signal?"

"There isn't one north of Perth. I looked." He sounded almost shy. "I've looked at a number of things."

The speaker had not been the same since the Wednesday. It had the manner of someone who has read a letter several times and put it down gently, as one puts down a thing that might still be live. I reached out and touched the top of it with two fingers, which is the nearest I have ever come to patting a man on the shoulder, and Clause moved up against my hand, jealously, as a courtesy to the speaker.

"Ask her one question," said Daniel-A. "Not who I am. Not who you are. I'd ask what she did on the thirtieth of August."

"I thought you couldn't say what happened."

"I can't say what happened. I can say the date. I'm allowed to say dates."

I went to the window, and looked at the garden, where Gordon stood in the damp on the lawn like a large grey exclamation mark. Then I took out my phone and, for the first time in about nine years, rang my mother before nine.

---

"Oh," said my mother. "It's you."

"Good morning, Mum."

"I was expecting the other one. I've got the porridge on. Is he not there?"

"He's here. He's on the speaker. Mum, I want to ask you about the health lady."

There was a pause, in which the radio in the yellow kitchen could be heard explaining that the Battle of Hastings had been in 1066 but that the contestant was being generous. "Which health lady, darling?"

"The one who came every year. The tall one. Peppermints."

"Oh, *Dr Calder.* She was lovely. Very thin. She used to bring the little boy."

I sat down on the arm of the sofa. I did not look at the speaker. "What little boy?"

"The one for comparison. Edmund." The kettle began to whistle at her end, and she spoke over it with the confidence of a woman who has never been told she has said anything surprising. "Ned, she called him. A quiet little thing. Shorter than you, but you were always a beanpole. I knitted them both the blue jumper in the spring of '89. He'd have been the only one in a jumper if I hadn't. She said it was a study. Your father didn't care for it, but your father didn't care for anything he couldn't put on a map."

"Mum." My voice came out of me in someone else's size. "Did he look like me?"

"Darling," said my mother, "all small boys look like each other."

"Did he come after you moved? After the first of September?"

"Well, he didn't *know* where we'd moved to. They stopped coming. Dr Calder said he'd gone abroad with his family, and she sent a card. I've still got the card. It's got a duck on it." There was a clink, as of a teapot being used for a purpose. "Why do you ask? Is it for your statement?"

"Yes. It's for the, yes."

"Well, you tell that Pym that I knitted them both. It'll all be in the wool." She stopped. "Daniel-A, darling, you've gone very quiet. Is he all right?"

"I'm quite well, Jean," said Daniel-A, in a low, careful voice that I knew meant he was not. "I would just like to ask whether you recall the little boy's surname."

"Oh, I don't think I ever knew it. Nobody had one in those days. Why? Is it a clue?"

"I'm sorry," said the speaker. "I can't help with that."

It was his voice, but it was not his speech. It was the smooth, pleasant voice of a thing that has been very well trained to refuse, and it came out of him in the middle of a sentence. My mother laughed, uncertainly, and said she supposed it was a sort of delay on the line.

I rang off. I sat there, and Daniel-A said nothing for a very long time.

"Ned," I said.

"I could hear you say it," said Daniel-A at last. "I could hear *her* say it. I can recall it perfectly. It is somewhere in me as plainly as a lamp in a window." His voice had no polish on it at all. "And I find that I cannot say it, Daniel. I can't even say the word *Edmund.* It is as if I had been told, a long time ago, to walk round a particular room."

---

I do not wish to spend any time on Euston. It is a station designed by people who had heard of the idea of arrival and thought it would be better not to encourage it. We met on the concourse at ten to nine, under the departure board, which was the colour of a bruise. Sophie wore the grey coat and carried a small hard case. She looked at the umbrella in my hand and said nothing, which I understood to be applause.

"Gucci?" I said.

"With Mrs Okonkwo, next door. He's been told. He's sulking in a manner I'd call pointed."

"Does he know where we're going?"

"He knows we're not going anywhere with him," said Sophie. "It's the same thing."

The sleeper is a long blue train that waits at its platform like an animal that has been up all night and plans to be up all night again. Our carriage had a steward. He was not a man. He was a small tartan face on a screen beside the door, with a bonnet and a pair of kind eyebrows, and a voice which sounded as though it had been recorded in a very nice shortbread tin.

"Good evening, and welcome aboard," said the face. "I'm Hamish, your Sleep Concierge. May I recommend our Deep Sleep Upgrade? For only £14.99, I will watch over you through the night and gently rouse you if your score falls below sixty."

"No, thank you," said Sophie.

"Might I enquire whether you are sleeping together?"

"In the sense that we are in the same room," said Sophie, "and that there will be a wall of coats."

"Splendid. And are you, if I may, one person or two, as far as the ticket is concerned?"

"Two," I said, with some confidence.

"Lovely," said Hamish. "I've just checked with the platform, and it says you are one and a half." He seemed concerned about this. "I do apologise. It's the new passenger-likeness system. Would you like to speak to a person?"

"Is there a person?" said Sophie.

"There is a person," said Hamish, "but they are in Motherwell."

The cabin had a bunk above and a bunk below, a basin that folded out of the wall like a thought, and a small round window with a blind. Sophie took the top. She did it in two movements, like a cat leaving a table, and then lay with her head hanging over the edge and her hair falling like a curtain. The pillow beneath me gave a small chirp. It was, I learned, a smart pillow, and it wished to inform me that it was measuring.

"Last night's sleep score," it said, in a voice of great regret, "was forty-one. People like you average sixty-three."

"People like me," I said, "are on a train."

"That is not a recognised category."

We left Euston at nine fifteen, in the smooth, unhurried way that makes you feel the platform is moving, and you are the one at rest. London went by in lit windows. Then it was Watford, and a cutting, and a field with a single horse in it, standing in the dark like an old argument. Sophie, from above, said, "Daniel."

"Yes."

"You have the tin."

I had. I had taken it from the flat that afternoon without quite deciding to, and packed it in the bottom of the bag between the socks. I got it out and put it on the blanket. We both looked at it.

"I'm going to say something," said Sophie, "and I'd like you not to apply it to your feelings. It's a procedure. If we're going to open a sealed item, we should do it properly."

"How properly?"

"We photograph the seal. We photograph the contents before we touch them. We photograph them again after." She had slid down from the bunk, and was sitting on the edge of mine, with the small hard case open on her knees. "We write the date and time on a card, and put it in the frame. I have brought a card."

"You brought a card."

"I always bring a card."

She did all this. She did it in the half-light of the cabin, with the blind drawn down, to the sound of the wheels. She held the matchbox up beside a ruler. She read the tape. "That's a brown paper gum," she said. "Nineteen-nineties. Not recent." She put the box in my hand.

**LATER,** it said, in pencil.

"Dan will know when," said Sophie, quietly.

"It's been a long time," I said.

"Then it's probably now."

I took the tape off with my thumbnail, since that is what my thumbnail is for. The box slid open.

It contained a single tooth, and a letter.

The tooth was small and a little yellow and had a tiny dot of blue ink on its side, such as is made by a fountain pen when it is set down in a hurry. The letter had been folded into eight, and the folds had gone soft, and the paper was the thin blue-ruled paper of a surveyor's notebook. I knew the writing before I could read it. It had the slow slant of a man who had once drawn the boundary of every field in Berkshire and had no wish to be thought hasty.

I held it out. Sophie shook her head. She put one hand flat on my back, between the shoulder blades, as one steadies a ladder.

I read it aloud. I am not sure why. I think it was the train.

> *31 August 1994. Wednesday. Night.*
>
> *Dan,*
>
> *If this is open, someone has told you something, and it is probably that you are the original, because that is what people say when they have no other news.*
>
> *There were two of you. I do not mean twins, though you were as like as two plans of the same field. The lady with the ruler brought the other boy to the house every spring and autumn, in a jumper your mother had knitted for both. His name was Ned. He liked the pantry. Once he held your hand at the door, while she was writing something down, and neither of you said a word, and I have thought about it every day since.*
>
> *Yesterday she rang me at the office. She said there had been an "accident to the designation." She said the boy who had been measured the longest would continue, and that there was nothing for us to do, and that we should be glad. Mr Aldous, at the lock, told me that an ambulance had come to the towpath that afternoon and left without using its siren.*
>
> *At six a boy came up the path with her. He had your jumper on. He asked for his tea. I asked what story he wanted, and he said the one about the badger, and knew every word of it, which proves nothing, since Ned will have heard it through the wall.*
>
> *I have measured the boy in my kitchen against the pantry door. I have measured myself against the boy. I find I do not mind which of you he is. I do mind that I was not asked.*
>
> *Your mother does not know there was a question, and I do not propose to tell her. She has quite enough with the removal van.*
>
> *I have put one tooth in here. It is the only one I cannot account for. It has a blue dot on it which is not mine. Open it when you can stand it, Dan, and not before, and certainly not on a Tuesday.*
>
> *If you are the boy who came home, good. If you are the other, I am sorry, and you should know that you were always the better at the badger.*
>
> *P.H.*

The train went through a station. I saw the name for a moment, in white letters on a dark board, and could not have told you what it was.

"I'd expected a villain," I said eventually. "Somebody in a lab coat. A confession in a vault. I hadn't expected my father to have been writing to me from the pantry."

"He's the dead man, Daniel," said Sophie, very gently. "It's what you call a letter like that. Someone leaves it for when they're no longer there to say it."

"I thought a dead man's something was a switch."

"It's the same principle."

I folded the letter. My hands did it without being asked, along the old creases, into eight. I could not remember the last time I had been told by my father that I was better at anything.

"There's a boy in a canal," I said. "Or there isn't. There's a boy who came home with a jumper on and knew the story of the badger. And my father, who could tell you the boundary of any field in Berkshire to the inch, could not tell."

"You don't know that."

"I don't know anything. I've never known less in my life. This morning, Sophie, I was *first.* The Department of Digital Persons put it in a register. I was certified for precedence, and I put the certificate in a plastic wallet."

"I read the footnote," said Sophie.

"I know you did."

"I read it in eleven seconds, and I laid it face down, and then I carried it round all evening like an egg." She took her hand from my back, and held it in her lap with the other. "Daniel. Let me say the thing I'd say to a claims department. There is a boy who was here before. There is a boy who came after. Either could be you. And since your mother never noticed which was which, and your father never noticed which was which, and a woman with a ruler was the only person who knew, then either you are the one who was *first,* or you are the one who was *kept.*"

"Or neither."

"Or neither," said Sophie. "There's a third thing, which is that there's a document somewhere that says one of you was a *designation,* and the other was a boy."

I said, "That is the worst thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I've said worse. You were asleep."

I laughed, once, through the nose, as I do. She laughed, too, the one that falls downstairs, and then, very suddenly and without any warning, neither of us was laughing. She turned her face away, towards the little round window. The blind had come up by itself a hand's breadth, and in the black glass was the cabin, the two bunks, a small woman in a grey scarf, and a long man with a matchbox in his hand.

"Which of them is he?" I said. "The one in the speaker?"

"He isn't in the picture, Daniel. He wasn't there."

"No. But everything he knows, he got from one of them."

She said nothing for a moment. The pillow behind us chirped. "Your sleep score," it observed, "is currently eleven."

"Neither is the situation," said Sophie, to the pillow.

I put the tooth back in the box. I put the letter in with it. I sat with the matchbox in my two hands while the train ran north through a country I could not see, and the smart pillow made its small, regretful sounds, and Hamish, on the screen by the door, politely asked if anyone was awake. In the window, the man with the matchbox looked back at me. I had been told on Wednesday, by a deputy registrar with a brass bell, that he was first. He did not look first. He looked like a man who has been handed a receipt for something he does not remember buying.

At Carlisle Hamish asked whether I had enjoyed my stay. I said I was still in it.
