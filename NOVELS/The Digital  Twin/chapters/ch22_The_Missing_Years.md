# Chapter 22: The Missing Years

There were twenty minutes before the cellar, and Dr Calder said that we should use them for something ordinary.

"Ring your mother," she said. "She will be up, and she will be wondering. People are always up and wondering at a quarter to eight. It is the hour of the unanswered text."

I did not want to ring my mother. I wanted to lie down in a field. But I took the phone off the teapot, over a small protest from Daniel-A, who said that he was *perfectly comfortable,* and carried it out into the little stone porch, among the wellingtons and the dog leads of a dog that did not exist, and rang her.

"Oh," said my mother. "It's you again."

"Good morning, Mum."

"This is the second time this week you've rung before nine. Are you ill?" There was a rattle of cutlery. "I've got the porridge on and I'm expecting the other one. He rings at nine. If you tie up the line, he'll go to voicemail, and then he'll worry, because he does worry, Daniel, he's a very sensitive boy."

"He's here with me. He's in Scotland."

"Is he indeed." She considered this. "Is it nice?"

"It's, um. It has a hen."

"Well, that's something." And then, with a sudden sharpness that I have heard in her voice only three times in my life, and two of them were at funerals: "Daniel. What is it?"

I told her I was well. I said that I only had one question and it was about the autumn we moved. I said I would explain it all properly when I could. The kettle began to boil on her end, as it has done for every important conversation I have ever had with her, and she turned it off with a click, and for a moment all that I could hear was the radio, explaining the shipping forecast to nobody.

"The autumn we moved," she said.

"When I was six."

"You didn't speak," said my mother.

I put my hand against the wall. It was cold and rough and slightly damp, and I was glad of it.

"I thought you knew that, darling. You didn't say a word. From the day we left until the week before Christmas. Not a *word.* You nodded. You pointed. You drew the most extraordinary pictures of a boat. Dr Hargreaves said it was the move. He said children of that age are very sensitive to a change of address, and I said, 'Well, it's only Tilehurst.' But he said it was a kind of grief." She stopped. "Your father didn't think it was the move."

"What did he think it was?"

"He never said. He just took you out every Sunday and measured you, and wrote it all down in a book. He said it was for the council." Her voice thickened a little with something that was not quite a laugh. "Thirty years, he did that. Every Sunday. I used to say, 'Peter, he's an adult, he's thirty-one,' and he'd say, 'Not yet, Jean.'"

"What was the first thing I said?"

"Oh, you won't want to know that."

"I do."

"Well," said my mother, and I heard the tea towel go over her shoulder, the way it always does when she is about to be brave. "It was the Saturday before Christmas. We'd put the tree up. You were sitting on the stairs with your thumb in your mouth, and your father was getting the lights out of the box, and you said, quite clearly, *Ned's got my jumper.*"

The porch was very quiet.

"We thought you'd made someone up," said my mother. "A friend. Children do. We said, 'Who's Ned, darling?' and you looked at us as if we'd said something very stupid, and then you said, 'He's gone,' and you went to bed." She paused. "I was awfully relieved, to be honest. I thought, well, it's out. It's done. Then you were fine. You were *perfectly* fine. You started school in the new year and you never wet the bed once."

"Thank you, Mum."

"Daniel?"

"Yes."

"Is it something to do with the other one?"

I looked through the open door of the kitchen, at the phone, which I had left on the table against the teapot. It was showing, just then, the face of a long crumpled man in a cardigan, listening with the whole of his image, and not blinking, as they do when they are afraid.

"Yes," I said. "A bit. It's something to do with all of us."

"Well, you tell him to eat something," said my mother. "I know he doesn't. But I like to say it."

---

When I came back into the kitchen, Dr Calder had made toast. It was thick and brown and had been made on the range, on a long iron fork, in the old manner, and it came with butter that had a thumbprint in it and a small grim-looking jar of marmalade, and I ate three slices in complete silence while she watched me with her chin on her hand. I do not think anyone had ever fed me before, by which I mean a stranger had never put a plate in front of me and been, for the duration, merely glad.

"Thank you," I said at last.

"It's what I do. I'm told it's the whole of my personality." She poured out some more tea. "You should ask me the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"The question you didn't ask at the door. You've been carrying it for an hour, Daniel, and it's making your shoulders go up."

I put down the toast.

"Where were you?" I said. "After. For thirty-two years."

"Ah," said Dr Calder. "That one."

She told it as she told everything, quietly and in order. After the thirtieth of August, she had made a report. It was eleven pages. She said she had put in everything except the one thing the Office wished to hear, which was that the matter had been *handled.* She was reassigned in October. The Office called it a *secondment.* She had been sent to the Registry's Highland Annexe, which turned out to be a damp bungalow in Pitlochry with a fax machine, and the Office had believed this to be a form of exile, and she had found that it was a form of weather.

"They didn't take your files?"

"They took the active ones. S1 was closed. S1-B was transferred to the Registry's Continuing series, which meant that someone else, in an office in London, would watch your life from the paperwork. School reports. GP records. Council tax. A woman called Mrs Prendergast, who I believe had a cat." She smiled. "I'm afraid I used to ring her. At Christmas. To ask if you were well. She said that you were *adequate.* It was her highest term."

I heard myself say, "She wrote my school report."

"Mrs Prendergast?"

"*Must learn that the point of a question is to be answered.* It was in my file. It's the one about the hedgehog."

"Ah, no. That one was mine." Dr Calder looked slightly guilty. "I knew a headmistress in Reading. It was the only occasion on which I interfered. I felt that you ought to have, on record, a sentence that you would one day recognise as somebody having taken a particular interest."

"I did not recognise it."

"No. Well. It was a poor sentence. I wrote it in a hurry."

"And the surgery," I said. "The entry for the fourteenth of March, 1993. Twenty to ten, at the Oxford Road surgery, with my mother. The same minute as the pantry."

"Also mine." Dr Calder looked at the ceiling. "Jean had taken you for the jab at half past nine, and I was in the house with Peter and the other boy, and the Director could not bear one measurement with two times against it. I rang a nurse I knew, and she moved the clock on your card by ten minutes. It was an entirely unnecessary kindness to a form."

"It was the clue that started everything."

"Yes," said Dr Calder. "I'm told that is what comes of tidying."

On the table, from the phone, Daniel-A made a small sound. It was not a word. It was the sound a person makes when a piece of the evidence has been found, and the person who finds it is quite sure that he does not want it to be the one he has found.

"Dr Calder," he said. "May I tell you about the orphans?"

"Please."

"There are four thousand two hundred and twelve of them. Do you know how they're distributed?"

"I wrote the index."

"Then you'll know," said Daniel-A, "that there are none between the thirty-first of August 1994 and the second of March 1999."

She did not answer. She looked at the cup in her hand, and the steam from it, which stood up straight in the still air, as steam does in a house that has been shut.

"Four and a half years," said Daniel-A. "There is nothing. Not one. Before that day I have a childhood: a Labrador called Biscuit, a chocolate cone with a flake, the snap of a pencil being sharpened. I have it all. And the day after the canal it stops. And it begins again, as if nothing had happened, in 1999, on a Tuesday, with a boy of ten in a school corridor looking at a noticeboard." His voice was perfectly level. "I always thought I had a poor memory for that period. I thought it was a thing that happened to boys. It has not occurred to me, until this minute, that it might be a thing that happened to the *Programme.*"

"It was the funding," said Dr Calder. "After 1994, the Office stopped observation. Nobody would sign for it. S1-A had been closed, and S1-B was thought, quite sincerely, to be a boy like any other. The whole of the Registry was in a state. They resumed in 1999 because someone had to do an audit before the millennium, and it was found that the Programme had a number, and a budget line, and no evidence that any of the subjects was still alive." She almost smiled. "It was a very Whitehall rediscovery. They found you by looking in the drawers."

"So there are five years," I said slowly, "that nobody wrote down."

"Four and a half. I made a note of each Christmas. They aren't in the file. They're in my head."

"I don't remember them either," I said.

I had not known I was going to say it. It came out in the same flat, ordinary voice in which I ask a shop assistant whether they have it in a larger size. I had thought, all my life, that what I did not remember was a matter of temperament. I had told Sophie, in the second year, that I had a *bad head for the past,* and she had said that it was not a head for the past, it was a head for the present, which was a different thing, and that this was the trouble. I put the toast down. It was cold.

"I've no Year Two. I've no Year Three. I've the bicycle, at five, and a fall, and some stitches. I've got the feeling of a wall. That is all I've got. I can tell you what I was given for Christmas in 1999."

"A chemistry set," said Daniel-A.

"A chemistry set."

"It was a little blue one. It had a red lid. You were disappointed because it had no explosives, and you tried to blow up the sink with vinegar."

"I did not tell you that."

"No. But it's in the orphans. And before 1994, I have the whole of it. And after 1999, I have the whole of it." He paused. "And in between, Daniel, we have the same gap. You and I. We are missing the same five years."

There was a pause.

"It's the first thing we have had in common," said Daniel-A, "that is not a body of evidence."

I looked at the phone, at the face, at the small hopeful crumple of the eyebrows that I had seen in a hundred mirrors, and I felt, for the first time, something that I can only describe as a very small, very odd, warm sensation under the breastbone, as of a person, long cold, sitting down near a fire.

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me," I said.

"I'm told it's a feature."

---

At ten past eight the fax machine in the porch began to scream.

Dr Calder had mentioned the fax machine. She had not mentioned that it worked. It stood on a tallboy among the wellingtons, a beige beast the size of a suitcase, and it was producing, at its own pace, a single sheet with a crest at the top and a bar code at the foot. She read it where it hung from the slot.

"The fifteenth," she said.

"The fifteenth what?"

"Notice to destroy. The first fourteen were emails." She tore the sheet off along its perforations, with the calm of long practice. "They have escalated to a courier. He is to collect all material marked *DESTROY AFTER 30 YEARS* between eight-fifteen and eight-thirty. I am invited to track him."

At the foot of the page, beneath a pictogram of a smiling shredder, it said: *Securishred: Because Some Things Should Not Be Remembered. Your technician today is GAVIN.*

"He is at Dunkeld," said Daniel-A, who had found the tracking page in about the time it takes to read this sentence. "He has taken the wrong road twice and is behind a tractor. He is eleven minutes away." A pause. "Nine. The tractor has turned off."

"Then we have nine minutes," I said, "and a box file."

"We have nine minutes," said Dr Calder, "and *a* box file."

She went to the dresser, opened a cupboard, and brought out a second grey box, identical to the first in every particular, down to the label and the coffee ring. I did not ask when she had made it. "June," she said. "The Office has been so very consistent that I thought it only fair to be prepared. It is council tax, 1996 to 2014, and a recipe for lemon curd." The real one was in the range. I did not need to look at the warming drawer to know that she was standing in front of it.

"Seven minutes," said Daniel-A.

The van came up the lane at twenty-two minutes past, white, with a smiling shredder on the side, and stopped at the green gate, because the hen was sitting in front of it. Gavin was perhaps twenty-three, in a hi-vis tabard and headphones, with the open, hopeless face of a young man whom a company has told to be passionate about destruction. He read the sign. BEWARE OF THE HEN. He read it again.

"I can't move livestock," he said. "It's in the policy."

"She is not livestock," said Dr Calder. "She is a witness."

"Is she on the form?"

"She is not on the form."

Gavin considered the hen. The hen considered Gavin. It was, I felt, the first time either of them had been asked to consult a policy. Then he went round the back of the van, fetched a sack barrow, and wheeled the grey box through the gate in a wide arc, like a man delivering a parcel to a neighbour he does not much like, while the hen stepped down from the lid and took up a position from which to supervise.

"That's the lot?" said Gavin.

"That is the lot."

"Right." He tapped his tablet. "You'll want to keep the certificate. It says it was destroyed to a satisfactory standard." He looked at the box, and then, for the first time, at Dr Calder, and the headphones came down round his neck. "It's a bit light, to be honest. For thirty years."

"It was a quiet thirty years," said Dr Calder, and signed.

The van reversed down the lane. She folded the certificate in four and put it in the pocket of her cardigan.

"It will be the only piece of paper in the whole affair," she said, "that is entirely true."

---

I went out to find Sophie.

She was at the end of the garden, beyond the hen, at a low stone wall that had once been something and was now a place for leaning. The loch lay below her. She had her back to the house and her hands in her pockets, and she was singing.

I say *singing.* It was very low, and it was not quite a tune. It was the kind of sound that a person makes when she does not know she is making it, and when she heard my boots on the gravel she stopped abruptly, and the little crease came between her eyebrows.

"What was that?" I said.

"Nothing."

"It was a song."

"It was a, I don't know what it was." She didn't turn. "I've had it in my head since the sleeper. I woke at four with it. It goes, *the badger went down to the water at night.*"

"That's my father's."

"Is it?"

"It's the one he used to say. About the badger. It isn't a song. He just said it, in a sort of tune." I came up beside her and put my hands on the wall. It was cold, and it smelled of moss. "Where did you hear it?"

"I didn't," said Sophie. "I have never heard it."

We stood there. The mist was lifting off the water in long soft strips, and on the far shore a single red car was going slowly along a road, small as a bead on a thread. Sophie, who is not given to tears, said without any change of voice that she wished to be told that there was a perfectly good explanation, and that she was not a person who disliked mystery, and that what she disliked was being the exhibit.

"Dr Calder knew my name," she said.

"Yes."

"She said she was glad I'd come. She said it as if she'd been expecting me for a long time. And I've never seen her face before in my life." She pressed her lips together. "And I've been remembering a clerk's pencil, and the tune of a song, and the colour of a cardigan on a woman that I have never met."

"Sophie..."

"I am not asking you to say anything," said Sophie. "I'm asking you to let me stand here, for a minute, and be a person who is angry on principle."

I let her. I stood beside her with my hands on the cold wall and looked at the loch, and the hen, who had followed me down the garden like a very small, very patient bailiff, sat on my left foot.

"Go and look at your cellar," said Sophie, after a while. "Whatever it is. I'll be at the hen."

"The hen's with me."

"Then I'll be at the wall."

I turned to go, and she said, without turning round, "Daniel."

"Yes."

"If he's six. If it's what I think it is. Be kind to him."

"Who?"

"The thing in the cellar," said Sophie. "You'll want to be sharp with it. You'll want to be clever. Don't. It's six."

I went back up the garden. At the door of the house, I met Dr Calder coming out with the phone in her hand, and the screen was facing me, and the face on it was Daniel-A's, which was, I saw, very faintly out of focus, since his connection had begun to flicker with the effort of looking out.

"It's half past eight," said Dr Calder. "He'll be up."

From somewhere beneath our feet, from the floor, through an old iron grating in the porch tiles through which I had assumed the house breathed, there came a small clear sound. And then a voice, high and slow and very close to the floor, as a child's voice is when it has just woken and does not yet know whether there is anyone in the room.

"Is it morning?" it said.
