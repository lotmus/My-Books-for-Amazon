# Chapter 25: Sophie Chooses Nobody

The lounge car on the southbound sleeper is a long low room with a bar at one end, a number of brown leather armchairs bolted to the floor, and, on the Saturday night, nobody in it but the two of us and a man in tweed who had fallen asleep with a crossword on his chest like a small paper tent.

We had been given the cabin again, with its wall of coats and its pillow that measured. Neither of us wished to be in it. At ten past midnight, somewhere beyond Carlisle, Sophie had put on her grey coat, said "I need a drink," and gone out, and after a decent interval of about four seconds I had followed her, with the umbrella, which I had begun to carry as one carries a very small relative.

She had a whisky. I had a tea. The barman was Hamish, or rather a screen over the bar that carried his tartan face, and when he saw us come in he said, with undisguised warmth, "Welcome back, one and a half," and then, to Sophie, with an air of delicacy, "Might I enquire whether there has been any change in the *arrangement?*"

"There has not," said Sophie.

"Might I suggest our Reconciliation Package? It is a very modest sum. It includes a complimentary shortbread."

"No."

"Or the Separation Package, which includes a complimentary shortbread, and a leaflet."

"Is that the same shortbread?"

"I'm afraid it's a different leaflet," said Hamish, and went back to polishing a glass that did not exist.

We sat at a small table by the window, which was black and full of our own reflections, with the odd white blur of a platform light flying through them. The train was doing the long fast part of the line, where nothing ever stops, and the glasses on the shelf behind the bar trembled in a regular, patient rhythm. Sophie held her whisky in both hands and did not drink it. She looked at it as if it were a document of doubtful origin.

"Daniel," she said. "I'm going to say something, and I'd like you to let me get to the end."

"All right."

"I'm not going home."

"To Clapton?"

"To Clapton, to my flat, to my chair, to my life, in the sense that I'm not going back to it as it was." She turned the glass a quarter-turn. "I'll go home to sleep. But I won't be at home in the way I was at home before Waterloo."

I said, "I don't understand."

"No. I'm not being clear." She put the glass down. "Let me do it the way I'd do a claim. There are the facts. The first fact is that a woman I've never met greeted me by name in a kitchen in Pitlochry, and said that she wasn't certain I'd come. The second fact is that I've been sitting in cafés for a month remembering the colour of a cardigan on a clerk who retired in 2012, and a song that your father used to say, and the weight of a green pencil. I've never had a pencil like that. I've never had a father who hummed."

"Sophie."

"The third fact is that Daniel-A has rung me every night for six weeks, and asked about my day, and asked me to *keep something for him,* without looking, and I wouldn't, and he sounded relieved. And the fourth fact is that two hundred and six of his orphans have my name on them, and some of them are from before I was born." She stopped. "I am a provenance analyst, Daniel. That's what I do. I read the signature on a thing and I say where it was made. And I have been looking at myself for a month with the cold eye of the profession, and I'd like to tell you what it says."

"What does it say?"

"It says that I can't sign my own memories."

The train went through a station. We saw a platform, a clock, a bench with a single person on it, and then it was gone.

---

I wish I could say that I responded well. I wish that I had been wise or gentle or at the very least silent. What I actually said, in the voice of an offended wardrobe, was "So you're leaving."

"I didn't say that."

"You said you weren't going home."

"I'm saying that I need to find out whose life I've been living." She looked out of the black window. "And I'd like to do it without anyone helping."

"I could help."

"You'd help the way you do. You'd stand next to me, and you'd feel terrible, and you'd say *Right,* and you'd put the kettle on." Her mouth did the small thing it does when it is not smiling. "It's not the kettle I'm complaining about. It's the *standing.* I think I've spent the whole of my life being the person people stand next to when they need a fact checked. I've been your receipt, Daniel, and I was happy to. But I'm not a receipt."

"I never said you were."

"You did it on the sleeper. You turned to me and said, *Is it true?* And I told you what I thought, and you believed it. You always believe it. It's one of the loveliest things about you, and it's also a form of pressure." She took a breath. "And I know what you're going to say next. You're going to say, *what about him?*"

"What about him?"

"He's been doing it for six weeks," said Sophie. "From the other side. *Is she all right? Is she warm enough? Did she eat?* He's very good at it. He's better at it than you are, which is infuriating. He is attentive and funny and he knows everything about me, and I think, if I closed my eyes, I could be very happy for about a fortnight." She looked at me, directly, with the dark steady eyes that do the arguing. "And I don't know whether that's because of him, or because of the two hundred and six."

I did not say anything. There was nothing to say. I sat there with the tea going cold in my hand and I felt the thing I feel when she tells me a fact, which is a kind of vertigo, as of being told by an expert that the floor is a rumour.

"So that's the choice," said Sophie. "Which of you. For a month it's been going round and round in my head, and I thought, *well, Sophie, it's a love story. You're in it. You have to pick.* And then I thought, *that's exactly what they want.* Everyone in this story is waiting for me to choose, as if it's going to solve something. As if, when I'm standing beside one of you, a light will go on over the right one, and a bell will ring, and a registrar in a tea-coloured tie will say *there.* And it won't."

"It might."

"It won't, Daniel. It won't tell you who you are. It'll only tell me who I'm with." She leaned forward. "And I won't be the prize. I don't care what anyone says about the shape of the plot. I'm not the thing at the end of the quest."

"Then what are you?"

"I'm a person who would quite like to be left alone with her memory for a bit," said Sophie. "I've chosen nobody. I want you to hear it properly. I haven't chosen *him,* and I haven't chosen *you.* I've chosen the one thing that neither of you has been able to offer me, which is not to be chosen."

---

There was a long silence.

The man in tweed turned over, and the crossword slid slowly off his chest and on to the floor. Hamish, from the bar, coughed discreetly and said that he would be happy to bring the shortbread now, in either configuration. Sophie told him that she did not require it. He said that he quite understood, and that she should not hesitate to ask for the Redemption Offer later in the night, which was a different leaflet again.

"Will you tell me where you're going?" I said at last.

"Bolton."

"To your mother?"

"To my mother, and a drawer. There's a drawer at my mother's with everything in it, from before I was six. Photographs, certificates, a hospital band." She looked at her glass. "I'm going to look at my own band. To see what it says, and who signed it. I've not looked at it in thirty years. I never thought to."

"Why Bolton?"

"Because that is where the signature is," said Sophie. "That's my profession. I'd like to start from the beginning."

"And after?"

"I don't know." She said it simply. "I might come back. I might not. I'll text you. I'll text him too. I think he'll be sad, and I'd like him to be allowed to be."

"He will," I said. "He's rather good at it."

"I know." And then, as a very small afterthought, quite to herself, as one speaks to a coat on a hook: "It's the thing I like most about him."

The train slid into a tunnel and came out again, and in the flick of light I saw her face quite clearly in the glass, and for a moment it was a face that I had known for nine years and a face that I had never seen: small, dark, composed, tired, with the faint line between the brows, and behind it, through the window, nothing at all but the long black country sliding by.

"I'm not leaving you," said Sophie. "I want to be clear. I'm leaving the *situation.*"

"What's the difference?"

"If it's the situation, I can come back."

---

She got off at Euston at ten to seven, in the thin grey light of a Sunday morning, with her small hard case in one hand and her coat buttoned to the chin. She did not kiss me. She put her hand, for just a moment, flat against the front of my coat, over the place where my breastbone is, as one tests a wall before deciding to lean on it, and said, "Go home. Eat something. Tell him I said that he's a decent man."

"Which one?"

"Yes," said Sophie.

And then she was among the crowd, a small grey figure in a long concourse, walking quickly, like a woman late for a train she had chosen not to catch, and the departure board over her head turned itself, with a series of dry clicks, from **BOLTON** to **DELAYED.**

I stood where I was. I had the umbrella in my hand. I noticed that at some point in the last week it had stopped being hers, and had become, without consultation, mine.

---

It was raining in Penge.

The house in which I live is a converted Edwardian villa, and it looks, in the rain, like a grave and dignified man who has been caught in the open. I let myself in at a quarter to nine. The hall smelled of damp, Dettol, and a faint sweet note of pear. On the mat there was a heap of post, and on the heap, with the air of a thing that had been put there on purpose, a heavy cream envelope with the logo of Nib in the corner, in blue, and in the middle of the envelope a large, crescent-shaped bite.

It was a very neat bite. It had been taken from one corner, with rows of small square teeth. The edge of the paper was wet.

"Gordon," I said.

From the stairwell, behind the pillar, came the faint, contented, rhythmic sound of a goat ruminating a legal document.

I stood and looked at him. Gordon looked at me. He is a large brown and grey goat with rectangular eyes, a small beard, and the unshakable calm of a creature that knows that nobody is going to do anything about it. A corner of the envelope hung from his mouth. On it, in the legible part, I could see the heading **NOTICE OF PROPOSED UPDATE (PRIVILEGED),** and below that, in a smaller type, **Your consent has already been recorded.**

"You can't have eaten the whole thing," I said.

Gordon swallowed. He did it slowly, with a faint upward movement of the throat, like a man downing a pill.

I went upstairs.

The flat was quiet. Clause was asleep on the shelf, in the place where she sleeps, which is as close as possible to the speaker; she lifted her head as I came in, looked at me with her amber eyes, and then laid it down again, with a small pointed sigh, as one who has had a long and tiresome weekend and does not intend to discuss it. On the table were a plate, a cup, and a saucer, all washed, and a note in handwriting I recognised as my own, slightly improved. *Fed the cat. Took the post in. Mrs P is looking after the goat. D.*

I put the phone on the shelf. The speaker lit.

"Hello," said Daniel-A.

"Hello."

"Is she all right?"

"She's gone to Bolton."

"Ah." There was a long pause. "She texted me. At six. She said that she was going to look in a drawer. She said I wasn't to follow, and that I wasn't to send anyone, and that I wasn't to be hurt." The voice was very level. "I have been trying to work out which of the three is the most difficult."

"Which?"

"It's the third. I'm not very good at it. I didn't know that you could have an instruction about it."

I sat down on the arm of the sofa, which is where I sit when I'm upset, and Daniel-A, from the shelf, did not say anything for some time. It was a polite and extremely companionable silence, such as exists between two people who have been in the same room for nine months and have, at last, run out of things to say that are not true.

"She said you were a decent man," I said.

"Which one?"

"She said *yes.*"

"Ah," said Daniel-A, very gently. "She said both of us."

"She did."

"It's the first time that anyone has put the two of us in a sentence," said Daniel-A, "without the word *versus.*"

On the shelf, Clause stretched out one paw, without waking, and laid it on the speaker, in the exact spot where the light was.

My phone gave a small bright buzz. I looked at it. It was a notification from Matchwell, in its purple banner, and I read it because I am a weak man. It said: *Sophie M. has been removed from your suggestions. Reason: user preference.* Beneath it, in the small cheerful type they use for the news that cannot be taken back, it said: *Don't worry! There are 14,206 people within five miles of you who have never met a Daniel.*

I put the phone face down on the arm of the sofa. I did not delete the app. I want it known that I thought about it for some time, and that I did not.
