# PART VIII — Dinner

## 32. Ten Thousand Years of Editing

On a table in a botany department there is an ear of supermarket sweetcorn, and next to it there is a spike of teosinte. The sweetcorn is the length of a forearm, with several hundred soft yellow kernels in straight rows, and if you dropped it in a field nothing would happen. The kernels are stuck to the cob and the cob is wrapped in husk, and the seeds would rot in a heap without a hand to strip and scatter them. The teosinte spike is the length of a little finger. It holds about a dozen kernels, each sealed in a stone case hard enough to pass through a bird, and when it ripens it shatters and throws them.

These two plants are the same species. They can be crossed, the offspring are fertile, and the differences between them are the work of people in the Balsas River valley of southwestern Mexico who began, about 9,000 years ago, to keep the seeds of the plants they liked. Starch grains and phytoliths, tiny mineral traces that plants leave behind, from a rock shelter at Xihuatoxtla in that valley, reported by Dolores Piperno and colleagues in 2009, put maize at about 8,700 years old. A genetic study by Yoshihiro Matsuoka and John Doebley in 2002 put the single origin at about 9,000 years, from the teosinte that grows in the Balsas lowlands. The two clocks agree within a few centuries.

George Beadle, who would later share a Nobel for the one-gene-one-enzyme idea, proposed in 1939 that teosinte was the ancestor of maize and was told it was absurd. In 1972 he came back to it with a simple experiment. He crossed the two, grew about 50,000 second-generation plants, and counted how many looked like either parent. About one in five hundred did. That is what you expect if the difference rests on roughly four or five genes, not on hundreds. The conversion of a wild grass into a food plant had been, in genetic terms, a small edit.

Doebley spent the 1990s finding the edits. The first, published in *Nature* in 1997, was a gene called *teosinte branched1*, written *tb1*. Teosinte is a bush, with many branches each carrying a slim spike. Maize is a single stalk with one or two big ears. The difference is not in the protein the gene makes, which is the same in both, but in a control region upstream of it, where maize has a change that makes the plant produce more of the protein and so suppress its branches. A later study found that the change was a jumping piece of DNA, a transposon named Hopscotch, which had landed there about 23,000 years ago, long before any farmer, and sat in the wild population as a rare variant until people started choosing it. The second edit, published by Huai Wang and Doebley in 2005, was *tga1*, teosinte glume architecture. A single amino-acid change, a swap in one of the compact building blocks a protein is strung together from, turns the stone case into a soft cup, and the kernel is naked. Nobody in the Balsas valley knew any of this. They ate the plants with the fewest branches and the softest kernels and planted what was left.

Wheat is older. Its wild ancestors, einkorn and emmer, still grow on the hillsides of the Fertile Crescent, and a study by Manfred Heun and colleagues in *Science* in 1997, which fingerprinted 338 lines of einkorn, pointed at the Karacadağ mountains in southeastern Turkey as the place where the cultivated form began, about 10,000 years ago. The key trait in wheat is not branching but shattering. A wild wheat spike, when it ripens, breaks apart at the joints of its central stalk and the seeds fall. That is what a seed is for. Every so often a plant carries a mutation in one of the brittle-rachis genes, and its spike stays whole. In the wild that plant is a failure, since its seeds never leave it. In a field of people with sickles it is the only plant whose seeds make it home, and whose seeds are sown next spring. The harvest itself did the selecting, and no one had to intend it. Excavations by Ken-ichi Tanno and George Willcox, reported in 2006, suggest the shift from shattering to non-shattering took more than a thousand years, which is what you would expect from a selection nobody was managing.

Bread wheat added a second step. Some 8,000 to 9,000 years ago, near the southern shore of the Caspian, cultivated emmer crossed with a weedy goat grass known as *Aegilops tauschii*, and the hybrid kept all the chromosomes of both. The result carries three full genomes stacked together, about 16 billion letters, roughly five times the length of the human text, which is one reason the wheat genome was not assembled in full until 2018. The goat grass contributed the proteins that let dough hold gas. Bread is a chromosome accident that people noticed and kept.

The animals came earlier and are harder to date. The dog is the first domesticate, older than any crop, and it descended from the grey wolf somewhere between 15,000 and 30,000 years ago; some claims run older, and the field has argued for two decades about whether it happened once or twice. The oldest undisputed dog is a jaw from Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, buried with two people about 14,200 years ago. A 2020 study in *Science* led by Anders Bergström found that by 11,000 years ago there were already at least five distinct dog lineages, which means the split from wolves was well behind them. Cattle descended from the aurochs, a beast that stood taller than a man at the shoulder and whose last known individual died in a Polish forest in 1627. Taurine cattle were domesticated in the Near East about 10,500 years ago, and one 2012 analysis of ancient DNA estimated that the founding herd was as few as about 80 females, a number I would treat as an order of magnitude rather than a count. Zebu cattle were domesticated separately in the Indus valley. The chicken descends from the red junglefowl of Southeast Asia, and here the dates have recently shrunk: a 2022 reassessment of the bones, led by Joris Peters, identified the earliest secure evidence at Ban Non Wat in Thailand about 3,500 years ago, in a rice-growing village, and threw out older claims. The birds probably arrived at the rice, and the people followed the birds.

Darwin understood all of this before anyone knew what a gene was. The first chapter of the *Origin of Species* in 1859 is about pigeons, not finches, and his 1868 book *The Variation of Animals and Plants under Domestication* runs to two volumes on the same theme. The farm was his model for nature: variation appears on its own, a chooser keeps some of it, and over generations the chosen line walks away from the original. The chooser on the farm is a person. In the wild it is hunger, cold, and the neighbours. The mechanism is the same, and it runs on the fact this book keeps returning to, that every generation's copy of the text carries a few new letters that were not in the last one. Domestication did not create the variants in *tb1* or the brittle-rachis genes. It picked them up, already sitting in a population for thousands of years, and multiplied them.

---

For ten thousand years that was the whole toolkit: wait for a variant, notice it, keep it. In 1927 Hermann Muller showed that X-rays raised the mutation rate in fruit flies by a large factor, and the next year Lewis Stadler did the same in barley and maize. Breeders saw at once what that meant. If mutations were the raw material, and radiation made mutations, then a breeder need not wait. Seeds went into X-ray tubes, then, after 1945, into gamma fields, plots of crops exposed to a radioactive source, at places like Brookhaven National Laboratory, where a cobalt-60 source, a radioactive metal giving off penetrating rays, stood at the centre of a circular plot and the plants grew in rings at different doses. Chemicals such as ethyl methanesulfonate, which raise the mutation rate the same way, did the same job in a jar. In Britain in 1959 a woman named Muriel Howorth founded an Atomic Gardening Society and sent irradiated seeds to hobbyists.

The results are on the plate, and most of them carry no label. The database kept by the Food and Agriculture Organization and the International Atomic Energy Agency lists more than 3,400 registered varieties made this way. At Brookhaven the cobalt source stood in the middle of a round field and the crops grew in rings, the inner plants dead or stunted, a ring further out alive and full of new changes a breeder could keep or throw away. Golden Promise barley, from gamma-irradiated seed released in Britain in the 1960s, became a malting barley for Scotch whisky. One fruit shows the two eras in a single colour. Ruby Red grapefruit was a bud sport found on a Texas tree in 1929, decades before anyone aimed a neutron at a seed. Star Ruby, in 1970, and Rio Red, released in 1984, came from budwood irradiated with thermal neutrons at Texas A&M. None of these was regulated as anything other than a plant. A breeder who scrambles a genome at random with radiation and keeps the one seedling in ten thousand that turned out useful is doing conventional breeding, in the law's eyes, and the produce qualifies as organic. That fact will matter in the next chapter.

The largest single edit of the twentieth century was done with neither radiation nor a laboratory, and it fed something like a billion people. Norman Borlaug was born on a farm near Cresco, Iowa, on 25 March 1914, and in 1944 he joined a Rockefeller Foundation programme to improve Mexican wheat. His first invention was a schedule. He grew two generations a year, one in the Yaqui valley of Sonora in the north and one near Toluca in the highlands, shuttling seed between them, which halved the time to a new variety and, by accident, produced wheats that did not care what latitude they grew at. His second problem was stems. A wheat plant given fertiliser grows a heavy head on a tall stalk and falls over in the rain, and a fallen crop rots. What Borlaug needed was a short wheat.

Such a wheat already existed. In Japan a breeder named Gonjiro Inazuka had released a variety called Norin 10 in 1935, descended from a dwarf landrace named Daruma. In 1946 an agronomist with the American occupation, Cecil Salmon, mailed seed of it to the United States. Orville Vogel at Washington State crossed it with a local variety, and in 1953 he sent some of the crosses to Borlaug in Mexico. Borlaug crossed those into his rust-resistant lines and by 1962 had semi-dwarf wheats that stood about half the height of their parents and put the difference into grain. Mexico, which had imported half its wheat when he arrived, was reported self-sufficient by 1956 and exporting soon after. In 1963 the Indian geneticist M. S. Swaminathan invited him to India; in 1966, with famine in prospect, India imported about 18,000 tonnes of Mexican seed, and Pakistan did the same. India's wheat harvest, about 12 million tonnes in 1965, was reported at about 20 million by 1970. Pakistan declared itself self-sufficient in wheat in 1968, India in 1974. On 10 December 1970 Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in Oslo, the only plant breeder who ever has.

The dwarfing genes were named decades later. They are *Rht-B1b* and *Rht-D1b*, on two of wheat's three genomes, and a 1999 paper in *Nature* by Jinrong Peng and colleagues showed what they do: each makes a protein that ignores the growth hormone gibberellin, so the stem stops listening to the signal to elongate. The same year's paper pointed out that rice's Green Revolution variety, IR8, released in the Philippines in 1966, carries a mutation with the same effect in a gene known as *sd1*. Two crops, two continents, one mechanism: a broken hormone receiver.

The Green Revolution has a bill, and the bill is real. Semi-dwarf wheat only pays if you feed it, so it came bundled with nitrogen fertiliser, irrigation, and a narrowing of the varieties in the ground; those costs get their own chapter at the end of this part. But the method was the oldest one there is. Borlaug did not add a letter to the wheat text. He found a plant that already carried the letters he needed, in a Japanese field, and did what the Balsas farmers had done with their teosinte, faster and on purpose.

The oldest method is still in use. In Salina, Kansas, The Land Institute has spent decades turning a wild perennial grass, intermediate wheatgrass, into a grain sold under the trademark Kernza. A perennial keeps its roots in the ground from one year to the next, so the soil is not opened every spring. By 2025 growers had sown a few thousand acres across about nineteen American states. The harvest is still a niche flour and a beer ingredient, not a substitute for wheat, and the yield remains a fraction of an annual crop. That is breeding at the old speed, choosing which inexact copies to plant, and it is incremental on purpose.

In 1994 a company in California put a tomato on a shelf that carried a letter no tomato had ever had, and the argument about dinner changed shape.

## 33. The Tomato That Didn't Rot

In the third week of May 1994, in a supermarket in Davis, California, there was a bin of tomatoes with a leaflet beside it. The tomatoes carried the name Flavr Savr, sold under the brand MacGregor's, and the leaflet explained that they had been developed by a company named Calgene, a few miles away, using "genetic engineering." The Food and Drug Administration had said on 17 May that they were as safe as any other tomato. People bought them at a premium, and a reporter counted queues.

The idea was narrow. A tomato softens as it ripens because an enzyme called polygalacturonase digests pectin, the substance that cements a plant's cell walls together. Growers therefore pick tomatoes green and hard, ship them, and gas them with ethylene to turn them red, which is why a winter supermarket tomato tastes of nothing. Calgene's scientists inserted a second copy of the polygalacturonase gene, written backwards. The reversed copy makes an RNA, a chemical cousin of DNA that carries copied instructions, that pairs with the normal message and stops it being read, and the enzyme falls to a small fraction of its usual level. The tomato could ripen on the vine and still survive a truck. The insertion also carried a bacterial gene for resistance to the antibiotic kanamycin, used to pick out the cells that had taken up the new DNA, and much of the FDA's review was about whether that marker mattered. It concluded it did not.

The Flavr Savr was a commercial failure and a historical success. The variety it had been built on was mediocre, the tomatoes bruised in the machinery designed for hard green fruit, and Calgene lost money on every one. Monsanto bought the company in 1997 and the tomato disappeared. In Britain a tomato purée produced from a similar Zeneca tomato, clearly labelled, went on sale in 1996 and sold well until about 1999, when the mood changed. What the tomato proved was that the FDA would review such a food and the public, in 1994, would eat it.

The crops that mattered came two years later, and they were not about flavour. In 1996 Monsanto sold the first soybeans carrying a gene from a soil bacterium, *Agrobacterium* strain CP4, that made the plant indifferent to the herbicide glyphosate. Glyphosate kills plants by blocking an enzyme in the shikimate pathway, a chain of reactions that makes three amino acids, the compact building blocks proteins are strung together from, that plants need and animals do not; the bacterial version of the enzyme, from a gene written *CP4 EPSPS*, is not blocked. A farmer could now spray the whole field and kill everything but the crop. The same year brought corn and cotton carrying a gene from *Bacillus thuringiensis*, a bacterium found in diseased silkworms in Japan in 1901 and named ten years later after the German region of Thuringia. Bt, as it is called, makes crystal proteins that dissolve in the alkaline, meaning non-acidic, gut of a caterpillar or a beetle larva, bind to receptors, docking points on the gut's cells, and punch holes. A mammal's stomach is acid and its gut lacks the receptors, which is why Bt has been sprayed on crops as an insecticide since the 1930s and is allowed on organic farms. Putting the gene in the plant meant the plant produced its own.

The uptake was the fastest of any farm technology on record. The United States Department of Agriculture's annual survey reported that by 2024 about 92 percent of American corn acreage, 96 percent of soybean acreage and 97 percent of cotton acreage carried engineered traits, most of them herbicide tolerance, insect resistance, or both stacked together, two or more engineered traits in one seed. Worldwide, the industry's own count put the area at about 190 million hectares, not far short of the size of Mexico, in 29 countries by 2019, with the United States, Brazil, Argentina, Canada and India taking most of it. If you have eaten anything in the last twenty years containing corn syrup, soy oil or cottonseed oil in North America, you have eaten these.

What changed in those fields was not a secret yield gene. Herbicide-tolerant soy and corn let a farmer spray the whole field and leave the crop standing, which made it much easier not to plough. USDA economists reported that by 2006 about 45 percent of herbicide-tolerant soybean acres were under no-till, compared with about 5 percent of conventional soybean acres. The soil stayed put. Glyphosate use rose, and the weeds, as the last chapter of this part records, learned to live with it. Insect-resistant cotton did a simpler job: it cut the spraying aimed at bollworm. In China, where Bt cotton has been in the ground since 1997 and by about 2018 covered some 95 percent of the national crop, early surveys by Jikun Huang and colleagues recorded pesticide cuts of about half. India adopted the same trait from the early 2000s and saw similar drops, though pink bollworm later caught up where refuges were ignored. The myth is a magic harvest. The reality is two traits that changed how a field is weeded and how a cotton plant is defended.

The clearest case that the technique can do something nothing else could is a fruit. Nearly all of Hawaii's papaya grows in the Puna district on the Big Island, and in 1992 papaya ringspot virus arrived there. There is no cure for a plant virus and no resistant papaya existed. By the late 1990s production in Puna had roughly halved. Dennis Gonsalves, a Cornell plant pathologist who had grown up on a Hawaiian sugar plantation, had been working since the 1980s with Richard Manshardt, Maureen Fitch and Jerry Slightom on an idea borrowed from vaccination: put the gene for the virus's own coat protein into the plant. The mechanism, which nobody fully understood at the time, turned out to be that the plant treats the foreign RNA as a target and silences anything matching it, including the real virus when it arrives. The transgenic line, SunUp, a papaya carrying DNA from another species, was crossed with a popular yellow variety to give Rainbow, and seed was handed out to growers in May 1998. Rainbow papaya is now most of what Hawaii grows, and the virus is still there, in the weeds and the unimproved trees, doing nothing to it.

Golden rice is the opposite case, a success in the laboratory that took a quarter of a century to reach a field. Rice endosperm, the white part, contains no beta-carotene, the precursor of vitamin A, and in populations that live on rice the deficiency blinds and kills children. The World Health Organization has estimated that hundreds of thousands of children a year lose their sight to it, and that about half of those die within a year, though the figures are old and hedged. In *Science* on 14 January 2000, Ingo Potrykus of Zurich and Peter Beyer of Freiburg reported a rice carrying two added genes, a *psy* from daffodil and a *crtI* from a bacterium, that made the grain pale yellow with carotene. A 2005 version from Syngenta, with the daffodil gene swapped for the maize one, yielded about 23 times more. Then the arguments began: about patents, about Greenpeace's opposition, about whether a poor child would eat enough rice to matter, and about regulatory tests that took years each. In 2016 more than a hundred Nobel laureates signed a letter asking Greenpeace to stop. On 21 July 2021 the Philippines granted the first permit anywhere to grow it commercially, and farmers planted it in 2022 and 2023. In April 2024 the Philippine Court of Appeals revoked the permit, on the grounds that safety had not been sufficiently shown, and as I write the case is on appeal. Two decades and four months from the paper to the first commercial harvest, and then a court. I think the delay killed children, and it is fair to say so while also saying that the rice was never going to fix a diet on its own.

On the safety of eating such food, the evidence is as close to settled as anything in this book. In 2016 the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine in Washington published a review, *Genetically Engineered Crops: Experiences and Prospects*, that went through about 900 studies and two decades of health data from countries that ate engineered crops and countries that did not. It found no substantiated evidence of a difference in risk to human health between engineered and conventionally bred crops, and no pattern of environmental harm. It also noted, which the industry quoted less, that the crops had not raised the underlying rate of yield growth, though they had cut insecticide spraying and lowered the cost of weed control. A 2013 review by Alessandro Nicolia and colleagues had gone through 1,783 papers from the previous decade and reached the same place. The studies that grabbed headlines the other way, a 1998 potato experiment in Scotland and a 2012 rat study in France, did not survive scrutiny; the rat paper was retracted in 2013.

Distrust is not about the molecule, mostly. Surveys by the Pew Research Center in 2018 recorded about half of American adults saying engineered foods were worse for health, with the figure higher among people who said they knew least about the science. When people are asked what worries them, the answers cluster around who owns the seed, who profits, whether they were told, and whether anyone would admit it if it failed. Those are questions about institutions, and they are not stupid; the next two chapters take them seriously. The question about the molecule is, on the evidence, answered.

---

Labelling is where the two continents parted. In the United States a federal court in Washington, in *Alliance for Bio-Integrity v. Shalala* in 2000, upheld the FDA's refusal to require labels on foods made with recombinant DNA. Vermont passed a labelling law in 2014, the first in the country. A law signed in July 2016 created a national "bioengineered" disclosure, mandatory from 1 January 2022, a small green seal or a QR code, and overrode the state statutes. The European Union took the opposite path from the start. Directive 2001/18/EC governs the release of engineered organisms, and a 2003 regulation requires any food with more than 0.9 percent engineered content to say so. Approvals for cultivation are so slow and so political that only one engineered crop, an insect-resistant maize called MON810, has ever been grown commercially in the Union at any scale, mostly in Spain. In July 2018 the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that plants edited with new techniques such as CRISPR, the gene-editing tool from chapter 25, fall under the same directive, while plants mutated at random by radiation, the Golden Promise and Rio Red kind, are exempt because they have "a long safety record." A plant with one deliberate letter changed was regulated; a plant with thousands of random ones was not. The European Commission proposed a fix in July 2023. Parliament voted in February 2024, the member states in March 2025, and a provisional deal was reported in December 2025. On 17 June 2026 the Union adopted Regulation 2026/1388, which from 17 July 2028 will treat some edited plants, those whose changes could have arisen by ordinary breeding, more like conventional varieties, while more extensive edits stay under the old rules for genetically modified organisms. Seed of the easier category will still carry a label. Until mid-2028 the 2018 court ruling still governs a Spanish maize field the way it governed one in 2019. The implementing details were still being written as this book went to press.

While Europe argued, Japan ate the first CRISPR food. A Tokyo company named Sanatech Seed, spun out of the University of Tsukuba, edited a tomato gene written *SlGAD3* so that the enzyme it makes lost an inhibitory tail and ran freely, and the fruit accumulated four to five times the usual level of GABA, an amino acid sold in Japan as a calming supplement. The edit changes a native gene and adds no foreign DNA, and Japan's regulators accepted in December 2020 that it needed no special approval. Seedlings were sent to home growers in May 2021 and the fruit, named Sicilian Rouge High GABA, went on sale that September. Nobody had ever bought a CRISPR-edited food before. Two edited fish, a fat sea bream and a fast-growing tiger puffer, followed in Japan the same year. In the United States the first CRISPR organism to be waved through had been a white button mushroom that does not brown, edited by Yinong Yang at Penn State, which the USDA said in April 2016 fell outside its rules because no foreign DNA had been added; it was never sold. The Arctic apple, which also does not brown, is often listed with these but is older technology: Okanagan Specialty Fruits in British Columbia silenced the apple's polyphenol oxidase genes with an RNA-interference construct, the USDA deregulated it in February 2015, and it has been sold in bags of slices since 2017.

The mushroom and the Japanese tomato share a legal fact that is easy to miss. Neither carries a gene from another species. The editors cut a native gene, the cell glued the break, a few letters were lost or a switch was dropped, and that was the crop. Regulators' jargon for that class of change is SDN-1, site-directed nuclease type 1: a targeted cut repaired by the plant itself, with no foreign gene left behind. A transgenic crop, the Flavr Savr and Roundup Ready kind, carries DNA that was never in that species. The two are not the same molecule, and after about 2015 a growing list of countries began treating them as different in law. Argentina was first, in 2015, with a rule that an edited plant is not treated as a genetically modified organism if the finished seed has no new combination of genetic material. Japan followed the same logic for the GABA tomato. The USDA, in the 2016 mushroom letter and in later rules, has generally declined to regulate an edit that adds no plant-pest DNA.

The first gene-edited food actually sold in America was not CRISPR and was not a tomato. In February 2019 a Minnesota company named Calyxt began selling a high-oleic soybean oil, Calyno, from plants whose *FAD2* genes, which normally put extra kinks in the oil, had been knocked out with TALENs, the same family of scissors used on the hornless calves of the next chapter. The oil fried longer and made no trans fats. Yields disappointed, the company ran short of money, and on 31 May 2023 it merged into Cibus, a San Diego editor that licenses traits rather than selling a branded bottle. Calyxt's name disappeared. Cibus was still operating in 2026, working on canola, rice, and other crops, without a household product on a supermarket shelf.

Pairwise, founded in North Carolina, did put CRISPR food on a plate. In 2023 it sold a mustard green, Conscious Greens, edited to taste less like mustard, then stopped marketing it the next year and licensed the varieties to Bayer. By early 2026 the company reported selling compact gene-edited blackberries in Colombia, with a code on the punnet that led to an explanation, and was still testing a seedless version. Inari, started in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 2016, has been editing soy, corn, and wheat for yield without extra fertiliser, and as of late 2026 its soy designs were being trialed as commercial-ready. I have not seen a verified harvest figure that would let me treat that as a crop rather than a claim, so I will not.

China had already been planting a transgenic crop for a generation while the West argued about tomatoes. That crop was the Bt cotton of 1997. Genetically engineered papaya is grown there too. Corn and soy, the calorie crops, moved much more slowly. The Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, MARA, began registering engineered corn and soy varieties in 2023, and by 2025 industry counts put the planted area at a few million hectares, still a single-digit share of China's corn.

In December 2024 MARA issued the first biosafety certificate for a gene-edited rice event, one particular edit in one particular line, the thing a regulator actually signs off, and certificates for edited wheat as well. Without variety-registration standards those grains could not yet be sold as seed. Laboratories, including Gao Caixia's group at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, have published edited wheat that resists powdery mildew; that is a paper, not yet a field of bread. The political sentence is food security, the rice bowl held in Chinese hands. The field, as of late 2026, was still mostly cotton that makes its own insecticide, plus a cautious expansion of the same two traits, herbicide tolerance and insect resistance, that Monsanto put in American bags in 1996.

The purple tomato is the transgenic one. Cathie Martin at the John Innes Centre in Norwich added two snapdragon genes that switch on anthocyanin, the pigment of blackberries, throughout the fruit rather than just the skin, and in a 2008 paper in *Nature Biotechnology* her group reported that cancer-prone mice fed the tomatoes lived longer than mice fed red ones. The USDA decided in September 2022 that it posed no plant-pest risk, and in February 2024 the company sold seed packets to American home gardeners, the first time anyone had been able to grow a transgenic plant in a back garden for pleasure.

The labelling argument sits between the edited fruit and the transgenic one, and the honest position is that people are entitled to know and that the knowledge, so far, has told them nothing about their health. The animals we eat are a harder case, because breeding has already changed them more than any gene ever will.

## 34. Animals on the Menu

There is a photograph in a 2014 paper in *Poultry Science* that is worth more than most of the arguments about engineered food. It shows three chickens, side by side, all 56 days old, all fed the same ration in the same barn at the University of Alberta. The bird on the left is from a strain frozen genetically in 1957, the middle one from 1978, the one on the right from 2005, the Ross 308 that fills most broiler houses in the world. The 1957 bird weighs 905 grams. The 1978 bird weighs 1,808. The 2005 bird weighs 4,202 grams, more than four and a half times the first, and its breast alone is a larger share of the carcass. Martin Zuidhof and his colleagues did not change the feed, the light or the housing between the three. The whole difference is in the text.

No laboratory did that. It was done by weighing chickens and choosing parents, generation after generation, by a handful of companies whose pedigree lines trace to the 1950s. A modern broiler, a chicken bred and raised for meat, reaches slaughter weight in about six weeks, and the feed it takes to make a kilogram of bird has roughly halved since 1957. Dairy cattle went the same way: the milk from an average American cow has roughly doubled since 1970, to something over 10,000 kilograms a year. A commercial turkey has a breast so large that it cannot mate and has been bred by artificial insemination since the 1960s. Danish sows are bred to wean more than 30 piglets a year. The chicken genome was read in 2004, and since about 2009 breeders have used chips that read tens of thousands of markers to predict a young animal's value before it has produced anything, a method called genomic selection that Theo Meuwissen and colleagues proposed on paper in 2001. But the engine is the old one: variation, choice, repeat.

The bill for the broiler is paid by the broiler. Fast growth outruns the skeleton, and leg disorders, heart failure from fluid in the abdomen, and sudden death are the recognised costs of the 2005 bird; a condition known as woody breast, in which the muscle turns hard and fibrous, was first described about 2012 and now affects a substantial fraction of heavy birds. The parents of broilers, the breeder flocks, would eat themselves infertile if allowed, so they are fed a restricted ration and are hungry for most of their lives. Zuidhof's paper is a fair document because it does not hide this. A gene edit that makes a chicken resist a virus is a small thing next to what selection has already done to the chicken, and any honest discussion of editing animals has to start from that.

---

The first engineered animal approved for anyone's dinner was a fish, and it took twenty-six years. In 1989 Garth Fletcher and Choy Hew at Memorial University in Newfoundland put into an Atlantic salmon a growth-hormone gene from a Chinook salmon, wired to the control switch of an antifreeze-protein gene from the ocean pout, a fish that lives in water near freezing. Salmon normally make growth hormone in summer; the pout switch runs all year, so the fish grew through the winter and reached market weight in about eighteen months instead of about three years. The company that took it on, AquaBounty, filed with the FDA in 1995. The FDA treated the inserted gene as a veterinary drug, which set the template for every animal since, and approved it on 19 November 2015, with the conditions that the fish be sterile females, carrying three sets of chromosomes instead of the usual two, which leaves them unable to breed, and raised in tanks on land. Canada approved it in 2016 and the first fillets, about four and a half tonnes, sold there in 2017 without a label. In the United States an import block written into a spending bill held it up until 2019, and the first American-raised fish, from tanks at Albany, Indiana, sold in mid-2021. Grocery chains, under pressure from campaigners, declined to stock it. In December 2024 the company announced it was ending fish farming altogether and sold the Indiana site. The fish worked; the market did not.

Cattle showed what can go wrong on the other side of the microscope. Most dairy calves are dehorned, with a hot iron or a caustic paste, because horned cows injure each other and the people who handle them. Some beef breeds, Angus among them, are naturally hornless, and the reason is known: a stretch of 212 letters on chromosome 1, duplicated and pasted in place of 10 others, an allele called Celtic *POLLED*. You could breed it into Holsteins, but you would drag a beef genome along and lose decades of milk selection. In 2015 a Minnesota company, Recombinetics, used an editing tool known as TALENs, a predecessor of CRISPR, to write the Celtic allele into Holstein cells and cloned two calves from them, named Spotigy and Buri. The paper appeared in *Nature Biotechnology* in 2016. Buri went to the University of California, Davis, where Alison Van Eenennaam's group bred him, and six hornless calves were born in 2017. Brazil planned a herd.

In 2019 scientists at the FDA, reading the company's own published sequence data, found that next to the edit in Buri's genome sat the entire bacterial plasmid, a small ring of DNA that bacteria use to ferry genes around, used to deliver the edit. The passenger was a few thousand letters, including genes for resistance to two antibiotics, a speck against one set of the cow's genome, about 2.7 billion letters. Nobody had looked. Some of Buri's calves inherited the plasmid and some did not. The horns were gone, the edit was clean, and the extra passenger had ridden in with the repair template while everyone was checking the wrong place. Brazil cancelled. The lesson was not that editing is dangerous; the plasmid does nothing in a cow. The lesson was that you read the whole genome afterwards, because the copy made at the edit site is never only the copy you asked for.

The FDA learned too, and by 2022 it had a faster lane. A related company, Acceligen, edited the prolactin receptor gene, *PRLR*, in beef cattle so that the protein is truncated, which gives the animal the short, slick coat that Senepol cattle from the Caribbean carry naturally and that keeps them cool in heat. In March 2022 the FDA said the two edited calves and their offspring were low risk, on the grounds that the edit reproduced an allele already in the food supply, and let them go to market without a full drug review. That decision took months rather than decades.

The plant side of the same decade has been quieter than the brochures. Drought-tolerant corn exists, both as ordinary breeding and as a Monsanto trait, DroughtGard, that carries a cold-shock gene from a soil bacterium; USDA surveys reported modest, sometimes statistically invisible, gains in dry years, and nothing a farmer could count on in a severe drought. A cereal that makes its own nitrogen fertiliser is still a laboratory hope. The hornless calf and the slick-coated steer, and the PRRS-proof pig that follows, are the cases where an edit does in one generation what a pedigree would take decades to drag across a breed, and they are already adjacent to the plate.

Pigs are the biggest case so far. Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome, PRRS, appeared in American herds in 1987 and in Europe in 1990. The virus causes abortions and pneumonia, vaccines work poorly, and the cost to the American industry has been estimated at about $1.2 billion a year in a 2024 analysis, up from about $664 million in an earlier one. The virus gets into a pig's immune cells through a receptor protein encoded by the gene *CD163*. In 2015 Randall Prather's group at the University of Missouri used CRISPR to knock the gene out, and the pigs, reported in *Nature Biotechnology* in 2016, simply did not get infected. Genus, the British breeding company that owns PIC, refined the edit to remove only the section of the gene the virus needs, exon 7, one of the gene's coding stretches, so that the rest of the protein keeps its other jobs. At the end of April 2025 the FDA approved the edited pigs for food, the first CRISPR-edited animal it had cleared. Genus said it would wait for approvals in the export markets, Mexico, Canada, Japan and China among them, before selling pork. The same agency had in December 2020 approved a pig from Revivicor with the *GGTA1* gene knocked out, which removes a sugar called alpha-gal from its cells; that pig was cleared both for people with a meat allergy triggered by the sugar and as a source of organs, and its kidneys were transplanted into human patients from 2021 onward.

The chicken edit is the one to watch. In 2023 a group led by Mike McGrew at the Roslin Institute in Edinburgh, where Dolly was born, reported in *Nature Communications* chickens with a small change to *ANP32A*, a gene the influenza virus needs to copy itself. Nine of ten edited birds shrugged off a low dose of bird flu. At a high dose the virus found a way round, in some birds, by adapting to use related proteins. That is partial resistance, and it is a working result, not a settled one; a fully resistant bird would need edits in three genes, and whether such a bird is healthy is unknown. Given that bird flu has killed or forced the culling of hundreds of millions of poultry since 2020, the incentive to succeed is considerable.

---

Cloned animals have been in the food chain longer than edited ones, and quietly. On 15 January 2008 the FDA issued a risk assessment of nearly a thousand pages concluding that meat and milk from cloned cattle, pigs and goats, and from their offspring, were as safe as from any other animal, and that no label was needed. Campaigners had petitioned the agency in 2006 for a moratorium and for labels. They did not get either, and no later lawsuit forced a cloned-food label onto a package. The industry agreed a voluntary hold on selling the clones themselves, which was easy, since a cloned calf costs on the order of $15,000 to $20,000 to make and no one eats a $20,000 animal. What the clones are for is breeding. A prize bull is cloned, the copy stands at stud, and the offspring, which are ordinary calves conceived the ordinary way, go to market. In the European Union the Parliament voted in 2015 to ban cloning for food and the import of clone offspring, and the proposal has sat there since; the offspring almost certainly arrive anyway, in the form of semen and embryos imported from the Americas, and no test can tell them from any other calf. China announced a cattle-cloning plant in Tianjin in 2015 with a stated aim of 100,000 animals a year, a figure I would not take at face value. Argentina and Brazil clone polo horses and breeding cattle as a routine service.

Then the welfare argument, which is the one that will not go away. The philosopher Bernard Rollin put it as a question about which way the fix runs: do you change the cage to suit the animal, or change the animal to suit the cage? The hornless cow is the easy case. Dehorning is painful, the polled allele exists in nature, and a Holstein born without horns has lost nothing. Almost everyone who has looked at it, including the animal-welfare organisations, counts that as a gain. The PRRS pig is harder. Less disease is plainly good for the pig that would have had it. But a herd that cannot catch the disease can be packed tighter, and the edit then becomes a licence for the conditions that made the disease matter. The broiler is the hardest, because there the modification is already done, by the oldest method, and the puzzle is whether an edit that fixed the legs of a 4,200-gram bird would be a kindness or a way of building a heavier one. There is an old thought experiment, associated with the Danish philosopher Peter Sandøe, about breeding hens that are blind, since blind hens in crowded cages peck each other less and seem less stressed. Most people recoil from it. It is worth asking why, given that the same people eat the Ross 308.

I think the test has to be the animal's own experience, not the tool used on it. An edit that removes a source of pain, in a life that is otherwise the same, is good. An edit that lets the life get worse while the numbers get better is not, however it came about. And a change produced by fifty years of weighing chicks deserves the same test as a change produced by CRISPR in an afternoon, which is the point Zuidhof's photograph makes without a word.

Whatever we do to the animals stays in the barn. What we do to the plants goes out into the fields, and there the risks are real, only not the ones on the placards.

## 35. What Is Actually Risky

In a soybean field in Macon County, Georgia, in the summer of 2005, there were plants standing over the crop that should not have been alive. They were Palmer amaranth, a pigweed from the Sonoran desert that grows five to seven centimetres a day, reaches two or three metres, and drops a few hundred thousand seeds from each female plant. The field had been sprayed with glyphosate, which had killed pigweed reliably for thirty years. These had shrugged it off. Stanley Culpepper of the University of Georgia confirmed the resistance the next year, and by 2010 a paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, PNAS, by Todd Gaines and colleagues had shown how it worked: the plants carried up to 160 extra copies of the *EPSPS* gene, the very gene the herbicide targets, and simply made more of the enzyme than the spray could block. A later study found the copies riding on a loop of DNA outside the chromosomes, which lets the number climb fast. Within a decade cotton and soybean growers across the American South were paying crews to pull it by hand.

Nobody engineered the weed. The Roundup Ready system had kept glyphosate cheap and simple and the only thing most farmers used, year after year, on tens of millions of hectares. A single herbicide applied that widely is the strongest selection pressure a plant population has ever felt, and the copy of the weed's text that survived it was the one with a mistake in the right place. Ian Heap's international survey of herbicide-resistant weeds, kept since the 1980s, now lists more than 270 species with resistance to at least one herbicide, and more than 50 with resistance to glyphosate. The industry's answer was crops tolerant of older herbicides, dicamba and 2,4-D, sold from 2016. Dicamba evaporates in warm weather and drifts, and in 2017 farmers reported damage on about 3.6 million acres of neighbouring soybeans that had not been engineered to survive it. A federal court threw out the dicamba registrations in June 2020 and again in February 2024. Herbicide-resistant weeds are the largest real cost of engineered crops so far, and they are a cost of how the crop was used, not of the gene.

The insects did the same thing more slowly. When Bt crops were approved in 1996, the Environmental Protection Agency required that a share of every field, originally 20 percent for corn, be planted with non-Bt seed, so that susceptible insects would survive there, mate with any resistant ones from the Bt rows, and dilute the resistance. Where the refuges were kept, the plan worked for a long time. Where they were not, it did not. Fall armyworm in Puerto Rico overcame one Bt protein by 2006. Western corn rootworm in Iowa overcame another by 2009. In India, where Bt cotton reached more than 90 percent of the crop and refuges were widely ignored, the pink bollworm was resistant by about 2010. A 2023 review by Bruce Tabashnik and colleagues counted 26 cases of what they call practical resistance worldwide. The counter-example is Arizona, where the same pink bollworm was eradicated by 2018, using Bt cotton together with the release of billions of sterile moths, and the USDA declared the United States free of the pest that October. Resistance is not a property of the technique. It is a property of selection, which is to say of copying with errors under pressure, and it answers to management.

Gene flow is the third real risk, and the record on it is mixed in a way that rewards reading. In 2003 a glyphosate-tolerant creeping bentgrass, developed by Scotts and Monsanto for golf courses, escaped from test fields in central Oregon, and a study by Lidia Watrud and colleagues showed its pollen had fertilised wild grass up to 21 kilometres away. The USDA fined Scotts $500,000 in 2007; the grass is still growing in irrigation ditches in Oregon and Idaho, where nobody wants a weed that ignores the standard herbicide. In Canada, canola volunteers carrying two and then three different herbicide-tolerance genes, from crosses between neighbouring fields, turned up within a few years of the crops' release. In November 2001 a paper in *Nature* claimed to have found transgenes in traditional maize landraces in Oaxaca, the crop's homeland; *Nature* said the next year that the evidence did not justify the paper's publication, a later survey turned up nothing, and a still later one turned up a little. The honest summary is that some flow into landraces has probably happened at low levels, and that maize pollen has been crossing fields since the Balsas valley. In 2000 a Bt corn called StarLink, approved only for animal feed because one of its proteins had not been cleared as a possible allergen, surfaced in taco shells, and about 300 products were recalled at a cost reported in the hundreds of millions of dollars. In 2006 an unapproved herbicide-tolerant rice from Bayer showed up in the American supply, Europe and Japan restricted imports, and Bayer settled with growers for $750 million in 2011. Nobody was harmed by eating any of it. Everyone was harmed by the mess.

Monoculture is older than any of this and remains the deepest risk. The Irish potato famine of 1845 to 1852 happened because most of the island grew a single clone, the Lumper, and a single water mould exploited it; about a million people died and about a million left. In 1970 a fungus destroyed about 15 percent of the American corn crop in one summer because about 85 percent of hybrid seed carried the same male-sterile cytoplasm, adopted to save breeders the cost of detasselling, and the fungus had a toxin that fit it. The banana you buy is a Cavendish, a single sterile clone grown worldwide, which replaced the Gros Michel after a soil fungus known as Panama disease wiped that clone out by the 1960s. A new strain of the fungus, TR4, reached Colombia in 2019 and the Cavendish has no defence. In February 2024 Australia approved a Cavendish carrying a resistance gene, *RGA2*, copied from a wild banana, the first engineered banana anywhere. Whether the fix for a monoculture is a single engineered clone is a fair question, and the answer, on the evidence of 1845 and 1970, is that it buys time and not safety.

---

The corporate story is where public anger has settled, and it deserves a plain account. Monsanto was founded in St Louis in 1901 to make saccharin. It later made PCBs and was one of the manufacturers of Agent Orange, and in 1974 it launched glyphosate under the name Roundup. The glyphosate patent ran out in 2000; the company's future by then lay in seeds carrying the tolerance gene, launched in 1996, and it bought seed companies through the following decade. Every bag came with a contract forbidding the farmer to save seed. Two court cases tested that. In Canada, Percy Schmeiser, a canola grower from Saskatchewan, was ruled in 2004 by the Supreme Court of Canada, by five votes to four, to have infringed Monsanto's patent by knowingly growing canola with the gene, whatever its origin, though he owed nothing because he had not sprayed and had earned no profit from the trait. In the United States, Vernon Bowman, an Indiana farmer, bought ordinary soybeans from a grain elevator, most of which carried the gene, and planted them. The Supreme Court ruled on 13 May 2013, unanimously, that planting a patented seed makes new copies and is infringement, however the seed was obtained. Justice Kagan's opinion noted that the rule was about self-replicating things and might not extend to every case. It was, in the plainest way, a ruling about copying.

Bayer bought Monsanto for $63 billion in a deal completed on 7 June 2018 and retired the name. Two months later a California jury awarded $289 million, later cut to about $20 million, to a groundskeeper named Dewayne Johnson who had used Roundup and had lymphoma. The scientific ground of that and the tens of thousands of suits that followed is a 2015 finding by the International Agency for Research on Cancer that glyphosate is "probably carcinogenic," a category it shares with red meat and hot drinks, while the American, European and other regulators have concluded it is not likely to cause cancer at real exposures. In 2020 Bayer set aside about $10 billion to settle most of the claims, and cases were still being tried in 2025. The company was, by 2024, worth less than it had paid. Whatever one thinks of the herbicide, it is worth being clear that the litigation is about a spray, not a gene.

The seed market did not stay a Monsanto story. Pioneer Hi-Bred, founded in Iowa in 1926 by Henry A. Wallace, had taught America hybrid corn decades before anyone inserted a bacterial gene. DuPont bought Pioneer in 1999. Dow and DuPont merged in 2017 and spun their agriculture business out on 1 June 2019 as Corteva Agriscience, which still sells Pioneer bags. Syngenta was assembled in 2000 from the farm arms of Novartis and AstraZeneca. China National Chemical Corporation, ChemChina, completed its purchase of Syngenta in 2017 for about $43 billion, then grouped Syngenta with other state farm assets as the Syngenta Group in 2020. BASF, which had been strong in chemicals and weak in seeds, bought Bayer's divested soybean, canola, vegetable, and much of its cotton seed businesses, plus the LibertyLink herbicide-tolerance trait, as the price of the Monsanto deal. By the early 2020s the proprietary seed market sat in four laps: Bayer, Corteva, the Syngenta Group, and BASF, with a combined share reported as roughly 60 percent. That is a real concentration of power over food, and it is not a property of DNA; it is a property of patent law, which reaches back to a 1980 Supreme Court decision that a living organism could be patented and a 2001 decision extending full utility patents to plants. Patents also expire. The first Roundup Ready soybean patent lapsed in 2015, and farmers can now buy and save generic tolerant seed.

The revolutionary change was legal and commercial: a patented seed you may not save, two traits that reorganised weeding and insect control, and four companies where there had been more. The biological change was incremental, the same copy-and-choose logic as teosinte, aimed at a spray and a moth. Drought, nitrogen, and a perennial grain that could replace wheat are still, as of late 2026, either modest, unpublished as a farm result, or a few thousand acres. The PRRS pig and the hornless calf are closer to a step-change, because they do in one generation what a pedigree would take decades to drag across a breed.

I would guess, and it is a guess, that by 2035 a farm in Heilongjiang is planting Chinese insect-resistant corn from a domestic bag, Dabeinong's or another, some of it transgenic and some of it edited, and that the farmer is spraying less for borers while still walking the rows for weeds. ChemChina bought a seed and chemical company because the official aim, repeated in MARA documents and in the annual rural-policy papers, is food security on a fifth of the world's people and a much smaller share of its good land. By 2050 a visitor to a public crop gene bank in Beijing may be looking at drawers of rice and wheat whose letters were changed on purpose, after the variety-registration rules that did not yet exist in 2026 have been written. That is not a cartoon super-crop. It is cotton's insect gene, soy's herbicide gene, and whatever drought and nitrogen work has actually survived a northern field, copied into more of the hectares that fill a rice bowl.

Now the risk that is not one. Every meal you have ever eaten contained foreign DNA, in quantity. A person takes in something between a tenth of a gram and a gram of DNA a day, from every cell of every plant, animal, fungus and bacterium on the plate, which comes to tens of thousands of foreign genes per mouthful. Stomach acid and enzymes chop it to fragments, the fragments are absorbed or excreted, and in the whole of medicine there is no case of a gene from a carrot or a cow taking up residence in a human chromosome. An ear of Bt corn contains one bacterial gene among the roughly 32,000 to 40,000 of its own; the salad beside it contains the entire genomes of a lettuce, a tomato and whatever was living on them. The Bt protein has no receptor in a mammal's gut and is digested like any other. The one allergen scare that had substance, a soybean carrying a Brazil-nut protein developed by Pioneer in the mid-1990s, was caught by the company's own testing, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1996, and never sold; that is the system working, not failing. The 2016 National Academies review, the 2013 review of 1,783 papers, and the health records of two continents over three decades all say the same thing. On the eating of engineered food, the question is settled. Thirty years of argument have been spent on the wrong risk while the weeds grew. I think that is the right way to hold the ledger.

There is a counter-institution to the patent, and it is a door in a mountain. On 26 February 2008 the Svalbard Global Seed Vault opened on Spitsbergen, about 1,300 kilometres from the North Pole, a tunnel driven 120 metres into the rock and cooled to minus 18 degrees. It has room for about 4.5 million seed samples and holds, by 2025, more than 1.2 million, deposited by national and international gene banks that keep title to what they send; the vault is a backup, not an owner. The first withdrawal came in 2015, when the international centre for dryland agriculture, ICARDA, could no longer reach its own seed bank in Aleppo and asked for its copies back, grew them out in Morocco and Lebanon, and returned fresh seed over the following years. Water from thawing permafrost got into the entrance tunnel in 2016 and 2017, and the entrance was rebuilt. The oldest such bank is in St Petersburg, founded by Nikolai Vavilov, who died in a Soviet prison in 1943 for the crime of doing genetics; during the siege of Leningrad several of his staff starved to death in a building full of seed rather than eat the collection. The vault on Svalbard is their idea, made cold.

Ruby Red grapefruit, the bud sport found on a Texas tree in 1929, began as an accident in an orchard. Rio Red, the deeper fruit irradiated at Texas A&M and released in 1984, came decades later. The vault holds a copy of every crop against the day a field fails, and the fields will fail, now and then, because every seed sown in them is a copy that differs a little from the one in the mountain, and some of those differences belong to the weeds.
