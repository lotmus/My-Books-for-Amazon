# PART VI — What We Can Do Now

## 25. Scissors from Bacteria

The most consequential laboratory tool of the century began as a complaint from the dairy industry. Yogurt and cheese are made by feeding milk to bacteria, mostly *Streptococcus thermophilus* and *Lactobacillus*, and bacteria have their own predators. A virus that infects bacteria is called a phage, and a phage outbreak in a starter culture can ruin a production run the way a frost ruins a vineyard. Food-culture companies lose money to phages every year. In the early 2000s, scientists working for Danisco, the Danish culture and ingredients company, were trying to understand why some of their strains survived an attack and others did not. The answer, reached in 2007, was that the surviving bacteria had filed the fingerprints of the phage in their own genome.

The repeats themselves had been sitting in the literature for twenty years, unexplained. In 1987 Yoshizumi Ishino and colleagues at Osaka University were sequencing a gene in *Escherichia coli* and reported, in the *Journal of Bacteriology*, an odd formation downstream of it: a brief sequence of about 29 letters repeated several times, with stretches of roughly 32 different letters between the repeats. It looked like a stutter. Nobody knew what it was for.

Through the 1990s Francisco Mojica, at the University of Alicante, kept going back to the salt marshes at Santa Pola, on the coast south of the city. The microbes that live in that brine carry the same stutter. He sequenced the odd region, found the repeats again, and then found them in species after species as public databases filled. In 2002, working with Ruud Jansen, he and his colleagues gave the pattern a name that has since become a verb: clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats, CRISPR. In 2005 his group published the guess that explained the marshes. The spacers between the repeats matched the DNA of viruses. The stutter was a filing cabinet of enemies.

Two years later, in *Science*, Rodolphe Barrangou, Philippe Horvath and their Danisco co-authors showed it experimentally. Infect a culture, take the survivors, and the survivors have a new spacer in their CRISPR array, copied from the attacker. Delete the spacer and resistance is lost. Change the associated *cas* genes, the genes that build the cutting machinery, and resistance is lost. Bacteria, it turned out, have an adaptive immune system with a memory, and the memory is written in DNA. This is one of the clean satisfactions of biology: a question about spoiled yogurt, answered properly, turns out to be a puzzle about how one of life's oldest arms races is fought.

The step from immune system to tool took five more years, and it depended on working out exactly how the memory is used. The array is transcribed into RNA and chopped into pieces, each carrying one spacer. In the system that became famous, the one from *Streptococcus pyogenes*, that piece pairs with a second small RNA, called tracrRNA, described by Emmanuelle Charpentier's group in *Nature* in 2011, and the pair loads into a large protein known as Cas9. The RNA is the address. The protein is the machinery. Cas9 slides along DNA, and wherever the 20 or so letters of the guide match, and wherever the match is immediately followed by a brief motif the protein insists on, it cuts both strands.

That insisted-upon motif matters more than its ugly name suggests. It goes by the name protospacer adjacent motif, or PAM, and for the standard Cas9 it is any letter followed by two Gs. It is the reason the enzyme does not shred the bacterium's own filing cabinet, and it is also the first constraint any user meets: you cannot cut wherever you like, only within reach of a PAM. Since a PAM turns up every dozen letters or so in a random stretch of DNA, this is usually a mild annoyance rather than a wall.

![Figure 25. Cas9 guided to a target by RNA.](Figures/figs/fig25.png)

In June 2012, *Science* published a paper from Jennifer Doudna's laboratory at Berkeley and Charpentier's, then in Umeå, with Martin Jinek as first author. It showed that the two RNAs could be fused into one synthetic guide, and that by changing the letters of that guide you could send Cas9 to any address you chose in a test tube. The paper is calm in tone and enormous in consequence. It means a cutting enzyme is now programmable by typing, and the typing is cheap. A guide RNA can be ordered like a pizza.

By January 2013 the tool was working inside human cells, in near-simultaneous papers from Feng Zhang at the Broad Institute and George Church at Harvard, with others close behind. The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 2020 went to Charpentier and Doudna. The credit for the jump into human cells, and the patents that followed, have been argued over in tribunals and law journals ever since, which is what happens when a tool worth billions is invented in several places within months.

---

Cutting is not editing. Cas9 makes a break; the cell repairs it, and what you get depends on which repair crew arrives. The fast, sloppy crew performs non-homologous end joining, which glues the ends back together and often loses or gains a few letters in the process. That is enough to wreck a gene, which is exactly what you want if the gene is doing harm. The careful crew performs homology-directed repair, which copies a template to rebuild the missing stretch. Supply your own template and you can write in a chosen sequence. The trouble is that the careful crew mostly works in cells that are preparing to divide, and it is far less efficient than the sloppy one. For most of the last decade, breaking has been easy and writing has been hard.

Two refinements from David Liu's laboratory at Harvard changed that. In 2016, Alexis Komor and colleagues published base editing in *Nature*: a disabled Cas9 that binds without cutting both strands, welded to an enzyme that chemically converts one base into another in place. The first version turned C into T. An adenine version, converting A into G, followed in 2017. No break, no repair lottery, just a single letter rewritten. In 2019, Andrew Anzalone and colleagues published prime editing, which uses a longer guide that carries both the address and the new text, plus a reverse transcriptase, an enzyme that writes DNA from an RNA template, to write that text into the nicked strand. In principle prime editing can make any small change: swap letters, insert a few, delete a few. In practice its efficiency varies by target in ways still being worked out.

As of late 2026 those two refinements have left the dish. Base editors are in living patients, which is the next chapter. Prime editing has been tried in people too. A 7 December 2025 paper in the *New England Journal of Medicine* reported two patients with chronic granulomatous disease, a rare immune failure in which white blood cells cannot kill certain microbes, whose blood-forming stem cells had been taken out, prime-edited to restore two missing letters in *NCF1*, and put back. The neutrophils, one class of those white cells, regained the chemical burst they use against infection, at least across the months of follow-up in the paper. Two patients are not a medicine. They are the first evidence that a nick-and-write editor, not only a cutter or a letter-swapper, can work in a person.

Cas9 is not the only enzyme in the cabinet, either. Cas12a, first described in 2015 under the name Cpf1, recognises a different motif, uses a shorter guide and leaves staggered rather than blunt ends. Cas13 targets RNA instead of DNA, which means a temporary edit with nothing permanent left behind, and it has been pressed into service for diagnostics. Smaller Cas proteins matter for a dull but decisive reason: the standard *Streptococcus pyogenes* Cas9 is a large protein, and packaging it into the adeno-associated viral vectors, harmless viruses hollowed out and used as delivery trucks for genes, is a tight fit. Much of the engineering of the last decade has been about size, motif requirements and fidelity rather than about the cutting itself.

The tool also changed how ordinary biology is done, which is easy to overlook next to the medical headlines. Pooled CRISPR screens knock out every gene in the genome, one per cell, across millions of cells at once, then ask which knockouts survive a drug or a virus or a starvation. That kind of experiment used to be a career. It is now a few weeks of work, and it has produced much of what we know about which genes a cancer cell depends on. The same approach, run with guides that switch genes on or off rather than break them, maps regulation as well as function.

The mechanism is settled science. Guides find their addresses, Cas9 cuts, base editors convert, cells repair. What is not settled is precision in a living animal, and this is where honest accounts and press releases part company. A guide 20 letters long will sometimes tolerate a mismatch or two. One parental set runs to 3.1 billion letters, and a cell carries two copies of it, so the genome contains a great many near-matches. Cutting at the wrong address is called an off-target effect, and it is measurable, reducible, and never quite zero. High-fidelity Cas9 variants, shorter guides, careful design software and whole-genome checks have pushed off-target rates down substantially since 2013. There are also on-target surprises: large deletions, rearrangements, and the occasional loss of a whole chromosome arm have all been reported after cutting exactly where intended.

There are also cellular consequences that nobody predicted from the biochemistry. In 2018 two groups reported that cells with an intact p53 damage-response pathway, the cell's built-in alarm system for broken DNA, resist being edited, so that the cells which survive an editing procedure are enriched for those with a weakened response, which is exactly the population one would rather not expand. The finding was argued over and its practical importance is still debated, but it illustrates the general lesson: a double-strand break is not a neutral act of typing. It is a kind of injury, and the cell treats it as one.

Then there is mosaicism. Edit a dish of cells and you get a mixture: some edited as you wanted, some edited differently, some not at all. In a treatment made from a patient's blood stem cells, a mixture can be good enough, because a large minority of corrected cells may be all the patient needs. In an embryo, a mixture is a different matter, since every cell of the resulting person descends from that mosaic. It is one of the several reasons the embryo work described later in this book was reckless as well as unlawful.

The address of the sickle-cell change in *HBB* is known to the letter, a single A where a T should be, and a base editor can in principle make that conversion without cutting the strand at all. The distance between "in principle" and "in a child" is measured in delivery, dose, safety data and about a billion dollars, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

What CRISPR actually did to biology was not to make editing possible. Gene targeting existed before it, through homologous recombination, the same template-copying repair described above, in mouse embryonic stem cells, work that earned Capecchi, Evans and Smithies the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2007, and through the zinc-finger and TALEN proteins of the 2000s. Those methods worked. They were also slow, expensive, and specialised: each new target meant engineering a new protein, which took a skilled laboratory months. CRISPR replaced protein engineering with a search string. That is why the technique spread through biology in about eighteen months, faster than almost any method before it, and why a graduate student in 2014 could knock out a gene in a week for the price of a set of reagents.

One further use deserves naming, because it is the only application of this technology that can spread on its own. A gene drive is an edit that copies itself onto the matching chromosome in every generation, so that instead of being inherited by half a carrier's offspring it is inherited by nearly all of them. Built with CRISPR, drives have been demonstrated in caged mosquitoes, crashing the population by making females infertile, and they are the most plausible route yet proposed to eliminating malaria transmission by *Anopheles gambiae*. They are also, by design, irreversible in the wild. No drive has been released in the field, the governance discussions run through the Convention on Biological Diversity and the affected African states, and the technical question of whether resistance evolves first remains open. Everything about gene drives is a working proposition rather than a settled one.

The first patient to be treated with it was a mother of four from Mississippi, and she was dosed in the summer of 2019.

## 26. The First Cures

Victoria Gray had lived with sickle-cell disease for thirty-four years: the crises that feel like being crushed, the hospital stays, the transfusions, the exhaustion. In the summer of 2019, at a research hospital in Nashville, she was given back her own blood stem cells after they had been removed, edited in a laboratory, and multiplied. The date usually reported is 2 July 2019. She was the first person in the United States to receive a CRISPR-based therapy for a genetic disease. When journalists spoke to her in the years that followed she described a life with the crises gone.

The edit is a clever piece of indirection. Sickle-cell disease is caused by a single change in *HBB*, the gene for adult beta-globin, and the obvious move is to correct that letter. The therapy does something else. Before birth, we run on a different version of the protein, fetal hemoglobin, encoded by *HBG1* and *HBG2*, and after birth a repressor protein called BCL11A switches it off. Cut the enhancer, a stretch of DNA that acts as an on-switch for a nearby gene, that turns on *BCL11A* in blood-forming cells and the switch never flips: the patient makes fetal hemoglobin as an adult, and fetal hemoglobin does not sickle. The faulty gene is left exactly where it is. The cure works around it. Biology is full of these workarounds, and the people who find them deserve more credit than they get.

![Figure 26. A blood-collection tube and needle, the first step of taking cells out of a patient to edit them.](Figures/figs/fig26.jpg)

The field took thirty years to reach Nashville, and the reason starts with how badly the first attempt fared. On 14 September 1990, at the National Institutes of Health, a four-year-old named Ashanti DeSilva received her own white blood cells carrying a working copy of the gene for adenosine deaminase, an enzyme her immune cells needed to work properly, which she lacked. The team was led by W. French Anderson, Michael Blaese and Kenneth Culver. It was the first authorised gene-therapy trial in a human being. The results were ambiguous, since the children in that trial also stayed on the enzyme-replacement drug, and arguments about how much the gene therapy contributed lasted years. The optimism, however, was enormous, and by the late 1990s hundreds of trials were running.

On 17 September 1999, Jesse Gelsinger died in Philadelphia, four days after being injected with an adenoviral vector, a hollowed-out virus used to ferry the gene inside, carrying a gene for an enzyme of the urea cycle, the body's chemical pathway for clearing toxic ammonia from the blood. He was eighteen, he had a mild form of his disease, and he was a volunteer in a safety trial rather than a patient seeking treatment. His immune system reacted massively to the huge dose of virus; his organs began failing one after another. The subsequent investigations found problems in consent documents, in the reporting of animal data and of earlier patients' liver enzymes, and in the financial interests of the investigators and the university. Trials were suspended. The field's reputation went into a hole it did not climb out of for a decade.

Worse followed. Between 2000 and 2002 a French team led by Alain Fischer and Marina Cavazzana-Calvo reported that children with X-linked severe combined immunodeficiency, known as bubble-boy disease, had been given working immune systems by a retroviral vector. It was a real and durable success. Then several of those children developed leukaemia. The vector had inserted itself near a growth-promoting gene and switched it on. The mechanism is now well understood, and it is a sobering lesson about what "delivery" means: a virus that parks your gene in the genome parks it somewhere, and somewhere matters. Later vectors were redesigned to insert less dangerously, and self-inactivating designs largely solved the problem, but a child had to get cancer first for the lesson to be learned.

Sickle-cell disease is a good measure of how long that hole lasted. Its cause has been known at the molecular level since the 1950s, when Linus Pauling called it a molecular disease and Vernon Ingram pinned it to a single amino acid. It affects, by most estimates, around 100,000 people in the United States and many millions worldwide. It is the best-understood genetic disease there is, and until the 2020s the treatments on offer were a drug from the 1990s, transfusions, pain control and, for a lucky few with a matched sibling donor, a bone-marrow transplant. Understanding a disease completely and being able to do nothing much about it is the ordinary condition of medical genetics, and it is worth keeping in mind whenever a new gene is announced with fanfare.

The comeback was gene addition, the original idea of 1990, finally made to work through twenty years of unglamorous engineering of vectors, doses and manufacturing. Luxturna, Zolgensma, Hemgenix: each delivers a working gene by virus. None of them edits anything. The launch prices sit in the table in Chapter 43, one list, so this chapter does not restock them.

Editing arrived on the market in the last weeks of 2023. The therapy Victoria Gray received was licensed by Vertex and CRISPR Therapeutics under the name Casgevy, generically exagamglogene autotemcel, for sickle-cell disease and beta-thalassaemia. Britain's regulator approved it on 15 November 2023, the first approval of a CRISPR medicine anywhere. The American approval followed on 8 December 2023. The United States wholesale acquisition cost is in that same table. In the trials, the great majority of treated patients stopped having the pain crises that define the disease. That is the settled part.

---

The unsettled part is everything around the edit. The procedure is not an injection. The patient's stem cells must be mobilised and harvested, which is itself hard in sickle-cell disease. The cells are edited in a facility, checked, and frozen. Meanwhile the patient receives high-dose busulfan chemotherapy to empty the bone marrow so the edited cells have somewhere to live. That conditioning causes infertility in most patients, weeks of hospitalisation, and a small but real risk of death. It is the same brutal preparation used for a bone-marrow transplant, and it is the main reason a therapy that a healthy country's specialist centre can deliver is nearly impossible to deliver in the countries where most people with sickle-cell disease actually live: roughly three quarters of the world's cases are in sub-Saharan Africa. A $2.2 million treatment requiring a transplant unit is not, in any ordinary sense of the word, a cure for sickle-cell disease. It is a cure for a few hundred people who can reach it.

The prices raise a problem that medicine has not had to face before. A one-time treatment with a decades-long benefit is, in economic terms, an asset bought outright rather than a service rented monthly, and no insurance system in the world is designed for that. Manufacturers have offered outcome-based contracts, refunding part of the price if the effect fades, and public payers have experimented with subscription arrangements. In the United States, a federal model for state Medicaid programmes to negotiate sickle-cell gene therapies together began operating in 2025. Whether any of this survives contact with a hundred approved therapies rather than a handful is unknown. I think the pricing question will decide how much of this chapter matters to ordinary people, more than any remaining scientific problem will.

The way out of the transplant unit is to edit cells where they sit. In 2021, Intellia and Regeneron reported the first results for NTLA-2001, an infusion of lipid nanoparticles, tiny fat-based capsules that ferry their cargo into cells, carrying Cas9 instructions and a guide, aimed at the liver, in patients with transthyretin amyloidosis. The nanoparticles drain to the liver almost by accident of physiology, the liver cells take them up, the gene that makes the rogue protein is knocked out, and blood levels of that protein fell sharply and stayed down. No cells leave the body. No chemotherapy. The liver is the easy organ, because everything in the blood goes through it, and getting the same trick to work in muscle, brain or lung is an unsolved delivery problem rather than an editing one.

Editing has also quietly improved a therapy that was already working. CAR-T treatment, in which a patient's own T cells, a type of immune cell, are given a receptor that makes them hunt a cancer, was first approved in 2017 for a childhood leukaemia. It is effective and it is laborious, because the cells must be made individually for each patient, and some patients are too ill to wait or too depleted to supply cells at all. Editing allows donor cells to be used instead: knock out the receptor that would make them attack the recipient, knock out the marker that would make them attack each other, and you have an off-the-shelf product. In 2022 a thirteen-year-old girl in London with a T-cell leukaemia that had exhausted every other option received base-edited donor T cells at Great Ormond Street Hospital and went into remission, meaning the leukaemia retreated and its signs disappeared. Several such trials are now running. The cells are not a cure in the sense of a corrected genome; they are a weapon manufactured by editing.

Then came the case that changed how the field talks about its own future. KJ Muldoon was born in Philadelphia in the summer of 2024 with a severe deficiency of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1, an enzyme of the urea cycle, caused by mutations in *CPS1*. Without that enzyme, ammonia accumulates. The condition is usually lethal in infancy, and the standard hope is a liver transplant that a newborn may not survive to receive. A team at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and the University of Pennsylvania, with Kiran Musunuru and Rebecca Ahrens-Nicklas among the leaders, designed a base editor for his particular mutation, tested it in cells and in animals, obtained regulatory clearance, manufactured it, and infused it in February 2025. The interval from diagnosis to dose was about six months. The *New England Journal of Medicine* paper of 15 May 2025 reports two infusions, at about seven and eight months of age. Later hospital and university accounts of the same case have spoken of a third dose. I will follow the paper. KJ tolerated higher doses of protein, needed less of his ammonia-scavenging medicine, and was sent home.

I think that six-month interval is the most important number in this chapter. A single edit for a single child cannot be priced the way a mass-market drug is priced, and the regulatory machinery was built for batches of a thousand patients, not one. If bespoke edits are to help the roughly 300 million people living with rare diseases, almost all of whom have no treatment of any kind, the platform has to be approved rather than each product. Regulators in the United States and Europe spent 2025 discussing exactly that, and the phrase being used is a plausible-mechanism pathway. Whether it can be made safe is a working puzzle, not a settled one.

It is also worth being careful with the word cure. In sickle cell, the results so far look like a durable remission of the disease's defining symptom, measured over a handful of years in a few dozen people. In the amyloidosis trials, a harmful protein is suppressed rather than a tissue repaired. For KJ, one child is doing better than expected. None of this is a promise about forty years from now, because nobody has forty years of data on an edited human being. The honest claim is that we have gone from a technique to a medicine in eleven years, which almost nothing in the history of therapeutics has done, and that we do not yet know what the twentieth-year follow-up looks like.

The next puzzle is whether we can write DNA as fluently as we now correct it, which turns out to be a different kind of problem altogether.

## 27. Writing from Scratch

On 20 May 2010, a team at the J. Craig Venter Institute reported in *Science* that they had booted up a bacterium whose entire genome had been built in a chemistry laboratory. They took the genome sequence of *Mycoplasma mycoides*, about 1.08 million letters, had it synthesised as more than a thousand overlapping cassettes, ready-built DNA segments of about a thousand letters each, designed to overlap at their ends, assembled them in yeast into a single circular chromosome, and transplanted it into a related bacterium, *Mycoplasma capricolum*. The recipient's own chromosome was lost as the cells divided and were selected. What remained began following the new instructions, divided, and produced colonies that were, by any test applied, the other species. They called it JCVI-syn1.0. Into the sequence they had written watermarks, coded stretches of DNA carrying the names of contributors, an email address, and three quotations, so that the artificial genome could be told from the natural one. One of the three was a line attributed to Richard Feynman, and the team wrote it in the wrong words; Feynman's own blackboard read "What I cannot create, I do not understand," and the synthetic genome carried a garbled version, which was later corrected. Even the first synthetic genome had a copying error, made by people rather than by the polymerase.

The road there had been walked in stages, and each stage caused an argument. In 2002 a team at Stony Brook led by Eckard Wimmer assembled a poliovirus from ordered DNA fragments and showed it was infectious in mice, which drove home the point that a virus is a sequence and the sequence is public. In 2003, Venter, Hamilton Smith and Clyde Hutchison reported synthesising the 5,386-letter genome of phage phi X174, the same little virus Sanger had read in 1977, in about two weeks. Reading and writing had met in the same organism, twenty-six years apart. The step from 5,386 letters to 1.08 million was mostly a matter of assembly: errors accumulate with length, so the work is to build short accurate pieces and stitch them, which the Venter team did by handing the final stitching to yeast, an organism that is unreasonably good at joining overlapping DNA.

The claim advanced at the time, and it is the right claim, is narrow: the software was synthetic, the hardware was borrowed. Nobody built a cell. They copied an existing text with high fidelity, added a signature, and installed it in a working machine. This is closer to transplanting an operating system than to writing one. Even so, it settled a real question. A genome is nothing more than its sequence. Get the letters right and the chemistry does not care whether they originated in an ancestor or in a synthesiser.

The more interesting result arrived six years later. In 2016 the same institute published JCVI-syn3.0, a genome cut down by trial and error to 531,000 letters and 473 genes, the smallest set that will still run a free-living cell in rich medium. That project revealed something humbling. Of those 473 genes, a substantial minority, reported at the time as around 149, had no known function. They could not be removed without killing the cell, and nobody could say what they did. A minimal cell, built deliberately by the people who understood cells best, still contained a hundred and fifty mysteries. I find that the single most useful fact in synthetic biology, because it sets the honest ceiling on how well anyone can claim to understand a genome. Later work has pinned functions on some of them, and the list of unknowns has shrunk, but it has not reached zero.

Yeast came next, and yeast is a proper organism with a nucleus and chromosomes. The Sc2.0 consortium, coordinated by Jef Boeke, set out to rebuild all sixteen chromosomes of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* from synthetic DNA, not as a copy but as a redesign: repetitive elements removed, transfer-RNA genes relocated, stop codons, the three-letter stop signals from chapter 8, standardised, and recombination sites inserted so that the finished yeast can be prompted to scramble its own genome on command. The first synthetic chromosome, synIII, was published in 2014, much of it built by undergraduates in a teaching course. In 2023 the consortium reported the great majority of the genome complete, including a strain carrying multiple synthetic chromosomes at once and a purpose-built extra chromosome to hold the relocated transfer-RNA genes. Bacteria have also been recoded: in 2019 Jason Chin's group in Cambridge reported *Escherichia coli* rebuilt with about four million synthetic letters and a compressed genetic code that uses 61 codons instead of 64, which frees the three spare codons to be reassigned to amino acids nature does not use.

It is worth knowing how DNA is actually written, because the chemistry sets the limits. Almost all synthetic DNA is still made by the phosphoramidite method, developed by Marvin Caruthers and colleagues around 1980, which adds one letter at a time to a growing chain anchored to a solid support, in four steps per letter, with organic solvents. Each cycle works well over 99 percent of the time, which sounds excellent until you compound it: at 99.5 percent per step, a 200-letter product is correct in only about a third of molecules, and a 500-letter product in fewer than one molecule in ten. Set that beside the cell. The copying machine of chapter 7, with its proofreading and the repair crews behind it, gets roughly one letter wrong in a billion. The best chemistry in the world, writing from nothing, gets something like one in two hundred wrong. That is why synthesis companies sell short pieces and why everything lengthier is assembled from them and then sequenced to check. Printing the reactions onto chips has cut the cost per letter enormously, and enzymatic synthesis, which uses a polymerase in water instead of solvents, has been promising longer and cleaner products for several years now. It is working technology, not yet a settled replacement.

If DNA is a text, it can hold texts. In 2012, George Church's group encoded a book of about 53,000 words, plus images, into DNA and read it back. In 2013, Nick Goldman and Ewan Birney at the European Bioinformatics Institute encoded a mixed archive, including all of Shakespeare's sonnets and a clip of Martin Luther King's "I have a dream" speech, with an error-correcting scheme that let them recover the files intact. In 2017 Yaniv Erlich and Dina Zielinski borrowed a coding trick from video streaming and stored a complete computer operating system, a short film and a gift card, recovering every bit; they estimated a density of about 215 petabytes per gram. The attraction is density and durability. A gram of DNA can in principle hold hundreds of petabytes, each petabyte enough to store hundreds of thousands of feature films, and DNA in cool dry conditions survives for thousands of years, as the next chapter shows in some detail. The obstacles are cost and speed: writing is still far slower and dearer than any magnetic medium, and reading requires a sequencer. In 2019 a Microsoft and University of Washington team showed the whole loop running without hands, writing the word "hello" into DNA and reading it back automatically, and it took about twenty-one hours for five bytes. DNA storage is a working technology for archives nobody needs to open often, and speculative for anything else.

---

The most consequential piece of DNA writing so far was not a genome at all. It was a message. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman, working at the University of Pennsylvania through years of rejected grants, published in 2005 that swapping in a modified building block called pseudouridine, in place of one of RNA's ordinary letters, stopped synthetic messenger RNA, the molecule that normally carries a gene's instructions out to the cell's protein-building machinery, from triggering the innate immune alarm that had made it useless as a drug. That, with lipid nanoparticle delivery developed elsewhere, is the whole basis of the mRNA vaccine. When the sequence of the new coronavirus was published in January 2020, the design of a vaccine took days, because designing an mRNA vaccine is a typing exercise: take the sequence of the target protein, adjust the codons, add the caps and tails, order the DNA template. Two small changes made the difference between a good vaccine and a poor one, both written in at the keyboard: two letters' worth of amino acids swapped for prolines, an idea from Barney Graham and Jason McLellan's earlier coronavirus work, which hold the spike protein in the shape it has before it fuses with a cell. The first human dose went into an arm on 16 March 2020, roughly two months after the sequence appeared. By November the large trials were reporting about 95 percent protection against symptomatic disease, and on 2 December 2020 the United Kingdom became the first country to authorise one. Karikó and Weissman received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2023.

That is what fluent writing looks like when the product is a short instruction rather than an organism. Manufacturing billions of doses was an industrial feat, and the clinical trials were conventional and large, but the act of writing was trivial in a way that would have been unimaginable in 1990. The message does not touch the genome. It is read for a few days and then broken down, which is part of why it could be written so freely.

Proteins are the other half of writing, and until recently they were the harder half. A gene's sequence determines the shape its protein folds into, the point Christian Anfinsen established with his ribonuclease in chapter 6, but for sixty years nobody could compute the shape from the sequence. Then in November 2020, at the CASP14 blind competition, a contest where teams predict protein shapes without being shown the answers first, DeepMind's AlphaFold2 predicted structures at accuracy comparable to experiment for most targets, a median score of about 92 on the contest's hundred-point scale, where the best entries two years earlier had scored around 60. In 2021 the method was published and the code released; by 2022 a public database held predicted structures for over 200 million proteins, which is essentially every protein sequence known. Separately, David Baker's laboratory in Seattle had spent two decades on the inverse problem, designing sequences that fold into shapes not found in nature, beginning with a small designed protein called Top7, reported in 2003, whose fold no natural protein had, and moving on to designed binders, molecules built to grab onto a specific target, enzymes and nanoparticle vaccines. One of those, a COVID-19 vaccine built on a designed protein cage, was approved in South Korea in 2022, the first medicine made of a protein designed from scratch on a computer. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded half to Baker for protein design and half jointly to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for structure prediction.

![Figure 27. A designed protein next to its sequence.](Figures/figs/fig27.png)

The gap between prediction and design deserves a plain statement, because press coverage routinely blurs it. Predicting the fold of a natural protein is close to settled for ordinary single-domain proteins, and still unreliable for disordered regions, for large assemblies, for the effect of a single mutation on stability, and for how a protein moves. Designing a new protein that binds a chosen target is now working technology with a real success rate, helped since about 2023 by generative models that produce candidate shapes for the protein's backbone, the folded scaffold the rest of the sequence hangs from. Designing a novel enzyme that catalyses a reaction efficiently remains hard, and the best designed enzymes are generally far slower than natural ones. Designing a metabolic pathway that runs efficiently in a living cell is a matter of building and testing many versions rather than computing the answer, and designing a cell, or an organism, is not on the table at all. The gap between predicting a shape and predicting a behaviour is the whole of biology, and no amount of structural accuracy closes it by itself.

So where is writing still hard? Three places, mainly. Synthesis itself: standard chemistry builds accurate DNA only a few hundred letters at a time, so every lengthy construct is an assembly problem, and cost falls slowly compared with sequencing. Function: we can write any sequence, and we usually cannot predict what a novel sequence will do in a living cell, which is why synthetic-biology projects still run on cycles of build, test and fix. And regulation in the older sense of the word: a genome is not a list of parts but a network of switches whose behaviour depends on context, cell type and history, and no design tool captures that yet.

Against those limits, set what writing has already delivered in industry, which is more than most people notice at the supermarket. Yeast carrying a designed pathway now makes a precursor of the antimalarial drug artemisinin, developed in Jay Keasling's laboratory and handed to a manufacturer, which began production in 2013 as a supplement to the plant harvest. Engineered microbes make enzymes for detergents, vitamins, flavours, fragrances and the rennet in most hard cheese. They make soy leghemoglobin, a heme protein, carrying the same iron-holding ring that colours blood red, that gives one brand of plant-based burger its meaty taste, cleared by the American regulator in 2018. Each of these is a pathway of one or several genes, taken from one organism, tuned, and installed in another that can be grown in a tank. That is real writing, done at industrial scale, and it works because the products are compact molecules whose chemistry is understood, not because anyone has designed a cell.

There is also biosecurity. If a virus is a sequence, and sequences can be ordered, then the barrier to reconstructing a dangerous pathogen is the screening practices of synthesis companies rather than the difficulty of the chemistry. Horsepox, a relative of smallpox, was reconstructed from ordered fragments by a Canadian team in 2017 for about a hundred thousand dollars, and the paper started an argument that has not ended. Most commercial suppliers now screen orders against lists of sequences of concern, voluntarily and unevenly, and in 2023 a framework was agreed to tighten that screening, though it binds no one by law. I think mandatory, audited screening of synthesis orders is the cheapest safety measure available to this field, and the fact that it is still largely voluntary is the least defensible thing about the current arrangement.

Writing, then, is a set of skills at very different stages: copying a genome, settled; minimising one, done once and still not fully understood; writing a therapeutic message, routine; writing a protein, improving fast; writing a life form from first principles, not begun. Meanwhile the most remarkable reading of the last twenty years was done not on living cells at all, but on bones.

## 28. Reading the Dead



In 2008, Russian excavators working in a cave in the Altai mountains of southern Siberia recovered the tip of a finger bone from a layer that also held stone tools and animal teeth. The fragment was small enough to sit on a fingernail. It went to Leipzig, to the department of Svante Pääbo at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, and in 2010 the team reported that the mitochondrial DNA in it, the separate handful of genes passed down only from mothers, belonged to no known human population. It was not modern human and not Neanderthal. Later that year a draft nuclear genome confirmed it. A girl who died in that cave, probably somewhere between 50,000 and 100,000 years ago, belonged to a sister group of the Neanderthals that no one had ever identified from a skull, because no one had a skull. Her people are now called Denisovans, after the cave, and for years they were known almost entirely from a finger, a few teeth and a genome.

Reading a genome from a fossil should not work. DNA begins to break down at death. Water attacks the backbone, cutting long molecules into fragments of a few dozen letters; cytosine loses its amino group and reads as thymine, especially at the frayed ends of fragments; bacteria and fungi move in, so that a typical ancient bone yields a soup in which the human DNA may be one per cent of the total or less. Then there is the modern contamination, which is the hardest problem of all, since the people handling the bone are made of exactly the molecule being sought. Early attempts fell into that trap. A report of DNA from an Egyptian mummy in 1985, by Pääbo himself, later proved to be contamination, and much of the ancient-DNA literature of the 1990s did not survive scrutiny.

The field had begun, as it happens, with a horse. In 1984 Russell Higuchi, working with Allan Wilson at Berkeley, recovered short stretches of DNA from a 140-year-old piece of dried muscle from a quagga, a half-striped southern African zebra hunted to extinction in the nineteenth century, and showed it was a zebra rather than a horse. The following year came the mummy paper. What looked like the start of a rush turned out to be a decade of false positives, because the polymerase chain reaction, the copying trick that unlocked such work, will amplify a single stray modern molecule just as happily as an ancient one.

What rescued the field was a set of unglamorous techniques: clean rooms with filtered air and positive pressure, extractions from the dense petrous part of the temporal bone, which preserves DNA better than the rest of the skeleton, sequencing libraries, the prepared batches of DNA fragments a sequencer actually reads, designed for brief fragments, and, best of all, turning the damage into a signature. Because deamination accumulates in a characteristic pattern at fragment ends, genuine ancient DNA has a chemical fingerprint of age that a modern contaminant does not. The damage that destroys the evidence is also what authenticates it.

The Neanderthals had been waiting for this since 1856, when quarrymen blasting a limestone cave in the Neander valley near Düsseldorf threw out a skullcap and some limb bones that a local schoolteacher thought worth keeping. For a century and a half they were argued about entirely from anatomy: brutish or not, ancestor or cousin, capable of speech or not. In 1997, Matthias Krings, Pääbo and colleagues published in *Cell* a brief stretch of mitochondrial DNA taken from that original 1856 specimen. It fell well outside the range of living humans, which said cousin rather than ancestor, at least in the maternal line. Getting nuclear DNA, the part that would show whether the cousins had children together, took another thirteen years and a change of sequencing technology.

On 7 May 2010, the same year as the Denisovan announcement, Pääbo's group published a draft Neanderthal genome in *Science*, built mainly from three bones from Vindija cave in Croatia at low coverage. High-quality genomes followed: a Denisovan in 2012, a Neanderthal from the Altai in 2014, each read many times over, in the end more accurately than many genomes of living people. Pääbo received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2022 for founding the field. There are now several hundred sequenced ancient humans, and the number grows monthly.

The headline result is that Neanderthals did not entirely vanish. People whose ancestry lies outside sub-Saharan Africa carry roughly 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA, a legacy of interbreeding as modern humans moved into Eurasia, probably mostly between about 50,000 and 60,000 years ago. The proportion is a population average and varies between individuals. Each person carries a different set of Neanderthal segments, so that summed across many living people a good fraction of the Neanderthal genome survives, scattered. Denisovan ancestry is concentrated further east: a few percent in Papuans and Aboriginal Australians, less in mainland East Asia. The remains themselves record the mixing directly. One individual from Denisova cave, reported in 2018, had a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.

Some of the inherited segments do work. The clearest case is *EPAS1*, a gene involved in the response to low oxygen. Tibetans carry a version that dampens the usual response of thickening the blood at altitude, which protects against the complications of chronic mountain sickness. In 2014, Emilia Huerta-Sánchez and colleagues reported in *Nature* that the Tibetan version closely matches the Denisovan sequence. A population that lived and died in Siberia tens of thousands of years ago supplied an adaptation that people on the Tibetan plateau now depend on. Other archaic segments affect immune signalling, skin and hair, fat metabolism and, in a few studies, the risk of particular diseases, while much of the archaic material appears to have been selected against, since large stretches of the genome carry almost none.

Ancient genomes have also rewritten the prehistory of living populations, which is in some ways the more disruptive result. In 2015 two large studies, one led from Harvard by David Reich and one from Copenhagen by Eske Willerslev, reported that a large movement of people from the steppe north of the Black and Caspian seas, associated with the Yamnaya culture, reached central Europe around 5,000 years ago and contributed a substantial share of the ancestry of most Europeans today. Archaeologists had argued for a century about whether pots move or people move. The genomes said that in this case people moved, in numbers, and that the population of Europe has been replaced or heavily diluted more than once. Similar work has traced the peopling of the Americas, the spread of farming from Anatolia, and the arrival of lactase persistence, which the ancient samples show becoming common surprisingly late.

Individuals can be read too, in detail that feels indecent. The man who came out of a melting glacier on the Italian and Austrian border in 1991, known as Ötzi, died about 5,300 years ago with an arrowhead in his shoulder. His genome, published in 2012 and revised with better data in 2023, says he had brown eyes, was lactose intolerant, carried an elevated genetic risk of heart disease, harboured the bacterium that causes stomach ulcers, and had ancestry mostly from early Anatolian farmers with little of the steppe component that arrived in the region later. A body found in ice has yielded a medical record and a family history five millennia after the funeral that never happened.

Ancient DNA reads more than people. Bronze Age teeth have yielded *Yersinia pestis*, the plague bacterium, with reported cases going back over 4,000 years and at least one from roughly 5,000 years ago in Scandinavia, and those early genomes lack the genes that later made flea transmission efficient. Medieval burial pits from the Black Death have given whole plague genomes. Tuberculosis, leprosy, hepatitis B, smallpox and the 1918 influenza virus have all been recovered from human remains. Environmental sediments carry DNA too: a study in 2022 reported DNA roughly 2 million years old from northern Greenland, describing a forest ecosystem from the sediment alone, and mammoth teeth from Siberia have given sequences reported as over a million years old. There is a limit somewhere, set by chemistry and temperature, and the field keeps pushing at it.

---

Which brings us to the animals in the press releases. Colossal Biosciences, an American company founded in 2021, has declared de-extinction its stated aim, with the woolly mammoth, the thylacine and the dodo named as projects. In March 2025 it announced woolly mice: laboratory mice carrying several edits in genes affecting hair length, texture and fat metabolism, chosen using mammoth sequences. In April 2025 it announced three pups, named Romulus, Remus and Khaleesi, described as dire wolves. The dire wolf, *Aenocyon dirus*, died out about 13,000 years ago and was a distinct genus, not a large grey wolf. The work as described involved sequencing ancient dire wolf material and then making a reported 20 edits in 14 genes in grey wolf cells, followed by cloning.

It is worth being exact about what such an animal is. Twenty edits is twenty edits. The grey wolf and the dire wolf lineages are separated by millions of years and by a number of genetic differences that credible estimates put in the hundreds of thousands or higher. The pups are grey wolves with a small number of engineered changes, some affecting coat colour and size, presented in the shape of an extinct animal. Many palaeogeneticists said so publicly and bluntly at the time. The company's own framing has at points been that it aims for functional proxies, animals that act and look the part without truly being the extinct species, rather than genetic replicas, which is a defensible goal described in undefensive language elsewhere in its publicity.

I think the honest way to hold this is to separate three claims. That ancient genomes can be read well enough to identify candidate genes: settled. That an edited living species can be given some traits inferred from an extinct one, and that the techniques developed along the way are useful for conservation genetics, for rescuing inbred populations such as the black-footed ferret, and for widening the gene pool of species down to their last hundreds: working, with real results already. That an extinct species can be brought back: speculative, and with the definition of "back" doing most of the work. A mammoth-like elephant would be an elephant with edits, raised without a herd, in a tundra that has changed. Whether it should be attempted at all is an argument about conservation budgets and animal welfare more than about genetics, and the strongest case against is simply that the money and attention would save more species if spent on habitat.

None of this diminishes what the fossils have actually given us, which is a family history no document records. Before 2010, the relationship between modern humans and Neanderthals was a debate about the shape of brow ridges. Now it is a matter of counting segments in living genomes and matching them to a bone from Croatia or a finger from Siberia. The dead did not leave letters. They left their text, damaged, fragmented, deaminated at the ends, mixed with the DNA of the soil and of the archaeologists, and we have learned to reassemble it well enough to say who slept in which cave and who their grandmothers were. Every one of those fragments is a copy of a copy of a copy, degraded in ways we can measure, and it is precisely the imperfections, the broken ends and the misread letters, that tell us how old the message is and how far it travelled to reach us.
