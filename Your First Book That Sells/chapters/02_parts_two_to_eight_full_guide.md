## Before the Full Guide

The full guide goes back over the same ground in production order and adds what Part I left out: the edge cases, the marketplace detail, the reasons, and the tests. It does not re-teach Part I. Where a rule was given there in full, the chapters here name the chapter and assume the figure is already on your sheet.

## Chapter 16: Why Self-Publish

Self-publishing through Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP) turns a long chain of agents, editors, and contracts into one step: you upload a file, set a price, and the book usually goes on sale within about 72 hours, with no one’s permission but your own. The barrier to publishing dropped to almost nothing. The barrier to publishing well did not move. This book is about the second barrier.

### The three doors

Traditional publishing pays an advance and a royalty set by contract, provides editing, design, and distribution, and takes control of price, cover, and timing. It moves on a timeline of years, and for a debut author without a platform the door is narrow.

Hybrid and vanity publishers charge the author to publish. A few are transparent about what each fee buys. Many charge high prices for things that cost little or nothing to do yourself, such as an ISBN, an ebook conversion, or a KDP upload. Chapter 29 shows how to tell them apart.

Self-publishing makes you the publisher. Nobody stops you and nobody helps you, unless you hire the help. In exchange you keep your rights, earn the royalties in Chapter 3 and Chapter 17, and can change a price or fix a typo the same day. The cost is that editing, cover, formatting, metadata, pricing, launch, and advertising become your job, in your own hours or your own money.

**Recommendation.** Treat self-publishing as a small business from the first book: hire or barter for the editing you cannot do, design the cover for its shelf, and keep records. The platform is the same for every author; what differs is how well the job is done.

![The self-publishing pipeline as ten boxes: choose and write, format, cover, metadata and page, price, launch, ads, get paid, Select or wide, next book, each labelled with its chapter.](../figures/workflow.png)

### Doing both

The paths are not exclusive across a career. Some authors self-publish first and later sign with a publisher; others get rights back on an out-of-print book and republish it themselves. Nothing here asks you to choose forever.

## Chapter 17: How Amazon Actually Pays You

Chapter 3 covers the per-sale keep; this chapter assumes that figure is on your sheet and adds what Chapter 3 left out: the edge cases of the ebook royalty, other marketplaces, Kindle Unlimited, and print.

### The ebook royalty: the details behind the cliff

**Platform rule [3] [4].** These conditions all apply to the 70 percent option on Amazon.com:

- It applies only to sales in Amazon’s listed territories. Sales elsewhere earn 35 percent.
- For sales to customers in Brazil, Japan, Mexico, and India, the ebook must also be enrolled in KDP Select (Chapter 28) and priced inside that marketplace’s 70 percent band.
- Your ebook list price must be at least 20 percent below the Amazon list price of any print edition of the same book.
- Books that consist mainly of public-domain content earn 35 percent only.
- If Amazon matches a lower price found elsewhere and that price falls below the 70 percent minimum, the sale earns 35 percent.
- The July 2026 band change did not switch anyone automatically. A book already on the 35 percent option and priced between $2.99 and $12.99 stays there until you change it in your pricing settings.

The 35 percent option has its own floor, which depends on file size. On Amazon.com the minimum list price is $0.99 for files under 3 MB, $1.99 from 3 MB to under 10 MB, and $2.99 at 10 MB or more. The maximum is $200.

![Line chart of royalty kept against list price: the 35 percent line rises from $0.35 at $0.99; the 70 percent line runs from $1.88 at $2.99 to $8.88 at $12.99 inside the shaded band; at $13.99 the royalty falls back to $4.90 on the 35 percent line.](../figures/royalty_cliff.png)

That figure is the cliff. Priced one cent outside the band, the rate does not taper; it halves.

**Worked example.** At $4.99, a 2 MB file keeps $3.28. Priced instead at $2.49, below the band, it keeps 0.35 × $2.49 = $0.87. Cutting the price by $2.50 cut the keep by $2.41. At $13.99, just above the band, it keeps 0.35 × $13.99 = $4.90, less than the $8.88 kept at $12.99.

**Worked example.** File size matters more than most authors expect. A 20 MB illustrated book at $9.99 pays $3.00 delivery on Amazon.com, so it keeps 0.70 × ($9.99 − $3.00) = $4.89, not the $6.78 a 2 MB file would keep. Compress images before you trust any keep, and use KDP’s estimate for your actual file.

### Other marketplaces

**Platform rule [3] [4].** Each marketplace publishes its own band and delivery rate in its own currency. Examples, checked 3 October 2026:

| Marketplace | 70% band | Delivery per MB |
|---|---|---|
| Amazon.com | $2.99–$12.99 | $0.15 |
| Amazon.co.uk | £1.77–£12.99 | £0.10 |
| Amazon.de, .fr, .es, .it, .nl | €2.69–€12.99 | €0.12 |
| Amazon.ca | C$2.99–C$12.99 | C$0.15 |
| Amazon.com.au | A$3.99–A$15.99 | A$0.15 |

EU prices include VAT, and the royalty is figured on the price without VAT, so the same-looking price keeps less. Set each marketplace’s price from its own page, not from a currency converter.

### Kindle Unlimited and the page-read fund

**Platform rule [13] [10].** If your ebook is in KDP Select, Kindle Unlimited subscribers can borrow it and you are paid for pages read, measured in Kindle Edition Normalized Pages (KENP), not your Word page count. Each month Amazon sets a KDP Select Global Fund, splits it among countries, and divides each country’s share by the pages read there. You can earn for at most 3,000 pages per title per customer. For July 2026 Amazon announced a fund of $67.6 million. KDP also runs a separate, discretionary monthly All Stars bonus for Select titles that reach certain customer-engagement marks, with its own pool size announced alongside the fund each month. Amazon does not publish a single per-page rate; your own royalty report shows what your pages earned, and monthly figures are finalised around the 15th of the following month.

**Worked example.** At a hypothetical $0.0045 per page, one full read of a 300-page (KENP) book pays 300 × $0.0045 = $1.35. That is less than the $3.28 kept on a $4.99 sale, but it may come from a reader who would never have paid $4.99. Whether that trade suits your book is the question in Chapter 28.

### Print: paperback and hardcover

**Platform rule [7] [19].** A print royalty is (rate × list price) − printing cost. The printing cost is subtracted after the rate, not multiplied by it. On Amazon.com the rate is 60 percent at a list price of $9.99 or more and 50 percent at $9.98 or less, for both paperbacks and hardcovers. Other marketplaces draw the line elsewhere: £7.99 in the UK, €9.99 in the eurozone, C$13.99 and A$13.99 in Canada and Australia. Paperbacks sold through Expanded Distribution earn 40 percent minus printing cost; Expanded Distribution is not offered for hardcovers, which sell only directly through Amazon. Printing cost depends on the marketplace, page count, and ink type (and, for hardcovers, trim size). KDP sets a minimum list price so the royalty can never be negative.

**Worked example.** A paperback with a $4.00 printing cost: at $14.99 it keeps (0.60 × $14.99) − $4.00 = $4.99; at $8.99 it drops to 50 percent and keeps (0.50 × $8.99) − $4.00 = $0.50. Through Expanded Distribution at $14.99 it keeps (0.40 × $14.99) − $4.00 = $2.00. Use KDP’s calculator for your real printing cost.

### Reading your own numbers

Policy changes; your reports describe your own book. Chapter 27 explains which report to trust for what. Read them weekly during a launch and monthly afterwards.

## Chapter 18: Fiction and Nonfiction Take Different Paths

Part I followed one book from one genre: a cozy mystery. The arithmetic before it — the keep, the royalty bands, the ad cap, the review rule — does not know or care what genre you write. Everything from here forward, though, is production order, and a few of the methods ahead do fork by whether your book is a story or a guide. This chapter names the forks once, so Parts III to VIII do not have to qualify every sentence.

### Shelves and keywords point at different things

**Recommendation.** Fiction categories sit on a genre and subgenre shelf (Mystery, then Cozy; Romance, then Contemporary); a reader there is choosing a kind of story. Nonfiction categories sit on a topic or professional shelf (Business & Money, Health, Reference); a reader there is choosing a kind of help. The three-category rule and the “closest true shelf, not the biggest one” advice in Chapter 23 apply to both, but the shelf you are matching your book against looks different depending on which one you are writing.

Keywords follow the same fork. Chapter 23’s seven-box rule is identical either way, but the phrases that fill it are not: a fiction reader searches on genre, trope, and feel — “cozy mystery,” “slow burn romance,” “clean fantasy no gore” — while a nonfiction reader searches on problem and outcome — “meal prep for beginners,” “SQL for data analysts,” “first apartment budget.” Write your seven for the reader you actually have, not for the example in Chapter 5.

### The hook changes what it promises

Chapter 4’s two-sentence test still applies to both: a stranger should be able to say who the book is for from the first two lines. What those two lines promise is where fiction and nonfiction split. A novel’s hook promises a character, a stake, and a genre feeling, the way Part I’s ferry-office mystery does. A nonfiction hook promises a problem solved and a reason to trust the person solving it, the way Chapter 23’s “why this author can deliver it” shapes a nonfiction description. Neither hook benefits from borrowing the other’s shape; a guidebook opening with “when one vanishes” confuses the shopper it is trying to reach.

### Reviews ask different questions of the same rule

**Platform rule [1] [2].** The review rule itself does not change: optional, honest, no rating requested, the same roster-and-invitation method from Chapters 6 and 7 for every genre. What changes is what you are screening advance readers for. A novel needs readers who read in that genre and will judge the story on its own terms. A nonfiction book needs readers who either have the problem it solves or enough standing in the subject to judge whether the solution actually works; a glowing review from someone who never had the problem is worth less than a flat one from someone who did.

**Field practice.** Nonfiction authors often publish from an existing platform — a blog, a podcast, a channel — built before the book exists. Pat Flynn’s Smart Passive Income site, built years before his own books, is a widely known example of an audience preceding the book rather than the book building the audience cold, the way Part I’s launch does for a debut novelist with no list yet. Neither path is required, and the launch chapters (Chapters 6, 7, and 25) assume no existing audience on purpose; a platform mainly changes which of those steps you have already done before you start.

### A catalog means something different in each case

Chapter 10’s book-two arithmetic holds for both, but “book two” is not the same idea. A fiction catalog usually continues a world or a character, so a reader who liked book one is choosing more of the same story engine. A nonfiction catalog more often ladders by level (a beginner guide, then an intermediate one) or spreads sideways across separate problems for the same reader (Chapter 30 has both shapes). Plan your next book as the kind of sequel your own genre actually rewards, not as a copy of Part I’s example.

### What does not change

Price bands, delivery charges, the review rule, the ad cap, the AI-disclosure rule, and every number in this book apply exactly the same way regardless of genre. Only the shelf, the keywords, the hook, the reader screen, and the shape of a catalog fork. Chapter 24 gives the price ranges that happen to differ by category of book; that is a pricing question, not a different set of royalty rules.

# Part III — Writing and Producing the Book

## Chapter 19: Choosing What to Write

“Write the book only you can write” is good advice and a weak business plan on its own. A book that sells sits where two things meet: something you can write well, and something a group of readers is already looking for. Amazon’s own store is a free research tool for finding that meeting point.

### Four checks in the store

**Field practice.** These are common checks, not Amazon rules. In the category you are considering:

- Compare Best Sellers with Hot New Releases. Long-standing bestsellers show past demand; recent releases that already have reviews show demand now.
- Compare review counts with publication dates. Many reviews on an old book show the category had readers; a fair number on a book a few months old shows readers are buying now.
- Look at who publishes there. A shelf held by a few large publishers with deep backlists is hard to enter. A shelf of single-book authors is easier to be seen on, though sometimes it is fragmented because demand is small.
- Read the one- and two-star reviews of the current leaders. They tell you what readers wanted and did not get. That gap can become your outline.

**Field practice.** A hypothetical beginner’s hobby guide: the top of the list is held by three books six to ten years old; two books published in the last 90 days already have dozens of reviews; four single-book authors sit in the top twenty; and the low-star reviews of all three leaders complain that there is no troubleshooting for humid climates. None of that guarantees a sale. It does give you a shelf with live buyers and one checkable gap to lead with in your subtitle and description (Chapter 23).

### Write to a shelf

Writing to a shelf means knowing, before you draft, which existing shelf your book belongs on and what a reader there expects. It is the opposite of chasing a trend: by the time a trend shows on the charts, many authors have seen it. A book for one clearly defined reader, with a specific level, goal, and constraint, gives its cover, title, and sample one message to send.

### Standalone or series

A standalone earns once per reader. A series or a set of related guides can earn again when a reader finishes one and buys the next. Chapter 10 shows the arithmetic; Chapter 30 shows how a catalog extends it. For nonfiction, a beginner, intermediate, and advanced set, or books that each solve one part of a larger problem, work the same way.

## Chapter 20: From Draft to Manuscript

A finished draft and a finished manuscript are not the same object. The gap between them is where most self-published books lose readers, because the person who wrote a book reads what they meant, not what is on the page.

### The passes, in order

- Developmental edit: is the structure right, with nothing important missing and nothing repeated?
- Line edit: does each paragraph read well, at the right level for the stated reader?
- Copyedit: grammar, consistency of names, numbers, and capitals, and a check of every fact a reader could look up.
- Proofread: the last pass, on the near-final formatted file, catching typos and accidents introduced by editing.

These are different skills. **Recommendation.** If you can hire only one, hire the copyedit; it catches the errors readers notice consciously.

### A minimum revision checklist

- Read the book aloud, or have text-to-speech read it to you.
- Check every number and every cross-reference after the last reorganisation.
- Run one adversarial pass, looking only for claims you cannot back up and promises the book does not keep.
- Give the book to one reader who has never seen it and ask where they got confused or bored.
- Run at least one pass that is not simply reading again: a fact-check against sources, a search for repeated words, or a consistency script.

AI tools can help with some of these passes. What KDP requires you to disclose, and who owns AI-written text, are in Chapter 33. Whatever tools you use, a person accountable for the book makes the last call on every sentence and every number.

## Chapter 21: Formatting That Doesn’t Break Kindle

Ebook formatting and print formatting solve different problems. Treating one file as correct for both is how books end up with a page reference that means nothing on a phone, or a paperback with no page numbers.

### Ebooks: let the reader’s device do the layout

A Kindle reader can change font, size, and spacing, so a reflowable ebook has no fixed pages. That has three consequences.

- Use real heading styles (Heading 1, Heading 2), not bold text that only looks like a heading. KDP builds the book’s navigation from a proper table of contents, and Word or Kindle Create builds that table from heading styles.
- Do not number pages in the contents and do not refer to “page 47.” Refer to chapters and sections instead.
- Skip a print-style index. If a reference book needs one, that is a reason to offer a print edition.

Keep the rest plain: standard paragraph styles, images sized to work on a phone, and no text boxes or multi-column layouts. Check the book in KDP’s previewer at more than one device size before you publish. The Kindle Publishing Guidelines are the full rulebook [9].

### Print: everything the ebook did not need

- A contents page with page numbers, because print pages are fixed.
- Page numbers and, usually, running headers.
- Front matter in the expected order: title page, copyright page, optional dedication, contents, then the text.
- A trim size; 6 × 9 inches is common for nonfiction, 5.5 × 8.5 inches for shorter guides.
- Margins with room for the gutter, which grows with page count.
- Bleed only if something prints to the edge of the page.
- A cover sized with KDP’s cover calculator and template, because spine width depends on page count, paper, and ink.
- A printed proof, ordered and read before you publish.

## Chapter 22: Covers That Sell in a Thumbnail

Chapter 5 gives the thumbnail test; this chapter assumes your title passes it and covers what the test does not.

### Genre conventions exist because readers use them

Shoppers scan a category page by pattern: they have learned what a thriller looks like, and what a business book looks like. **Recommendation.** Before you commission or design a cover, open the current bestsellers in your exact subcategory and note what they share: palette, type weight, how much is image and how much is type, and where the author name sits. Break a convention only on purpose, after you know what it signals.

### Hierarchy: title, subtitle, author

For most nonfiction the eye should move from title (the hook) to subtitle (the specific promise) to author name. Put your name first only if readers already search for it. If all three are the same size, the eye has nowhere to start.

**Platform rule [17].** The title, subtitle, and author name on the cover must match what you enter in KDP, and a cover may not closely imitate another book’s layout, colours, fonts, or images.

### DIY, template, or designer

- KDP’s Cover Creator is free and recognisable as a template: fine for testing, rarely enough for a book you will advertise.
- Premade templates and freelancers working from a brief range, as **field practice**, from tens of dollars to a few hundred, and can look professional if the template was built for your genre.
- A designer who works in your category costs more, often several hundred dollars and up, and knows the conventions without being told.

Whichever you choose, the finished cover goes through the thumbnail test before you pay the final invoice.

# Part IV — Getting Discovered

## Chapter 23: Categories, Keywords, and Your Product Page

A shopper finds a book by searching or by browsing. Keywords serve the first path, categories the second, and the product page turns either kind of visit into a sale. All three are free to set and free to change.

### Keywords

**Platform rule [18] [17].** KDP gives you up to seven keywords or short phrases; KDP recommends phrases of two or three words. KDP asks you to avoid:

- words already in your title, contributor names, or chosen categories;
- claims about quality or rank, such as “best” or “bestselling,” and promotional words such as “free”;
- time-sensitive words such as “new” or “on sale”;
- other authors’ names, other books’ titles, and brands you do not own;
- Amazon program names such as “Kindle Unlimited” or “KDP Select.”

Misleading keywords are not a grey area; KDP states a zero-tolerance policy for metadata meant to mislead.

**Recommendation.** Build your seven from two free sources: the suggestions that appear as you type in Amazon’s search bar, and the words readers use in reviews of comparable books. Search each phrase on Amazon before you keep it; if the results are not books like yours, drop it. Keywords can be changed as often as you like, so revisit them once the book has a few weeks of data.

### Categories

**Platform rule [15].** You choose up to three categories in KDP, based on your primary marketplace and audience, and you can change them later under Edit details; a change can take up to 72 hours to show. Categories differ by marketplace and format. KDP may move a book whose category does not match its content, and KDP Support does not recommend categories.

**Field practice.** Many authors choose the most specific true category they can, because a book with modest sales is more visible near the top of a small category than lost in a large one, and a high place in a small category can earn a “Best Seller” badge on that category’s list. Amazon does not publish how its ranks are calculated. **Recommendation.** Choose for accuracy first; a wrong shelf brings the wrong readers, and KDP does not tolerate misleading categorisation.

### Your product page

**Platform rule [30] [17].** The description field holds up to 4,000 characters, including any HTML tags, and supports simple formatting such as bold, italics, headings, and lists. It may not contain review quotes or testimonials, requests for reviews, prices or availability, time-sensitive offers, links, or keyword lists.

![Six boxes showing how a shopper reads a product page: thumbnail, title and subtitle, first two lines, bullets and fit, sample, and price and reviews, each with the shopper’s question; below, the content KDP does not allow in a description.](../figures/product_page_flow.png)

**Recommendation.** A shape that works for most nonfiction:

1. A hook of one or two sentences naming the reader’s problem or goal (Chapter 4).
2. One short paragraph on what the book delivers and why this author can deliver it, stated truthfully.
3. Five to eight concrete bullets: “the exact price where the royalty rate halves,” not “learn about royalties.”
4. One line saying who the book is for and who it is not for (Chapter 2).

**Worked example.** Before: “This book covers how to publish on Amazon KDP. It talks about royalties, pricing, categories, keywords, and marketing.” After: “Most first books lose money for a reason that has nothing to do with the writing: nobody showed the author what one sale pays. This guide shows the arithmetic every time. Inside: the price where your royalty rate halves; the keyword mistakes KDP itself warns against; a break-even test you can run before you spend on ads. For first-time authors of a serious ebook; not for advanced advertisers.” Same facts, but the reader and the promise now come first.

### Two more free pieces

Amazon Author Central gives you a free author page with a biography, a photo, and all your books, and is where publishers can add editorial reviews, which are separate from customer reviews. A+ Content adds image-and-text modules lower on the page; check your KDP Marketing page for what is available to your account.

## Chapter 24: Pricing for Profit

Chapter 3 gives the keep and Chapter 17 the cliff and the edge cases; this chapter is about where inside the band your book should sit, and when to move.

### The default range

**Recommendation.** For most first nonfiction ebooks on Amazon.com, start between $3.99 and $6.99: inside the 70 percent band, high enough not to look slight, and low enough to be an easy decision against a felt problem. A narrow professional or technical book solving an expensive problem can test higher in the band. A first novel usually sits between $2.99 and $4.99.

**Test it yourself.** Change only the price, by one step, and hold it for at least two to four weeks before you judge (the keep test’s date line). Compare money kept, not copies sold.

### Two paths: debut or series

The right price for book one depends on whether a paid book two already exists.

![Decision chart: if no paid book two exists, launch inside the 70 percent band and raise by a dollar only when reviews exist and sales hold; if a series exists, book one can be discounted or free while later books carry the margin.](../figures/pricing_paths.png)

**Recommendation for a debut or standalone.** Launch inside the 70 percent band, usually $2.99 to $4.99. Do not launch at $0.99 unless a list or a paid promotion is ready to use it; with no sequel, there is nothing to recover the lost keep. Hold the launch price through the advance-copy window and the first weeks. Raise by $1 when a handful of specific reviews exist and sales have not depended on the low price; if sales collapse, you have found the ceiling, so go back.

**Worked example.** A different author raises from $3.99 (keep $2.58) to $6.99 (keep $4.68) after a strong first month, expecting more money from the same readers. Sales fall from 25 a month to 8. Before: 25 × $2.58 = $64.50. After: 8 × $4.68 = $37.44. The higher price kept less, not more — the ceiling sat somewhere below $6.99, so the honest move is back down, not a second guess at a still-higher price.

**Recommendation for a series.** Once book two is live, book one can become the introduction: a short discount, a Countdown Deal or free days if it is in KDP Select (Chapter 28), or a permanently free price if it is wide. Books two onward stay in the 70 percent band and carry the margin. A box set usually sits inside the band, a little under the sum of its parts. Cut book one only when read-through to book two is real; Chapter 10 has the arithmetic for checking.

**Platform rule [4].** A permanently free ebook is not a price you can set on KDP. Amazon may match a lower price, including free, that it finds for the same book elsewhere, and a matched free price earns no royalty. Authors who want a permanently free book one therefore set it free at another retailer and rely on that match, which means the book cannot be in KDP Select.

### Print has its own floor

KDP sets a minimum print list price so the royalty cannot go negative (Chapter 17). Treat that minimum as a floor, not a target; at the floor the royalty is close to zero. Remember also the 70 percent rule that the ebook must be at least 20 percent below the print list price.

### When to move the price

- Raise when proof exists, when a sequel launches, when a promotion ends, or when comparable books on your shelf have moved. Raise a dollar at a time and wait a full week before deciding again.
- Cut for a scheduled promotion you can announce, not out of impatience on a quiet day.
- Never tie a price cut to a review. The free or discounted copy is the only concession allowed (Chapter 6).

## Chapter 25: Launching Without an Audience

A launch does not need a large existing audience. It needs concentration: the reviews, the purchases from people who asked to hear from you, and any promotion you run, landing in the same short window.

**Field practice.** A concentrated launch is widely used because recent sales can improve a book’s place on Amazon’s best-seller and new-release lists, which are recalculated often. Amazon does not disclose the full ranking algorithm, so treat the size of the effect as unknown and do not pay for a launch on the promise of a ranking.

### Pre-orders: a deadline you set for yourself

**Platform rule [14].** Pre-orders are for new ebooks only. A pre-order created on or after 2 September 2026 can be set up to 18 months ahead. You can have up to 10 active pre-orders. The final manuscript must be uploaded and submitted at least 72 hours before the release date, or the pre-order is cancelled and customers are told. The first missed deadline or cancellation is forgiven once per account; a second removes your pre-order privileges for six months across the whole catalog. If you lower the price during the pre-order, earlier buyers pay the lower price.

**Recommendation.** Use a pre-order only for a book whose text is finished. Treat the 72-hour deadline as a date a week earlier.

### Advance readers

Chapter 6 gives the review rule, and Chapter 7 the invitation. Chapter 32 covers who can post where, how to run a roster, and what to do with a bad first review. This chapter only times them: send the files two to three weeks before publication so readers can finish, and send the store link on the day reviews can be posted.

### Launch week

**Recommendation.** Put the review link, the note to your list, and any capped ad (Chapter 9) on the same publication day rather than spreading them out. Use the six-week calendar in Chapter 11. A specific, accurate category (Chapter 23) gives a small burst of sales the best chance to be visible; it does not guarantee a badge.

## Chapter 26: Amazon Ads Without Wasting Money

Chapter 9 sets the cap and the break-even click price; this chapter assumes those are on your sheet and adds campaign structure and ACOS, the figure the ads console reports.

### Automatic first, manual second

**Field practice.** Many authors start with one automatic-targeting Sponsored Products campaign, in which Amazon chooses where to show the ad from the book’s own details. After a couple of weeks its search-term report shows which customer searches led to clicks and sales. Phrases that converted move into a manual campaign where you set each bid; phrases that spent without selling become negative keywords. The loop then repeats.

### Break-even ACOS

ACOS (advertising cost of sales) is ad spend ÷ ad-attributed sales × 100%. Your break-even ACOS is keep ÷ list price × 100%. Below it, the ad pays for itself; above it, the ad costs more than it keeps. It is the same test as Chapter 9’s break-even click price, read from the other side.

**Worked example.** A $4.99 ebook keeping $3.28 has a break-even ACOS of 3.28 ÷ 4.99 ≈ 66%. Spend $10 and sell two copies ($9.98 in sales, $6.56 kept): ACOS ≈ 100%, about $3.44 lost. Spend $10 and sell five ($24.95 in sales, $16.40 kept): ACOS ≈ 40%, about $6.40 ahead.

![Curve of royalty minus ad spend per $10 spent against ACOS for a $4.99 ebook keeping $3.28: positive below the break-even line at about 66 percent, negative above it.](../figures/break_even_acos.png)

Break-even is a line, not a target. A small, deliberate loss during launch week can be a reasonable choice if it is capped and dated; the danger is running every campaign that way. If the book is part of a series, sales of later books can justify a higher ACOS on book one, but only by the read-through you have actually measured (Chapter 10).

### Budget discipline

- Start with a small daily budget and let a campaign gather enough clicks to mean something before you judge it.
- Read the search-term report, not just the campaign total; one converting phrase often hides several that never sell.
- Add a search term as a negative only after it has had enough clicks with no sales to be a signal rather than chance.
- Change bids weekly, not daily; small campaigns are mostly noise from day to day.
- Keep page-read income in its own column, and do not credit the ad with every page read that week.

# Part V — The Business Side

## Chapter 27: Getting Paid: Taxes, Payments, and Reporting

The most common reason a first royalty payment is late or smaller than expected has nothing to do with sales: it is an incomplete tax interview. This chapter is the plumbing that makes sure the money reaches you at the rate it should.

### The tax interview sets your withholding

KDP will not pay royalties until you complete its tax interview in your account settings. United States persons complete Form W-9. Non-US individuals complete Form W-8BEN, and non-US entities Form W-8BEN-E.

**Platform rule [21].** Royalties from US sales are US-source income. For a non-US person the default withholding is 30 percent, taken before payment. Many countries have a tax treaty with the United States that lowers or removes that withholding on royalties, but only if you claim it in the tax interview, with a taxpayer identification number and the treaty details the form asks for. Without a valid claim, withholding stays at 30 percent. A W-8 form stays valid until the end of the third calendar year after the year you sign it; then you update your tax profile.

**Recommendation.** Look up your country’s current treaty entry, or ask an accountant who handles cross-border royalties, before your first sale. If your country’s tax number takes time to obtain, start now.

### How and when you are paid

**Platform rule [20].** KDP pays monthly, about 60 days after the end of the month in which a sale was reported, or about 90 days for paperbacks sold through Expanded Distribution: a February sale is paid at the end of April. Direct deposit has no minimum. Wire and check payments, where offered, wait until the balance reaches a threshold, for example $100 on Amazon.com, after any withholding.

### Reading your reports

- The dashboard shows near-real-time estimated units and royalties. Watch a launch there; do not do your accounts from it.
- The Prior Months’ Royalties report shows final figures for a closed month, after returns and once the page-read rate is set. This is the number that matches what you will be paid.
- The Payments report shows what was actually sent to your bank, about 60 days later.

Expect the dashboard and the final report to differ a little. That is normal.

### Treat it like a business

Keep a record of every expense against your royalties: editing, cover, ads, and software. Consider a separate bank account once the money is more than trivial. Whether royalty income counts as self-employment income or passive income depends on your country and on how actively you publish, and it changes what you can deduct and what you owe. That is a question for an accountant once the amounts justify it, not for a general guide.

## Chapter 28: KDP Select vs. Going Wide

Once a book is ready, you decide whether its ebook is sold only through Amazon or everywhere. Neither answer is right for every book.

### What Select asks for, and what it gives back

**Platform rule [5] [6] [28] [13].** KDP Select is a free 90-day enrollment for ebooks only.

- For the whole term the ebook must be exclusive to Amazon: not sold, given away, or distributed anywhere else, including your own website or a bulk-emailed file. Paperback, hardcover, and audio editions may be sold anywhere.
- The ebook joins Kindle Unlimited and earns from pages read (Chapter 17).
- In each term you may run either up to five free days or one Kindle Countdown Deal, not both.
- A Countdown Deal runs only on Amazon.com and Amazon.co.uk, after the book has been enrolled for at least 30 days. On Amazon.com the list price must be between $2.99 and $24.99. The deal lasts up to seven days, the price must cut by at least $1, and the list price must stay unchanged for 30 days before and 14 days after. You keep your selected royalty rate during the deal, so a book on the 70 percent option earns 70 percent even below $2.99, minus delivery.
- Sales to Brazil, Japan, Mexico, and India earn 70 percent only for enrolled books (Chapter 17).
- The term renews automatically unless you untick renewal.

**Recommendation.** Put the renewal date in your calendar the day you enroll, so a decision to go wide is not made for you by default.

### What going wide gives you

Going wide means selling the ebook through other retailers and library platforms, by uploading to each store yourself or through a distributor that reaches several for a share of the sales. Your income no longer depends on one platform’s rules, and some readerships buy more often elsewhere or borrow through libraries. You give up page-read income and Select’s promotions, and you manage more accounts.

![Two boxes comparing KDP Select and wide, both leading to one 90-day test; renew Select if page reads carry real weight, or untick renewal and go wide if other stores or libraries matter more.](../figures/select_vs_wide.png)

### Choosing without guessing

**Test it yourself.** Enroll the first book for one term. Record sales, pages read, and the page-read money from the final monthly reports. At the end of the term, decide the next term from those numbers.

**Worked example.** A 300-page (KENP) ebook at $4.99 keeps $3.28 per sale. Suppose 300 readers in one term. If all 300 buy it, wide or in Select, the term keeps 300 × $3.28 = $984. If instead 120 of them read it in full through Kindle Unlimited at a hypothetical $0.0045 per page, the term keeps 180 × $3.28 + 120 × 300 × $0.0045 = $590.40 + $162 = $752.40. That same $752.40 is a loss or a gain depending on one fact only you can know: if those 120 would have bought the book anyway, Select cost you $984 − $752.40 = $231.60; if they would never have paid $4.99 at all, Select earned you $752.40 that wide would have earned nothing on. Select wins only if it brings readers who would not have paid $4.99 at all, and that number is exactly what one real term tells you.

**Field practice.** Authors widely report that Kindle Unlimited matters most for fast-reading series fiction such as romance, fantasy, and thrillers, and less for many nonfiction categories. That is a pattern, not a rule, and your own term is the only evidence that counts for your book.

## Chapter 29: Avoiding the Traps

Everything in this book so far is free and documented. That is exactly what a set of scams and shortcuts is built to hide. None of the traps below requires bad intent; most are walked into by authors who meant well.

### Vanity and predatory publishers

The shape repeats: a fee, from hundreds to many thousands of dollars, for things that cost nothing (an ISBN, a KDP upload, a basic conversion) or cost far less elsewhere. Warning signs:

- Unsolicited praise for a manuscript you never sent them.
- Upfront fees for “access” to distribution that KDP already gives you free.
- Vague promises of bestseller status, bookstore placement, or film interest, with pressure to sign quickly.

Freelance editors, designers, and formatters are legitimate paid services. **Recommendation.** Ask one question of every fee: what exactly is this paying for, and could I get that same thing done elsewhere for less? A legitimate freelancer answers at once.

### Fake reviews and rank manipulation

Buying reviews, trading them, or paying for a “ranking boost” breaks Amazon’s rules (Chapter 6). **Platform rule [2].** Amazon’s stated consequences for guideline violations include removing content, restricting features, suspending or terminating accounts, and withholding payments. **Recommendation.** No version of this trade is worth the account; the honest advance-reader method in Chapter 32 reaches the same goal.

### Volume publishing and AI flooding

Mass-producing thin, lightly reviewed books to flood a category is the same trap at scale. KDP now limits every account to two new titles per format per week (Chapter 15), requires disclosure of AI-generated content (Chapter 33), and removes books that create a poor customer experience [8]. A flood of weak books under one name also damages trust in your better books, because a reader’s next click is often “more by this author.”

### Copyright and licensing traps

Two mistakes recur. The first is assuming something is public domain when it is not: status depends on dates and on the country, and a translation, annotation, or edited edition of an old work can carry its own copyright (Chapter 33). The second is using an image without a licence that covers commercial use in a book. Read the licence for every image, every time.

### Two triggers that need no bad intent

- Duplicate or near-duplicate content: the same book, or a lightly reworded version, under a new title, cover, or account. A relaunch that changes only the cover can look like this.
- Breaking Select exclusivity by forgetting: a book enrolled months ago, later included in a website giveaway or a bundle elsewhere (Chapter 28).

Add misleading keywords (Chapter 23) and a skipped AI disclosure (Chapter 33), and you have the common causes of account trouble that need no malice at all. Run them as a checklist before every launch.

### The best filter

Many paid courses sell information that KDP’s own help pages give away. **Recommendation.** Before paying for any course that claims to know “the algorithm,” spend twenty minutes checking whether its claims are just KDP policy with a mark-up. Amazon does not publish its ranking algorithm, so nobody can sell it to you.

# Part VI — Playing the Long Game

## Chapter 30: From One Book to a Catalog

Chapter 10 has the book-two sum; this chapter assumes it and adds the other ways a catalog earns more than its books do separately.

### Why a catalog outearns its parts

A reader who finishes one of your books sees your others on your author page, in “more by this author,” and in Amazon’s recommendations, at no extra marketing cost. Five related books are not five separate problems of finding readers; they are one problem followed by four chances to sell again.

The fixed costs of learning are also paid once. Learning KDP’s upload flow, your ad account, a cover style for your shelf, and how to run advance readers does not reset for book two. **Field practice.** Authors commonly report that a second book takes less effort to launch than the first, mostly because the learning is already paid for.

**Field practice.** A beginner’s hobby guide planned from the start as three short books (getting started, common mistakes, a first big project) gives each finished book a place to send its readers, instead of waiting years for one large volume. An author running two unrelated lines, say cozy mysteries and regional cookbooks, spreads risk instead: a slow month in one category does not empty the other. Both apply the same principle to different situations.

### Bundles and an owned audience

A box set of existing books is a new product at almost no creation cost (Chapter 24 for its price). An email list, built from a sign-up in your books’ back matter that offers a short extra, gives you a launch audience you control instead of one that depends on Amazon’s placements. **Recommendation.** Start the list with book one, write to it only when you have something real to say, and never add anyone who did not ask.

### Audiobooks

ACX is Amazon’s audiobook platform, distributing to Audible, Amazon, and Apple Books. There are three ways to get a finished audiobook there:

- Narrate it yourself: your time and decent recording equipment. A real option for nonfiction if your voice suits it.
- Royalty share: no upfront fee; you and the narrator split the royalty. The narrator is, in effect, betting on the book too.
- Pay per finished hour: a flat fee per finished hour of audio. You keep the whole royalty and carry the whole risk.

**Worked example.** At a hypothetical $300 per finished hour, a six-hour audiobook costs $1,800 upfront. Royalty share costs nothing upfront but halves every future audio royalty. Paying per hour suits a book with proven sales; royalty share suits a first audiobook on an unproven title, because it caps the downside at zero.

KDP also offers audiobooks with virtual voice, a beta that turns eligible ebooks into AI-narrated audio at little cost; check your KDP account for eligibility before planning around it. ACX pays more for exclusive distribution than non-exclusive; check ACX’s current royalty table before you choose, because it has changed before.

Not every book belongs in audio. A book that leans on charts and worked numbers, like this one, loses much of its value without the page. Narrative nonfiction, memoir, and fiction do not.

## Chapter 31: A Realistic First-Year Plan

**Field practice.** Self-publishing income is very uneven. A small share of books and authors earn well, and a large share earn little or do not cover their costs. Nothing in this book changes that distribution. What it can change is your odds within it, by doing the plain, checkable work: real editing, an accurate category, honest reviews, capped spending, and a second book.

**Recommendation.** Treat the first book as tuition, not a lottery ticket. Its most valuable output may be what you learn about your shelf, your readers, and your process, which book two then applies at lower cost.

### A month-by-month shape for a first book

| Months | Work | Chapters |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Write, and confirm the shelf and the reader against the store before the draft is finished | 18, 2 |
| 3 | Edit, design the cover, open advance-reader sign-ups | 19, 21, 7 |
| 4 | Format, set metadata and price, launch | 20, 22, 23, 24 |
| 5–6 | Read the final reports, run a capped ad test, decide on Select renewal | 9, 25, 26, 27 |
| 7–12 | Start the next book; build the list and, once there are three books, a box set | 10, 29 |

The months will stretch around real life. The order matters more: research before writing, editing before launch, data before the next decision.

The honest measure of the first year is not the first royalty number. It is whether you end the year with a repeatable process and a second book under way.

# Part VII — One Topic, in Depth

## Chapter 32: The First-Review Problem

This chapter assumes the review rule from Chapter 6 and the invitation from Chapter 7. It takes up what those chapters left out: why reviews come before spending, who can actually post, how to run a roster of advance readers, how to deliver the file, and what to do with the first bad review.

### Why proof comes before spending

A book with an empty review section asks every stranger to be the first to trust it. **Field practice.** Many authors find that ads and paid promotions perform poorly on pages with no reviews, and many promotional newsletters require a minimum number of reviews before they will feature a book. Amazon does not publish whether or how review count affects ranking, and this book does not guess. The case does not need a hidden formula: shoppers see the empty section, newsletter editors use it as a gate, and conversion is what turns paid clicks into kept money (Chapter 9).

So the order is: honest reviews from readers who asked to read the book, then traffic. Recruit while the file is still a draft, because readers cannot post until the book is live and cannot read a book overnight.

**Field practice.** Free days on KDP Select tend to produce downloads rather than reviews. A small group of readers who applied to read the book in its genre is a different population. Authors who count report much higher posting rates from such a group than from anonymous free downloads, but no published study gives a figure you can plan on. **Test it yourself.** Count invitations sent, files opened, and reviews posted for your own first campaign, and plan the second from that.

### Who is allowed to post on Amazon

**Platform rule [2] [1].** On Amazon.com a customer must have spent at least $50 on Amazon.com with a credit or debit card in the past 12 months to post a review or a star rating. Promotional discounts do not count toward the $50. Other marketplaces publish their own rules. A free copy does not make anyone eligible, and the conflict-of-interest rules from Chapter 6 still apply.

That is why a brand-new Amazon account can read your advance copy and still find the review button closed. **Recommendation.** Prefer advance readers who already review other books. Never tell anyone to spend money so that they can review you; that turns the review into a condition. A review from an advance copy carries no Verified Purchase badge, and that is fine. Do not ask readers to buy the live book to earn the badge, and do not refund them for doing so.

![Flowchart of review eligibility: for Amazon, a reader with a personal or financial connection may not review, a review that was required or rewarded breaks the rules, and a reader needs $50 of card spending on Amazon.com in the past 12 months; for Goodreads there is no spend minimum, and pre-publication raters confirm reading and name the source of their copy.](../figures/review_eligibility.png)

### Goodreads is a different door

**Platform rule [16].** Goodreads has no spend minimum and allows ratings before publication. Since its December 2025 update, a reader rating a pre-publication book must confirm they read it (a reader who did not finish may say so), is asked how they got the copy (author or publisher, a giveaway, or another source), and that source is to be shown with the review. A reader who has only shelved a book as Want to Read cannot rate it.

Treat the two sites as two jobs: Goodreads while advance readers are still reading, Amazon from the day the page is live. A Goodreads rating is not an Amazon review, and readers should never hide that their copy was free.

### Running the roster

**Recommendation.** Treat the sign-up form as an application, not a broadcast. Ask for a name, an email, three books in your genre read in the last year, a link to an existing review profile, the device they read on, a finish-by date they agree to, and a required checkbox confirming that any review is optional and honest. Review applicants before sending anything. Share the form link, never the file.

Recruit in this order: people on your own list, then your own channels, then groups that exist for advance copies, after reading each group’s rules and posting once. If a week brings no sign-ups, post the same request in one other suitable group before concluding anything. Never fall back on relatives; they cannot review on Amazon anyway (Chapter 6).

After each campaign, track three numbers: files sent, files opened, and reviews posted. Invite back the readers who actually read and responded, whatever they thought. A long list of people who never open the file is a vanity number.

### Delivering the file

Send an EPUB, not a Word file, and lock the text before anyone reads it; a review of a draft describes the draft.

**Platform rule [29].** A Kindle reader can email the EPUB to their own Send to Kindle address, from an email address they have approved in their Amazon account settings, or open it in a reading app that supports EPUB.

**Field practice.** Delivery services such as BookFunnel exist so that you do not have to teach this to each reader. Reader-matching services such as BookSirens or Booksprout can supply readers when you have no list; NetGalley reaches librarians and reviewers. They sell downloads by interested readers, not reviews, and a review must stay optional. **Recommendation.** If you pay for one, use one service on one title, with a cap, and judge it by the three numbers above.

### The first bad review

**Platform rule [1] [2].** Amazon removes reviews that break its guidelines, for example spam, harassment, private information, conflicts of interest, or compensated reviews. A critical review from a reader who disliked the book breaks none of them and will not be removed for being negative.

**Recommendation.** Do not reply. A public reply stays under the review and usually reads worse than the review. If the review names a real fault, such as a broken table or a missing chapter, fix the file; that helps every later reader. A page of nothing but praise can look as if nobody felt free to be honest, so recruit for honest reactions, not unanimous ones.

### A sequence that keeps the money

1. Lock the manuscript, make the EPUB, and price the ebook inside the 70 percent band (Chapters 3 and 24).
2. Open the sign-up form and send files two to three weeks before publication (Chapter 11).
3. Publish, send the review link, and hold serious ad spending until a few honest reviews exist.
4. If the roster is thin, run one capped reader-matching campaign.
5. When proof exists, raise the price by a dollar if sales hold, or hold the floor and start book two.
6. When book two exists, decide whether book one becomes the introduction (Chapter 24).
7. Only then spend on free days, newsletters, or ads.

The letters for every step are in Chapter 13. The live-page letter can link straight to Amazon’s review form, with your own ASIN in place of the placeholder: https://www.amazon.com/review/create-review?asin=YOURASIN

### Claims that do not survive a spreadsheet

- That a debut should open at $0.99 to get reviews. Reviews come from people who agreed to read, not from a keep of $0.35.
- That $1.99 is a friendly compromise. It is on the 35 percent option and keeps less than $2.99 does (Chapter 3).
- That free days produce reviews. They produce downloads.
- That a hundred reviews are needed before anyone buys. The costly gap is zero.
- That reviews from family, if disclosed, are a plan. Amazon does not allow them.

# Part VIII — Using AI Well

## Chapter 33: Using AI Without Losing the Business

AI tools can speed up much of the work in this book. This chapter covers where they help, the exact disclosure rule, who owns what an AI writes, and the one process that looks professional but is not.

### Where AI earns its place

Used as a fast first pass that a person then judges, AI is useful at most stages:

- Cover concepts (Chapter 22): rough directions to react to before a designer makes the real one. An AI-generated final cover image is AI-generated content and must be declared.
- Description and ad-copy drafts (Chapters 23 and 26): several variants for you to choose from and rewrite.
- Keyword brainstorming (Chapter 23): candidate phrases that you then test in Amazon’s search bar and check against KDP’s keyword rules.
- Research support: explaining a concept, testing your understanding, or summarising your own notes, after which you write the explanation yourself.

### Building background knowledge, legitimately

Writing well about a subject you do not know cold takes research, and drawing on several kinds of sources is normal. A sound process has three steps. First, a general search to learn the current terms and questions. Second, an AI tool used as a patient colleague: “explain X,” not “condense these three books.” Third, reading the books already published on the subject, with your notes kept as private background. Close the notes before you draft, and write from what you now understand, in your own structure and with your own examples. The line is crossed when the condensed notes themselves become the draft you polish.

### The disclosure rule, stated precisely

**Platform rule [8].** KDP requires you to tell it about AI-generated content (text, images, or translations) when you publish a new book or edit and republish an existing one. Images include the cover and interior artwork.

- AI-generated: an AI tool created the actual content. It is AI-generated even if you edited it substantially afterwards.
- AI-assisted: you created the content and used AI tools to edit, refine, error-check, or otherwise improve it, or to brainstorm ideas. AI-assisted content does not need to be declared.

You are responsible for making sure all content, AI-generated or AI-assisted, follows KDP’s guidelines, including intellectual-property rights.

![Decision chart: for each piece of text, image, or translation, if an AI tool created the actual content, even if heavily edited, it is AI-generated and must be declared; if you created it and a tool only edited, refined, error-checked, or helped brainstorm, it is AI-assisted and needs no declaration.](../figures/ai_disclosure.png)

“I used AI to help me write” and “AI wrote it and I edited it” are therefore different answers, even when the pages look alike. Which one is true is a fact about how the book was made.

### Who owns what the AI wrote?

Disclosure answers what Amazon needs to know. Ownership is a separate question, and in the United States text an AI produced on its own belongs to nobody.

**Platform rule [23] [24].** The U.S. Copyright Office will not register material whose expressive content was generated by a machine. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter* the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit held in March 2025 that the Copyright Act requires a human author, and on 2 March 2026 the Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal, which leaves that ruling in place. Declining a case is not a ruling on the merits, but the practical position is clear: a work with no human author has no copyright.

That does not leave AI-assisted books unprotected. The same ruling says the requirement does not bar works made with the help of AI; what counts is the human contribution. Your own sentences are yours, and so are your creative selection, arrangement, and changes. In its January 2025 report on copyrightability, the Copyright Office concluded that prompts alone, however detailed, do not make you the author of what comes back. How much human shaping turns AI material into your work is still being argued, so do not plan a business around the generous end of that line. Other countries draw it differently; the United Kingdom, for one, has a separate rule for computer-generated works.

An AI service’s terms typically say the output is yours to use. That is permission, not ownership: the company promises not to claim the text against you, but it cannot give you a copyright the law says does not exist. You may publish the output; you cannot stop others from copying the parts no human wrote.

Take a tempting case: a long, sharp conversation with a chatbot, say about whether time is real, that you want to turn into a book.

- You may. Check that service’s terms first.
- Disclose it. Every answer the AI wrote is AI-generated content, however much you trimmed it.
- Know what you own. Your questions, commentary, creative edits, and your choice and order of excerpts can be protected. The AI’s paragraphs, copied as they came, cannot.
- Make it yours. The more of the book is your own explanation, argument, and structure, the more you own and the more a reader is buying.
- Save the transcript and every draft. The service may not keep them, and dated drafts are your evidence of the human contribution.

### The trap that looks like a process

One pattern deserves a name because it is packaged to look like professional workflow: take a few existing books on a subject, have AI condense and merge them, then run a “similarity audit” to find and remove close parallels with those particular sources until the draft no longer looks derived.

The audit step is the tell. Legitimate research has no reason to scrub a manuscript against a short list of specific books; that step exists because the process expects resemblance and is built to hide it. Facts and ideas are not protected by copyright, but an author’s expression of them, including wording, analogies, and structure, is [25]. A workflow whose last step erases detectable similarity to particular copyrighted books produces a derivative work dressed to pass inspection, and “an AI tool did the condensing” is no defence. It also puts the whole account at risk (Chapter 29), not just one title.

### What copyright protects, and two legitimate ways to build on another work

**Platform rule [25] [26] [27].** U.S. copyright protects the expression of an idea, not the idea or the facts. Fair use is decided case by case on four factors, so no single argument settles it.

None of these, on its own, makes a derivative work lawful:

- “I changed a set percentage of it.”
- “AI wrote the new wording.”
- “I credited the original author.”
- “It’s transformative.” Transformative purpose is part of one factor, not a pass.
- “It’s for education,” or “I didn’t charge for it.” Purpose and commercial use are weighed, not decisive.
- “Only the structure is the same.” Structure can be expression.
- “It’s a parody.” Parody is a narrow category that must comment on its source; the label does not create it.

There are two legitimate ways to build directly on someone else’s work: get a licence from the rights holder, or adapt a work that is verifiably in the public domain in every country where you will sell. Duration rules vary by country, and a modern translation, introduction, annotations, or cover art on an old edition can carry their own copyright. Verify the exact edition, and every layer of it, before you build on it.

*General principles only, not legal advice. For a specific manuscript, ask a lawyer.*
