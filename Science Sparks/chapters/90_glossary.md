# Glossary

*Each entry names the place where the idea is properly taught. On a Kindle, tap the reference to jump there.*

## A

**Aharonov-Bohm effect.** The shift in an electron's interference pattern caused by a magnetic field confined to a region the electron never enters, showing that the potential, not just the field, matters. *See* Chapter 176; Chapter 177.

**Allele.** One of the alternative versions of a gene; you carry two for most genes, one from each parent. *See* Chapter 298; Chapter 301.

**Amplitude.** The number attached to each possible outcome of a quantum measurement; its squared size gives the probability, and unlike probabilities, amplitudes can cancel. *See* Chapter 144; Chapter 158.

**Ancient DNA.** Genetic material recovered from old bones and teeth, damaged and fragmentary, which revealed among other things that most living people carry a little Neanderthal ancestry. *See* Chapter 272; Chapter 313.

**Anthropic principle.** The observation that we can only find ourselves in a universe whose conditions allow observers, which explains some apparent fine-tuning without requiring that anyone did the tuning. *See* Chapter 101.

**Antiparticle.** A particle with the same mass as its partner but opposite values of properties like charge; when the two meet, they can annihilate into radiation. Some, like the photon, are their own antiparticle. *See* Chapter 39; Chapter 244.

**Arrow of time.** The one-way direction of everyday events from past to future, which physics traces to entropy rising from an unusually orderly beginning. *See* Chapter 30; Chapter 34.

## B

**Base pair.** Two DNA letters joined across the double helix, A always with T and G always with C, which is what lets each strand serve as a template for the other. *See* Chapter 290; Chapter 291.

**Bell's theorem.** John Bell's 1964 proof that no theory in which particles carry preset local answers can match quantum predictions; experiments have repeatedly sided with quantum mechanics. *See* Chapter 151; Chapter 160.

**Big Bang.** The hot, dense early state from which the observable universe has been expanding for about 13.8 billion years; it happened everywhere at once, not at one point in space. *See* Chapter 67; Chapter 68; Chapter 70.

**Black hole.** A region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can climb back out once it has crossed the event horizon. *See* Chapter 38; Chapter 81; Chapter 337.

**Block universe.** The picture in which past, present, and future events all exist alike in four-dimensional spacetime, with no universal "now" sliding through them. *See* Chapter 66; Chapter 96.

**Boson.** A particle, such as the photon, that can share a state with any number of identical others; force-carrying particles are bosons. *See* Chapter 162; Chapter 238.

## C

**Cambrian explosion.** The burst, beginning about 540 million years ago, in which most major animal body plans appear in the fossil record within a few tens of millions of years. *See* Chapter 267.


**Chaos.** Behavior governed by exact laws that is still unpredictable in practice, because tiny differences in starting conditions grow exponentially; the butterfly gets the credit, and the blame. *See* Chapter 50; Chapter 76.

**Chromosome.** One of the long, packaged DNA molecules in a cell's nucleus; humans have 23 pairs, one set from each parent. *See* Chapter 296; Chapter 297.

**Cloning.** Making an organism genetically identical to another, as with Dolly the sheep in 1996; the copy shares the genome but not the upbringing or the memories. *See* Chapter 314; Chapter 315.

**Codon.** A three-letter word in DNA or RNA that specifies one amino acid, or tells the cell to stop building a protein. *See* Chapter 293.

**Collapse.** The short name for the jump from a quantum list of possibilities to one recorded result when a measurement is made; what physically happens there is still debated. *See* Chapter 147; Chapter 161.

**Complex number.** A number with a real part and an imaginary part built on the square root of minus one, which turns out to be the natural language for waves and quantum amplitudes. *See* Chapter 11; Chapter 223.

**Conservation of energy.** The law that the total energy of an isolated system never changes; energy only moves between forms, which is how the universe keeps the books. *See* Chapter 29; Chapter 46.

**Convergent evolution.** The independent evolution of similar features in unrelated lineages, such as the eyes of octopuses and of people, because similar problems favor similar solutions. *See* Chapter 270.

**Cosmic microwave background.** The faint glow left from when the universe first became transparent, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, now cooled to under three degrees above absolute zero. *See* Chapter 68; Chapter 69.

**CRISPR.** A gene-editing tool borrowed from a bacterial immune system that uses a guide RNA to steer a cutting enzyme to a chosen spot in DNA. *See* Chapter 310; Chapter 311.

**Curl.** A measure of how much a vector field swirls around each point, like the spin a tiny paddle wheel would pick up in flowing water. *See* Chapter 20.


## D

**Dark energy.** Whatever is making the expansion of the universe speed up; it makes up about two-thirds of the cosmic energy budget and remains unidentified. *See* Chapter 78; Chapter 103.

**Dark matter.** Unseen matter that reveals itself only through its gravity, outweighing ordinary matter roughly five to one. *See* Chapter 77.

**Decibel.** A logarithmic unit comparing two power levels, in which every ten decibels means a factor of ten. *See* Chapter 10.

**Decoherence.** The leaking of quantum interference into the environment, which makes large objects behave classically but does not, by itself, pick which outcome occurs. *See* Chapter 152; Chapter 161.

**Derivative.** The instantaneous rate of change of a function, or the slope of its graph at a single point. *See* Chapter 16.

**Dirac equation.** Paul Dirac's 1928 equation for the electron, consistent with special relativity, which accounted for electron spin and predicted antimatter. *See* Chapter 241; Chapter 243.

**DNA.** The double-stranded molecule, wound into a double helix, that stores hereditary information as a sequence of four chemical letters, A, C, G, and T. *See* Chapter 261; Chapter 288; Chapter 290.

**Dominant.** Describes an allele whose effect shows even when only one copy is present. *See* Chapter 298; Chapter 301.

**Doping.** Adding a tiny amount of a chosen impurity to a semiconductor to give it extra electrons or extra holes, which is what makes diodes and transistors possible. *See* Chapter 56.

**Double-slit experiment.** Sending particles one at a time through two openings, which builds up an interference pattern that no single path could produce. *See* Chapter 145; Chapter 158.

## E




**Emergence.** The appearance of large-scale patterns and laws, such as temperature, that are absent in the individual parts but follow from how those parts behave together. *See* Chapter 41; Chapter 170.

**Endosymbiosis.** The engulfing of one cell by another that kept it as a permanent resident; mitochondria and chloroplasts began this way. *See* Chapter 265.

**Entanglement.** A situation in which two or more particles share one quantum state, so their measured results are correlated more strongly than any preset plan allows. *See* Chapter 150; Chapter 159; Chapter 160.

**Entropy.** A measure of how many microscopic arrangements fit what you see on the large scale; it tends to rise, which is why eggs break and do not unbreak. *See* Chapter 30; Chapter 66; Chapter 342.

**Epigenetics.** Chemical marks on DNA and its packaging that switch genes on or off without changing the letters themselves. *See* Chapter 299.

**Equivalence principle.** Einstein's observation that, in a small enough region, free fall in gravity cannot be told apart from floating in empty space. *See* Chapter 36; Chapter 335.

**Eugenics.** The discredited program, launched by Francis Galton, of improving humanity by controlling who reproduces; it led to forced sterilizations and worse. *See* Chapter 304.

**Eukaryote.** An organism whose cells have a nucleus and internal compartments, including all animals, plants, and fungi. *See* Chapter 265.

**Euler's formula.** The identity linking the exponential function to sine and cosine, which lets rotating waves be handled as simple complex numbers. *See* Chapter 9; Chapter 12.

**Event.** In relativity, a single point in spacetime, one place at one moment, such as a firecracker going off. *See* Chapter 62; Chapter 345.

**Event horizon.** The boundary around a black hole beyond which no signal can ever reach an outside observer; it is a fact about which events can connect, not a physical surface. *See* Chapter 80; Chapter 81; Chapter 337.

## F

**Fermion.** A particle, such as the electron or a quark, that refuses to share a quantum state with an identical twin, which is why matter takes up room. *See* Chapter 162; Chapter 238.

**Feynman diagram.** A sketch of particles meeting and exchanging photons that stands for a precise term in a QED calculation. *See* Chapter 164; Chapter 202.


**Fine-structure constant.** The number, about 1/137, that sets the strength of the electromagnetic interaction; it has been measured to great precision, and nobody can say why it has that value. *See* Chapter 164.

**Fitness.** In biology, reproductive success: how many surviving offspring an organism leaves, not how fast it runs or how much it lifts. *See* Chapter 259.

**Fourier transform.** The method of breaking any signal into a sum of pure sine waves of different frequencies, the mathematics behind audio equalizers and MRI scans. *See* Chapter 22.

**Frame of reference.** The viewpoint, with its own rulers and clocks, from which motion is measured; an inertial frame is one that is not accelerating. *See* Chapter 27.

**Function.** A rule that assigns exactly one output to each input, like a machine that turns every number fed in into one number coming out. *See* Chapter 3.

## G

**Gauge symmetry.** Freedom to shift the phase of a field independently at every point without changing anything measurable; demanding it forces electromagnetism into existence. *See* Chapter 197; Chapter 230; Chapter 247.


**Gene.** A stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for one product, usually a protein, and is passed from parent to offspring. *See* Chapter 262; Chapter 293.

**Genotype.** The genetic instructions an organism carries at a given gene or across its whole genome, as opposed to how those instructions are actually expressed. *See* Chapter 298; Chapter 301.

**Genetic drift.** Change in gene frequencies by pure chance rather than selection, strongest in small populations. *See* Chapter 259.

**Genome.** The complete set of DNA in an organism; the human version runs to about three billion letters. *See* Chapter 294; Chapter 322.

**Geodesic.** The straightest possible path through curved spacetime, which freely falling objects follow without any force pushing them. *See* Chapter 36; Chapter 336.

**Germline editing.** Changing the DNA of eggs, sperm, or embryos, so that the change is inherited by all future descendants. *See* Chapter 329.

**Gravitational wave.** A ripple in the shape of spacetime itself, sent out by accelerating masses such as merging black holes and first detected directly in 2015. *See* Chapter 37.

## H

**Hamiltonian.** The expression for a system's total energy, which in quantum mechanics also sets how the state changes in time. *See* Chapter 146; Chapter 228.

**Hawking radiation.** The faint thermal glow that quantum theory predicts a black hole should emit, slowly losing mass; small holes glow hotter than large ones. *See* Chapter 83; Chapter 156.

**Healthspan.** The part of life spent in good health, as distinct from lifespan, which counts every year whether you enjoyed it or not. *See* Chapter 110.

**Heritability.** The share of the variation of a trait within a particular population that is due to genetic differences; it says nothing about any one individual. *See* Chapter 298; Chapter 299.

**Higgs field.** A field filling all space whose interaction with particles gives them mass; its ripple, the Higgs boson, was found in 2012. *See* Chapter 39; Chapter 162.

**Holographic principle.** The idea, suggested by black-hole entropy, that the information in a region can be fully encoded on its boundary rather than throughout its volume. *See* Chapter 83; Chapter 342.

## I



**Inflation.** A proposed burst of extremely rapid expansion in the first instant, which would explain why the universe is so smooth and so flat. *See* Chapter 73; Chapter 74.

**Integral.** The accumulated total of a quantity that changes continuously, such as the area under a curve or the distance covered at a varying speed. *See* Chapter 17.

**Interference.** The adding and canceling of waves or amplitudes, which produces bright and dark stripes and is the signature of quantum behavior. *See* Chapter 145; Chapter 158.

## K

**Kin selection.** Evolution of behavior that helps relatives, because relatives share copies of the same genes. *See* Chapter 262; Chapter 264.


## L

**Lagrangian.** The kinetic energy minus the potential energy of a system; adding it up along a path gives the action, from which the equations of motion follow. *See* Chapter 227.

**Least action.** The principle that a system moves along the path for which the action is stationary; nature is lazy in a mathematically precise way. *See* Chapter 48; Chapter 227.

**Length contraction.** The shortening of a moving object's measured length along its direction of travel; the object itself feels nothing unusual. *See* Chapter 340; Chapter 341.

**Light cone.** The boundary, around any event, separating everything that could have affected it, everything it could affect, and everything out of reach either way. *See* Chapter 63; Chapter 345.

**Limit.** The value a quantity approaches as its input gets ever closer to some point, whether or not it ever arrives. *See* Chapter 15.

**Logarithm.** The power to which a base must be raised to give a number; it turns multiplication into addition. *See* Chapter 9.


**Lorentz factor.** The number that says how strongly time dilates and lengths contract at a given speed; it is almost exactly one at everyday speeds and grows without limit near light speed. *See* Chapter 333; Chapter 340.

**LUCA.** The last universal common ancestor, the population of single cells from which all life on Earth today descends. *See* Chapter 256.

## M


**Many-worlds interpretation.** The reading of quantum mechanics in which the state never collapses and every possible outcome happens, each in its own branch. *See* Chapter 100; Chapter 153.

**Mass extinction.** An event in which a large fraction of all species dies out in a geologically short time; there have been five major ones. *See* Chapter 269.

**Matrix.** A rectangular grid of numbers that represents a linear transformation, such as a rotation or a stretch, or a system of equations. *See* Chapter 25.

**Maxwell's equations.** The four equations that describe all of classical electricity, magnetism, and light. *See* Chapter 186; Chapter 229.

**Measurement problem.** The unsolved question of why a quantum measurement yields one definite result when the governing equation only ever changes the whole list of possibilities. *See* Chapter 153.

**Messenger RNA.** A working copy of a gene that carries its instructions from the DNA to the ribosomes, where proteins are built. *See* Chapter 293.

**Multicellularity.** Life built from many cooperating cells that divide the labor; it evolved independently more than twenty times. *See* Chapter 266.

**Mutation.** A change in the DNA sequence, from a single letter to a large rearrangement, caused by copying errors, radiation, or chemicals. *See* Chapter 295.

## N

**Natural selection.** The process by which heritable traits that help organisms survive and reproduce become more common over generations; nobody designs it, and nobody needs to. *See* Chapter 258; Chapter 259.

**Neutrino.** A nearly massless, electrically neutral particle that interacts so weakly that trillions pass through your body every second. *See* Chapter 45.

**No-cloning theorem.** The proof that no process can make an exact copy of an unknown quantum state while keeping the original, which is what makes quantum cryptography secure. *See* Chapter 154; Chapter 165.

**Noether's theorem.** Emmy Noether's result that every continuous symmetry of nature's laws comes with a conserved quantity, such as energy from symmetry in time. *See* Chapter 46; Chapter 245.

**Nucleosynthesis.** The forging of atomic nuclei; in the first few minutes after the Big Bang it made most of the universe's helium. *See* Chapter 70.

**Nucleotide.** The basic building block of DNA and RNA: a sugar, a phosphate, and one of the four bases, linked end to end to form the strand. *See* Chapter 290; Chapter 291.

## O



**Out of Africa.** The well-supported account that modern humans evolved in Africa and spread across the rest of the world within roughly the last 70,000 years. *See* Chapter 272.

## P

**Panspermia.** The idea that life, or its ingredients, could travel between planets on meteorites or dust, so that Earth's life might have started elsewhere. *See* Chapter 284.

**Pauli exclusion principle.** The rule that no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state, which gives atoms their shell structure and the periodic table its shape. *See* Chapter 162; Chapter 238.

**Phenotype.** The observable traits an organism actually ends up with, produced by its genotype interacting with its environment and development. *See* Chapter 298; Chapter 301.


**Photon.** The smallest possible packet of light or other electromagnetic energy. *See* Chapter 158; Chapter 182.

**Pilot-wave theory.** The interpretation in which every particle always has a definite position and is guided by a wave that never collapses; the price is open nonlocality. *See* Chapter 153.

**Planck's constant.** The tiny number that sets the scale of quantum effects, linking a photon's energy to its frequency. *See* Chapter 158; Chapter 228.

**Polygenic score.** An estimate of genetic risk built by adding up the small effects of many DNA variants; useful for populations and blunt for individuals. *See* Chapter 326.

**Prisoner's dilemma.** A game in which each of two players does better by betraying the other, yet both do worse than if both had cooperated; it models the evolution of cooperation. *See* Chapter 264.

**Proper time.** The time actually ticked off by a clock carried along a particular path through spacetime. *See* Chapter 65; Chapter 340; Chapter 347.

**Protein.** A molecule folded from a chain of amino acids that does most of the work in a cell, from digesting food to building hair. *See* Chapter 293.

## Q

**Quantum computer.** A machine that processes information as qubits and exploits superposition and interference to solve certain problems far faster than ordinary computers. *See* Chapter 166.

**Quantum field.** A quantity spread through all of space whose smallest vibrations are what we call particles; every electron is a ripple in the same electron field. *See* Chapter 162.

**Quantum gravity.** The still-missing theory that would combine quantum mechanics and general relativity, needed to describe black-hole interiors and the first instant. *See* Chapter 38.

**Quantum key distribution.** A method of sharing a secret key in which any eavesdropping disturbs the quantum signals and gives itself away. *See* Chapter 165.

**Quantum Zeno effect.** The freezing of a quantum system's change by measuring it often enough; a watched quantum pot really does not boil, though an ordinary one is unimpressed. *See* Chapter 155.

**Quark.** One of the fundamental particles that make up protons, neutrons, and other particles, bound together in twos or threes by the strong force and never seen alone. *See* Chapter 163.

**Qubit.** A quantum bit, which can be in a superposition of 0 and 1 until measured. *See* Chapter 166.

## R

**Recessive.** Describes an allele whose effect shows only when both copies of the gene carry it. *See* Chapter 298; Chapter 301.

**Red Queen hypothesis.** The idea that species must keep evolving just to stay level with their rivals and parasites, named for the Looking-Glass queen who runs to stay in place. *See* Chapter 263; Chapter 271.

**Redshift.** The stretching of light toward longer, redder wavelengths, which for distant galaxies records how much space has expanded while the light was traveling. *See* Chapter 68.

**Relativity of simultaneity.** The fact that two distant events that happen at the same moment for one observer can happen at different moments for another. *See* Chapter 61; Chapter 334.

**Renormalization.** The procedure that absorbs the infinities of QED into measured values of charge and mass, best seen as translating between descriptions at different scales. *See* Chapter 164; Chapter 210; Chapter 212.


**Ribosome.** The cell's protein factory, which reads messenger RNA three letters at a time and links amino acids in the order it specifies. *See* Chapter 293.

**RNA world.** The hypothesis that early life used RNA both to store information and to speed up chemical reactions, before DNA and proteins took over those jobs. *See* Chapter 255.

## S

**Sanger sequencing.** Frederick Sanger's method for reading DNA letter by letter, which decoded the first genomes. *See* Chapter 306.

**Schrödinger equation.** The 1926 equation that governs how a quantum state changes smoothly in time when no one is measuring it. *See* Chapter 146; Chapter 233.

**S-curve.** The typical shape of a technology's progress, slow at first, then fast, then leveling off, which makes extrapolating from the steep middle a mistake. *See* Chapter 118.

**Selfish gene.** Richard Dawkins's view of evolution from the gene's standpoint, in which bodies are vehicles that genes build to get themselves copied. *See* Chapter 262.

**Semiconductor.** A material, such as silicon, whose ability to conduct electricity can be precisely controlled, the basis of all modern electronics. *See* Chapter 56.

**Sexual selection.** Selection driven by competition for mates, which explains the peacock's tail and other features that are costly for survival. *See* Chapter 263.

**Singularity.** A place where general relativity's description of spacetime breaks down, so that free-fall paths cannot be continued; a sign the theory is incomplete. *See* Chapter 84; Chapter 337.

**Spacetime.** Space and time combined into a single four-dimensional arena, in which every event has a place and a moment. *See* Chapter 35; Chapter 62.

**Special relativity.** Einstein's 1905 theory built on two rules: physics is the same for all steadily moving observers, and so is the speed of light. *See* Chapter 35; Chapter 333.

**Spin.** A built-in quantum angular momentum carried by particles, which comes in fixed amounts and is not literally anything spinning. *See* Chapter 54; Chapter 236.

**Standard Model.** The theory listing the known fundamental particles and the three forces besides gravity that act on them; it works extremely well and is known to be incomplete. *See* Chapter 39.

**Strange loop.** Douglas Hofstadter's term for a system that, by moving through its own levels, ends up referring to itself, which he proposed as the root of a self. *See* Chapter 40; Chapter 275.

**String theory.** A proposal that fundamental particles are tiny vibrating strings, which could unify all the forces including gravity but has not yet made a tested prediction. *See* Chapter 47.

**Strong force.** The force that binds quarks into protons and neutrons and holds atomic nuclei together; at short range it is the strongest of the four forces. *See* Chapter 163.

**Superconductor.** A material that, below a critical temperature, carries current with zero resistance because its electrons move together in one coherent quantum state. *See* Chapter 178; Chapter 217.

**Superposition.** A quantum state that is a weighted combination of several possible outcomes whose amplitudes can still interfere; it is not merely not knowing which is true. *See* Chapter 144; Chapter 161.

**Symmetry.** A change you can make to a system that leaves its laws unchanged; each continuous symmetry comes with a conserved quantity. *See* Chapter 46; Chapter 245.

## T

**Tensor.** A mathematical object describing relationships among several directions at once, whose components change with the coordinates while the object itself does not. *See* Chapter 343; Chapter 346.


**Tidal force.** The stretching that arises because gravity pulls slightly differently on different parts of an object; it is the true signature of curved spacetime. *See* Chapter 81; Chapter 344.

**Time dilation.** The slowing of a moving clock, or of a clock deeper in gravity, compared with another observer's clock. *See* Chapter 35; Chapter 333; Chapter 340.

**Transistor.** A semiconductor device in which a small signal controls a much larger current, used as an amplifier or a switch; a modern chip holds billions. *See* Chapter 56.

**Tunneling.** The ability of a quantum particle to pass through a barrier it lacks the energy to climb over, which powers the Sun and some enzymes. *See* Chapter 158; Chapter 257.

**Twin paradox.** The puzzle of a space traveler who returns younger than the sibling who stayed home; there is no contradiction, because the two took different paths through spacetime. *See* Chapter 65; Chapter 333.

## U

**Uncertainty principle.** Heisenberg's rule that no quantum state can have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum; it is a fact about the state, not about clumsy instruments. *See* Chapter 148; Chapter 158.

**Unitary.** Describes the smooth, reversible change of an undisturbed quantum state, which keeps the total probability at exactly one. *See* Chapter 146.

## V

**Vacuum.** In quantum physics, the lowest-energy state of all fields, which is not empty but full of fluctuations with measurable effects. *See* Chapter 51.

**Vector.** A quantity with both size and direction, such as a velocity or a force, drawn as an arrow. *See* Chapter 14.

**Vector potential.** A field from which the magnetic field can be derived; in quantum mechanics it directly shifts the phase of charged particles. *See* Chapter 177; Chapter 230.

**Virtual particle.** A short-lived intermediate in a quantum calculation, best read as a term in the bookkeeping rather than a tiny object popping in and out of existence. *See* Chapter 51; Chapter 209.


## W

**Wave function.** The mathematical description of a quantum state, assigning an amplitude to every possible position or outcome. *See* Chapter 158; Chapter 231.

**Weak force.** The force responsible for some radioactive decay and for changing one kind of quark into another; it acts only over extremely short distances. *See* Chapter 39.

**Which-path information.** Any record of which route a particle took; the more of it exists, the weaker the interference pattern becomes. *See* Chapter 149.

**Wormhole.** A hypothetical tunnel connecting distant regions of spacetime; the mathematics allows one, but keeping it open would need exotic matter nobody knows how to make. *See* Chapter 43; Chapter 337.
