# The Complete QED Course

*Lessons 1 through 22 are written out in full — complete narrative lessons, each one built from explanations and analogies rather than worked problem sets — and each gets its own entry below. The remaining lessons are planned but not yet written; they appear as a short outline at the end.*

## Lesson 1 — Complex Numbers and Linear Algebra
Quantum mechanics runs on complex numbers because a complex number carries two things at once: a size and a phase. Quantum amplitudes have both. This is the same arrow drawn on a page from the EE course's own complex-numbers chapter, length and compass bearing bundled into one object, except the arrow's length now stands in for something about probability and its bearing carries no meaning on its own at all — only relative to some other arrow's bearing it happens to be compared against. Multiply an amplitude by its own complex conjugate, the same number with the sign of its imaginary part flipped, and the phase drops out, leaving an ordinary non-negative number. That number is a probability. When two routes lead to the same outcome, their amplitudes are added before that step, so their phases decide whether the routes reinforce or cancel. Only the phase difference between them matters. Shift every phase by the same amount and nothing measurable changes, in the same way that two correctly synchronized stopwatches, both started ten minutes apart, will never agree on "what time is it now" yet will agree forever on how much time elapsed between any two events either of them recorded. In polar form a complex number is a length and an angle, and multiplying two of them means multiplying the lengths and adding the angles, which makes phases easy to track.

Linear algebra supplies the stage. A vector is an object in its own right. Writing it as a column of numbers requires choosing a basis, and a different basis gives different numbers for the same vector. An inner product gives vectors a length and a notion of right angles, and a step-by-step recipe turns any set of independent vectors into unit-length, mutually perpendicular ones. A matrix is what an operator looks like once a basis is chosen. Change the basis and the matrix changes, but its trace, determinant and eigenvalues do not, because they belong to the operator and not to the description. Eigenvectors are the directions an operator merely stretches, and eigenvalues are the stretch factors — picture a rubber sheet being pulled taut: most directions marked on it twist as well as stretch when the sheet is pulled, but a couple of special directions only ever get longer or shorter, never twisted, and those are the sheet's eigenvectors. A state of definite spin, energy or momentum is exactly such a direction, and operators that commute share their eigenvectors, so the quantities they represent can have definite values together.

Different operators get different jobs. Those with real eigenvalues describe things you can measure, since measurements return real numbers — no bathroom scale has ever printed a reading of "three plus two i pounds," and any mathematical machine standing in for a real measuring device has to share that same restriction. Those that preserve lengths, and therefore total probability, describe time evolution and changes of basis, in the spirit of shuffling a deck of cards: however thoroughly you shuffle, you still have the same fifty-two cards you started with, just rearranged. Projectors, which squash a vector onto one direction, describe the act of measuring. The Pauli matrices are the first concrete example, a model of spin one-half. Prepare a particle with its spin along one axis, measure it along a perpendicular axis, and each answer comes up half the time: nature's coin flip, built into the mathematics rather than bolted on afterward. They return later inside the electron's own equation.

None of this is warm-up. Multiply a whole quantum state by a phase and every probability stays put. Now demand that this still holds when the phase is allowed to differ from place to place, and the electromagnetic potential is forced into existence to make it work. QED starts there.

## Lesson 2 — Calculus and Differential Equations
Every dynamical law in QED is a differential equation, and every prediction is an integral. An exponential keeps its shape when differentiated: it just gets multiplied by a constant. So for a linear equation with constant coefficients, guess an exponential solution and the calculus collapses into algebra. On a wave, a derivative in space brings down the wave number and a derivative in time brings down the frequency. Quantum mechanics reads those two factors as momentum and energy, which is why derivatives stand in for them throughout QED.

Integration by parts moves a derivative from one factor of a product onto the other, at the price of a minus sign and a leftover boundary term. Fields die away at great distances, so that leftover term is dropped. This keeps the momentum operator well-behaved, with real measured values, and it is how equations of motion are extracted from an action. The bell-curve integral is the most heavily used integral in the subject. Oscillator ground states, wave packets, path integrals and loop integrals all reduce to it, and differentiating with respect to a parameter generates the variants. In vector calculus, the curl of a gradient vanishes and so does the divergence of a curl, the reason electric and magnetic fields can be built from potentials.

An expansion in a small quantity is a controlled approximation. Expand the relativistic energy of a slow particle and out come the rest energy, Newton's kinetic energy, and the first relativistic correction, which shows up in the fine structure of hydrogen. QED itself is an expansion in one small number, the strength of electromagnetism. The transform between position and wave number turns derivatives into plain multiplication, and combined with its own inverse it produces the delta function, a spike of zero width and unit weight that later enforces conservation of energy and momentum in every amplitude — the mathematical idealization of a single, instantaneous hammer strike, all the force delivered in no time at all and at no other time whatsoever, yet still landing with a perfectly finite, nonzero effect. Squeeze a bell curve in position and its transform spreads out in wave number, so a sharp position means a fuzzy momentum. A short, percussive drum hit and a long, sustained flute note make the same trade in sound: the drum hit is sharply localized in time but you'd need a broad spread of pitches, a "click" rather than a note, to build that sharp a hit; the flute note is a nearly pure single pitch but only because it's spread out over a comparatively long stretch of time. Neither instrument is doing anything mysterious — the trade-off is built into what a wave is. That is the uncertainty principle, before any physics has been added.

The response of a system to one sharp kick is the seed of the propagator, which carries a particle from place to place in field theory. The harmonic oscillator is the prototype of every mode of every free field, and a damped one, with a complex frequency, is how an unstable particle looks: the imaginary part is its decay width. A struck tuning fork bundles up the same two facts in one physical object: how quickly it rings, its pitch, and how quickly that ringing dies away into silence. A complex frequency is just those two facts, pitch and fade, folded into a single number, with the ordinary real part giving the pitch and the imaginary part giving the fade. Plane waves solve a wave equation only if frequency and wave number are linked in a fixed way, read straight off the equation. Add a mass and that link becomes the relativistic relation between energy and momentum.

## Lesson 3 — Special Relativity
Two rules carry the whole theory. The laws of physics look the same in every inertial frame, and light travels at the same speed for everyone, however hard they chase it. Under the old Galilean rule for translating between observers, light would look slower to a pursuer, so something has to give, and what gives is time. Space and time get mixed. A moving clock runs slow and a moving rod shrinks along its direction of travel, and each observer says it is the other's clock and rod. There is no contradiction, because observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are simultaneous. Picture two lightbulbs mounted at the two ends of a moving train car, each one wired to fire only when a trigger signal reaches it from a switch sitting at the car's exact center. Because the switch is equidistant from both bulbs and light within the train's own frame travels at the same fixed speed in either direction, the signal reaches both bulbs, and both fire, at the very same instant of the train's own time — so a passenger sitting right at that center switch, equidistant from both bulbs and going nowhere relative to them, naturally sees both flashes arrive at once and calls them simultaneous. A friend stands on the platform, at the fixed spot the switch happens to be passing at the instant it fires. From her fixed vantage the outgoing trigger signal has to chase down the front bulb, which the train's own forward motion keeps carrying further away, while it only has to meet the rear bulb partway, since that same motion carries the rear bulb toward the signal instead. The rear bulb, then, actually fires slightly earlier by her platform clock than the front bulb does, even though the two firings are simultaneous by the train's own reckoning; both flashes then cross roughly the same remaining distance to reach her, so the one with the earlier start — the rear flash — is the one whose light gets to her first. Neither she nor the passenger made an error and no clock is broken; "at the same time" was never a fact about the two flashes themselves, only about who was asking, where they happened to be standing, and — as it turns out — how the flashes were triggered in the first place.

The atmospheric muon is the standard proof. Without time dilation it would travel well under a kilometer before decaying, and with it, several, far enough to reach the ground. From the muon's own point of view, the atmosphere is squashed thin instead. The full translation rule, named for Lorentz, also guarantees that adding speeds never gets past the speed of light.

One quantity survives the mixing: the interval between two events, a sort of distance in which time counts with the opposite sign to space. Everyone agrees on it, and its sign sorts pairs of events into those a slower-than-light signal can connect, those only light can connect, and those nothing can connect. That sorting is causality. Later it becomes the demand that fields at separated points cannot disturb each other, one of the reasons antiparticles must exist. Time measured along a particle's own path, its proper time, is agreed on by everyone too. Energy and momentum share the same structure: one combination of the two is the same for every observer, and it fixes the particle's mass. A massless particle such as the photon must always travel at light speed, has no rest frame, and carries energy set by its frequency.

Natural units set the speed of light and the reduced Planck constant both to one, so every quantity becomes a power of energy. In these units the strength of electromagnetism is a pure number, about one part in 137. Because it is small, QED can be calculated as a series of ever-smaller corrections instead of being solved outright. The energy available for making new particles grows in step with beam energy in a collider, but only as its square root for a beam hitting a stationary target, which is why colliders win. A particle splitting in two hands each piece a fixed energy, while a split in three gives a continuous spread, which is how the neutrino was inferred. The ordinary quantum wave equation cannot obey any of this, and QED begins by asking what replaces it.

## Lesson 4 — Four Vectors and Lorentz Transformations
Tracking relativistic effects by hand, one space dimension at a time, cannot cope with QED, which juggles four coordinates, four momentum components, four potential components and a field with sixteen entries. Four-vector notation makes the bookkeeping automatic. Every quantity carries labels that run over the four directions of spacetime, and a rule called the metric converts between the two kinds of label by flipping the sign of the space parts and leaving time alone. A label that appears twice, once up and once down, is summed over. Use up every label that way and the result is a number every observer agrees on. More generally, an equation whose leftover labels match on both sides can be seen to hold in every frame just by looking at it. Some textbooks use the opposite sign convention, which is a fine way to lose an afternoon.

The changes of frame that respect all this are the Lorentz transformations, those that preserve the interval. They are the mixed-sign cousin of ordinary rotations, and a boost to a moving frame is a rotation of a hyperbolic kind that mixes time with space. Rotating an ordinary photograph mixes up its "up-down" and "left-right" pixels in a fixed, reversible way without changing anything about the picture's actual content, only the label attached to each direction. A boost is the same sort of mixing, except it swaps time and space into each other rather than two space directions, and does it by stretching rather than by turning in a circle — which is exactly why speeds can add up strangely under a boost in a way two ordinary rotated directions never would. Add shifts in space and time and you have the larger family that physical laws are required to respect. The shifts lead to conservation of energy and momentum, the rotations to angular momentum and spin. The wave operator, a combination of second derivatives in time and space, is unchanged by any boost, so a wave equation built from it plus a mass term is automatically consistent with relativity. By the same test the ordinary quantum wave equation, with one time derivative against two space derivatives, fails on sight.

Objects with more labels, called tensors, transform with one Lorentz matrix per label. An antisymmetric one with two labels has exactly six independent parts, the same count as the electric and magnetic fields together. They are two faces of one field object, and a boost mixes them, so a moving observer sees a magnetic field where a resting one sees only an electric field. Think of a tin can viewed from directly above, where it looks like a plain circle, and the same can viewed from the side, where it looks like a rectangle: nobody would say the can secretly changed shape between the two glances, only that "circle" and "rectangle" were never separate objects to begin with, just two silhouettes of one three-dimensional thing seen from two different angles. Electric and magnetic fields are exactly that kind of silhouette, cast by one underlying field object, with your state of motion playing the part of the viewing angle.

Energy and momentum likewise form one four-vector whose invariant length is the particle's mass, and conserving it in a collision is a single equation true in every frame. A plane wave written with the right sign convention lets derivatives pull energy and momentum straight out of it, and its opposite-sign twin is the source of the antiparticle interpretation. Finally, a two-body collision has three frame-independent quantities: the square of the center-of-momentum energy, the square of the momentum transferred, and a third fixed by the other two. Every QED cross-section is written in terms of them.

## Lesson 5 — Classical Mechanics and Lagrangians
Newton's law is written in Cartesian coordinates, wants every force as a vector, and hides the link between symmetries and conservation laws. QED is built on a single scalar instead, the Lagrangian, which for a particle in a potential is kinetic energy minus potential energy in whatever coordinates suit. Add it up along a path and you have the action. Hamilton's principle says the path a system really takes is the one where the action is stationary: nudge the path slightly, endpoints held fixed, and the action changes only at second order. A lifeguard racing to a drowning swimmer offers a decent feel for what "the system picks the best path" is like: running is faster than swimming, so the fastest route to the swimmer isn't a straight line at all, but a bent path that spends more distance on sand and less in water, exactly the trade a good lifeguard makes without doing any algebra. Push on the picture a little harder, though, and it comes apart in two ways worth keeping straight: a lifeguard is actually choosing, deciding among options and picking the quickest one, while a rolling ball or an orbiting planet is choosing nothing at all — it simply behaves the one way it behaves, and it only looks retrospectively as though a choice were made. And a lifeguard's route is a true minimum, the fastest of all options, while the action, in general, is merely stationary, not necessarily least, and the name "least action" is a historical accident. The equations of motion that follow work in any coordinates. A pendulum's fixed rod needs no special treatment, since choosing the angle as the coordinate builds the constraint in. For a particle in a potential Newton's law comes back out, and the minus sign matters: kinetic plus potential would send the particle accelerating uphill, which is not what hills are for.

Two Lagrangians that differ by a total time derivative, or by an overall constant factor, give identical motion, a freedom field theory uses to discard surface terms. A coordinate that never appears in the Lagrangian has a conserved momentum attached. A potential depending only on distance from a center conserves angular momentum with no mention of torque, and a Lagrangian with no explicit time dependence conserves energy. These are the first examples of symmetry producing conservation, which later becomes a general rule. The relativistic free particle has an action proportional to its proper time, and demanding that this be the same for every observer delivers the correct relativistic momentum and energy without assuming them. Its actual path maximizes proper time, which is the twin paradox in one sentence.

For a charged particle, write down a Lagrangian containing only the electric and magnetic potentials, no forces at all, and the whole Lorentz force falls out, magnetic part included. The momentum paired with position is no longer mass times velocity but picks up an extra contribution from the vector potential, rather like a bank balance that turns out to include not just the cash in your pocket but a running tab already built up with the field you happen to be moving through — the first hint that potentials, not fields, are the fundamental quantities. In covariant form a charge couples to the potential through the only scalar available that is linear in it, and that coupling is the classical seed of the electron-photon interaction in QED.

Coupled oscillators split into independent normal modes, each swinging at a single frequency. Let a chain of masses and springs grow infinitely fine, and the discrete coordinates become a continuous field obeying a wave equation. That is the bridge from particles to fields.

## Lesson 6 — Hamiltonian Mechanics
The Lagrangian is the best language for writing a theory down and spotting its symmetries. The Hamiltonian is the best language for quantizing it. Moving between them means trading velocity for momentum as the basic variable. For ordinary systems the Hamiltonian is the total energy expressed through position and momentum, and it stays constant whenever the rules do not change with time. The trade fails when momentum cannot be turned back into velocity, which signals constraints, and the electromagnetic field is the important case: its gauge freedom is why gauge fixing will be needed. Hamilton's equations replace each second-order equation with two first-order ones, and together they carry the system through phase space, the space of all positions and momenta. Trajectories there never cross, much like train tracks properly laid out in a rail yard so that no two trains, scheduled on different tracks, ever collide at a junction: if two phase-space trajectories ever did cross, the system sitting at that exact crossing point would have no way of knowing which route to continue along, so determinism itself is what forbids the crossing. That's the classical version of a quantum state determining its own future. A cloud of possible states changes shape but never volume, rather like a lump of dough that can be rolled, stretched, or folded into any shape a baker likes without the total amount of dough ever changing — the classical cousin of quantum probability being conserved. Phase-space area comes in units of Planck's constant, the earliest hint of quantized energy levels and the origin of the recurring factors of two pi in momentum integrals.

A bracket operation on phase-space quantities governs how any of them changes in time, and it obeys the same algebra as the commutator in quantum mechanics. Conserved quantities generate the very symmetries they belong to: momentum shifts a system along, angular momentum rotates it, and energy moves it forward in time. That is the link between symmetry and conservation in Hamiltonian dress.

Two examples matter most for QED. Rewrite the oscillator using a complex combination of position and momentum, and its energy becomes a simple product while its motion becomes a steady rotation at positive frequency. Quantized, those variables become the creation and annihilation operators, and a free field is infinitely many such oscillators, one for each momentum. A charged particle's Hamiltonian follows from a single substitution, replacing momentum by momentum minus charge times potential. That minimal coupling is the classical forerunner of the covariant derivative in QED.

Quantization itself is one rule: replace each bracket by the corresponding commutator, scaled by Planck's constant. What the rule does not settle is the order of quantities that no longer commute. Different orderings differ by a constant, and for the oscillator that constant is the zero-point energy, which is why even a quantum oscillator in its lowest state is not quite still. In field theory the constant is infinite, and normal ordering is the tidy way of removing it. The electron field follows the same steps with anticommutators in place of commutators.

## Lesson 7 — Maxwell's Equations
Four rules run all of classical electricity and magnetism. Electric charge is the source of the electric field, so field lines begin on positive charge and end on negative — much like a park fountain and its drain, water springing outward from one and disappearing down the other, with a beginning and an end clearly marked. Magnetic field lines get no such luxury. They never begin and never end, because nobody has ever found an isolated magnetic pole; a magnetic field line is less like water between a fountain and a drain and more like the loop of a racetrack, endlessly closed on itself with no start line and no finish line anywhere along it. A magnetic field that changes in time drives the electric field around in circles, which is how every power station earns its living. A current drives the magnetic field around in circles, which is how every electromagnet works. Add the rule for how fields push on charges and the loop closes: fields move charges, and charges make fields.

The last rule was broken until Maxwell mended it. Charge a capacitor through a wire and the current runs up to one plate and stops dead at the gap. The old rule then says the magnetic field around the wire depends on which surface you stretch across it, a flat disk or a balloon passing between the plates, which is plainly absurd. Maxwell's repair was to let a changing electric field make a magnetic field too. Between the plates the changing field carries exactly as much "current" as the wire does, and the absurdity vanishes. He added it because the equations demanded it, not because anyone had seen it. As a bonus, conservation of charge drops straight out: charge can move, but it cannot be created or destroyed.

The payoff is light. In empty space a changing magnetic field makes an electric field, a changing electric field makes a magnetic one, each holds the other up, and the pair sails off with nothing pushing. Their speed depends only on two constants you can measure with charges and currents on a workbench, no lamps required. Maxwell worked out the number and it matched the speed of light. Light is an electromagnetic wave, and two whole subjects collapsed into one. The equations also never say who is doing the measuring, a loose thread Einstein would later pull.

The wave has a shape worth keeping. The electric and magnetic parts wiggle at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, rising and falling together. There are two independent sideways directions, so there are two polarizations, and the photon inherits precisely that count. QED is this theory made quantum. Even the units get tidied: sweep the fudge factors away and the only number left is the strength of the coupling, about one part in 137.

## Lesson 8 — Electromagnetic Potentials and Gauge Freedom
Because magnetic field lines never end, the magnetic field can always be written as the swirl of some other field, the vector potential. Because a changing magnetic field drives the electric field, the electric field can then be written as the downhill slope of a voltage, plus a correction for how fast the vector potential is changing. Do that and two of Maxwell's four rules are satisfied automatically, whatever potentials you pick. Six field numbers at every point are traded for four potential numbers, and the two rules involving charges and currents become the only equations left to solve.

The catch is that the potentials are not unique. Add the slope of any function to the vector potential, subtract the matching rate of change from the voltage, and the electric and magnetic fields do not budge. Think of measuring a mountain's height from sea level or from your kitchen floor. The slopes come out the same either way. Gauge freedom is that idea with the floor allowed to move from place to place and moment to moment, provided the two potentials are adjusted in step. It is not a symmetry relating two different situations. It is one situation described in many equivalent ways, and the excess information in the potentials is exactly the freedom to choose. A voltage that wobbles in time but is the same everywhere describes empty space just as well as no voltage at all.

One choice of gauge gives each potential its own wave equation with the charges as the source, so disturbances travel at the speed of light and the potential here and now reflects the charges as they were one light-travel time ago. Wiggle a charge and the potential far away follows, late by just the time light takes to cross the gap. Another choice makes the voltage respond instantly to the charges, which sounds like a breach of causality. It is not, because the voltage alone cannot be measured. The delay reappears in the vector potential, and the electric field, which you can measure, stays properly causal.

Why bother? Because quantum mechanics writes the motion of a charged particle in terms of the potentials, not the fields. Change gauge and the particle's wave function must change its phase in step, or the physics would change. It's the same relativity of reference point Chapter 5 used for voltage — measuring from sea level or your kitchen floor — turning up again here, except this time the freely movable reference is a phase rather than an elevation. Now demand more: let that phase be adjustable independently at every point in space. The equations survive only if a compensating field exists to soak up the difference, and that field behaves precisely like the electromagnetic potential. Local phase freedom forces the electromagnetic field to exist. That one argument is the spine of QED.

## Lesson 9 — Wave Functions and Hilbert Spaces
Fire a thousand identically prepared electrons at a screen and each lands somewhere different, though the pattern of landings has a definite shape. Quantum mechanics accepts this and drops the track altogether. A particle gets a wave function instead, a complex number at every point in space. Square its size and you have the probability of finding the particle there. Add that up over all space and you must get one, because the particle is somewhere. Nothing derives this rule. You adopt it because nature keeps agreeing. The overall phase of the wave function means nothing by itself, but the phase of one part relative to another decides how the parts interfere.

The mathematics behind it is friendlier than it sounds. Wave functions can be added, scaled and compared, so they behave as vectors with infinitely many components, and a state with total probability one is an arrow of length one. Choose any set of mutually perpendicular states and the arrow splits into pieces along them. The squared size of each piece is the probability of that outcome, and the probabilities add to one for the same reason the squares of the two short sides of a right triangle add up to the square of the long one. The wave function is only one set of coordinates for the arrow, the set you get by asking where. Ask about energy instead and you get different coordinates for the same state. A picture to keep: a plain arch, the kind a child draws for a hill, laid across a box carries better than 99.8 percent of its probability in the lowest energy state, so an energy measurement would almost always find the ground state.

Probability also flows. It cannot vanish here and reappear there, so it slides from place to place like charge, obeying the same local bookkeeping. And when two possibilities are added, the probability of the sum is not the sum of the probabilities. Drop two stones into a still pond at once and watch the ripples spread and cross: where two overlapping ripples both happen to be rising, the water there climbs higher than either ripple alone would have made it, and where one ripple rises while the other falls, they can partly or entirely cancel, leaving the water flatter than a single ripple would have left it. Adding two quantum amplitudes and squaring the result produces exactly that kind of reinforcement and cancellation in the probabilities — a cross term appears, positive or negative depending on the relative phase, with no counterpart at all in simply adding up two independent chances, the way you'd add two customers' separate chances of showing up at a shop. That is interference, the double-slit pattern in embryo. Every scattering calculation in QED adds amplitudes first and squares afterward.

One warning. The spread in a particle's position describes the scatter across many identically prepared copies. It says nothing about the precision of a single measurement, or about clumsy instruments.

## Lesson 10 — Operators and Observables
Every quantity you can measure is a machine that acts on the wave function. The position machine multiplies it by the position. The momentum machine takes its slope, which is why a state of definite momentum is a pure wave: the tighter the crinkles, the more momentum. Measure something and the only answers you can ever get are the special values for which the machine leaves a state's shape alone and merely rescales it. Afterward the system sits in the matching state, so measuring again straight away gives the same answer. For the answers to be real numbers, and for different answers to belong to perpendicular states, the machine must be of a well-behaved kind, and every observable is. The average you quote is the weighted mean of the possible answers, weighted by their probabilities. It belongs to many identical runs, never to a single one.

Now the twist: order matters. Multiply by position and then take the slope, and you get something different from taking the slope first. It's the same reason putting on a sock and then a shoe gives a working outfit while putting on a shoe and then a sock does not: two operations can each be perfectly sensible on their own and still refuse to give the same result, or even the same kind of result, depending on which one goes first. The difference is never zero. It is a fixed amount set by Planck's constant, whatever the state. Quantities whose order makes no difference can be sharp together. Position and momentum cannot. That is the uncertainty principle, and its honest reading is about ensembles. Prepare many copies identically, measure position on some and momentum on the rest, and the two spreads can never both be small. Their product has a floor, and the bell-shaped packet is the one shape that sits exactly on it. This is not a story about clumsy instruments jostling a single particle.

The prize is conservation. How fast the average of a quantity changes depends on whether it matters if you apply that quantity before or after the energy machine. If it makes no difference, the quantity never changes. A free particle keeps its momentum. A particle in a central force keeps its angular momentum. A potential that looks the same on both sides of the origin keeps mirror-image symmetry, so its energy states sort themselves into even and odd. Hunting for conservation laws is hunting for things that commute with the energy.

QED will reuse the trick wholesale. Swap the classical brackets for these order-dependence rules and you have the recipe for quantizing anything, fields included.

## Lesson 11 — The Schrödinger Equation
Something must say how a wave function changes in time, and that something is energy. The rate of change of the wave function is set by the energy machine acting on it. This single rule is linear, so a mixture of solutions is itself a solution, and it keeps total probability at one forever. Take the special solutions first. In a stationary state the shape never changes; only a phase spins, at a rate set by the energy, so every probability stays put. Think of an ordinary clock: its hands sweep around steadily and never stop, yet the face itself, the printed numbers you actually read, looks identical in any photograph taken at any moment. A stationary state is like that clock face — a phase sweeps around underneath, invisibly, while every measurable probability sits frozen, exactly as still as the numbers on the dial. Squeeze a particle into a box and the allowed energies are those where a whole number of half-waves fit, rising as the square of that number. Now mix two of them. The relative phase turns at a rate set by the energy difference, and the probabilities start to move. A particle in an equal mix of the lowest two box states sloshes to and fro, once per beat, and the word is not just a metaphor: mix two musical tones of close but slightly different pitch and you hear a slow throb rising and falling at the difference between their frequencies, the very same throbbing exchange that a mix of two energy levels produces in probability rather than loudness. In a system with fixed rules, all change is beating between energies.

A free particle is a packet of waves with different momenta. The packet drifts at the classical speed, momentum over mass, and it spreads, because the faster components pull ahead of the slower ones. The spreading time is the mass times the squared width, divided by the reduced Planck constant. For an electron pinned to atomic size that is about a ten-thousandth of a trillionth of a second, which is why an atomic electron is not a tiny planet on a track and must be described by stationary states. For a one-gram bead held to a millimeter it is roughly three hundred quadrillion years, so beads behave classically.

The oscillator earns the most attention. Its energies come in equal steps above a floor that is not zero, and two operators climb and descend the ladder one step at a time. A free field is one such oscillator for every momentum and polarization, and a particle is one step up a ladder. Even the ground state keeps half a step of energy, a leftover that shows up as a measurable force between uncharged metal plates.

Finally, the equation cannot be the last word. It is first order in time and second order in space, so a change of frame scrambles it. It belongs to Galileo's world, not Einstein's, and it is the itch that leads on to Dirac.

## Lesson 12 — Momentum and Position
Position and momentum are two ways of describing the same state, connected by the trick of taking any shape apart into pure waves. The momentum description is a second wave function whose squared size gives the chance of each momentum, and it carries exactly the same information as the first. Nothing gained, nothing lost, like giving a location as a street address or as latitude and longitude. In the momentum description, momentum is plain multiplication and position becomes a slope, the mirror image of the usual arrangement, yet the rule that order matters comes out identical. It has to, because that rule belongs to the state, not to the coordinates used to write it down.

Why bother with a second description? Because in it a free particle is trivial. Each momentum component just turns its own phase at its own rate, and no component pushes on any other. Put the components back together and you recover the drifting, spreading packet, with the spreading now visible as faster components running ahead. That is why free particles will be handled in momentum terms from here on, and why every mode sum in QED is a sum over momenta.

Uncertainty is now geometry. A shape and its decomposition into waves cannot both be narrow. Build a sharp spike and you need waves of every wavelength; use a single wave and you have something spread over all space. Squeeze one and the other swells. The bell curve is the one shape that strikes the best bargain, and the lowest state of the oscillator is that bell curve in both descriptions at once, jittering at the minimum the rules allow. It is the same state that becomes the vacuum of a quantum field.

The picture to keep is the propagator, the amplitude for a free particle to get from one place to another in a given time. Its size is the same for every destination. Release a particle at a single point and, in this nonrelativistic theory, it is equally likely to be found anywhere at the very next instant: perfect knowledge of position means unbounded momentum. That is the theory overreaching, since nothing may outrun light, and it hints at why a relativistic version is needed. Still, the propagator is the direct ancestor of the one that drives every Feynman diagram.

## Lesson 13 — Angular Momentum
Turn a book a quarter turn about one axis, then a quarter turn about another. Now do it in the opposite order. The book ends up in a different place. Rotations do not commute, and angular momentum, which generates rotations, inherits the habit. The three components of angular momentum cannot all be sharp together, because swapping the order of two of them leaves a remainder equal to the third, scaled by Planck's constant.

What can be sharp is the total size of the angular momentum and its component along one chosen axis, and pure algebra delivers the whole spectrum without a single differential equation. Ladder operators step the component up or down by exactly one unit, the basic quantum of angular momentum, and leave the total alone — much like a staircase with fixed-height steps: you can go up one step or down one step, never half a step, and climbing or descending the staircase says nothing at all about how tall the building itself is. A ladder that steps in whole units and cannot run forever needs a top rung and a bottom rung, placed symmetrically about zero. Call the top rung the quantum number. The squared length of the angular momentum is that number times one more than that number, in the unit squared. That is always bigger than the top rung squared, so the arrow can never lie along the chosen axis. Its length always beats its longest shadow, and the leftover is a sideways jitter that uncertainty insists on. A classical spinning top has no such problem.

Whole numbers or halves? The algebra allows both. But orbital motion, a particle going around a center, must have a wave function that returns to itself after one full turn about the axis, and that forces whole numbers. The shapes on a sphere that satisfy this are the spherical harmonics, which give atomic orbitals their spheres, dumbbells and more elaborate lobes. Whole-number top rungs always give an odd count of projections: one, three, five. Keep that in your pocket. It is about to matter.

Rotational symmetry pays a dividend. If the force depends only on distance from the center, nothing changes under rotation, angular momentum commutes with energy, and it is conserved. That is why hydrogen's energy states can be labeled by total angular momentum and its component. And the ladder method is a template: any operators that follow the same rotation rules have the spectrum found here, whatever they are made of, which is how the same trick will solve spin next.

## Lesson 14 — Spin and Pauli Matrices
Send silver atoms through a magnetic field that is stronger at the top than at the bottom and the beam splits in two. Not a smear, which is what a classical spinning magnet would give, and not three or five spots, which is what any whole-number orbital angular momentum would give. Two. Silver's outer electron has no orbital motion to speak of and still carries angular momentum. That is spin, and for the electron it is one-half of the basic unit. It is not a little ball turning. It is an intrinsic quantum label, and it fits the smallest possible ladder: two rungs, no more. Electrons, protons, neutrons and quarks all have it. The photon has spin one but, being massless, only two of its three possible states, the same count as light's two polarizations.

Spin is represented by half the basic unit times the Pauli matrices, small two-by-two tables of numbers that obey the same rotation rules as orbital angular momentum. A spin state is a pair of complex numbers, and measuring along any axis you choose can give only up or down along that axis. Prepare an electron definitely up along one axis and along a perpendicular axis it is a fair coin toss. A spinning coin, caught mid-air before it lands, is the natural comparison, and it very nearly works, except in the one respect where it's flatly wrong, and that wrongness is the entire point. An ordinary coin mid-flip already secretly is heads or tails, tumbling along a path that a sufficiently fast camera could, in principle, call before it ever lands; its outcome along that "sideways" axis is hidden information, not missing information. An electron prepared spin-up along one axis and then asked about a perpendicular axis carries no equivalent hidden answer waiting to be uncovered. There is no faster camera, no finer instrument, no secret setting sitting on the electron that anything could have read off in advance. The question genuinely has no answer until it's asked, and only half the time does asking it produce "up." Run it as an experiment. Prepare spin up along a sideways axis, pass the beam through a vertical filter, and half comes out up, half down. Take the up beam through a sideways filter again and it splits in half again. The vertical measurement wiped out the sideways knowledge — not because it clumsily disturbed some pre-existing sideways value, but because asking the vertical question is exactly what stripped the sideways question of its own claim to have an answer at all. Two components of spin can never be sharp together.

Spin has another oddity. Turn a spin one-half through one full circle and its state comes back multiplied by minus one. Only two full turns restore it, the same one-extra-lap oddity a strip of paper shows once you twist it and glue the ends into a Möbius band: trace a finger along an ordinary loop and one trip around brings you back to your starting side, but trace the same finger along the twisted band and it takes two full trips before you're back where you actually started. A lone electron cannot show this, since an overall sign carries no probability, but interference between two paths can.

Two spin one-half particles combine into a triplet of total spin one, symmetric under swapping the particles, and a singlet of total spin zero, antisymmetric. Measure one half of a singlet pair and find up, and the other, along the same axis, is certain to be down. In general, totals run from the difference of the two spins to their sum in unit steps. An electron with one unit of orbital angular momentum and spin one-half has a total of one-half or three-halves, and the gap between those two is the doublet fine structure of atomic spectra. The electron's relativistic equation will need four components, built from two-component pieces like these.

## Lesson 15 — Perturbation Theory

Almost nothing worth calculating can be solved exactly, so physicists cheat, honestly. Solve a nearby problem that can be done precisely, treat the difference as a small nudge, and correct the answer layer by layer. The first layer is charmingly simple: a level's energy shifts by the nudge averaged over the state being nudged. The second layer mixes in other states, the nearer in energy the more, and always pushes connected levels apart. The lower one sinks, the upper one rises, like strangers on a bench each shuffling away from the other.

The recipe breaks when states share the very same energy, because it divides by the gap and the gap is zero. The cure is to work out first which mixtures of the tied states the nudge prefers. Put hydrogen in an electric field. Its second shell holds four states of equal energy, and the field connects only two: a round one and a dumbbell pointing along the field. Mixed evenly, they form lopsided clouds with a built-in electric dipole, one rising and one falling in step with the field. The other two stay put, so one level splits into three. The ground state, with no partner, responds only to the square of the field.

Fine structure is the same method on the real atom. The electron moves fast enough for relativity to nudge its kinetic energy, and, circling the proton, it feels in its own frame a magnetic field that grips its spin — the same tin-can trick from Lesson 4, electric and magnetic fields as two faces of one object, showing up here as a genuinely new magnetic field that only exists because the electron's own frame is moving relative to the proton's. Together they make the energy depend on total angular momentum, orbit and spin combined, instead of on the orbit's shape alone. The effect is smaller than the main energy by about the square of the fine-structure constant, roughly one part in nineteen thousand: a few hundred-thousandths of an electron-volt, against level spacings of an electron-volt or more.

This is QED in miniature. The small number controlling the expansion is the fine-structure constant, about one part in 137, and each extra photon exchanged costs about another factor of it. The series does not strictly converge; past some order, adding terms makes things worse. Nobody minds. The first few terms already agree with experiment to better than one part in a billion, which is more than can be said for most weather forecasts.

## Lesson 16 — Identical Particles

Two electrons are not merely hard to tell apart; they have no individual identity at all. Identical twins are the natural everyday comparison, and they fall short in exactly the one way that matters: however alike two twins look, there remains, at least in principle, some fact of the matter about which twin is which — a hidden birthmark, a DNA test, a continuously trackable path each of them has walked since the day they were born, that a good enough observer could in principle follow to tell them apart. Two electrons share no such fact, even in principle. No tag, no serial number, no experiment that could say which electron in a helium atom is the one that was there a moment ago — not because nobody has built a good enough instrument yet, but because there is no fact of the matter sitting there for any instrument to uncover. So swapping two identical particles cannot change anything observable, and swapping twice restores everything. That leaves one swap with only two possible effects on the state: it returns unchanged, or it returns with its sign flipped. Nature uses both. The unchanged kind, bosons, have whole-number spin, photons among them. The flipped kind, fermions, have half-number spin: electrons, protons, neutrons, quarks. There is no third kind.

The sign flip does all the work. Build a two-fermion state as first particle here and second there, minus second here and first there. Put both in the same state and the pieces cancel, leaving nothing. That is Pauli's exclusion principle, and it is arithmetic, not an extra law. Electrons pile into shells rather than sinking into the lowest orbit, chemistry gets its periodic table, matter takes up room, and a white dwarf star is held up against its own gravity by electrons refusing to share states. Bosons face no such rule and rather like company.

The surprise is exchange energy. Two electrons repel, and the repulsion knows nothing about spin. Yet the whole state must flip on a swap, which ties the spin arrangement to the spatial arrangement. Parallel spins force the spatial part to flip, so the electrons stay out of each other's way, repel less, and sit lower in energy — somewhat like two commuters who've learned, without any explicit agreement, to always take opposite ends of a crowded platform, not because either was told to, but because the arrangement itself makes their shared commute a little less unpleasant. A force blind to spin has produced an energy that depends on it. That is why electrons in different orbitals prefer parallel spins. In helium's ground state both share one orbital, so the spatial part cannot flip and the spins must point opposite ways: exclusion seen from the other side.

For QED, two things carry forward. Pauli's rule is here simply declared; once the electron becomes a quantum field, its algebra builds the minus sign in automatically. And when two electrons scatter, there are two indistinguishable routes to one result, and one carries a minus sign relative to the other, where bosons would add them instead. Why spin and this behavior go together is, for now, observation; the proof waits for relativity.

## Lesson 17 — Klein-Gordon Equation

Schrödinger built his equation from the slow-motion energy formula. Use the honest relativistic one instead, in which energy squared is momentum squared plus mass squared, swap energy and momentum for rates of change in time and space, and out comes the Klein-Gordon equation. Time and space appear alike, two derivatives each, so it passes the relativity test that Schrödinger's equation fails. The slow-motion limit works too: strip off the fast wobble at the rest-energy frequency, drop the second time derivative, and Schrödinger's equation reappears. For an electron with one electron-volt of kinetic energy, the discarded piece is about two parts in a million.

Then come the bills. Being second order in time, the equation gives every momentum two plane waves, one of positive energy and one of negative, much as a pendulum equation gives two roots and both count. Negative energy has no floor, so a particle could shed light and fall forever. Worse, the density that counted probability in Schrödinger's theory, rebuilt here, is positive for the positive-energy wave and negative for the other, and a mixture takes whichever sign wins. Negative probability is nonsense. It is not a clumsy definition either: a second-order equation needs the wave and its rate of change as starting data, and that extra freedom lets the density go either way.

Chopping out the negative-energy waves by hand fails, since any interaction mixes them back in. The way out was to keep the equation and change the reading, much like discovering that a gauge you'd built to measure one thing was faulty for that job, only to realize it had been correctly measuring something else entirely all along. Stop treating the wave as the probability of finding one particle; treat it as a field. The awkward density becomes electric charge density, which may sensibly be negative because charge comes in two signs, and the negative-energy waves become antiparticles. Read this way, the equation is exactly right for charged particles with no spin, the charged pion among them.

It is also a template: a relativistic wave equation, its plane waves, a troubled single-particle reading, and a rescue by promotion to a field. That plot runs again for the electron, which has spin and needs Dirac's equation instead.

## Lesson 18 — Why the Schrödinger Equation Is Not Enough

Two equations, two failures. Schrödinger's has one time derivative against two space derivatives, an imbalance no change of observer can respect. Klein-Gordon balances them, but being second order in time it lets probability go negative. What is wanted is an equation that is first order in time, so today's wave settles tomorrow's and the density is a plain sum of squares, yet still honors the relativistic relation between energy, momentum and mass. Easy to wish for. Hard to grant.

The obvious move, taking the square root of the relativistic formula before turning momentum into derivatives, gets the slow-motion limit right. But in ordinary space the root unrolls into an infinite string of ever higher derivatives, so the wave's value here would depend on its values arbitrarily far away. That is an equation that gossips with the entire universe.

Dirac's way around was to leave the root alone and look for something linear in momentum and mass whose square gives precisely the right relation, with no leftovers. Squaring a sum throws up cross terms, so any two coefficients must cancel when multiplied in one order and then the other, while each squares to one. Ordinary numbers cannot manage it: if two numbers multiply to zero, one of them is zero, and the scheme collapses. Matrices can, since they do not care about order, the same order-sensitivity already met back in Lesson 10's sock-and-shoe example: multiplying two matrices in one order can give a different answer than multiplying them the other way around, including landing on zero from two things that are each individually nonzero. So the coefficients must be matrices, and the wave function must have several components.

How many? The Pauli matrices supply three suitable coefficients, but no fourth exists among two-by-two matrices, and a fourth is needed, for mass. Odd sizes are out because each matrix must pair its states off one for one. The smallest that works has four rows and four columns, so the wave function carries four numbers at every point. Spin was never put in. Those four will sort themselves into two spin orientations for the particle and two for its antiparticle, forced by nothing but a first-order equation that respects relativity. It is a habit of theoretical physics, and it returns: turn a vague demand into precise algebra, solve it, and see what the solution insists on.

## Lesson 19 — Dirac Equation

Put the four matrices of the last lesson where the coefficients belong and you have Dirac's equation: first order in time and space, acting on a wave function with four components. Multiply through by the mass matrix, call the results the gamma matrices, and the whole equation fits on one short line that looks the same to every observer. The gamma matrices carry the shape of spacetime in their multiplication rules: each squares to a plain number, plus for time and minus for space, and swapping any two different ones flips the sign. The geometry of relativity has been smuggled into matrices.

A sanity check: apply the operator twice and out comes the Klein-Gordon equation, for each of the four components. So every solution honors the relativistic relation between energy, momentum and mass. Dirac's equation really is a square root of Klein-Gordon, extracted by algebra instead of by an infinite sprawl of derivatives.

The electron at rest is the memorable case. There are four solutions. Two have energy equal to the rest energy, one for each spin orientation. Two have minus the rest energy, again one for each spin. Spin appears without being asked. The negative-energy pair is there too, uninvited, and a first-order equation does not make it leave. Set the electron moving and the lower pair of components becomes tied to the upper pair, in proportion to the momentum, rather like a faint echo that rides along behind a shout: barely noticeable when the shout is soft, and steadily louder the harder the shout is thrown. For a slow electron the tie is about half its speed as a fraction of the speed of light, so the lower pair stays tiny.

Three questions are left hanging on purpose: whether the density is finally positive, how spin is organized, and what to make of the negative energies. The next lessons answer them. Meanwhile the positive-energy and negative-energy solutions are the very spinors that will stand for incoming and outgoing electrons and positrons in every Feynman diagram, and the equation itself becomes the free-electron part of QED's master formula.

## Lesson 20 — Gamma Matrices

The gamma matrices are less a topic than a toolkit, and the whole kit follows from one rule: each squares to a plain number fixed by spacetime's geometry, and swapping two different ones flips the sign. No explicit matrices are needed. Add up each gamma matrix times its own mirror image over all four directions and you get the number of dimensions, four. Sandwich any momentum, written in gamma-matrix form, between a gamma matrix and its twin and it returns with its sign flipped and its size doubled. Two moves, and long chains shrink.

Multiply all four together, with a factor of the imaginary unit to keep it tidy, and you get a fifth matrix. It squares to one and flips sign against each of the other four. It splits every spinor into a left-handed half and a right-handed half, the chirality, using two simple projectors: half of one minus it, and half of one plus it — the same sorting a bin of screws would get from a machine that only checks which way the threads spiral, left-handed or right-handed, and ignores every other property, length, material, head shape, entirely. Electromagnetism treats the halves evenly, indifferent to handedness the way an ordinary wrench doesn't care which way a screw spirals so long as it fits; the weak force does not, which is why chirality earns its keep later.

The real prize is the trace, the sum down the diagonal, a single ordinary number. The trace of the identity is four. The trace of any odd number of gamma matrices is zero, for a neat reason: slip in two copies of the fifth matrix and slide one around the loop, and an odd string becomes minus itself. The trace of two is four times their relativistic dot product. The trace of four is four times the sum over the three ways of pairing up the indices, with alternating signs. A thicket of matrices collapses into dot products of momenta.

That is why it matters. Every unpolarized cross-section calculation in the second half of the course begins with a trace like the one from electron-muon scattering: brackets of momentum plus mass sandwiched between vertex factors. Expand it, discard every odd term, and the four-gamma rule delivers a handful of momentum products and one term carrying the metric of spacetime. A few lines of algebra, no matrix in sight.
