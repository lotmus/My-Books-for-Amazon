# The Complete QED Course

This is a course in quantum electrodynamics, the theory of light and electrons, to be read in order, each lesson standing on the one before. Skip ahead and the gamma matrices will be waiting, unamused.

*Status: Settled. QED is the most precisely tested theory in physics.*

## Lesson 1 — Complex Numbers and Linear Algebra
Quantum mechanics runs on complex numbers because a complex number carries two things at once: a size and a phase. Quantum amplitudes have both. This is the same arrow drawn in *Foundations*, Chapter 21, length and compass bearing bundled into one object. Here the arrow's length stands in for something about probability, and its bearing means nothing on its own. It only matters relative to the bearing of some other arrow it gets compared with.

Multiply an amplitude by its own complex conjugate, the same number with the sign of its imaginary part flipped, and the phase drops out, leaving an ordinary non-negative number. That number is a probability. When two routes lead to the same outcome, their amplitudes are added before that step, so their phases decide whether the routes reinforce or cancel. Only the phase difference between them matters. Shift every phase by the same amount and nothing measurable changes. Two stopwatches started ten minutes apart will never agree on what time it is, yet they will agree forever on how much time passed between any two events both of them recorded. In polar form a complex number is a length and an angle, and multiplying two of them means multiplying the lengths and adding the angles, which makes phases easy to track.

Linear algebra supplies the stage. A vector is an object in its own right. Writing it as a column of numbers requires choosing a basis, and a different basis gives different numbers for the same vector. An inner product gives vectors a length and a notion of right angles, and a step-by-step recipe turns any set of independent vectors into unit-length, mutually perpendicular ones. A matrix is what an operator looks like once a basis is chosen. Change the basis and the matrix changes, but its trace, determinant and eigenvalues stay fixed, because they belong to the operator itself rather than to the description.

Eigenvectors are the directions an operator merely stretches, and eigenvalues are the stretch factors. Pull a rubber sheet taut and most directions marked on it twist as well as stretch, but a couple of special directions only get longer or shorter. Those are the sheet's eigenvectors. A state of definite spin, energy or momentum is exactly such a direction, and operators that commute share their eigenvectors, so the quantities they represent can have definite values together.

Different operators get different jobs. Those with real eigenvalues describe things you can measure, since measurements return real numbers. No bathroom scale has ever printed a reading of "three plus two i pounds," and any mathematics standing in for a measuring device has to respect the same restriction. Operators that preserve lengths, and therefore total probability, describe time evolution and changes of basis, in the spirit of shuffling a deck of cards: however thoroughly you shuffle, you still hold the same fifty-two cards. Projectors, which squash a vector onto one direction, describe the act of measuring.

The Pauli matrices are the first concrete example, a model of spin one-half. Prepare a particle with its spin along one axis, measure it along a perpendicular axis, and each answer comes up half the time: nature's coin flip, built into the mathematics from the start. They return later inside the electron's own equation.

None of this is warm-up. Multiply a whole quantum state by a phase and every probability stays put. Now demand that this still holds when the phase is allowed to differ from place to place, and the electromagnetic potential is forced into existence to make it work. QED starts there.

## Lesson 2 — Calculus and Differential Equations
Every dynamical law in QED is a differential equation, and every prediction is an integral. An exponential keeps its shape when differentiated: it just gets multiplied by a constant. So for a linear equation with constant coefficients, guess an exponential solution and the calculus collapses into algebra. On a wave, a derivative in space brings down the wave number and a derivative in time brings down the frequency. Quantum mechanics reads those two factors as momentum and energy, which is why derivatives stand in for them throughout QED.

Integration by parts moves a derivative from one factor of a product onto the other, at the price of a minus sign and a leftover boundary term. Fields die away at great distances, so that leftover term is dropped. This keeps the momentum operator well-behaved, with real measured values, and it is how equations of motion are extracted from an action. The bell-curve integral is the most heavily used integral in the subject. Oscillator ground states, wave packets, path integrals and loop integrals all reduce to it, and differentiating with respect to a parameter generates the variants. In vector calculus, the curl of a gradient vanishes and so does the divergence of a curl, which is why electric and magnetic fields can be built from potentials.

An expansion in a small quantity is a controlled approximation. Expand the relativistic energy of a slow particle and out come the rest energy, Newton's kinetic energy, and the first relativistic correction, which shows up in the fine structure of hydrogen. QED itself is an expansion in one small number, the strength of electromagnetism.

The transform between position and wave number turns derivatives into plain multiplication. Combined with its own inverse it produces the delta function, a spike of zero width and unit weight that later enforces conservation of energy and momentum in every amplitude. It is the mathematical idealization of a single hammer strike: all the force delivered in no time at all, still landing with a finite effect.

Squeeze a bell curve in position and its transform spreads out in wave number, so a sharp position means a fuzzy momentum. A drum hit and a flute note make the same trade in sound. The drum hit is sharply localized in time, and building it takes a broad spread of pitches, a click rather than a note. The flute note is nearly a single pure pitch because it lasts a comparatively long time. The trade-off is built into what a wave is. That is the uncertainty principle, before any physics has been added.

The response of a system to one sharp kick is the seed of the propagator, which carries a particle from place to place in field theory. The harmonic oscillator is the prototype of every mode of every free field, and a damped one, with a complex frequency, is how an unstable particle looks: the imaginary part is its decay width. A struck tuning fork shows both facts at once, how fast it rings and how fast the ringing dies away. A complex frequency folds pitch and fade into a single number, the real part giving the pitch and the imaginary part the fade.

Plane waves solve a wave equation only if frequency and wave number are linked in a fixed way, read straight off the equation. Add a mass and that link becomes the relativistic relation between energy and momentum.

## Lesson 3 — Special Relativity
Two rules carry the whole theory. The laws of physics look the same in every inertial frame, and light travels at the same speed for everyone, however hard they chase it. Under the old Galilean rule for translating between observers, light would look slower to a pursuer, so something has to give, and what gives is time. Space and time get mixed. A moving clock runs slow and a moving rod shrinks along its direction of travel, and each observer says it is the other's clock and rod. There is no contradiction, because observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are simultaneous.

Mount a lightbulb at each end of a moving train car, each wired to fire when a trigger signal reaches it from a switch at the car's exact center. In the train's frame the signal travels at the same speed in both directions and has equal distances to cover, so both bulbs fire at the same instant of train time. A passenger sitting at the switch sees both flashes arrive together and calls them simultaneous.

A friend stands on the platform at the spot the switch is passing when it fires. From her vantage the trigger signal has to chase the front bulb, which the train keeps carrying away, while the rear bulb rides forward to meet it. So by her clock the rear bulb fires first, and its flash reaches her first. Neither observer made an error and no clock is broken. "At the same time" was never a fact about the two flashes alone; it depends on who is asking.

The atmospheric muon is the standard proof. Without time dilation it would travel well under a kilometer before decaying, and with it, several, far enough to reach the ground. From the muon's own point of view, the atmosphere is squashed thin instead. The full translation rule, named for Lorentz, also guarantees that adding speeds never gets past the speed of light.

One quantity survives the mixing: the interval between two events, a sort of distance in which time counts with the opposite sign to space. Everyone agrees on it, and its sign sorts pairs of events into those a slower-than-light signal can connect, those only light can connect, and those nothing can connect. That sorting is causality. Later it becomes the demand that fields at separated points cannot disturb each other, one of the reasons antiparticles must exist.

Time measured along a particle's own path, its proper time, is agreed on by everyone too. Energy and momentum share the same structure: one combination of the two is the same for every observer, and it fixes the particle's mass. A massless particle such as the photon must always travel at light speed, has no rest frame, and carries energy set by its frequency.

Natural units set the speed of light and the reduced Planck constant both to one, so every quantity becomes a power of energy. In these units the strength of electromagnetism is a pure number, about one part in 137. Because it is small, QED can be calculated as a series of ever-smaller corrections instead of being solved outright.

The energy available for making new particles grows in step with beam energy in a collider, but only as its square root for a beam hitting a stationary target, which is why colliders win. A particle splitting in two hands each piece a fixed energy, while a split in three gives a continuous spread, which is how the neutrino was inferred. The ordinary quantum wave equation cannot obey any of this, and QED begins by asking what replaces it.

## Lesson 4 — Four Vectors and Lorentz Transformations
Tracking relativistic effects by hand, one space dimension at a time, cannot cope with QED, which juggles four coordinates, four momentum components, four potential components and a field with sixteen entries. Four-vector notation makes the bookkeeping automatic. Every quantity carries labels that run over the four directions of spacetime, and a rule called the metric converts between the two kinds of label by flipping the sign of the space parts and leaving time alone. A label that appears twice, once up and once down, is summed over. Use up every label that way and the result is a number every observer agrees on.

More generally, an equation whose leftover labels match on both sides can be seen to hold in every frame just by looking at it. Some textbooks use the opposite sign convention, which is a fine way to lose an afternoon.

The changes of frame that respect all this are the Lorentz transformations, those that preserve the interval. They are the mixed-sign cousin of ordinary rotations, and a boost to a moving frame is a rotation of a hyperbolic kind that mixes time with space. Rotating a photograph mixes its up-down and left-right directions in a fixed, reversible way and leaves the picture's content alone. A boost does the same kind of mixing between time and a space direction, sweeping along a hyperbola where a rotation sweeps along a circle.

Add shifts in space and time and you have the larger family that physical laws are required to respect. The shifts lead to conservation of energy and momentum, the rotations to angular momentum and spin. The wave operator, a combination of second derivatives in time and space, is unchanged by any boost, so a wave equation built from it plus a mass term is automatically consistent with relativity. By the same test the ordinary quantum wave equation, with one time derivative against two space derivatives, fails on sight.

Objects with more labels, called tensors, transform with one Lorentz matrix per label. An antisymmetric one with two labels has exactly six independent parts, the same count as the electric and magnetic fields together. They are two faces of one field object, and a boost mixes them, so a moving observer sees a magnetic field where a resting one sees only an electric field.

A tin can seen from directly above looks like a circle, and from the side like a rectangle. Nobody thinks the can changed shape between glances; circle and rectangle are two silhouettes of one three-dimensional object. Electric and magnetic fields are silhouettes of that kind, cast by one underlying field, with your state of motion playing the part of the viewing angle.

Energy and momentum likewise form one four-vector whose invariant length is the particle's mass, and conserving it in a collision is a single equation true in every frame. A plane wave written with the right sign convention lets derivatives pull energy and momentum straight out of it, and its opposite-sign twin is the source of the antiparticle interpretation. Finally, a two-body collision has three frame-independent quantities: the square of the center-of-momentum energy, the square of the momentum transferred, and a third fixed by the other two. Every QED cross-section is written in terms of them.

## Lesson 5 — Classical Mechanics and Lagrangians
Newton's law is written in Cartesian coordinates, wants every force as a vector, and hides the link between symmetries and conservation laws. QED is built on a single scalar instead, the Lagrangian, which for a particle in a potential is kinetic energy minus potential energy in whatever coordinates suit. Add it up along a path and you have the action. Hamilton's principle says the path a system really takes is the one where the action is stationary: nudge the path slightly, endpoints held fixed, and the action changes only at second order.

A lifeguard racing to a drowning swimmer gives a feel for it. Running is faster than swimming, so the quickest route is a bent path that spends more distance on sand and less in water, a trade a good lifeguard makes without algebra. The one difference to keep in mind: the lifeguard's route is a true minimum, while the action is merely stationary, so the name "least action" is a historical accident.

The equations of motion that follow work in any coordinates. A pendulum's fixed rod needs no special treatment, since choosing the angle as the coordinate builds the constraint in. For a particle in a potential Newton's law comes back out, and the minus sign matters: kinetic plus potential would send the particle accelerating uphill, which is not what hills are for.

Two Lagrangians that differ by a total time derivative, or by an overall constant factor, give identical motion, a freedom field theory uses to discard surface terms. A coordinate that never appears in the Lagrangian has a conserved momentum attached. A potential depending only on distance from a center conserves angular momentum with no mention of torque, and a Lagrangian with no explicit time dependence conserves energy. These are the first examples of symmetry producing conservation, which later becomes a general rule.

The relativistic free particle has an action proportional to its proper time, and demanding that this be the same for every observer delivers the correct relativistic momentum and energy without assuming them. Its actual path maximizes proper time, which is the twin paradox in one sentence.

For a charged particle, write down a Lagrangian containing only the electric and magnetic potentials, no forces at all, and the whole Lorentz force falls out, magnetic part included. The momentum paired with position picks up an extra contribution from the vector potential on top of mass times velocity, like a bank balance that includes a running tab with the field you are moving through. It is the first hint that the potentials are the fundamental quantities. In covariant form a charge couples to the potential through the only scalar available that is linear in it, and that coupling is the classical seed of the electron-photon interaction in QED.

Coupled oscillators split into independent normal modes, each swinging at a single frequency. Let a chain of masses and springs grow infinitely fine, and the discrete coordinates become a continuous field obeying a wave equation. That is the bridge from particles to fields.

## Lesson 6 — Hamiltonian Mechanics
The Lagrangian is the best language for writing a theory down and spotting its symmetries. The Hamiltonian is the best language for quantizing it. Moving between them means trading velocity for momentum as the basic variable. For ordinary systems the Hamiltonian is the total energy expressed through position and momentum, and it stays constant whenever the rules do not change with time. The trade fails when momentum cannot be turned back into velocity, which signals constraints, and the electromagnetic field is the important case: its gauge freedom is why gauge fixing will be needed.

Hamilton's equations replace each second-order equation with two first-order ones, and together they carry the system through phase space, the space of all positions and momenta. Trajectories there never cross. If two did, a system sitting at the crossing point would have no way of knowing which route to continue along, so determinism itself forbids it, much as a well-laid rail yard never sends two scheduled trains through the same junction at once. That is the classical version of a quantum state determining its own future.

A cloud of possible states changes shape but never volume, like dough that can be rolled, stretched or folded without the amount of dough changing. This is the classical cousin of quantum probability being conserved. Phase-space area comes in units of Planck's constant, the earliest hint of quantized energy levels and the origin of the recurring factors of two pi in momentum integrals.

A bracket operation on phase-space quantities governs how any of them changes in time, and it obeys the same algebra as the commutator in quantum mechanics. Conserved quantities generate the very symmetries they belong to: momentum shifts a system along, angular momentum rotates it, and energy moves it forward in time. That is the link between symmetry and conservation in Hamiltonian dress.

Two examples matter most for QED. Rewrite the oscillator using a complex combination of position and momentum, and its energy becomes a simple product while its motion becomes a steady rotation at positive frequency. Quantized, those variables become the creation and annihilation operators, and a free field is infinitely many such oscillators, one for each momentum. A charged particle's Hamiltonian follows from a single substitution, replacing momentum by momentum minus charge times potential. That minimal coupling is the classical forerunner of the covariant derivative in QED.

Quantization itself is one rule: replace each bracket by the corresponding commutator, scaled by Planck's constant. What the rule does not settle is the order of quantities that no longer commute. Different orderings differ by a constant, and for the oscillator that constant is the zero-point energy, which is why even a quantum oscillator in its lowest state is never quite still. In field theory the constant is infinite, and normal ordering is the tidy way of removing it. The electron field follows the same steps with anticommutators in place of commutators.

## Lesson 7 — Maxwell's Equations
Four rules run all of classical electricity and magnetism. Electric charge is the source of the electric field, so field lines begin on positive charge and end on negative, like water springing from a park fountain and vanishing down a drain. Magnetic field lines get no such luxury. They never begin and never end, because nobody has ever found an isolated magnetic pole. A magnetic field line is closed on itself like a racetrack, with no start line and no finish line.

A magnetic field that changes in time drives the electric field around in circles, which is how every power station earns its living. A current drives the magnetic field around in circles, which is how every electromagnet works. Add the rule for how fields push on charges and the loop closes: fields move charges, and charges make fields.

The last rule was broken until Maxwell mended it. Charge a capacitor through a wire and the current runs up to one plate and stops dead at the gap. The old rule then says the magnetic field around the wire depends on which surface you stretch across it, a flat disk or a balloon passing between the plates, which is plainly absurd. Maxwell's repair was to let a changing electric field make a magnetic field too. Between the plates the changing field carries exactly as much "current" as the wire does, and the absurdity vanishes. He added the term because the equations demanded it, before anyone had observed its effects. As a bonus, conservation of charge drops straight out: charge can move, but it cannot be created or destroyed.

The payoff is light. In empty space a changing magnetic field makes an electric field, a changing electric field makes a magnetic one, each holds the other up, and the pair sails off with nothing pushing. Their speed depends only on two constants you can measure with charges and currents on a workbench, no lamps required. Maxwell worked out the number and it matched the speed of light. Light is an electromagnetic wave, and two whole subjects collapsed into one. The equations also never say who is doing the measuring, a loose thread Einstein would later pull.

The wave has a shape worth keeping. The electric and magnetic parts wiggle at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, rising and falling together. There are two independent sideways directions, so there are two polarizations, and the photon inherits precisely that count. QED is this theory made quantum. Even the units get tidied: sweep the fudge factors away and the only number left is the strength of the coupling, about one part in 137.

## Lesson 8 — Electromagnetic Potentials and Gauge Freedom
Because magnetic field lines never end, the magnetic field can always be written as the swirl of some other field, the vector potential. Because a changing magnetic field drives the electric field, the electric field can then be written as the downhill slope of a voltage, plus a correction for how fast the vector potential is changing. Do that and two of Maxwell's four rules are satisfied automatically, whatever potentials you pick. Six field numbers at every point are traded for four potential numbers, and the two rules involving charges and currents become the only equations left to solve.

The catch is that the potentials are not unique. Add the slope of any function to the vector potential, subtract the matching rate of change from the voltage, and the electric and magnetic fields do not budge. Measure a mountain's height from sea level or from your kitchen floor and the slopes come out the same either way. Gauge freedom is that idea with the floor allowed to move from place to place and moment to moment, provided the two potentials are adjusted in step.

Gauge freedom is one situation described in many equivalent ways, and the excess information in the potentials is exactly the freedom to choose. Two different physical situations never enter. A voltage that wobbles in time but is the same everywhere describes empty space just as well as no voltage at all.

One choice of gauge gives each potential its own wave equation with the charges as the source, so disturbances travel at the speed of light and the potential here and now reflects the charges as they were one light-travel time ago. Wiggle a charge and the potential far away follows, late by just the time light takes to cross the gap. Another choice makes the voltage respond instantly to the charges, which sounds like a breach of causality. The voltage alone cannot be measured, though. The delay reappears in the vector potential, and the electric field, which you can measure, stays properly causal.

Why bother? Because quantum mechanics writes the motion of a charged particle in terms of the potentials. Change gauge and the particle's wave function must change its phase in step, or the physics would change. This is the same relativity of reference point that *Foundations*, Chapter 5, used for voltage, sea level or kitchen floor, with a phase now playing the movable reference.

Now demand more: let that phase be adjustable independently at every point in space. The equations survive only if a compensating field exists to soak up the difference, and that field behaves precisely like the electromagnetic potential. Local phase freedom forces the electromagnetic field to exist. That one argument is the spine of QED.

## Lesson 9 — Wave Functions and Hilbert Spaces
Fire a thousand identically prepared electrons at a screen and each lands somewhere different, though the pattern of landings has a definite shape. Quantum mechanics accepts this and drops the track altogether. A particle gets a wave function instead, a complex number at every point in space. Square its size and you have the probability of finding the particle there. Add that up over all space and you must get one, because the particle is somewhere. Nothing derives this rule. You adopt it because nature keeps agreeing. The overall phase of the wave function means nothing by itself, but the phase of one part relative to another decides how the parts interfere.

The mathematics behind it is friendlier than it sounds. Wave functions can be added, scaled and compared, so they behave as vectors with infinitely many components, and a state with total probability one is an arrow of length one. Choose any set of mutually perpendicular states and the arrow splits into pieces along them. The squared size of each piece is the probability of that outcome, and the probabilities add to one for the same reason the squares of the two short sides of a right triangle add up to the square of the long one.

The wave function is only one set of coordinates for the arrow, the set you get by asking where. Ask about energy instead and you get different coordinates for the same state. A picture to keep: a plain arch, the kind a child draws for a hill, laid across a box carries better than 99.8 percent of its probability in the lowest energy state, so an energy measurement would almost always find the ground state.

Probability also flows. It cannot vanish here and reappear there, so it slides from place to place like charge, obeying the same local bookkeeping. And when two possibilities are added, the probability of the sum differs from the sum of the probabilities. Drop two stones into a still pond. Where two ripples both rise, the water climbs higher than either alone would lift it; where one rises as the other falls, they partly or entirely cancel.

Adding two quantum amplitudes and squaring the result produces the same reinforcement and cancellation in the probabilities. A cross term appears, positive or negative depending on the relative phase, with no counterpart when you add two independent chances, such as two customers' separate chances of showing up at a shop. That is interference, the double-slit pattern in embryo. Every scattering calculation in QED adds amplitudes first and squares afterward.

One warning. The spread in a particle's position describes the scatter across many identically prepared copies. It says nothing about the precision of a single measurement, or about clumsy instruments.

## Lesson 10 — Operators and Observables
Every quantity you can measure is a machine that acts on the wave function. The position machine multiplies it by the position. The momentum machine takes its slope, which is why a state of definite momentum is a pure wave: the tighter the crinkles, the more momentum. Measure something and the only answers you can ever get are the special values for which the machine leaves a state's shape alone and merely rescales it. Afterward the system sits in the matching state, so measuring again straight away gives the same answer.

For the answers to be real numbers, and for different answers to belong to perpendicular states, the machine must be of a well-behaved kind, and every observable is. The average you quote is the weighted mean of the possible answers, weighted by their probabilities. It belongs to many identical runs, never to a single one.

Now the twist: order matters. Multiply by position and then take the slope, and you get something different from taking the slope first. A sock and then a shoe makes a working outfit; a shoe and then a sock does not. Two operations can each be perfectly sensible and still give different results depending on which goes first. For position and momentum the difference is never zero. It is a fixed amount set by Planck's constant, whatever the state.

Quantities whose order makes no difference can be sharp together. Position and momentum cannot. That is the uncertainty principle, and it is a statement about ensembles. Prepare many copies identically, measure position on some and momentum on the rest, and the two spreads can never both be small. Their product has a floor, and the bell-shaped packet is the one shape that sits exactly on it. Clumsy instruments jostling a single particle have nothing to do with it.

The prize is conservation. How fast the average of a quantity changes depends on whether it matters if you apply that quantity before or after the energy machine. If it makes no difference, the quantity never changes. A free particle keeps its momentum. A particle in a central force keeps its angular momentum. A potential that looks the same on both sides of the origin keeps mirror-image symmetry, so its energy states sort themselves into even and odd. Hunting for conservation laws is hunting for things that commute with the energy.

QED will reuse the trick wholesale. Swap the classical brackets for these order-dependence rules and you have the recipe for quantizing anything, fields included.

## Lesson 11 — The Schrödinger Equation
Something must say how a wave function changes in time, and that something is energy. The rate of change of the wave function is set by the energy machine acting on it. This single rule is linear, so a mixture of solutions is itself a solution, and it keeps total probability at one forever.

Take the special solutions first. In a stationary state the shape never changes; only a phase spins, at a rate set by the energy, so every probability stays put. A clock's hands sweep around without stopping, yet the printed face looks the same in every photograph. A stationary state is that face: the phase sweeps around underneath, and every measurable probability sits as still as the numbers on the dial. Squeeze a particle into a box and the allowed energies are those where a whole number of half-waves fit, rising as the square of that number.

Now mix two of them. The relative phase turns at a rate set by the energy difference, and the probabilities start to move. A particle in an equal mix of the lowest two box states sloshes to and fro, once per beat. The word is meant literally. Play two tones of slightly different pitch and you hear a slow throb at the difference between their frequencies; two mixed energy levels produce the same throb in probability instead of loudness. In a system with fixed rules, all change is beating between energies.

A free particle is a packet of waves with different momenta. The packet drifts at the classical speed, momentum over mass, and it spreads, because the faster components pull ahead of the slower ones. The spreading time is the mass times the squared width, divided by the reduced Planck constant. For an electron pinned to atomic size that is about a ten-thousandth of a trillionth of a second, which is why an atomic electron cannot be a tiny planet on a track and must be described by stationary states. For a one-gram bead held to a millimeter it is roughly three hundred quadrillion years, so beads behave classically.

The oscillator earns the most attention. Its energies come in equal steps above a floor that is not zero, and two operators climb and descend the ladder one step at a time. A free field is one such oscillator for every momentum and polarization, and a particle is one step up a ladder. Even the ground state keeps half a step of energy, a leftover that shows up as a measurable force between uncharged metal plates.

Finally, the equation cannot be the last word. It is first order in time and second order in space, so a change of frame scrambles it. It belongs to Galileo's world rather than Einstein's, and it is the itch that leads on to Dirac.

## Lesson 12 — Momentum and Position
Position and momentum are two ways of describing the same state, connected by the trick of taking any shape apart into pure waves. The momentum description is a second wave function whose squared size gives the chance of each momentum, and it carries exactly the same information as the first, like giving a location as a street address or as latitude and longitude. In the momentum description, momentum is plain multiplication and position becomes a slope, the mirror image of the usual arrangement, yet the rule that order matters comes out identical. It has to, because that rule belongs to the state and not to the coordinates used to write it down.

Why bother with a second description? Because in it a free particle is trivial. Each momentum component just turns its own phase at its own rate, and no component pushes on any other. Put the components back together and you recover the drifting, spreading packet, with the spreading now visible as faster components running ahead. That is why free particles will be handled in momentum terms from here on, and why every mode sum in QED is a sum over momenta.

Uncertainty is now geometry. A shape and its decomposition into waves cannot both be narrow. Build a sharp spike and you need waves of every wavelength; use a single wave and you have something spread over all space. Squeeze one and the other swells. The bell curve is the one shape that strikes the best bargain, and the lowest state of the oscillator is that bell curve in both descriptions at once, jittering at the minimum the rules allow. It is the same state that becomes the vacuum of a quantum field.

The picture to keep is the propagator, the amplitude for a free particle to get from one place to another in a given time. Its size is the same for every destination. Release a particle at a single point and, in this nonrelativistic theory, it is equally likely to be found anywhere at the very next instant: perfect knowledge of position means unbounded momentum. That is the theory overreaching, since nothing may outrun light, and it hints at why a relativistic version is needed. Still, the propagator is the direct ancestor of the one that drives every Feynman diagram.

## Lesson 13 — Angular Momentum
Turn a book a quarter turn about one axis, then a quarter turn about another. Now do it in the opposite order. The book ends up in a different place. Rotations do not commute, and angular momentum, which generates rotations, inherits the habit. The three components of angular momentum cannot all be sharp together, because swapping the order of two of them leaves a remainder equal to the third, scaled by Planck's constant.

What can be sharp is the total size of the angular momentum and its component along one chosen axis, and pure algebra delivers the whole spectrum without a single differential equation. Ladder operators step the component up or down by exactly one unit, the basic quantum of angular momentum, and leave the total alone. Think of a staircase with fixed-height steps: you go up or down one step at a time, never half a step, and the climbing says nothing about how tall the building is.

A ladder that steps in whole units and cannot run forever needs a top rung and a bottom rung, placed symmetrically about zero. Call the top rung the quantum number. The squared length of the angular momentum is that number times one more than that number, in the unit squared. That is always bigger than the top rung squared, so the arrow can never lie along the chosen axis. Its length always beats its longest shadow, and the leftover is a sideways jitter that uncertainty insists on. A classical spinning top has no such problem.

Whole numbers or halves? The algebra allows both. But orbital motion, a particle going around a center, must have a wave function that returns to itself after one full turn about the axis, and that forces whole numbers. The shapes on a sphere that satisfy this are the spherical harmonics, which give atomic orbitals their spheres, dumbbells and more elaborate lobes. Whole-number top rungs always give an odd count of projections: one, three, five. Keep that in your pocket. It is about to matter.

Rotational symmetry pays a dividend. If the force depends only on distance from the center, nothing changes under rotation, angular momentum commutes with energy, and it is conserved. That is why hydrogen's energy states can be labeled by total angular momentum and its component. And the ladder method is a template: any operators that follow the same rotation rules have the spectrum found here, whatever they are made of, which is how the same trick will solve spin next.

## Lesson 14 — Spin and Pauli Matrices
Send silver atoms through a magnetic field that is stronger at the top than at the bottom and the beam splits in two. A classical spinning magnet would give a smear, and any whole-number orbital angular momentum would give three or five spots. Silver gives two. Its outer electron has no orbital motion to speak of and still carries angular momentum. That is spin, and for the electron it is one-half of the basic unit.

Spin is an intrinsic quantum label, with no little ball turning underneath, and it fits the smallest possible ladder: two rungs, no more. Electrons, protons, neutrons and quarks all have it. The photon has spin one but, being massless, only two of its three possible states, the same count as light's two polarizations.

Spin is represented by half the basic unit times the Pauli matrices, small two-by-two tables of numbers that obey the same rotation rules as orbital angular momentum. A spin state is a pair of complex numbers, and measuring along any axis you choose can give only up or down along that axis. Prepare an electron definitely up along one axis and along a perpendicular axis it is a fair coin toss.

A spinning coin is the natural comparison, and it breaks in the one place that matters. A coin in mid-flip is already headed for heads or tails, and a fast enough camera could call it before it lands. An electron prepared up along one axis carries no hidden answer about the perpendicular axis, and no finer instrument could read one off in advance. The answer comes into being when the question is asked, and half the time it is "up."

Run it as an experiment. Prepare spin up along a sideways axis, pass the beam through a vertical filter, and half comes out up, half down. Take the up beam through a sideways filter again and it splits in half again. The vertical measurement wiped out the sideways knowledge. There was no sideways value for it to jostle; asking the vertical question is what left the sideways question without an answer. Two components of spin can never be sharp together.

Spin has another oddity. Turn a spin one-half through one full circle and its state comes back multiplied by minus one. Only two full turns restore it. A Möbius band shows the same extra lap: trace a finger along an ordinary loop and one trip brings you back to your starting side, but on the twisted band it takes two trips. A lone electron cannot show this, since an overall sign carries no probability, but interference between two paths can.

Two spin one-half particles combine into a triplet of total spin one, symmetric under swapping the particles, and a singlet of total spin zero, antisymmetric. Measure one half of a singlet pair and find up, and the other, along the same axis, is certain to be down. In general, totals run from the difference of the two spins to their sum in unit steps. An electron with one unit of orbital angular momentum and spin one-half has a total of one-half or three-halves, and the gap between those two is the doublet fine structure of atomic spectra. The electron's relativistic equation will need four components, built from two-component pieces like these.

## Lesson 15 — Perturbation Theory

Almost nothing worth calculating can be solved exactly, so physicists cheat, systematically. Solve a nearby problem that can be done precisely, treat the difference as a small nudge, and correct the answer layer by layer. The first layer is charmingly simple: a level's energy shifts by the nudge averaged over the state being nudged. The second layer mixes in other states, the nearer in energy the more, and always pushes connected levels apart. The lower one sinks, the upper one rises, like strangers on a bench each shuffling away from the other.

The recipe breaks when states share the very same energy, because it divides by the gap and the gap is zero. The cure is to work out first which mixtures of the tied states the nudge prefers. Put hydrogen in an electric field. Its second shell holds four states of equal energy, and the field connects only two: a round one and a dumbbell pointing along the field. Mixed evenly, they form lopsided clouds with a built-in electric dipole, one rising and one falling in step with the field. The other two stay put, so one level splits into three. The ground state, with no partner, responds only to the square of the field.

Fine structure is the same method on the real atom. The electron moves fast enough for relativity to nudge its kinetic energy. Circling the proton, it also feels in its own frame a magnetic field that grips its spin. This is the tin-can trick from Lesson 4, electric and magnetic fields as two faces of one object: the magnetic field exists only because the electron's frame moves relative to the proton's. Together the two effects make the energy depend on total angular momentum, orbit and spin combined, instead of on the orbit's shape alone. The effect is smaller than the main energy by about the square of the fine-structure constant, roughly one part in nineteen thousand: a few hundred-thousandths of an electron-volt, against level spacings of an electron-volt or more.

This is QED in miniature. The small number controlling the expansion is the fine-structure constant, about one part in 137, and each extra photon exchanged costs about another factor of it. The series does not strictly converge; past some order, adding terms makes things worse. Nobody minds. The first few terms already agree with experiment to better than one part in a billion, which is more than can be said for most weather forecasts.

## Lesson 16 — Identical Particles

Two electrons have no individual identity at all. Identical twins are the obvious comparison, and they fail in the one way that matters: however alike two twins look, some fact says which is which, a birthmark, a DNA test, a path each has walked since birth. Two electrons share no such fact, even in principle. No tag, no serial number, no experiment can say which electron in a helium atom is the one that was there a moment ago, because there is nothing there for any instrument to find.

So swapping two identical particles cannot change anything observable, and swapping twice restores everything. That leaves one swap with only two possible effects on the state: it returns unchanged, or it returns with its sign flipped. Nature uses both. The unchanged kind, bosons, have whole-number spin, photons among them. The flipped kind, fermions, have half-number spin: electrons, protons, neutrons, quarks. There is no third kind.

The sign flip does all the work. Build a two-fermion state as first particle here and second there, minus second here and first there. Put both in the same state and the pieces cancel, leaving nothing. That is Pauli's exclusion principle, and it follows from arithmetic alone. Electrons pile into shells rather than sinking into the lowest orbit, chemistry gets its periodic table, matter takes up room, and a white dwarf star is held up against its own gravity by electrons refusing to share states. Bosons face no such rule and rather like company.

The surprise is exchange energy. Two electrons repel, and the repulsion knows nothing about spin. Yet the whole state must flip on a swap, which ties the spin arrangement to the spatial arrangement. Parallel spins force the spatial part to flip, so the electrons stay out of each other's way, repel less, and sit lower in energy, like two commuters who, without ever discussing it, always take opposite ends of a crowded platform. A force blind to spin has produced an energy that depends on it. That is why electrons in different orbitals prefer parallel spins. In helium's ground state both share one orbital, so the spatial part cannot flip and the spins must point opposite ways: exclusion seen from the other side.

For QED, two things carry forward. Pauli's rule is here simply declared; once the electron becomes a quantum field, its algebra builds the minus sign in automatically. And when two electrons scatter, there are two indistinguishable routes to one result, and one carries a minus sign relative to the other, where bosons would add them instead. Why spin and this behavior go together is, for now, observation; the proof waits for relativity.

## Lesson 17 — Klein-Gordon Equation

Schrödinger built his equation from the slow-motion energy formula. Use the full relativistic one instead, in which energy squared is momentum squared plus mass squared, swap energy and momentum for rates of change in time and space, and out comes the Klein-Gordon equation. Time and space appear alike, two derivatives each, so it passes the relativity test that Schrödinger's equation fails. The slow-motion limit works too: strip off the fast wobble at the rest-energy frequency, drop the second time derivative, and Schrödinger's equation reappears. For an electron with one electron-volt of kinetic energy, the discarded piece is about two parts in a million.

Then come the bills. Being second order in time, the equation gives every momentum two plane waves, one of positive energy and one of negative, much as a pendulum equation gives two roots and both count. Negative energy has no floor, so a particle could shed light and fall forever. Worse, the density that counted probability in Schrödinger's theory, rebuilt here, is positive for the positive-energy wave and negative for the other, and a mixture takes whichever sign wins. Negative probability is nonsense. A better definition will not rescue it: a second-order equation needs the wave and its rate of change as starting data, and that extra freedom lets the density go either way.

Chopping out the negative-energy waves by hand fails, since any interaction mixes them back in. The way out was to keep the equation and change the reading, like discovering that a gauge built for one job had been correctly measuring something else all along. Stop treating the wave as the probability of finding one particle; treat it as a field. The awkward density becomes electric charge density, which may sensibly be negative because charge comes in two signs, and the negative-energy waves become antiparticles. Read this way, the equation is exactly right for charged particles with no spin, the charged pion among them.

It is also a template: a relativistic wave equation, its plane waves, a troubled single-particle reading, and a rescue by promotion to a field. That plot runs again for the electron, which has spin and needs Dirac's equation instead.

## Lesson 18 — Why the Schrödinger Equation Is Not Enough

Two equations, two failures. Schrödinger's has one time derivative against two space derivatives, an imbalance no change of observer can respect. Klein-Gordon balances them, but being second order in time it lets probability go negative. What is wanted is an equation that is first order in time, so today's wave settles tomorrow's and the density is a plain sum of squares, yet still honors the relativistic relation between energy, momentum and mass. Easy to wish for. Hard to grant.

The obvious move, taking the square root of the relativistic formula before turning momentum into derivatives, gets the slow-motion limit right. But in ordinary space the root unrolls into an infinite string of ever higher derivatives, so the wave's value here would depend on its values arbitrarily far away. That is an equation that gossips with the entire universe.

Dirac's way around was to leave the root alone and look for something linear in momentum and mass whose square gives precisely the right relation, with no leftovers. Squaring a sum throws up cross terms, so any two coefficients must cancel when multiplied in one order and then the other, while each squares to one. Ordinary numbers cannot manage it: for numbers, one order plus the other is simply twice the product, which vanishes only if one of them is zero, and the scheme collapses. Matrices can, because their products depend on order, the sock-and-shoe lesson from Lesson 10. Two nonzero matrices can give products in the two orders that are exact negatives of each other. So the coefficients must be matrices, and the wave function must have several components.

How many? The Pauli matrices supply three suitable coefficients, but no fourth exists among two-by-two matrices, and a fourth is needed, for mass. Odd sizes are out: each matrix must pair its states off one for one, plus one against minus one, which needs an even count. The smallest that works has four rows and four columns, so the wave function carries four numbers at every point.

Spin was never put in. Those four will sort themselves into two spin orientations for the particle and two for its antiparticle, forced by nothing but a first-order equation that respects relativity. It is a habit of theoretical physics, and it returns: turn a vague demand into precise algebra, solve it, and see what the solution insists on.

## Lesson 19 — Dirac Equation

Put the four matrices of the last lesson where the coefficients belong and you have Dirac's equation: first order in time and space, acting on a wave function with four components. Multiply through by the mass matrix, call the results the gamma matrices, and the whole equation fits on one short line that looks the same to every observer. The gamma matrices carry the shape of spacetime in their multiplication rules: each squares to a plain number, plus for time and minus for space, and swapping any two different ones flips the sign. The geometry of relativity has been smuggled into matrices.

A sanity check: apply the operator twice and out comes the Klein-Gordon equation, for each of the four components. So every solution honors the relativistic relation between energy, momentum and mass. Dirac's equation really is a square root of Klein-Gordon, extracted by algebra instead of by an infinite sprawl of derivatives.

The electron at rest is the memorable case. There are four solutions. Two have energy equal to the rest energy, one for each spin orientation. Two have minus the rest energy, again one for each spin. Spin appears without being asked. The negative-energy pair is there too, uninvited, and a first-order equation does not make it leave.

Set the electron moving and the lower pair of components becomes tied to the upper pair, in proportion to the momentum, like an echo riding behind a shout, faint when the shout is soft and louder as it grows. For a slow electron the tie is about half its speed as a fraction of the speed of light, so the lower pair stays tiny.

Three questions are left open: whether the density is finally positive, how spin is organized, and what to make of the negative energies. The next lessons answer them. Meanwhile the positive-energy and negative-energy solutions are the very spinors that will stand for incoming and outgoing electrons and positrons in every Feynman diagram, and the equation itself becomes the free-electron part of QED's master formula.

## Lesson 20 — Gamma Matrices

The gamma matrices are less a topic than a toolkit, and the whole kit follows from one rule: each squares to a plain number fixed by spacetime's geometry, and swapping two different ones flips the sign. No explicit matrices are needed. Add up each gamma matrix times its own mirror image over all four directions and you get the number of dimensions, four. Sandwich any momentum, written in gamma-matrix form, between a gamma matrix and its twin and it returns with its sign flipped and its size doubled. Two moves, and long chains shrink.

Multiply all four together, with a factor of the imaginary unit to keep it tidy, and you get a fifth matrix. It squares to one and flips sign against each of the other four. It splits every spinor into a left-handed half and a right-handed half, the chirality, using two simple projectors: half of one minus it, and half of one plus it. Picture a machine that sorts a bin of screws by which way the threads spiral and ignores length, material and head shape. Electromagnetism treats the two halves evenly, the way a wrench is indifferent to thread direction so long as it fits. The weak force does not, which is why chirality earns its keep later.

The real prize is the trace, the sum down the diagonal, a single ordinary number. The trace of the identity is four. The trace of any odd number of gamma matrices is zero, for a neat reason: slip in two copies of the fifth matrix and slide one around the loop, and an odd string becomes minus itself. The trace of two is four times their relativistic dot product. The trace of four is four times the sum over the three ways of pairing up the indices, with alternating signs. A thicket of matrices collapses into dot products of momenta.

That is why it matters. Every unpolarized cross-section calculation in the second half of the course begins with a trace like the one from electron-muon scattering: brackets of momentum plus mass sandwiched between vertex factors. Expand it, discard every odd term, and the four-gamma rule delivers a handful of momentum products and one term carrying the metric of spacetime. A few lines of algebra, no matrix in sight.

## Lesson 21 — Dirac Spinors

Summing the squared sizes of the four components, as for Schrödinger's wave, does not give an invariant, because a boost, unlike a rotation, does not preserve that sum. It is the same reason, back in Lesson 3, that an ordinary sum of squared time and squared space doesn't give every observer the same answer, and only the interval, with its deliberately mismatched sign between time and space, does. The repair is the Dirac adjoint, which flips the sign of the lower pair first. Now the combination is a true scalar, the same for every observer.

Sandwich the gamma-matrix combinations between adjoint and spinor and you get sixteen independent quantities, exactly what a four-by-four matrix has room for: a scalar, a vector, an antisymmetric tensor, an axial vector and a pseudoscalar. QED uses the vector one, since the electric current must be a four-vector. The axial one is the weak force's business.

Spin is read off from the gamma matrices, and at rest it acts as ordinary spin one-half on each pair of components. Nothing was assumed; the equation says so. For a moving electron the useful label is helicity, the spin's component along the direction of travel, and for anything with mass it is only an observer's opinion. Overtake the electron in a sufficiently fast train and it seems to move backwards while its spin still points where it did, so the helicity flips. Nothing overtakes light, so the photon's helicity is the same for everyone, and that's the reason photon states are labeled by it.

The workhorse identity is the completeness relation. Add up, over both spin states, each spinor times its adjoint and you get a single matrix: the momentum, written in gamma matrices, plus the mass, with the mass sign reversed for antiparticle spinors. No one has to follow spin states one at a time; a sum over spins becomes a trace, ready for the previous lesson's machinery.

Divide by twice the mass and it becomes a projector that picks out positive-energy spinors. Its partner picks out negative-energy ones, and together they make the identity. The trace of the first is two: the two spin states, counted. Every unpolarized cross-section in the second half of the course leans on this, usually without comment, so memorize it outright.

## Lesson 22 — Antiparticles

A first-order equation cured the negative probability but left the negative energies alone. They are still there at every momentum, and an electron allowed to fall into them would radiate forever. Dirac's first answer was dramatic: every negative-energy state is already occupied, an infinite sea of electrons, and the exclusion principle bars anything falling in. A photon carrying at least twice the electron's rest energy can kick one out, leaving a hole, and a hole in a full sea acts like a particle of the same mass and opposite charge. The absence of a negative charge is the presence of a positive one.

A bubble in a glass of water works the same way. It is only a patch where liquid is missing, yet it rises and drifts and bumps into things as though it were an object in its own right. In the electron sea, one electron's worth of negative charge has gone missing from a spot, and that gap moves and interacts precisely like a positively charged particle.

Dirac first wondered whether the hole was the proton, but that is nearly two thousand times too heavy. In 1932 cosmic-ray tracks showed a positive particle with the electron's mass. The positron had been predicted before it was found, which is the satisfying way around. The sea had troubles, though: an infinite background charge to be politely ignored, and no way to serve bosons, which have no exclusion, yet the charged pion has an antiparticle too.

The cleaner reading needs no sea. A negative-energy solution running backward in time is a positive-energy antiparticle running forward. Run a film of a stone dropping into a pond in reverse and you see a stone leaping out of the water into a waiting hand. Nobody ran time backward to make that version; it is the same film read in the other direction. The negative-energy solution is the same equation read the other way, and what it describes is an ordinary antiparticle moving forward in time with positive energy.

Same mass, same spin, opposite charge, and the same spinors do the job with no new mathematics, only a new reading. The new reading pays for itself: it lets annihilation and pair production be computed with positive-energy particles and ordinary conservation laws. A lone photon cannot make a pair, since its energy is fixed by its momentum and any pair carrying that momentum has more energy. A heavy nucleus, soaking up momentum at almost no energy cost, makes it possible.

Nothing here is special to the electron. Any relativistic quantum theory of a charged particle, of any spin, must contain an antiparticle of equal mass and opposite charge, forced by structure rather than added as an assumption. Charge conjugation, swapping every particle for its antiparticle, leaves QED unchanged; no electron-positron difference has turned up beyond the sign of the charge and what follows from it. And one diagram, read with lines turned around, describes both Compton scattering and electron-positron annihilation.

## Lessons 23–30 — Fields, Symmetry and What Stays Put

The debt comes first. The Klein-Gordon equation, second order in time, let probability go negative, which is the sort of result that should send a theorist back to bed. Dirac's equation is first order in time, so the old trick from Lesson 11 works again: multiply the equation by the conjugate wave function, do the same to the conjugate equation, and subtract. What survives is a density built from the sum of the squared sizes of the four components, which cannot go negative, plus a matching flow. That conserved current (Lesson 23) finally closes the problem opened back in Lesson 17. Multiplied by charge, the same current is what Maxwell's equations will want as a source.

Now the subject changes from particles to fields. A field assigns every point of space its own number, free to wiggle in time, the way a weather map gives every location a temperature that rises and falls through the day. The difference is that a field has a value at every point, so the system has one coordinate per point of space, which is an enormous number of coordinates for something that fits in a lecture hall. Treated as a dynamical system (Lesson 24), it is the chain of masses and springs from Lesson 5, filed down until it looks smooth.

The Lagrangian density (Lesson 25) is the energy bookkeeping at each point: kinetic-style terms for how fast the field changes, minus potential-style terms for how it varies in space and how heavy it is. Added up over all space and time it gives the action, and because every observer must agree on that total, the density itself has to be a scalar. Demand that the action be stationary and the Euler-Lagrange equations (Lesson 26) hand back the field equations, the field version of the rule Lesson 5 used for particles. Feed in the right density and out come Klein-Gordon, Dirac and Maxwell, three famous results from one recycled principle.

Noether's theorem (Lesson 27) is the best bargain in physics: every continuous symmetry of the action comes with a conserved quantity, no extra assumptions required. Continuous symmetries (Lesson 28) are changes you can dial in by any small amount, after which you ask whether the action even noticed. Slide everything along in time and it does not, so energy is conserved; slide it in space and momentum is; rotate it and angular momentum is.

Conserved currents (Lesson 29) generalize the pattern: each symmetry yields a density with a flow obeying the same continuity rule as the current of Lesson 23. Whatever leaves a small region must cross its boundary, and the total stays fixed as long as the fields politely die away at infinity.

Global phase symmetry (Lesson 30) is the plainest symmetry going: shift the electron field's phase by the same amount everywhere at once. Think of moving every clock in a country forward one hour for daylight saving time. No train's schedule changes relative to any other train's; only the labels on the clocks do. Nothing measurable changes, so Noether hands over a conserved current, and it is the very current of Lesson 23.

Its total is electric charge. A real field has no phase to shift and stays neutral; a complex one can carry charge. Conservation of charge stops being bookkeeping and becomes a consequence of symmetry. Note the word global, though: the same shift, everywhere, no exceptions. Lesson 38 asks what happens once each point gets to choose its own shift, and the answer is QED.

## Lessons 31–37 — How Fields Turn into Particles

Relativity forces the change of viewpoint from particles to fields (Lesson 31). Einstein's equivalence of mass and energy means particles can be made and unmade: light becomes an electron and a positron, and the pair becomes light again, an accounting trick nature performs without asking permission. A wave function built for a fixed head count cannot describe a world where the number keeps changing mid-story. So the right variable is a field, with particles as its ripples. There is one electron field, and every electron is identical for the same dull reason every ripple on one pond is made of the same water.

Quantizing the simplest case, the scalar field (Lesson 32), follows the recipe of Lesson 6. The field and its momentum become operators obeying the same uncertainty rule as position and momentum. A free field then splits into independent momentum modes, each a harmonic oscillator, the prototype from Lesson 2, finally getting the career it always wanted.

Each oscillator gets ladder operators (Lesson 33): one adds a quantum of energy and momentum, one removes it, and that quantum is what a particle actually is. For a charged field, the two kinds of wave from Lesson 17 sort themselves out: positive-frequency waves remove particles and negative-frequency ones create antiparticles. The density that once went negative becomes charge density, which is allowed the privilege.

The natural home for all this bookkeeping is Fock space (Lesson 34). Start with the vacuum, every mode empty, and apply creation operators to add particles at will; the count is no longer fixed, which is rather the point. A state is simply a list of how many quanta sit in each mode, like a blank sheet of music paper with notes written in. Bosons pile any number into one mode without complaint.

The bill arrives too: every oscillator carries zero-point energy, and infinitely many oscillators sum to an infinite total. Normal ordering, which writes every removal operator to the right of every creation operator, simply discards it, a piece of housekeeping physicists perform with a straight face and no apology. Only energy differences are measured, so nothing observable is lost.

Dirac's field gets the same treatment (Lesson 35), its expansion built from two kinds of wave: positive-energy spinors multiplying operators that remove electrons, negative-energy spinors multiplying operators that create positrons. No infinite sea required this time. But the ladder rules cannot be the boson ones. Quantize a half-integer-spin field with ordinary commutators and its energy has no floor, while distant measurements would disturb one another in ways relativity forbids.

Anticommutation relations (Lesson 36) cure both. Swapping the order of two fermion operators flips the sign instead of leaving things unchanged, and as a bonus two identical creation operators cancel outright. That is the exclusion principle arriving as arithmetic rather than as a rule handed down from on high: no mode holds more than one electron.

Light comes last, with the quantized electromagnetic field (Lesson 37). Light in a box is standing waves, each an oscillator, a photon one quantum of one mode. The potential carries four components, yet a photon manages only two polarization states, gauge freedom having retired the other two before anyone could count them. Keep only the transverse waves and the two physical polarizations show themselves plainly, at the cost of hiding relativistic symmetry in the notation, which is the usual QED bargain: clarity here, elegance there, never both at once.

## Lessons 38–44 — The Symmetry That Creates Light

The phase symmetry of Lesson 30 was global: one shift, applied everywhere, like a memo telling the whole office to wear blue on the same Friday. Local phase symmetry (Lesson 38) lets every point in spacetime pick its own phase instead, every employee choosing a color independently. In an office that is merely a fashion problem. The electron field's equation, though, cares about how the phase changes from one point to the next, so letting every point choose freely breaks the equation on the spot: differentiating the field also differentiates the now-varying phase and leaves a stray term behind.

Canceling it needs a new field that shifts by a gradient whenever the phase does. That field is the electromagnetic potential, carrying the gauge freedom Lesson 8 introduced under a gentler name. Reading off the result (Lesson 39) gives the interaction itself, the electron's current multiplied by the potential, the classical coupling of Lesson 5 in quantum dress. Nobody writes light into the theory by hand. It shows up uninvited and refuses to leave, more than can be said for most houseguests.

The fix gets packaged as the covariant derivative (Lesson 40), the ordinary derivative plus a piece containing the potential, built so it transforms exactly as the electron field does. A treadmill that reads out your true walking speed, correcting for whatever the belt is doing underneath, is the right idea: the covariant derivative subtracts the spurious drift a locally varying phase would add and leaves only the genuine rate of change. Put it in place of the ordinary derivative in the free Dirac Lagrangian and the interaction appears unbidden, just what the new derivative was carrying all along.

The QED Lagrangian (Lesson 41) is the Dirac electron with its covariant derivative, plus a term for the free electromagnetic field. That term is built from the field tensor (Lesson 42): an antisymmetric bundle of derivatives of the potential whose six independent pieces are the electric and magnetic fields previewed in Lesson 4. It survives a gradient shift of the potential unchanged; a photon mass term would not, which is the tidy reason light stays massless.

Vary the potential and Maxwell's equations emerge, sourced by the electron's current; vary the electron field and Dirac's equation emerges in the field's presence. Remarkably little freedom is left: mass and charge fix the whole theory.

Charge (Lesson 43) finally gets its proper job title: the strength of the link between the electron field and the photon field. Combined with Planck's constant and the speed of light, it becomes the fine-structure constant, a stubbornly plain number near one part in 137 that nobody has ever derived from first principles, though not for lack of trying. Being small, it invites the expansion of Lesson 15, each extra photon exchanged costing roughly another factor of it.

Read as a picture, the interaction term is the electron-photon vertex (Lesson 44): one electron line in, one out, one photon attached, all at a single point. The entire drama of QED reduces to this one small junction, repeated the way a single shape of Lego brick builds everything from a toy car to a castle. Its value is the charge, for strength, times a gamma matrix tying the photon's polarization to the current of Lesson 23. Turn the lines around and the same vertex describes a positron, a pair forming, or a pair annihilating. A whole theory of light and matter built from one recurring doodle is either admirable economy or a shortage of imagination, and the experiments keep siding with economy.

## Lessons 45–52 — How Feynman Diagrams Turn into Numbers

Perturbation theory (Lesson 15) wants a precisely solvable starting point and a small nudge. In QED the starting point is the free fields and the nudge is the interaction. Propagators (Lesson 45) carry a disturbance from place to place, each the response of a free field to a single sharp kick. In momentum space each becomes a fraction whose denominator measures how far the momentum sits from the mass shell, the relation energy, momentum and mass are supposed to respect.

Real particles live on the shell and behave themselves. Virtual ones, the intermediate stages of a process, do not. The popular image is a loan: borrow energy the books don't allow and repay it fast enough, with the uncertainty principle setting the terms. Keep the image loosely. Nobody has ever caught a virtual particle mid-violation, because an off-shell line is a piece of the calculation's internal bookkeeping and was never an event that could be observed. A tiny imaginary nudge in the denominator, Feynman's prescription, sends positive-energy waves forward in time and negative-energy ones backward, which is to say antiparticles forward.

Dirac's equation is first order, so the electron propagator (Lesson 46) carries a numerator too: momentum written in gamma matrices plus the mass, the same combination Lesson 21 found by summing over spin states. The photon propagator (Lesson 47) has no mass to insert, so its denominator is the momentum squared alone, which is why photon exchange gives an inverse-square force of unlimited range. A heavy carrier particle gives a force that dies off sharply beyond a range set by its mass, which is why the forces inside a nucleus stay cooped up there.

The gauge freedom that bothered Lesson 37 leaves the photon's equation with no inverse until a gauge is fixed. Feynman gauge is the tidy choice, and physical answers, reassuringly, do not care which one wins.

Each interaction vertex (Lesson 48) joins two electron lines and one photon line. An electron line runs straight through vertices and ends only at a diagram's edges or closes on itself in a loop. Charge conservation comes free at every vertex, and an arrow pointing backward marks a positron, a convention nobody involved will apologize for.

The Feynman rules (Lesson 49) assign an algebraic factor to every leg, line and vertex, turning a diagram into a formula with the field theory left in the cupboard. Each vertex costs a factor of the charge, so fewer vertices means a bigger contribution, and exchanging two identical fermions flips a sign, as Lesson 16 promised.

Adding the diagrams for a chosen initial and final state gives the scattering amplitude (Lesson 50): add first, square afterward, so routes interfere as in Lesson 1. Split off the spike enforcing conservation of energy and momentum and what remains is the invariant amplitude the diagrams compute.

External lines then need dressing with external states (Lesson 51): an electron brings a spinor, a positron the other spinor of Lesson 19, a photon a polarization vector. A sum over unmeasured spins collapses into a trace courtesy of Lesson 21.

That spike comes from momentum conservation at vertices (Lesson 52): at every vertex, incoming energy and momentum equal outgoing, because the vertex could sit anywhere and summing over every location forces the balance. It is Noether's theorem again, translation symmetry from Lesson 27 turning up in diagram form. Virtual lines carry energy and momentum without honoring the mass relation, strict on totals and relaxed on the paperwork. A loop-free diagram has every internal momentum fixed from outside; add a loop and momentum circulates freely, which is exactly where the infinities move in and refuse to leave.

## Lessons 53–60 — Doing the Actual Sums

Everything since Lesson 23 has been throat-clearing, useful throat-clearing, but throat-clearing all the same. Lessons 53 through 60 are where the machine built from gauge symmetry, propagators and vertex factors finally earns its keep by predicting numbers a detector can check.

Electron-muon scattering (Lesson 53) is the friendliest opener: two different particles, one photon exchanged between them, no risk of a particle being confused with itself. The amplitude is a photon propagator sandwiched between two currents, and squaring it, averaging over spins, produces the trace gymnastics that Lessons 20 and 21 spent so long drilling. At low energy the result reduces to Rutherford's familiar scattering formula, with the spin corrections bolted on.

Electron-electron scattering (Lesson 54) adds the complication that the two outgoing electrons are identical, so the amplitude needs both the direct exchange and the swapped, minus-signed alternative from Lesson 16's fermion bookkeeping. Forget the minus sign and the answer is simply wrong, which is nature's way of reminding you that electrons do not carry name tags.

Electron-positron annihilation (Lesson 55) and Compton scattering (Lesson 56) are close cousins, related by reading a Feynman diagram with a line turned around, so that an incoming particle becomes an outgoing antiparticle. It is the reversed film from Lesson 22 applied to a whole diagram: one sketch, read two ways, tells the stories of two different-looking processes. Annihilation turns matter and antimatter into a fleeting virtual photon that becomes some other particle pair, a muon and antimuon for instance.

Compton scattering, light bouncing off an electron, needs two diagrams added together rather than subtracted, since no identical fermions are being swapped. Its result reproduces Compton's wavelength shift and, at low energy, the classical Thomson cross section.

Pair production (Lesson 57) runs the same diagrams in yet another direction: two photons colliding to make an electron and a positron, provided their combined energy in the center-of-mass frame covers the two rest masses. Two photons need no help. A single photon does, and as Lesson 22 already showed, it needs something nearby, usually a nucleus, to absorb the recoil.

None of this is useful to an experimentalist until an amplitude becomes a number with "events per second" attached. Converting amplitude to cross section (Lesson 58) folds in the available phase space, how many ways the outgoing particles can share the energy and momentum on offer, and divides by the incident flux. The cross section is an effective target area; multiply it by the beam's intensity and you get the event rate.

Decay rates (Lesson 59) are the same idea aimed at a single unstable particle rather than a collision, asking how quickly it vanishes into its decay products. The rate's inverse is the lifetime. Both calculations lean on the kinematics already set up back in Lesson 4.

The group closes with experimental predictions (Lesson 60), less a new idea than an accounting exercise. Take the cross sections and decay rates just built, fold in the exact way a real detector is blind to some events and biased toward others, and produce a number that can be compared fairly with data. Detectors cover limited angles, miss low-energy particles and have finite resolution, and each of those enters the prediction. It is an unglamorous lesson by design. Physics that cannot survive contact with a detector's fine print is not yet physics.

## Lessons 61–69 — Loops, Infinities, and Cleaning Up After Yourself

Every diagram so far had particles going from one place straight to another: tree diagrams, named for their branching-but-never-looping shape. Loop diagrams (Lesson 61) add a closed circuit inside a diagram, where a virtual particle, or several, can carry any momentum at all and nobody outside gets to know which. Quantum mechanics insists you add up every possibility consistent with the boundary conditions, so the calculation becomes an integral over all that unmeasured momentum. Loops are suppressed by an extra factor of the fine-structure constant, which is why they are small corrections, and why they are where the precision lives.

Three loops matter most, one for the photon, one for the electron, and one for the junction between them. Vacuum polarization (Lesson 62) has a virtual electron-positron pair briefly popping out of a photon and back in. The pairs act like a dielectric between two charges, partly polarizing and canceling some of the field passing through, the way a real dielectric does in *Foundations*, Chapter 6. Here there is no material at all, just empty space that is forever, fleetingly, not quite as empty as its name suggests. Its effect on precision measurements gets a second look in Lesson 77.

Electron self-energy (Lesson 63) is the electron interacting with its own photon field, emitting and reabsorbing a virtual photon. The loop shifts the electron's effective mass, and it is one of the places where a divergence first demands attention. Vertex corrections (Lesson 64) dress up the basic electron-photon coupling itself with an extra photon stretched across the vertex. They change how an electron responds to a magnetic field, which makes them the source of the anomalous magnetic moment of Lesson 75.

Add up the contribution of a loop and, embarrassingly often, the integral refuses to converge: push the loop momentum to ever larger values and the answer keeps growing instead of settling down. Measuring a coastline on ever more detailed maps behaves the same way: every zoom reveals wiggles that add length, and the total never settles. That is an ultraviolet divergence (Lesson 65), so called because the trouble sits at high frequency, or short distance, and not because it burns anyone.

The sane response is bookkeeping, which regularization (Lesson 66) supplies. Tweak the calculation, say by cutting off the momentum at some large value or by pretending spacetime has a few fewer dimensions than four, so the integral gives a finite, if temporarily silly-looking, answer. The original theory comes back once the trick is switched off.

The regularized but still messy result then goes through renormalization (Lesson 67), the least loved and most essential idea in the whole course. Think of the number written into the original equation as a wholesale price and whatever an experiment measures as the retail price, with every markup already folded in. A shopper only ever sees the shelf price. The mass and charge that appear in the original QED equation were never measured directly; they are bare parameters, decorated by an infinite cloud of virtual particles before anything is observed.

Renormalization absorbs the divergent parts of the loops into a redefinition of those bare quantities, so the theory is written in terms of the measured mass and charge, and only finite, physically meaningful leftovers remain to predict anything. QED needs just this handful of redefinitions to tame every divergence at every order, which is what makes it renormalizable. It sounds like cheating and was widely accused of being exactly that for a couple of decades, until it kept getting the right answer to more decimal places than any rival scheme.

One of those leftovers is that the electron's effective charge is not quite constant. Running electric charge (Lesson 68) shows the vacuum polarization loops of Lesson 62 screening the bare charge at long distance, so a probe that gets closer, at higher energy, sees a slightly stronger charge. A heckler in a crowded room works the same way: muffled from the back, with more crowd doing more muffling, and closer to full volume the nearer you push to the front. That's a mild and rather droll defiance of the "constant" in fundamental constant.

The renormalization group (Lesson 69) is the formal machinery tracking how the coupling drifts, or runs, with the energy scale being probed. Its central equation says how fast the coupling changes per step in energy, and for QED the answer is slowly and upward. It is the tool that later lets QED be fitted neatly inside the larger Standard Model.

## Lessons 70–78 — What Survives the Housekeeping

Gauge freedom, the freedom to shift the electromagnetic potential without changing any physics, was a convenience in Lesson 8 and becomes an outright headache once the field is quantized: the photon's equations refuse to invert cleanly unless that freedom is pinned down first. Gauge fixing (Lesson 70) picks a convention, commonly the one flagged as the residual freedom left over in Lesson 8, purely to make the algebra tractable. No observable prediction cares which convention got picked, the way a cake doesn't care whether the recipe was written in cups or grams.

Ward identities (Lesson 71) are the formal guarantee behind that promise. They follow from charge conservation and tie together different pieces of a calculation, such as a vertex correction and a propagator correction, so their gauge-dependent parts cancel on cue. One consequence is that the vertex and self-energy infinities cancel each other in the charge, leaving only vacuum polarization to renormalize it. That is why the electron, the muon and the proton, with very different clouds of virtual particles, all end up with exactly the same measured charge magnitude.

Gauge invariance (Lesson 72) is the payoff: every physical answer, cross section, decay rate, magnetic moment, comes out the same regardless of the arbitrary bookkeeping convention used along the way, which is either deeply reassuring or a little suspicious depending on temperament.

Loops bring a second kind of infinity, at the opposite end of the energy scale from Lesson 65's. Infrared divergences (Lesson 73) show up when a virtual photon inside a loop carries almost no energy at all, and the calculation blows up there instead of at high energy. Massless photons can be arbitrarily soft, and that is where the trouble comes from.

The resolution, soft photons (Lesson 74), is refreshingly physical. No real detector can tell an electron that scattered without incident from one that scattered while shedding an undetectably faint photon, so the two must be added together as one measurable outcome, and the infinities from each piece cancel. The trick turns out to be a plain statement of what "detecting an electron" actually means.

The last stretch turns loop corrections into the most stringent tests QED has ever passed. The anomalous magnetic moment (Lesson 75) asks how much stronger a spinning electron's little bar magnet is than Dirac's tidy tree-level equation predicts, once all the loop corrections, from Lesson 61 onward, are stacked up. Theory and experiment agree to somewhere past the ninth decimal place, a level of concord that borders on the unreasonable for a theory built out of infinite series that do not, strictly, even converge.

The Lamb shift (Lesson 76) is the same style of triumph applied to hydrogen. Two atomic levels that the Dirac equation predicts should sit at exactly the same energy are nudged apart by loop effects, mostly the electron's self-energy. The splitting was measured in 1947, before the theory could compute it, and the push to explain it helped drive the renormalized QED of the late 1940s.

Vacuum polarization and precision (Lesson 77) folds the running charge of Lesson 68 back into these precision numbers. Close to the nucleus an electron sees more of its unscreened charge, so the attraction there is slightly stronger, and in hydrogen this nudges the levels in the opposite direction from the self-energy, a small but clearly measured piece of the Lamb shift. Every precision prediction must include these loops, and the heavier particles that can appear in them as well.

Precision tests of QED (Lesson 78) round out the group by surveying how thoroughly the theory has been interrogated. The sharpest check compares values of the fine-structure constant extracted from very different experiments, such as the electron's magnetic moment, the recoil of atoms absorbing photons, and the quantum Hall effect. If QED were wrong somewhere, those numbers would disagree. So far the theory has consistently refused to be caught out.

## Lessons 79–86 — A Different Toolkit, and Where QED Fits

Everything to this point built amplitudes diagram by diagram, one Feynman rule at a time. Path integrals (Lesson 79) offer an entirely different, and in some ways more candid, starting point. Instead of solving an equation of motion for one classical trajectory, add up a phase for every conceivable path or field configuration, weighted by the classical action from Lesson 25, with the paths far from the classical one mostly canceling each other out.

Remember the lifeguard from Lesson 5, picking the one fastest route to a drowning swimmer? A path integral sends every conceivable lifeguard out at once along every conceivable path, zigzagging, looping back, wading needlessly out to sea, each casting a vote in the form of a phase set by the action that path used up. Nearly all the votes have wildly mismatched phases and cancel into irrelevance. Only the bundle of paths clustered near the sensible route agrees, so the classical path is simply what survives the vote. It is the same physics from a different camera angle, and considerably better suited to some questions than a diagram ever was.

Generating functionals (Lesson 80) package that sum into a single object, with an artificial source term added, from which every correlation function can be pulled out by repeated differentiation with respect to the source. It works like a vending machine: press the right combination of buttons and out comes the specific quantity you wanted, with nothing pre-made on a shelf.

Effective actions (Lesson 81) go a step further, summing loop effects into a modified action whose classical-looking equations already include the quantum corrections. The classic example is the Euler-Heisenberg action, in which loops of virtual electrons add small terms that let light scatter off light, something Maxwell's equations alone forbid.

Functional methods (Lesson 82) supply the calculus needed to make any of it tractable: differentiating and integrating with respect to an entire field configuration rather than a single number. Electron fields need a twist, anticommuting numbers in place of ordinary ones, which is how the path integral reproduces the anticommutators of Lesson 36.

Feynman rules from the path integral (Lesson 83) closes the loop, so to speak, by showing that the diagrams of Lesson 49 were there all along, hiding inside an expansion of the path integral's interaction term. Every vertex is one power of that term, and every propagator is what the free part of the integral hands back.

The final lessons put QED into settings well beyond a cold vacuum. QED at finite temperature (Lesson 84) asks what the theory looks like inside a hot plasma, such as the early universe. It uses a slightly sly device in which ordinary time is swapped for an imaginary, periodic version whose period encodes the temperature, hotter meaning shorter. Glue the ends of a paper strip into a loop and the circumference becomes a new number the straight strip never had; here that number is the temperature. Boson fields repeat around the loop and fermion fields flip sign, a last echo of Lesson 36.

QED in external fields (Lesson 85) considers strong background fields treated exactly instead of as small corrections. Make an electric field strong enough, around a billion billion volts per meter, and the vacuum itself starts behaving oddly: it sparks electron-positron pairs out of nothing, the Schwinger effect, because at that strength the field does an electron's rest energy of work across roughly one electron Compton wavelength, enough to pull a virtual pair apart into real particles.

Connection to the Standard Model (Lesson 86) is the capstone, situating everything since Lesson 38 within the larger structure of particle physics. QED's local phase symmetry is one piece of a bigger gauge structure. Electromagnetism and the weak force descend from a single electroweak symmetry, and a further piece of machinery, the Higgs field, breaks it down to the one this entire course has patiently built from the ground up, leaving the photon massless and the weak force's carriers heavy.

It is the same kind of merger Maxwell made in *Foundations*, Chapter 12, where electricity and magnetism turned out to be a single electromagnetic field wearing two faces depending on how you looked at it. Electromagnetism and the weak force merge in the same spirit, one structure wearing two very different-looking faces once the energies drop low enough for the disguise to hold. QED is the low-energy shadow of a slightly grander theory, which is either humbling or exactly what you should expect from a theory that started by insisting an electron's phase could be twiddled independently at every point in space.
