## 32. Ten Thousand Years of Editing

Domestication is itself genetic engineering, just conducted far more slowly, by selecting among naturally occurring variants over many generations rather than editing DNA directly. The everyday comparison worth borrowing here is the photocopier image from Chapter 3, run with a twist: rather than copying the same original every time, a breeder copies only the single best-looking specimen from this generation and uses it, and it alone, as the "original" from which the next generation is drawn — no editing, no direct rewriting of a single letter, just a relentless, repeated act of selective recopying, generation after generation, until traits that started out as rare accidents become the reliable default.

Maize was domesticated from a wild grass called teosinte in Mexico's Balsas River valley roughly 9,000 years ago, through changes concentrated in just a handful of genes — among them tb1 and tga1 — which control how much a plant branches and how tightly its kernels are encased. Wheat domestication, some 10,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent, hinged on a different kind of mutation: one that stopped the seed head from shattering and scattering its seeds naturally, so that they instead stayed put on the stalk waiting to be harvested — meaning the annual harvest itself did much of the selecting, favoring exactly the plants that were, in the wild, worst at reproducing on their own. Bread wheat as we know it today resulted from a later natural cross between domesticated wheat and a wild goat grass, a hybridization event that stacked a third full genome onto the plant in one step.

Animal domestication followed its own separate timelines: dogs from wolves, somewhere between 15,000 and over 30,000 years ago, possibly the earliest domesticated species of all; cattle from wild aurochs around 10,500 years ago (the aurochs itself was an imposing animal, standing taller than a man at the shoulder, and the very last one died in a Polish forest in 1627); and chickens from red junglefowl, with dates recently revised to around 3,500 years ago, in a rice-growing village — suggesting the birds were likely drawn in by the rice rather than deliberately sought out, with the people coming to the birds only afterward.

Deliberate mutagenesis breeding — inducing random mutations with radiation or chemicals to accelerate the appearance of useful new variants — has been practiced since the 1920s, and has produced more than 3,400 officially registered "mutant" crop varieties, including Golden Promise barley and red grapefruit. These varieties carry no special labeling and are treated by regulators as ordinary "conventional" breeding, despite arguably being a far blunter intervention than modern gene editing.

An American agronomist's Green Revolution wheat, developed through the 1950s and 60s, incorporated a Japanese dwarfing gene originally found in a variety called Norin 10, which prevented the plant from toppling over ("lodging") once heavy fertilizer use made it grow taller and top-heavy. The resulting yield increases were dramatic across Mexico, India, and Pakistan, and its creator was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970 — the only plant breeder ever to receive it. The actual dwarfing genes responsible weren't molecularly identified until decades after they were already being bred into crops worldwide — wheat's in 1999, the parallel gene in rice a few years later — and both proved to act on a growth hormone called gibberellin, the wheat genes by blunting the plant's response to it and the rice gene by cutting its production.

## 33. The Tomato That Didn't Rot

The Flavr Savr tomato, brought to market by Calgene in 1994, was the first genetically engineered food ever sold commercially — engineered to ripen more slowly so it could stay on the shelf longer without rotting. Commercially, it flopped. Regulatorily and symbolically, though, it mattered enormously, proving that both regulators and consumers would accept such a product on the shelf at all.

Real, widespread adoption came two years later, with Monsanto's 1996 launch of glyphosate-tolerant ("Roundup Ready") soybeans and insect-resistant Bt corn and cotton. By 2024, engineered traits accounted for somewhere between 92 and 97 percent of all US corn, soy, and cotton acreage — engineered crops had, in practical terms, simply become American agriculture.

Some individual success stories stand out clearly. Hawaii's Rainbow papaya, engineered for virus resistance and approved in 1998, single-handedly saved the state's papaya industry from an outbreak of ringspot virus that had been devastating it. Golden Rice, engineered to carry extra vitamin A and developed back in 2000, took more than two decades to actually reach commercial planting, finally arriving in the Philippines in 2021 amid persistent opposition — only for a court to revoke its planting permit in 2024.

On safety, the scientific consensus is about as strong as these things get: a comprehensive 2016 National Academies review of roughly 900 separate studies found no evidence of any health-risk difference between engineered and conventionally bred crops. Earlier scare studies that had once seemed to suggest otherwise — a 1998 potato study and a 2012 rat study — did not hold up under scrutiny, and the latter was ultimately retracted by its publishing journal.

Survey data consistently shows that public distrust of engineered food centers on corporate control and a lack of transparency, not on any specific molecular safety concern — people worry less about the gene and more about who owns it.

Regulation diverges sharply by region, and the split partly comes down to a distinction worth being explicit about: an older-style "GMO," like the Flavr Savr tomato above, typically has a whole foreign gene from some other species spliced in, using the recombinant-DNA techniques from Chapter 20, while a modern "gene-edited" crop, using the CRISPR-style tools from Chapter 25, often just tweaks a few of the plant's own existing letters, with nothing foreign added at all. The United States settled on mandatory "bioengineered" food labeling starting in 2022. The European Union, by contrast, heavily restricts cultivation of engineered crops (only a single crop has ever been approved for growing there) and, somewhat inconsistently, regulates precisely gene-edited crops — no foreign DNA, remember — under the same strict rules as older-style GMOs with foreign genes spliced in, while exempting randomly radiation- or chemical-mutated crops from that scrutiny entirely — even though the latter involves a far less targeted intervention.

Japan approved the first CRISPR-edited food for retail sale, a high-GABA tomato, in 2021, and the first transgenic plant sold for home gardeners was a purple tomato engineered for enhanced anthocyanin content, launched in the US in 2024.

## 34. Animals on the Menu

Conventional selective breeding has already reshaped farm animals more radically than any single gene edit has managed so far: a modern broiler chicken reaches slaughter weight at more than four times the mass of a 1957 bird raised on identical feed, a transformation achieved wholly through decades of ordinary selective breeding, with no genetic engineering involved at all.

That intensive selection carries real welfare costs, though. Fast-growing broilers are prone to leg disorders, heart failure, and a muscle abnormality called "woody breast," while the parent flocks bred to produce them are typically kept on calorie-restricted diets to prevent them from growing too large to breed successfully — leaving those breeding birds chronically hungry by design.

Genetic engineering has had a much rockier commercial path in animals than in plants. AquaBounty's salmon, engineered with an added growth-hormone gene and approved in the US in 2015 and in Canada in 2016, did reach market weight faster than ordinary salmon — but retailers largely refused to stock it, and the company exited fish farming altogether in 2024.

Gene-edited hornless dairy cattle, produced in 2015 to spare the animals a painful dehorning procedure, ran into an embarrassing technical snag: researchers later discovered that residual bacterial plasmid DNA, including antibiotic-resistance genes, had been accidentally carried along with the intended edit — a cautionary lesson in verifying the entire genome after an edit, not just the specific site that was targeted.

More recent approvals suggest the technology is finding firmer footing. In April 2025, the FDA approved CRISPR-edited pigs resistant to the PRRS virus for the US food supply, often described as the first full FDA approval of a CRISPR-edited food animal, though the agency had already issued a low-risk determination for heat-tolerant, gene-edited cattle back in 2022; it had earlier approved pigs edited to reduce allergens and serve as organ donors, in 2020, whose kidneys have actually been transplanted into human patients since 2021.

Not every attempt has fully succeeded. Chickens edited at Roslin Institute for partial resistance to bird flu, reported in 2023, showed clear promise, but the virus partially adapted around the single edit used — suggesting that full resistance will likely require stacking multiple edits together rather than relying on just one.

The central welfare question applies equally to breeding and editing: does a given change truly reduce an animal's suffering (removing horns, say), or does it merely make it easier to keep the animal in worse conditions at greater scale (a disease-resistant pig housed more densely than it otherwise could be)? The right test, on this view, is always the animal's actual lived experience — not whether the change that produced it came from a breeding program or a gene-editing lab.

## 35. What Is Actually Risky

The risky aspects of engineered agriculture need separating from the mostly unfounded fears about eating it. Herbicide-resistant "superweeds" are a real problem: Palmer amaranth became resistant to glyphosate by 2005, in some cases carrying up to 160 extra copies of the very gene the herbicide targets. But this resistance is a consequence of how the herbicide was used — sprayed relentlessly across huge, uniform acreages — rather than anything intrinsic to the engineered gene itself.

Insect resistance to Bt crops tells a similar story regarding management, not molecular danger. Where farmers ignored requirements to plant non-Bt "refuge" crops nearby (intended to dilute the buildup of resistant insects), resistance emerged relatively quickly, as happened with pink bollworm in India. Where those refuge requirements were properly followed, as in Arizona, resistance was successfully prevented and even reversed.

Gene flow from engineered crops into wild or weedy relatives is a verified phenomenon, though a modest one in practice: escaped engineered bentgrass and volunteer canola plants have both spread their introduced genes into nearby wild populations. Unapproved engineered crops have also occasionally slipped into the general food supply — StarLink corn in 2000 and an unapproved rice variety in 2006 both triggered costly recalls, though neither produced any documented harm to health. Nobody was harmed by eating any of it; everyone was harmed by the mess of the recall itself.

The single deepest risk in agriculture, though, has nothing to do with genetic engineering at all: it's monoculture, growing vast acreages of genetically identical plants. The Irish Potato Famine (a single potato clone), the 1970 US corn blight (caused by a uniform male-sterile trait bred into nearly the entire national corn crop), and the Cavendish banana's ongoing vulnerability to Panama disease all trace back to exactly this kind of genetic uniformity, regardless of whether the crop involved was ever genetically engineered.

Corporate consolidation in the seed industry — with Bayer (having acquired Monsanto), Corteva, Syngenta, and BASF now controlling roughly 60 percent of the proprietary seed market — and the patent litigation that has followed (including Supreme Court cases against farmers Percy Schmeiser and Vernon Bowman) are genuine concerns, but they're concerns about patent law and market concentration, not about genetic risk as such. Separately, litigation over glyphosate and cancer concerns the herbicide spray itself, not the engineered trait that tolerates it — and by 2024, by at least one measure, Bayer's market value had fallen below what it originally paid to acquire Monsanto in the first place.

It's also worth remembering that every ordinary meal already contains enormous quantities of "foreign" DNA from whatever plants and animals it's made of, all of it harmlessly digested like any other nutrient; there is no verified case, ever, of a plant or animal gene from food actually integrating into a human chromosome.

The Svalbard Global Seed Vault, opened in 2008, offers the counterweight: it preserves crop genetic diversity specifically as insurance against precisely the kind of monoculture failure described above — a hedge against risks that have nothing to do with gene editing and everything to do with how narrowly we've bred our food crops in the first place.

## 36. Three Billion Letters, One Race

The Human Genome Project pitted a publicly funded international consortium, formally launched in 1990, against a private competitor: Celera Genomics, founded in 1998 and led by a researcher who had previously been part of the public effort himself before striking out on his own.

The two sides took different technical approaches, both variations on the shredded-manuscript trick from Chapter 21. The public project used a careful, methodical "hierarchical shotgun" strategy: first sort the shredded pages roughly into their original chapters using a coarser map, and only then shred and reassemble the text within each chapter individually — slower, but with a running check against reassembling the pages in the wrong order. Celera bet on a faster but riskier "whole-genome shotgun" approach: shred the entire manuscript at once with no preliminary sorting into chapters at all, and trust the computer to reassemble the whole thing directly from the raw overlaps — a method its leader first proved out on a fly genome, and later, notably, on his own personal genome.

In 1996, the public project's leadership committed to something called the Bermuda Principles: releasing all sequence data freely and immediately, without delay for publication or patenting. It was both a strategic and an idealistic move, explicitly intended to prevent anyone from patenting the raw human gene sequence itself.

Competition from Celera pushed the public project to move faster than it otherwise might have, and the rivalry ended, somewhat artificially, with a jointly announced "working draft" at the White House on 26 June 2000, with President Clinton and Prime Minister Blair presiding — a negotiated political truce rather than an actual scientific finish line.

Draft papers from both teams were published side by side in February 2001, in Nature and Science respectively, and the "finished" sequence was formally announced on 14 April 2003, deliberately timed to coincide with the fiftieth anniversary of Watson and Crick's double-helix paper. The total public cost came to roughly $2.7 billion, delivered under budget and ahead of the original schedule.

Celera, meanwhile, found itself unable to profitably sell access to a genome that was already freely available from the public project, and pivoted its business toward drug discovery instead.

## 37. What the Genome Turned Out to Say

A famous scientific betting pool on how many genes the human genome would turn out to contain — guesses ranged from 26,000 to well over 150,000 — was won by the person who made the lowest realistic guess, around 25,947. The actual figure settled even lower still, at close to 20,000 protein-coding genes: a count comparable to, and in some cases barely different from, far simpler organisms like roundworms and fruit flies.

Human complexity isn't built from having more genes; it's built from doing more with the genes available. Alternative splicing (introduced back in Chapter 8) lets some 20,000 genes generate over 100,000 distinct proteins, and the regulatory sequences controlling when and where those genes switch on actually occupy more of the genome than the genes themselves do.

Statistics comparing genomes need to be read carefully, because the same underlying data can produce very different-sounding headlines depending on what exactly is being counted. The oft-repeated claim that any two humans are "99.9 percent identical" refers to single-letter differences alone (which still add up to on the order of 3 million differences per pair of genomes); counting larger structural differences as well brings that similarity figure down closer to 99 percent. The comparison between humans and chimpanzees follows the same pattern: about 98.8 percent identical if you only count matched, aligned single letters; closer to 96 percent once you count insertions and deletions; and lower still if you count regions that can't be aligned between the two species at all. Both the "99.9 percent" and "98.8 percent" headline figures are technically true, and both need a footnote — they're answers to two different questions that only sound like they're arguing with each other.

Even the "finished" 2003 genome wasn't quite finished: it covered only something in the neighborhood of 92 percent of the full sequence, since highly repetitive regions kept resisting the short-read technology available at the time.

The Telomere-to-Telomere consortium closed that remaining gap in 2022, using long-read sequencing on a genetically uniform cell line to produce the first complete human genome — 3,054,815,472 letters, adding something like 200 million previously unseen letters to the record and predicting an estimated 2,000 additional genes nobody had previously cataloged, though only around a hundred of them code for protein.

One further limitation remained: any single reference genome, largely derived from one anonymous donor from Buffalo, New York, inevitably represents human genetic diversity rather poorly. The Human Pangenome Reference Consortium addressed this in 2023 by building a multi-genome "graph" reference drawn from 47 genetically diverse individuals, adding 119 million letters and some 1,100 extra gene copies that the standard single-person reference had simply been missing all along.

## 38. Biobanks and the Million

Population-scale "biobank" studies link genetic data to detailed health records across hundreds of thousands of volunteers, hunting for disease associations through sheer statistical scale rather than through any single family's inheritance pattern.

The UK Biobank pioneered this model, recruiting 502,000 participants between 2006 and 2010 and releasing whole-genome data for close to 490,000 of them in 2023; it's open to any researcher willing to pay an access fee, though no individual results are ever returned to the participants themselves. Its volunteer pool skewed healthier, wealthier, and less likely to smoke than the average person in Britain — which is, more or less, just what happens whenever a study relies entirely on people who volunteer.

The comparable American effort, "All of Us," opened for enrollment in 2018 and had signed up more than 800,000 participants by 2025; unlike the UK Biobank, it deliberately emphasizes historically underrepresented populations and does return actionable health findings directly to its participants.

Other major biobanks fill out the global picture: Iceland's deCODE, the China Kadoorie Biobank, Estonia's national biobank, Finland's FinnGen, the United States' Million Veteran Program, and the UK's separate, disease-focused 100,000 Genomes Project.

The engine behind most of this research is the genome-wide association study, or GWAS. The basic idea is closer to brute-force pattern-spotting than to any kind of educated guessing: take two enormous stacks of "photographs" — one stack of people who have a given disease, one stack of people who don't — and compare them position by position across several million cataloged genetic positions, flagging any single position where the letter reliably differs between the two stacks more often than pure chance would explain. It's a method that works precisely because it doesn't need a hypothesis going in; it just needs enough photographs and enough positions to check. This became possible once common human genetic variants had been cataloged in the first place (the HapMap project, completed in 2005) alongside increasingly cheap genotyping chips. GWAS mostly replaced an earlier, fairly unsuccessful approach called "candidate gene" studies, which guessed at likely genes in advance rather than scanning the whole genome impartially — the older method's equivalent of checking only the pixels you already suspected mattered, and mostly finding nothing. The first true GWAS, in 2005, turned up a completely unexpected gene, CFH, involved in macular degeneration — a result nobody had predicted going in, which was rather the point.

The cumulative findings show that common diseases typically involve hundreds or thousands of individually tiny-effect variants, most of them sitting in regulatory, non-coding regions of the genome rather than in genes themselves — flagged positions that, plotted out chromosome by chromosome, form the tall, narrow spikes of a "Manhattan plot," named for its visual resemblance to a city skyline. A single flagged spike, though, is only ever a marker of association, not proof of cause — it says "the letter here reliably differs between the sick stack and the healthy stack," not "this letter is what made anyone sick" — and because a real disease typically involves hundreds or thousands of such spikes acting together, no single one of them, examined alone, tells you much of anything about any one person's fate.

Polygenic risk scores attempt to sum up all these small individual effects into one overall predictive number. They're useful at the statistical extremes — identifying, say, the top percentile of people at meaningfully elevated heart disease risk — but they remain weak tools for predicting any individual's outcome in the broad middle of the distribution, where most people actually sit. They're also substantially less accurate for non-European ancestries, a direct consequence of study populations that were roughly 79 percent European in the earliest, foundational studies.

The real payoffs of this research have been genuine, if less headline-grabbing: identifying causal, not merely correlated, risk factors — variants in a gene called PCSK9, for instance, led to a whole new class of cholesterol-lowering drugs — and generally improving the success rate of drug development whenever genetic evidence already supports a proposed drug target.

## 39. The Spit Kit

Consumer DNA tests measure less than most customers probably assume. The Prologue's three-billion-letter book comparison makes the shortfall concrete: sequencing, the technology built up over Chapters 21 and 24, actually reads every page of that book, cover to cover. A consumer genotyping chip does something much narrower — it checks a fixed, pre-agreed spot-check list, flipping straight to roughly 640,000 specific, previously bookmarked pages and reading only those, rather than opening the book at page one and reading straight through. Take 23andMe, founded in 2006: it uses a genotyping chip that checks on the order of 640,000 predetermined positions in the genome — about 0.02 percent of the total — rather than sequencing the whole thing. That means it can only report on variants already known and cataloged in advance; it's fundamentally incapable of flagging a typo on some page nobody thought to bookmark, let alone detecting anything novel.

The company's regulatory history has been bumpy. The FDA halted sales of its health reports in 2013, on the grounds that the company was making unapproved "medical device" claims. Health reporting was gradually permitted again from 2015 onward, starting with carrier-status reports and expanding by 2018 to include a limited set of BRCA variants.

The underlying business model has always depended heavily on data, not just testing fees: close to 80 percent of customers consented to having their data used for research, which the company monetized through partnerships like a $300 million deal with GlaxoSmithKline in 2018. The company went public in 2021, then filed for bankruptcy in March 2025, following a major 2023 data breach that had exposed profile information for nearly 6.9 million users; it was acquired out of bankruptcy in mid-2025 by a nonprofit run by its own founder. The bankruptcy proceedings forced an oddly blunt legal question: were the genetic profiles of fifteen million people simply an asset, comparable to the company's office furniture, to be sold off to satisfy creditors? The court's answer was yes. The buyer promised to honor the company's existing privacy policy — a promise, and nothing more.

The ancestry percentages the company reports are statistical estimates of resemblance to reference populations, not literal genealogical records — closer to being told which regional accents your own voice most resembles, out of a fixed set of recorded accent samples, than to being handed an actual, verified family tree. Change the set of recorded accents on file and the same voice can get matched differently, which is exactly why these percentages shift every time the underlying reference panels get updated; and notably, full siblings routinely receive different ancestry percentages from each other, since inheritance doesn't hand out a neat, identical average to every child, for reasons already worked out back in Chapter 11.

Which brings the tube from the Prologue back around. Anna, Ruth, and Theo's saliva would have gone through exactly this pipeline: a chip reading a few hundred thousand predetermined spots, an ancestry estimate that quietly reshuffles itself with every database update, and a consent form for data use that may well outlive the company that collected it. Whatever their results said, it told them less about who they specifically are than about which reference populations a database happened to already contain — the freckles Theo actually shares with Ruth, the family had already worked out for themselves, the old-fashioned way, back in Chapter 11.

DNA-relative matching, which measures the actual length of shared DNA segments (in units called centimorgans) between users, is a considerably more reliable tool, and has surfaced plenty of "not parent expected" surprises — donor-conceived people discovering half-siblings they never knew existed, and, in one case, a fertility doctor's (Donald Cline's) undisclosed biological children finding each other and him decades later.

Health reports themselves mix two very different kinds of information: high-confidence, essentially binary Mendelian findings, like the lactase-persistence variant discussed back in Chapter 6, alongside much softer, probabilistic disease-risk estimates that require real interpretive care and shouldn't be mistaken for a diagnosis.

## 40. What a Genome Can Tell You

It's tempting to hear "carries the BRCA1 variant" or "has the Huntington's expansion" and picture something like a light switch: a single fact about a person's DNA that's either on or off, and that flips the associated disease on or off right along with it. Genetic testing done properly, in a clinical rather than recreational context, hinges on a single crucial number that consumer contexts often skip past, and that number exists precisely because the light-switch picture is sometimes right and often badly wrong: penetrance, meaning the actual fraction of variant carriers who go on to develop the associated condition at all. Some variants really do behave close to a light switch. Most behave more like a dimmer, or a weighted coin — nudging the odds substantially without ever guaranteeing the outcome — and knowing which kind of variant you're looking at changes everything about what the result should actually mean to the person holding it.

BRCA1 and BRCA2 make a good worked example. Mary-Claire King spent seventeen years tracking the first gene through affected families before identifying its location in 1990; Myriad Genetics identified BRCA1 itself in 1994, and a separate research group identified BRCA2 in 1995. A 2013 Supreme Court ruling in the Myriad case barred the patenting of naturally occurring gene sequences, which sharply cut the cost of testing for these variants. Carriers face a lifetime breast cancer risk of roughly 69 to 72 percent and an ovarian cancer risk of 17 to 44 percent, against a general population baseline of only about 12 to 13 percent for breast cancer — numbers stark enough that when Angelina Jolie publicly disclosed her own BRCA status and preventive surgery in 2013, referrals for genetic counseling approximately doubled in what's since been called "the Angelina effect."

Huntington's disease, discussed earlier for its inheritance pattern, also illustrates penetrance from the opposite end — genuinely close to that light switch after all: the causative expansion in the gene HTT, identified in 1993, is nearly completely penetrant, meaning virtually everyone who carries it eventually develops the disease. A predictive test has existed for decades, but most people at risk decline it — only somewhere between 10 and 20 percent choose to take it — since no available treatment can change the disease's ultimate course, and many people would simply rather not know.

The APOE ε4 variant sits at a much softer end of the penetrance spectrum, closer to the dimmer than the switch: it raises Alzheimer's risk something like threefold with one copy and eightfold to twelvefold with two, but falling well short of being deterministic. It became newly clinically relevant in 2023, once new antibody-based Alzheimer's drugs were found to cause more brain swelling specifically in carriers of the variant, making testing for it a meaningful part of treatment decisions.

Pharmacogenomics — using genetic testing to guide drug choice and dosing — is already a mature, practical application. Variants in the gene CYP2C19 affect how well a patient responds to the blood thinner clopidogrel (Plavix); a specific HLA gene variant is linked to dangerous hypersensitivity reactions to the HIV drug abacavir; and variants in CYP2C9 and VKORC1 affect proper dosing of the blood thinner warfarin. All three already carry official FDA label warnings.

Two other applications are well-established and actionable: carrier screening, which tests prospective parents for recessive conditions before conception (in practice since testing for Tay-Sachs disease began in 1971), and newborn screening, the routine heel-prick blood test performed on virtually every baby since 1963, now expanding toward pilot programs for full newborn genome sequencing, such as the UK's 2024 Generation Study.

## 41. What It Cannot Tell You

Even the best-studied polygenic trait of all, height — based on 5.4 million people and 12,111 identified variants — only explains close to 40 percent of height variation among people of European ancestry, and considerably less in other ancestries. That single statistic captures the real ceiling on genomic prediction for complex traits generally: more data keeps refining the picture, but a hard limit remains.

Part of the confusion around penetrance estimates comes from "ascertainment bias," a problem that shows up well outside genetics too: judging how dangerous a dog breed is purely by interviewing emergency-room patients will always make that breed look more dangerous than it really is, since calm, uneventful dogs of the same breed never show up in that particular waiting room to be counted. Studies that recruit families specifically because a disease already runs strongly through them make the identical mistake: they tend to inflate how penetrant a given variant looks, since those families were selected precisely because the variant was doing a lot of visible work in them. Broader, population-wide biobank data, drawing on people who weren't selected for having an affected relative, typically shows meaningfully lower practical penetrance for the very same variants.

There's also the long-running puzzle of "missing heritability": early GWAS studies explained only a small fraction of the heritability estimates that twin studies had suggested should exist — like knowing for certain that a sum of money is missing from an account, without being able to find the specific transaction that took it. Much of that gap has since been closed just by recognizing that thousands of individually tiny-effect variants were quietly adding up, invisible to studies that weren't yet large enough to detect them — the "missing" money, it turned out, hadn't vanished in one large withdrawal at all, but had leaked out in thousands of transactions each too small to notice individually — a problem solved mostly by scaling studies up into the millions of participants — though some of the original gap, and legitimate scientific debate about it, persists.

The popular habit of referring to "the gene for" a trait is often flatly misleading. The so-called "FTO obesity gene," for instance, doesn't actually cause its effect through FTO itself; the true mechanism runs through two neighboring genes, IRX3 and IRX5, that the original variant happens to influence from nearby.

Polygenic scores for common diseases like heart disease and breast cancer identify meaningful elevated risk only at the statistical extremes — someone in the top 8 percent of a risk score might face roughly three times the average risk. But because so many more people sit in the broad middle of the distribution than at its extremes, most actual disease cases still occur in people with entirely average scores, simply because there are so many more of them to begin with.

There's also a serious equity problem baked into these tools: because the underlying studies were built predominantly on European-ancestry participants (something like 80 percent of the data), the resulting scores perform 4.5 times worse or so when applied to people of African ancestry — a gap that can only be closed by deliberately building larger, more genetically diverse studies.

Polygenic scores for educational attainment and intelligence are a particularly fraught case: they explain only on the order of 12 to 16 percent of the variance in the trait, and half of even that modest signal disappears once the comparison is made within families rather than across the general population — strong evidence that much of what these scores capture is really "genetic nurture," meaning parental environment correlated with parental genetics, rather than anything acting directly on the child. The verdict is unambiguous: such scores should never be used to judge individuals or groups.

Chance itself accounts for a great deal of the variation that remains unexplained. Developmental noise and somatic mutation mean that identical twins, despite sharing effectively 100 percent of their inherited DNA, still show disease concordance rates of only somewhere between 25 and 50 percent for many conditions — a reminder, echoing Chapter 14, of just how much room is left over once genetics has had its say.

One practical policy matter remains: since 2013, medical guidelines have recommended that clinical genome sequencing routinely report a specific list of "actionable" incidental findings — a list that had grown to 81 genes by 2023 — regardless of why the sequencing was originally ordered. Patients can decline this reporting, exercising what's sometimes called a "right not to know," as James Watson himself chose to do with his own APOE status back in 2008 — though it later proved to be at least partially inferable anyway, purely from the DNA surrounding that gene.

## 42. Who Else Is Reading

The 2018 arrest of the "Golden State Killer," Joseph James DeAngelo, marked a turning point in forensic genetics. Investigators uploaded a crime-scene DNA profile to a public genealogy database, GEDmatch, to find distant relatives of the unknown suspect and build outward from there into a full family tree — a technique now called investigative genetic genealogy. It launched a wholly new forensic tool, and a completely new privacy debate alongside it: the technique in practice places anyone with even a distant cousin into a kind of permanent genetic lineup, without their consent and without a warrant, resting on nothing sturdier than a hobby website's terms of service.

The centimorgan-based matching from Chapter 39 is exactly what makes this work at such an unsettling distance: a third cousin still shares enough measurable DNA to show up clearly as a match, even though the two of you may never have met and share only a set of great-great-grandparents. Because most Americans of European descent already have a third-cousin-or-closer match sitting in one of the large genealogy databases, essentially the entire population has become "findable" this way — not through anything they personally did, but through relatives who voluntarily uploaded their own DNA for entirely unrelated reasons.

A regulatory patchwork followed the technique's rise. GEDmatch shifted to an opt-in model for law enforcement searches in 2019; Department of Justice policy that same year restricted the method to violent crimes; and Maryland became the first state, in 2021, to require judicial authorization before it could be used at all.

Existing legal protections leave real gaps. The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 bars genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment, but explicitly excludes life insurance, disability insurance, and long-term-care insurance from its protections, and doesn't apply at all to small employers or to the military.

A foundational legal precedent, Moore v. Regents of California (1990), holds that people generally lose any property rights over biological samples once those samples are removed from their body — the same legal backdrop underlying the Henrietta Lacks case, in which cells taken without her consent in 1951 were used in massive research and commercial applications for decades afterward; a 2023 lawsuit against a company that had profited from her cells was eventually settled, though the terms remain confidential.

That same commercial logic extends further than most people probably expect: consumer DNA databases are legally treated as ordinary commercial assets, transferable in bankruptcy proceedings just like any other company property — exactly what happened when 23andMe's 2025 bankruptcy put roughly 15 million people's genetic data up for sale, as described in Chapter 39.

National police DNA databases raise their own separate consent and retention controversies — including the UK's database, the US FBI's CODIS system, and reportedly large-scale government programs in China and elsewhere — and these concerns aren't merely theoretical: a 2008 European Court of Human Rights ruling found against the UK specifically over its practice of indefinitely retaining the DNA profiles of people who had been arrested but never convicted of anything.

## 43. Fixing What Is Broken

A sharp and important line separates two kinds of gene editing, and the clearest way to see it is through the copying machinery built up over the course of this book. Somatic editing is like correcting a typo in one specific printed copy of a book already out in the world — the correction helps whoever is holding that one copy, and it dies with that copy, since nothing about the master file the copy came from has changed. Germline editing, by contrast, edits eggs, sperm, or embryos — the master file itself — so the correction, in principle, should show up in every copy printed from it from then on: every one of that person's descendants. Push the book comparison one step further, though, and it starts to mislead in a useful way: unlike a word processor's master file, which reproduces a correction perfectly and deterministically in every future copy, a germline edit doesn't get transmitted with that kind of guarantee at all. It's just one more allele now, subject to all the ordinary shuffling of meiosis and inheritance from Chapter 11 — it could be diluted out of a lineage by chance within a few generations, or fixed in every descendant, exactly like any other piece of DNA, with no special immunity from the normal genetic dice. What is uniquely different about it, and what makes the distinction worth taking seriously, is that once that edit is sitting in a fertilized embryo, it will be present in every one of the trillions of cells that embryo eventually grows into, with no way to edit out afterward, and no way to ask the resulting person's permission first. Everything approved for actual use so far, without exception, is somatic.

Casgevy, introduced in Chapter 26, is worth revisiting here as an illustration of what "fixing" a genetic disease actually tends to mean in practice: it doesn't repair the sickle cell mutation itself at all. Instead, it disables a separate gene, BCL11A, to reactivate a form of hemoglobin the body only normally uses before birth — a workaround, or a "detour," rather than a genuine correction of the underlying error.

A growing roster of approved gene therapies — Luxturna, Zolgensma, Zynteglo, Hemgenix, Roctavian, Casgevy, and its rival Lyfgenia — now treat single-gene diseases of the eye, blood, liver, and motor nerves, at price tags ranging from $850,000 to $3.5 million per treatment.

KJ Muldoon's 2025 treatment, already described in Chapter 26, raises a further question: should regulators create a streamlined "platform" approval pathway specifically for these ultra-rare, individually customized gene therapies, rather than forcing each one through the same lengthy process designed for drugs meant to treat thousands of patients at once?

The real bottleneck in this field usually isn't the editing technology itself but how the edit gets delivered into the body. Viral vectors, typically built from adeno-associated virus (AAV), have limited cargo capacity and carry serious immune-response risks — including some patient deaths in high-dose clinical trials. Lipid nanoparticles, the same delivery technology behind mRNA vaccines, work well for liver-targeted therapies but still can't reliably reach the brain, muscle, or lungs, which remain much harder targets.

An estimated 260 to 450 million people worldwide — commonly rounded to about 300 million — live with one of something like 7,000 recognized rare diseases, but only a small fraction of them will ever receive a therapy built specifically for their own mutation, given the enormous cost and awkward trial design such individualized treatments require. Closing that gap is exactly what a streamlined regulatory pathway is meant to address.

And even where a cure genuinely exists, cost and healthcare infrastructure create stark inequality in who can in reality access it — sickle cell gene therapies, for instance, remain essentially unavailable across most of sub-Saharan Africa, which is precisely where the majority of the world's sickle cell patients live.

## 44. The Line We Said We Would Not Cross

The 2023 Third International Summit on Human Genome Editing reaffirmed that heritable, germline human genome editing "remains unacceptable at this time," while explicitly endorsing continued development of somatic editing, which affects only the treated individual.

The case in favor of germline editing isn't imaginary: for couples where both partners carry two copies of a recessive condition, or where one partner carries two copies of a dominant one, every possible embryo would be affected, which means embryo selection — simply picking the healthiest embryo from several available — offers no way out at all. In that narrow situation, editing is the only route to an unaffected biological child.

The case against remains formidable, though. The future child obviously cannot consent to a change made before they exist; any error introduced would be inherited by all of that person's descendants, not just corrected in one generation and then forgotten; and both embryo mosaicism (uneven editing across an embryo's cells) and off-target or unintended DNA damage remain concrete, demonstrated technical risks — the very thing that went wrong in He Jiankui's flawed 2018 edits, described in Chapter 31.

Preimplantation genetic testing, in clinical use since 1990, already offers a much less fraught alternative for most couples in this situation: rather than editing any embryo's DNA, it just tests multiple IVF embryos and selects one that doesn't carry the disease-causing variant at all — a solution that covers the great majority of single-gene-disease-avoidance cases and considerably weakens the practical argument for germline editing.

A newer and more contested practice has emerged alongside this: commercial "polygenic embryo selection," offered since 2019 by companies like Genomic Prediction and Orchid, which uses population-based polygenic risk scores to rank and choose among IVF embryos for complex traits — including height, disease risk, and even educational attainment. The actual expected gains from this practice are strikingly modest: choosing the best of ten embryos might buy roughly 2.5 centimeters of extra height or about 2.5 IQ points, according to the underlying statistics — and major scientific societies have called the whole practice premature given both the modest benefit and the underlying scientific uncertainty.

Mitochondrial replacement therapy, sometimes called "three-parent" IVF, is a heritable technique already legally in use, permitted in the UK since 2015 specifically to prevent inherited mitochondrial disease. The "three-parent" label oversells what's actually being swapped: recall from Chapter 12 that mitochondrial DNA is a tiny, separate loop of instructions, entirely apart from the twenty-three-volume main set, passed down only through eggs — so the procedure replaces a donor's healthy mitochondria in place of a mother's damaged ones, while the intended parents' full nuclear genome, the twenty-three volumes that actually make someone who they are, stays completely untouched. It's closer to swapping in a working battery pack than adding a third parent's genetic contribution in any meaningful sense; the first babies born in the UK using the technique were reported to be healthy in 2025.

Internationally, 70 of 96 surveyed countries prohibit heritable human genome editing outright. It's worth breaking from the neutral tally of positions to say plainly where this book itself comes down, if only because pretending otherwise after forty-three chapters would be its own kind of dishonesty. The stance here is explicit and personal: supportive of somatic editing and conventional embryo selection, willing to tolerate but personally unwilling to use unproven polygenic embryo selection, supportive of mitochondrial replacement, and in favor of keeping germline nuclear editing off-limits for the time being — while rejecting "never" as a permanent, unrevisable absolute.

## 45. Enhancement

The real genetic variants that come closest to conferring "superhuman" abilities make a list far shorter than movies would suggest, and evolution has already effectively priced most of them: every single entry comes with a footnote explaining the catch.

Individuals with a deficiency in a gene that normally limits muscle growth, myostatin, show exceptional muscle mass from birth — documented in a Berlin boy diagnosed in 2004 — and the exact same underlying principle explains naturally double-muscled cattle breeds.

A mutation in the gene EPOR gave the Finnish cross-country skier Eero Mäntyranta naturally elevated hemoglobin and oxygen-carrying capacity — as close to a natural blood-doping allele as exists in humans, and he spent the rest of his life insisting, not unreasonably, that this didn't make him a cheat. The footnote here is that thicker blood also clots more readily, carrying its own health risks.

A specific 32-letter deletion in the gene CCR5 confers near-immunity to the most common strains of HIV — it's the same mechanism that enabled the celebrated "Berlin patient" HIV cure via bone marrow transplant in 2007 — but it also increases vulnerability to West Nile virus, and it was, not incidentally, the poorly executed target of He Jiankui's 2018 embryo edits described back in Chapter 31.

A handful of smaller-effect variants round out the list: ACTN3 affects fast-twitch muscle performance in elite athletes, though only modestly; a rare variant in the gene pair BHLHE41/DEC2 lets some people function normally on unusually short, restorative sleep (nobody actually knows whether those saved hours come with some hidden cost over a lifetime); and LRP5 variants produce unusually dense, fracture-resistant bones — carriers have reportedly walked away unhurt from car crashes that would break other people's hips, though some also apparently have trouble swimming, because dense bones sink rather than float.

PCSK9 loss-of-function stands out as a rare, seemingly unambiguous "pure gain": dramatically lower LDL cholesterol and heart disease risk, with no known downside at all — a discovery that directly inspired an entirely new class of cholesterol-lowering drugs, including evolocumab and others approved from 2015 onward.

Lactase persistence, already discussed in Chapter 6, remains the clearest example of a cost-free "enhancement" that spread naturally through a population — the product of an ordinary copying variation combined with the rise of dairy farming, not any kind of deliberate design.

That leaves sport and intelligence. Gene doping in athletics has been formally banned by the World Anti-Doping Agency since 2003, and while it's technically plausible, no confirmed case has ever been detected. And there is, notably, no known genetic "lever" for intelligence at all: polygenic scores for educational attainment explain only something like 12 to 16 percent of the variance in that trait, and roughly half of even that modest signal disappears again once compared within families — meaning an "edited genius," however popular a science-fiction premise it makes, remains utterly unsupported by anything currently known about the genome.

## 46. The Next Hundred Years

The next hundred years of genetics can be sorted by how confident the evidence really makes us: a "settled trajectory" of near-certain extrapolations from trends already well underway, a set of "working scenarios" supported by partial evidence, and a smaller set of frankly "speculative" possibilities that remain unproven.

On the settled trajectory: universal newborn genome sequencing, an expanding roster of somatic gene-therapy cures for single-gene diseases following the personalized model pioneered by KJ Muldoon's treatment, pharmacogenomic prescribing becoming a routine part of ordinary medical care, engineered crops and livestock becoming the unremarkable, unlabeled default rather than a special category, and ambient environmental DNA monitoring becoming something close to a public utility — which will, in turn, raise its own new privacy questions once it arrives.

Among the working scenarios: polygenic embryo selection likely normalizing in some countries while remaining banned in others, producing a genuine patchwork rather than a global consensus either way; "partial reprogramming" anti-aging therapy — briefly applying Yamanaka's reprogramming factors without fully reverting cells all the way back to an embryonic state — which has already shown real, if modest, rejuvenation effects in animal studies from 2016 and 2020, with human trials for eye disease now being planned and Altos Labs, launched in 2022 with nearly $3 billion in funding, aiming to turn the approach into a medicine; and genetic tools adjacent to "de-extinction" work continuing to aid useful conservation cloning efforts.

The purely speculative territory includes germline editing for a narrow set of diseases possibly becoming legal, though still rare, by 2050. On genome-based inequality, the likelier prediction runs against the popular imagination: rather than a "Gattaca"-style genetic caste system, inequality is more likely to show up as unequal access to existing cures — a gap already visible today — for a rather unglamorous reason: the traits that money and status actually want most, like intelligence and temperament, have no identifiable genetic lever to pull at all, while the traits that do have a workable lever, namely disease, are exactly the traits that public health systems have the strongest possible incentive to fix for everyone. Engineered human variants suited to extreme environments, like space or extreme heat, remain far off and ethically fraught enough that nobody should expect to see them within this timeframe at all.

The central, unifying prediction, stated plainly: by 2126, most single-gene diseases will be fixed in infancy as a matter of routine; prescribing, farming, and public health will all be thoroughly genome-informed; aging will be modestly extended in terms of healthy years rather than raw lifespan; and the species will remain, on the whole, recognizably itself. What will have changed is our relationship to our own genetic "text" — shifting from something that simply happens to us, passively, into something we actively read and carefully correct. But it will never become perfectly error-free — nor, in the end, should we particularly want it to be.

## A Short Timeline

The story traced across forty-six chapters can be compressed into a single connective thread of dates. It begins in 1865 and 1866, when Gregor Mendel presented and then published his pea-hybridization results in Brünn, and reaches back further still to 1869, when a Swiss chemist isolated "nuclein" — what we now call DNA — from white blood cells, decades before anyone suspected what he'd truly found.

Mendel's work then vanished from view until 1900, when three botanists independently rediscovered his ratios. A Drosophila genetics program began in 1910 at Columbia University, after a single spontaneous white-eyed mutant fly turned up, and the case for DNA itself as the hereditary molecule was built across four decades: the discovery of the bacterial "transforming principle" in 1928, its identification as DNA in 1944, and the phage experiment that confirmed it beyond doubt in 1952 — the same year Rosalind Franklin captured Photo 51.

The double helix followed almost immediately: Watson and Crick published DNA's structure on 25 April 1953 and its copying mechanism a month later, on 30 May. A landmark experiment demonstrated that replication really was semiconservative in 1958. The genetic code itself began yielding to the first cracked codon in 1961 (the same year researchers showed that sequence alone determines protein folding), and was fully deciphered by 1966.

The engineering era opened with the first functional recombinant-DNA gene cloning in 1973, followed swiftly by the Asilomar Conference's voluntary safety guidelines in 1975. Sanger sequenced the first complete DNA genome in 1977, the same year introns were discovered. PCR was conceived in 1983, and DNA fingerprinting followed the next year, in 1984.

The Human Genome Project formally began in 1990, alongside the first gene-therapy trial; Dolly the sheep was born on 5 July 1996; a draft human genome sequence was announced at the White House on 26 June 2000; and the project was declared complete in 2003, deliberately timed to the double helix's fiftieth anniversary.

The most recent chapters move quickly. Yamanaka reprogrammed adult cells into induced pluripotent stem cells in 2007. In 2010, researchers booted up the first cell running on a synthetic genome, and a separate team published the draft Neanderthal genome. CRISPR-Cas9 was published as a completely programmable gene-editing tool in 2012. He Jiankui announced the birth of germline-edited twin girls in 2018, a low point the field has spent years trying to move past; Victoria Gray became the first sickle cell patient treated with CRISPR-edited cells in 2019. The gap-free, telomere-to-telomere human genome was completed in 2022, the same year the Neanderthal-genome researchers won their Nobel Prize. Casgevy became the first approved CRISPR-based therapy, in both the UK and the US, in 2023. And in 2025, KJ Muldoon received a personalized base-editing treatment built specifically for his own CPS1 deficiency — a fitting point to pause the timeline, exactly where the account of the present catches up with it.
