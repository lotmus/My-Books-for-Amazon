## Storey 9: Vectors & Vector Algebra

A vector is a peculiar kind of object: it has a size and a direction, but no home address of its own. Think of it like the instruction "walk three miles northeast": that instruction means exactly the same thing whether you start from your house or from the coffee shop down the street, because it specifies only a distance and a direction, never a starting point. Draw an arrow pointing northeast, three inches long, on one side of a page, and draw an identical arrow — same length, same direction — somewhere else on the page entirely, and mathematically those are the exact same vector, just drawn in two different spots, because a vector doesn't care where it starts, only how far it goes and which way it points. If you want the vector that carries you from one specific point to another, you get it by subtracting the starting point from the ending point.

You combine vectors by placing one arrow's tail at the previous arrow's tip and seeing where you end up — or equivalently, by building a parallelogram out of the two vectors and taking the diagonal that starts where they meet — and it doesn't matter what order you add several vectors in or how you group them, you land in the same place regardless. Walk three blocks east and then two blocks north, versus two blocks north and then three blocks east, and you trace a different path along the way but end up on exactly the same corner either way — the order-doesn't-matter property, walked out on an actual street grid. You can also scale a vector by an ordinary number, which stretches it longer, shrinks it shorter, or, if the number is negative, flips it around to point the opposite way. The beautiful part is that these two operations, adding and scaling, obey a short, consistent list of rules — and plenty of things that don't look remotely like arrows, polynomials being a favorite example, obey those exact same rules too, which is why mathematicians study "vector spaces" as an idea far more general than just arrows in space. A polynomial doesn't have a length you can measure with a ruler, or a direction you could point a compass at, and yet you can add two polynomials together and scale one by a number using those very same rules — which is exactly the test mathematicians use to decide whether "vector" applies: not whether the object looks like an arrow, but whether it obeys the arrow's rules.

Once you pin vectors down with actual coordinates, finding a vector's length becomes a straightforward calculation: square each coordinate, add the squares together, and take the square root of the total — which, if you squint, is nothing but the Pythagorean theorem again, stretched from two dimensions into three. There's a standard trio of building-block vectors, one pointing purely along each of the three coordinate directions, and every vector in space can be built as one specific combination of those three building blocks, with no other combination doing the job — that's what makes them a basis.

Two operations let you ask deeper questions about a pair of vectors. The dot product multiplies the two vectors' lengths together and scales that by the cosine of the angle between them — think of it as measuring how much of one vector's effort actually lands in the direction the other one points, the way only the part of a rope's pull that's aligned with a sled's direction of travel actually helps drag it forward, while any sideways component is wasted. And conveniently, you can also get it directly from coordinates, by multiplying matching coordinates together and adding up the results, with both routes always agreeing. What makes the dot product so useful is that it comes out to exactly zero whenever the two vectors are perpendicular, and nonzero otherwise, making it a built-in angle detector. In three dimensions specifically, there's a second operation, the cross product, which takes two vectors and produces a brand new vector perpendicular to both originals — think of the axle of a spinning wheel, standing straight up out of the very plane the wheel spins in — with a length equal to the area of the parallelogram the original two vectors would sweep out. Stack a third vector onto that using what's called the triple product, and you get the signed volume of the parallelepiped — the tilted box — that all three vectors together span.

Vectors also give a way to describe entire lines and planes. A line is simply every point you can reach by starting at some known point and walking any multiple of a fixed direction vector. A plane is every point that, once you subtract off some known point on the plane, stands exactly perpendicular to a fixed vector normal to the plane. Stepping back to the general idea of a basis: it's any collection of vectors that are independent of one another, meaning none can be built from a combination of the others, while still being enough of them together to reach every point in the space — much like mixing every possible paint color from just red, blue, and yellow: those three are independent, since none can be mixed from the other two, and together they're sufficient, since nothing else is needed, and that pairing of properties is exactly what makes a basis a basis. The paint picture only stretches so far, though: mixing paint only ever lets you add pigment in, never take it back out, while a genuine vector-space basis needs negative coefficients too — the equivalent of subtracting some of a color back out — in order to reach every point in the space, not just the ones sitting somewhere in the same general direction as red, blue, and yellow themselves. And it's a remarkable fact that no matter which such basis you pick for a given space, you always need exactly the same number of vectors to do the job — that count is the dimension of the space itself.

Physics leans on every bit of this. Newton's second law, relating force, mass, and acceleration, is secretly a vector equation, since force and acceleration both have direction as well as size. The work done by a force is the dot product of that force with the displacement it causes, capturing the fact that only the part of the force pointing along your direction of travel actually does any work. Torque, the twisting effect of a force, is the cross product of the arrow from the pivot point to where the force acts with the force itself — think of pushing a door open near its hinge versus pushing near the doorknob: the same push produces far more twist the farther out from the hinge it's applied and the more it shoves sideways rather than straight into the door, and that combination of "how far out" and "how sideways" is precisely what the cross product is measuring. And vectors even hand you a slick one-line proof of the Pythagorean theorem, using the dot product of a vector with itself. One more charming fact for the road: draw the three medians of any triangle, the lines from each corner to the midpoint of the opposite side, and all three always cross at a single point — which is, in fact, nothing more exotic than the plain average of the triangle's three corners.

## Storey 10: Limits

Picture a friend walking toward a doorway, taking smaller and smaller steps as they close the distance, forever nearing it without technically arriving quite yet. Consider an idea that sounds almost too simple until you try to pin it down: the limit of a function, as some input approaches a particular value, is whatever number the function's output keeps crowding closer and closer to, as the input creeps closer and closer to that value. Notice the careful wording — the input gets close without ever landing on that value, and strangely, the function doesn't even need to be defined at that exact spot for the limit to exist there perfectly well. Here's where the doorway picture reveals its own gap, and the gap is the entire point: with a real doorway, what ultimately matters is what's actually standing there when your friend arrives — solid floor, a missing floorboard, a locked door. A limit doesn't care about any of that. It only cares about the trend of the approach, so even if the function has some entirely different value sitting right at that point, or no value defined there at all, the limit stays exactly what the approach was heading toward. A limit is a statement about the neighborhood surrounding a point, about the approach, not a statement about the point itself.

Mathematicians eventually wanted something sturdier than "gets close to," because "close" is a slippery word, so they built a rigorous version, the epsilon-delta definition. The idea runs like a game of chase: no matter how tiny a margin of error someone challenges you with around the target output, you have to be able to respond with a small enough margin around the input such that staying within your input margin, without ever landing exactly on the target input itself, guarantees you land within their output margin. Someone challenges you to land the output within a thousandth of the target; you counter with an input margin tight enough to guarantee it. They come back with a challenge a thousand times stricter; you tighten your input margin to match. It turns the fuzzy phrase "arbitrarily close" into a challenge-and-response test you can prove you always win, no matter how brutal the challenge gets.

Fortunately you rarely have to run that whole challenge-and-response game by hand, because a handful of limit laws let you compute most limits with ordinary algebra instead. Limits play nicely with addition, multiplication, and division, so long as, for division, whatever you're dividing by doesn't itself have a limit of zero. Consider a car's average speed over an ever-shorter stretch of a journey: the distance traveled and the time taken both shrink toward zero together, and "zero divided by zero" by itself tells you nothing about how fast the car was actually going at that instant — you need to know how those two quantities were shrinking relative to each other, not just that both happened to reach zero. The classic move when you run headfirst into a limit that looks like zero divided by zero, which tells you nothing by itself, is to factor or rationalize the expression until whatever was causing both the top and bottom to vanish cancels out cleanly — and only then plug in the value you were approaching.

Tracing a function's graph with a pen, never lifting it off the paper, is continuity in a nutshell, and each way it can fail corresponds to a specific reason you'd be forced to lift the pen. A function is called continuous at a point when the limit as you approach that point actually matches the value the function truly has right there — no gap, no jump, no surprise. When that fails, there are a few characteristic ways it can go wrong: a removable discontinuity is really just a single misplaced or missing point you could patch by redefining the function right there; a jump discontinuity happens when approaching from one side gives you a different answer than approaching from the other; and an infinite discontinuity is where the function rockets off toward infinity instead of settling anywhere. Functions that stay continuous across a whole interval come with a nice guarantee called the Intermediate Value Theorem — if the function takes one value at one end and a different value at the other, it must pass through every value in between somewhere along the way, since it can't jump over a value without touching it. If it was forty degrees at dawn and eighty by mid-afternoon, it must have been exactly sixty at some moment in between, whether or not anyone happened to be watching the thermometer right then — temperature doesn't teleport past values it hasn't touched.

Certain limits arrive in disguise, in forms like zero divided by zero, infinity divided by infinity, infinity minus infinity, or one raised to an infinite power, and the important thing to understand about these is that they aren't actual answers at all — they're a signal that you haven't finished the algebra yet, that the true value is still hiding and needs to be dug out. Separately, limits can describe behavior far out at the extremes: what a function settles toward as its input grows without bound describes a horizontal or slanted guide-line the graph hugs forever, while a function racing off toward infinity as its input approaches one particular finite spot describes a vertical guide-line the graph shoots up or down beside.

One of the cleverest tricks in this whole storey is the Squeeze Theorem, which handles a function you can't pin down directly by trapping it between two other functions you understand perfectly well, both of which happen to be closing in on the very same limiting value — squeeze the mystery function tightly enough between two known bounds, and it has no choice but to land where they do. Imagine a hiker whose exact position you can't track directly, but who you know for certain is always walking between two other hikers, one on either side, both of whom are converging on the very same spot: if both bracketing hikers end up there, the hiker sandwiched between them has nowhere else to go but the same spot. This is exactly how you prove that the ratio of the sine of a tiny angle, measured in radians, to that angle itself approaches exactly one as the angle shrinks toward nothing, by comparing the area of a thin wedge of a circle to the areas of two triangles pinching it from either side. The same squeezing trick tames wilder, oscillating functions too, showing that a function wiggling infinitely fast within fixed bounds, when multiplied by something shrinking toward zero, still settles down to zero overall. And here's a lovely payoff to end on: take nothing but the tools built up in this one storey and apply them to the defining ratio behind sine's own rate of change, and out pops cosine — a derivative computed completely from scratch, using nothing more than the humble idea of a limit.

## Storey 11: Differential

Here's the basic question calculus was invented to answer: if something is changing, how fast is it changing right now, this instant? Not on average over an hour, not over a year — right now. Think of a car's speedometer: it doesn't wait for the whole trip to end and then hand you the average, it gives a live reading updating instant by instant, and a derivative is the mathematical machinery for getting exactly that kind of instantaneous reading out of a quantity — position, cost, mass — that, left to itself, only ever hands you totals and averages. The trick is to look at how much a quantity changes over a tiny interval, divide by the length of that interval to get an average rate, and then imagine shrinking that interval down to nothing. Whatever number that average rate settles toward as the interval vanishes — that's the derivative. Geometrically, it's the slope of the line that just barely grazes the curve at a single point, the tangent line. But the idea shows up everywhere once you know to look for it: it's velocity if you're tracking position, marginal cost if you're tracking a factory's expenses, density if you're tracking mass spread through space. Anytime someone asks for the "instantaneous version" of some accumulating quantity, they're really asking for a derivative.

It's tempting to think that "drawable without lifting your pen" is basically the whole story, and for most casually sketched graphs that intuition serves you just fine. This next distinction surprises people, though: being smooth enough to have a derivative is a much stronger condition than merely being continuous, meaning drawable without lifting your pen. Take the absolute value function — the one that just measures distance from zero, so it looks like a V. It's perfectly continuous at the bottom of the V; there's no gap, no jump. But it has no derivative there, because the slope depends on which direction you sneak up on it from. Approach from the right and the slope looks like a steady climb of one unit up for every unit over. Approach from the left and it looks like a steady one-unit drop for every unit over. Since those two answers disagree, there simply is no single instantaneous rate of change at that sharp corner. Continuous, yes. Differentiable, no.

Once you accept that derivatives exist, you want a mechanical way to compute them without redoing the whole shrinking-interval limit every time, and four rules cover almost everything you'll ever need. Linearity says scaling a function or adding two functions together just scales or adds their derivatives — nothing exotic there. The product rule handles multiplying two changing quantities together: you can't just multiply their two rates of change, because that misses half the story. Picture a rectangle whose width and height are both growing at once, the way a photo grows when it's enlarged in both directions simultaneously: new area appears along one edge as the width grows, and separately along the other edge as the height grows, and both slivers of new area count, not just one. Instead you let one factor do the changing while you freeze the other, tally that contribution, then switch which one you freeze, and add both contributions together. The quotient rule does the analogous bookkeeping for division, but with a twist, because growing the bottom quantity shrinks the overall ratio rather than growing it, so the bottom's own rate of change ends up working against the top's instead of alongside it. Take the rate at which the top quantity is changing and multiply it by the bottom quantity's current value, then subtract the rate at which the bottom quantity is changing multiplied by the top quantity's current value — that difference is the tug-of-war between a growing numerator pulling the ratio up and a growing denominator dragging it back down. Finally, scale the whole result down by the bottom quantity squared, because the bottom isn't just pulling against the top once, it's diluting the entire ratio a second time simply by being the thing everything else is being divided by. But the real star of the show is the chain rule, for handling one function wrapped around another — a function of a function. Think of a chain of gears, each one turning the next: if the first gear starts turning twice as fast as before, and the second gear was already built to turn three times for every one turn of the first, the second gear now turns six times as fast overall, because the two rates multiply together — exactly how the chain rule multiplies the outer rate of change by the inner one. It says: take the rate of change of the outer function, evaluated at wherever the inner function currently sits, and multiply it by the rate of change of the inner function itself. Why is this the most powerful of the four? Because composition can nest arbitrarily deep — functions inside functions inside functions — and the chain rule threads straight through every layer, one multiplication at a time, no matter how many layers there are.

With those rules in hand, a short list of standard building-block derivatives lets you differentiate almost anything you'll meet in practice. For a simple power function, the derivative just drops the exponent down to become a multiplier out front, and knocks the exponent itself down by one — a pattern so clean it becomes second nature almost instantly. The exponential function built on Euler's number is even more remarkable: it is its own derivative, completely unchanged by the operation, which is really the whole reason that constant is worth naming in the first place. The natural logarithm's derivative is just the reciprocal of its input — a strange, tidy little fact. And the two basic trigonometric functions trade places under differentiation: the sine function's derivative is the cosine function, while the cosine function's derivative is the negative of the sine function, a pattern that, if you differentiate four times in a row, cycles right back to where it started.

Sometimes you don't have a tidy formula that spits one quantity out directly from another — you just have a relationship binding several changing quantities together, with none of them singled out as "the answer." Implicit differentiation, essentially the chain rule in disguise, lets you differentiate straight through such a relationship anyway, and it powers a whole genre of "related rates" problems. The classic version: a ladder leaning against a wall, its foot sliding away at a steady two feet per second. Because the foot's distance from the wall and the top's height up the wall are locked together — the square of one plus the square of the other always equals one hundred, meaning the ladder itself is ten feet long — you can differentiate that locked relationship through time and find out how fast the top is sliding down at any given moment, without ever solving for the top's height as a function of the foot's distance. Plug in the moment when the foot is six feet out from the wall, and out pops the answer: the top is sliding down at a rate of one and a half feet per second.

You can keep differentiating past the first derivative, and each additional round tells you something new. The second derivative — the rate of change of the rate of change — tracks concavity, whether a curve bends upward like a smile or downward like a frown. Push one step further and you reach what's whimsically called jerk, the rate of change of acceleration itself, which is why a badly driven car ride feels rough even when the average speed is perfectly smooth. One of the nicest practical uses of the first derivative alone is linear approximation: near any point you already understand well, the tangent line is a good stand-in for the actual curve close by. Take the square root function, which you know exactly at twenty-five, where it equals five. Use the tangent line there to estimate the square root of twenty-six, a nearby value you don't know off the top of your head, and you get approximately five point one. The true value is five point zero nine nine — close, and free for all practical purposes.

The Mean Value Theorem is one of those results that sounds almost too obvious to bother proving, and yet it underlies huge swaths of calculus. It says that, for a function that is continuous across an interval and has a derivative at every point strictly inside it, somewhere between the start and end there has to be a moment where the instantaneous rate of change exactly matches the average rate of change over the whole interval — which makes sense if you think about a road trip: if your average speed was sixty miles an hour, your speedometer must have read exactly sixty at some point along the way, even if it wandered above and below the rest of the time. Simple as it sounds, this fact is the engine behind proofs that a function with zero rate of change everywhere on an interval must be perfectly constant, and that a function with a positive rate of change everywhere on an interval must be strictly increasing.

Derivatives also tell you where a function is at its biggest or smallest, because at a peak or a valley the instantaneous rate of change either flattens to nothing or fails to exist at all — these special locations are called critical points, and finding the extreme values of a function almost always starts by hunting them down. A classic example: a farmer has one hundred meters of fencing and wants to enclose the biggest possible rectangular area using an existing barn wall as one side, so only three sides need fencing. Set up the area as a function of how far the fence runs out from the wall, differentiate, find where that rate of change hits zero, and you discover the optimal shape: make the side running parallel to the wall exactly twice as long as each of the two sides running perpendicular to it. Do that, and the enclosed area comes out to a maximum of one thousand two hundred fifty square meters.

## Storey 12: Integral

If differentiation is about breaking a quantity down into its instantaneous rate of change, integration is about running that process backward — building a whole quantity back up from a rate. Think of a faucet with a flow rate that keeps changing, sped up one moment, slowed down the next: hand someone a graph of that flow rate over time and ask how much water actually ended up in the bucket, and you've just asked them to integrate, to run the rate-to-total process in reverse from what a derivative does. It comes in two flavors that look similar but answer different questions. The definite integral is a single number: the signed area trapped between a curve and the horizontal axis over some specific stretch, computed as the limit of the areas of ever-thinner rectangles stacked up to approximate the region. The indefinite integral is a completely different kind of animal — not a number at all, but an entire family of functions, every antiderivative of the original function you started with, differing from one another only by whatever constant you tack on at the end.

Those rectangle approximations are worth dwelling on, because they show the true value getting squeezed from both sides. Take the simple curve that squares its input, over the stretch from zero to two, cut into four equal slices. Build rectangles that are a touch too tall, using the curve's height at the right edge of each slice, and you overshoot the true area, landing at three point seven five. Build rectangles using the curve's height at the left edge instead, and you undershoot, landing at one point seven five. As you slice the interval into more and finer rectangles, both approximations converge, squeezing in from above and below, toward the true area, which is exactly eight-thirds.

The single most important fact tying these two faces of integration together is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and it comes in two parts. The first part says that if you build a new function by accumulating area under a curve up to some point, and then differentiate that accumulated-area function, you get back exactly the original curve you started with — accumulation and differentiation undo each other, the same way a car's odometer reading, differentiated, gives you back its speedometer reading, and the speedometer reading, integrated over time, gives you back the odometer's running total. The second part is the one that actually makes integrals computable by hand: it says the signed area under a curve between two endpoints equals the difference between any antiderivative's values at those two endpoints, the right-hand value minus the left-hand one. That single fact converts the geometric problem of finding an area into the purely algebraic problem of hunting down an antiderivative, which is why so much of a first calculus course is just antiderivative-hunting practice.

Hunting down antiderivatives is a genuine skill with its own toolbox. Substitution runs the chain rule backward, spotting a hidden inner function and its rate of change bundled together inside the thing you're integrating. Integration by parts runs the product rule backward, trading one product for another that's hopefully easier to handle. There's also trigonometric substitution, which disguises an awkward expression as a trigonometric one to unlock it, and partial fractions, which breaks an unwieldy ratio into simpler pieces integrated one at a time. Think of the standard toolbox of functions — polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, the trig functions, and combinations of those — as a fixed pantry of ingredients. But the humbling part is that some perfectly ordinary-looking functions simply have no antiderivative expressible in the usual catalog of functions at all, no dish that pantry can cook up, no matter how skilled the cook. The bell curve — built from a negative exponential of a squared input — is the most famous example: it comes up constantly in probability, and mathematicians dealt with the problem not by giving up but by just inventing a new function, the error function, defined to be its antiderivative, and moving on.

Once you can find areas, a whole catalog of physical and geometric quantities open up, because they're all secretly areas or accumulations in disguise. You can find the area trapped between two curves, the volume of a solid formed by spinning a curve around an axis, the arc length of a curve, and the physical work done by a force over a distance. That last one has a lovely example in the law describing a stretching spring, which says the force needed to stretch it grows in direct proportion to how far it's already stretched — the more you've pulled it, the harder it pulls back. Stretch such a spring, one stiff enough to need two hundred newtons per meter of stretch, out to half a meter, and because the force isn't constant but keeps growing throughout the stretch, you can't just multiply force by distance; you have to integrate the growing force over the whole stretch. Do that, and the work done comes out to twenty-five joules.

It's tempting to assume that anything stretching out to infinity must enclose an infinite amount of whatever it's measuring, and usually that instinct is right. Sometimes the region you're measuring doesn't stay politely bounded — it stretches off toward infinity, or the curve itself shoots up to infinity somewhere in the interval. Improper integrals handle both situations by taking a limit, gradually pushing a boundary out toward infinity, or in toward a trouble spot, and seeing whether the accumulating area settles down to a finite number. Remarkably, an infinitely long strip running under the curve that's one over the square of its input, starting at one and heading off to infinity, encloses a perfectly finite area — exactly one square unit, despite going on forever. More generally, for the family of curves built from one over a power of the input, the area under an infinitely long strip converges to a finite number only when that power is bigger than one; drop to a power of one or below, and the area blows up to infinity instead.

And when no exact antiderivative exists at all, or when you're just handed a table of measured data rather than a clean formula, numerical methods step in to approximate the integral instead. The trapezoidal rule approximates each thin slice as a trapezoid rather than a rectangle, hugging the curve a bit more closely. A more refined method does even better by fitting a curved piece — a parabola — through each little group of points instead of a straight edge, and it happens to be exact, not just approximate, for any polynomial of degree three or lower.

## Storey 13: Multi-Variable Functions

Up to now every function has eaten a single number and spat out a single number. But plenty of real quantities depend on more than one thing at once — temperature depends on both latitude and longitude, say — so we need functions that eat a whole point, a pair of numbers, and spit out one number. Graph such a function and instead of a curve you get a whole surface floating in three-dimensional space, a landscape of hills and valleys. The trouble is that landscape lives in three dimensions and most of us can barely doodle in two, so cartographers had already solved this problem, independently and for entirely practical reasons: level sets, better known as contour lines, are the curves you get by slicing the landscape at a fixed height and looking straight down. Where the contour lines bunch up tightly, the terrain is steep; where they spread out lazily, the terrain is gentle. You get nearly the whole shape of the landscape without ever needing to picture it in three dimensions at all.

Limits, already a little subtle with one variable, become treacherous with two. In one variable there are really only two directions to approach a point from, the left and the right, so checking both settles the matter. In two variables there are infinitely many directions, and infinitely many curved paths besides, and they can each tell a different story — picture a fly approaching a tabletop's edge, which might glide straight in, spiral inward, or swoop along some looping path, and in principle each route could reveal something different waiting at the edge. To make that point vivid, take a function built like this: the top is one variable times the square of the other, and the bottom is the square of the first variable plus the fourth power of the second. Approach the origin along any straight line whatsoever, and the function's value dutifully shrinks to zero every single time — you'd be forgiven for concluding the limit is zero. But sneak up on the origin along a particular curved path instead, the one where the first variable matches the square of the second, and the function's value settles instead at one-half. Since straight-line approaches and this curved approach disagree, the limit simply does not exist at that point. The lesson: checking straight lines alone is never enough to prove a multivariable limit exists.

Partial derivatives handle differentiation in this multivariable world with a simple trick: freeze every variable except one, and differentiate as if that were the only variable that mattered. Think of tweaking a recipe one variable at a time: hold the baking time fixed and ask how the cake changes as you turn up the oven temperature, then hold the temperature fixed and ask how it changes as you extend the time — that's exactly the freeze-everything-but-one move a partial derivative performs. Do this twice, differentiating first with respect to one variable and then the other, and you might worry the order matters — does freezing the first variable and then the second give the same answer as doing it the opposite way? A reassuring guarantee steps in: as long as these mixed partial derivatives are continuous, the order you differentiate in doesn't matter at all. Under that condition you land on the same answer either way.

The single most useful object in this whole subject is the gradient, a little arrow built out of all of a function's partial derivatives at a given point. That arrow always points in whichever direction the function increases fastest, straight uphill in the steepest possible sense, and its length tells you exactly how steep that steepest ascent is. It also has a beautifully clean geometric property: the gradient arrow is always perpendicular to the contour line passing through that same point. This is exactly why a sensible hiker follows the contour lines on a map rather than crossing them: walking along a contour line means walking perpendicular to the steepest direction, which is precisely the gentlest possible route across the terrain.

Both the chain rule and implicit differentiation carry over from the single-variable world into this richer setting, letting you differentiate composite functions built by feeding the outputs of some functions into the inputs of others, and letting you handle relationships between several variables that aren't solved for any one of them in particular. The bookkeeping gets more elaborate, since there are more paths of dependency to track, but the underlying idea — that rates of change multiply and add their way through a chain of dependencies — survives completely intact.

Having derivatives in some limited sense doesn't guarantee genuine smoothness. It's entirely possible for a function to have perfectly well-defined partial derivatives at a point, meaning it behaves nicely along the two axis directions specifically, while still failing to even be continuous there. Consider a function built as the product of the two variables divided by the sum of their squares. Right at the origin, moving purely along either axis, the function is flat and well-behaved. But sneak up on the origin along the diagonal line instead, and the function stays locked at one-half the whole way in, refusing to settle down to whatever value sits at the origin itself. Good behavior along just two special directions told you almost nothing about how the function behaves everywhere else.

Finding the high points and low points of a multivariable function again starts with critical points, the places where the gradient arrow shrinks to nothing. But here a new subtlety appears that never shows up with a single variable: a critical point might be a real peak, a real valley, or something in between called a saddle point, shaped like the seat of a horse's saddle, rising in one direction while falling in another, simultaneously. To tell these apart, you build a single number out of the function's second partial derivatives, and its sign does most of the classifying: negative means a saddle, positive means a peak or a valley (told apart by whether the function curves downward or upward along an axis), and zero means the test cannot decide. The function built from the square of one variable minus the square of the other is the textbook saddle: sitting at the origin, it rises as you walk out along one axis and falls as you walk out along the other, both at once.

## Storey 14: Multi-Variable Calculus

Just as ordinary integration extends the idea of a sum to a smoothly varying single-variable function, double and triple integrals extend that same rectangle-slicing idea to regions in a plane or solids in space. Instead of chopping an interval into thin slices, you chop a two-dimensional region into tiny patches, or a three-dimensional solid into tiny chunks, multiply a function's value on each piece by the size of that piece, add up all those products, and take the limit as the pieces shrink to nothing. Think of estimating the total weight of an oddly-shaped loaf of bread by mentally chopping it into tiny cubes, weighing each cube, and adding up the results — that's a double or triple integral in everyday clothing, chopping up a region instead of an interval. The saving grace that makes these higher-dimensional integrals actually computable is a theorem that says you're allowed to evaluate them one variable at a time, as a sequence of ordinary single-variable integrals nested inside each other, provided the function is reasonably well-behaved and you've first sketched the region carefully enough to know the correct boundaries for each nested step.

Once you have this machinery, an entire catalog of physical quantities fall out as special cases, all computed by essentially the same procedure with a different weighting function plugged in. Leave the function being integrated equal to one everywhere and you get plain area or volume. Weight it by a density function instead and you get total mass. Weight it further and you get the center of mass, the balance point of an object, or moments of inertia, which measure how mass is distributed for the purposes of rotation. It's the same tool doing all this work; only the ingredient you feed it changes.

Sometimes the region you're integrating over fits some other coordinate system far more naturally than the standard grid — a circular region practically begs to be described using distance from the center and an angle, rather than horizontal and vertical position. Switching coordinate systems like this requires a correction factor called the Jacobian, which rescales how area or volume elements stretch or shrink under the change. Picture a Mercator map of the world, which distorts area so severely that Greenland looks about as big as Africa: to recover Greenland's true area from measurements taken on that flattened map, you'd need a correction factor telling you exactly how much the map stretches things at that particular latitude. The Jacobian is that same kind of correction factor, for switching between coordinate grids instead of between a globe and a flat map. For circular coordinates in a plane, that correction factor is simply the distance from the center itself; for spherical coordinates in space it's the square of the distance from the center times the sine of the angle measured down from the pole. The single most celebrated payoff of this trick is evaluating an integral that has no elementary antiderivative at all: the bell curve itself, integrated over the whole real line. You can't find its antiderivative directly, but if you square the integral, effectively turning it into a double integral over an entire plane, and then switch to circular coordinates, the previously intractable problem collapses into something genuinely easy, and the answer is the square root of pi.

Vector fields assign not a plain number but an arrow to every point in a region — think of the velocity of water flowing across a surface, different in strength and direction from place to place. Line integrals measure how much a vector field pushes along a specific path through that field, capturing notions like the work done moving through a force field, or the circulation of a fluid around a loop. A vector field earns the special title "conservative" when the work it does moving between two points never depends on which path you took to get there — equivalently, when the circulation around any closed loop is always exactly zero. A clean test settles the question: on a region with no holes, it happens exactly when the rate at which the field's first component changes as you move in the second direction matches the rate at which the field's second component changes as you move in the first direction. When a field passes this test, all the work calculations collapse down to evaluating a single potential function at the start and end points, no path-tracing required at all.

Surface integrals extend this same idea from curves to whole surfaces: instead of asking how much a field pushes along a path, flux asks how much of a field flows through a surface, like measuring how much water per second passes through a net dunked in a stream. Because a surface has two sides, you first have to choose which way it's considered to be facing, its orientation, since flux measured through one face is the negative of flux measured through the other.

Three theorems tie all of this together, each one converting a statement about a boundary into a statement about the interior it encloses, or vice versa. Take a company's finances, tallied two different ways: you could add up every internal transaction between departments, or you could just track what crosses the company's outer boundary, money coming in from customers, money going out to suppliers — and for the right kind of bookkeeping, both totals have to agree. One theorem converts the circulation of a field around the boundary of a flat region into a double integral, over the interior of that region, measuring the field's local tendency to spin, its curl. A curved-surface counterpart lifts that exact same idea off the flat plane and onto a curved surface floating in space. The Divergence theorem, also called Gauss's theorem, does the analogous thing one dimension up: it converts the total flux flowing out through a closed surface into a triple integral, over the solid region enclosed, measuring the field's local tendency to expand or spread out at each point, its divergence.

All three of these theorems are really the same idea wearing different costumes: what happens on the boundary of a region exactly matches what happens, added up point by point, throughout its interior. And this isn't some exotic higher-dimensional coincidence — it's the direct descendant of the simplest fact imaginable about ordinary sums. If you add up a string of consecutive differences, one after another, almost everything in the middle cancels out, and you're left with nothing but the difference between the very last value and the very first, a telescoping sum. All three of these boundary theorems are just that same telescoping cancellation, dressed up to work across continuous regions instead of discrete lists.

## Storey 15: Vector Calculus

A vector field, as we've already met, assigns an arrow rather than a plain number to every point in space — the local wind velocity, say, at every spot on a map. Three operators let you interrogate such fields at any point. The gradient, already familiar, starts from a plain number-valued field and produces an arrow field that points uphill, in the direction of steepest increase. Divergence takes an arrow field and produces a plain number at each point, measuring whether the field is locally expanding outward from that point or contracting inward toward it, built by adding up how fast each component of the field changes along its own corresponding direction — think of it as measuring whether a point acts like a tiny source spraying fluid outward or a tiny sink sucking fluid inward. Curl takes an arrow field and produces another arrow field, measuring local spin, whether the field is locally swirling around a point, with the direction of the resulting arrow telling you the axis of rotation by the right-hand rule, and its length telling you how fast the rotation is (twice the angular speed, to be exact).

A wonderfully simple running example makes both of these concrete: picture a vector field where every arrow is tangent to the circle around the origin passing through that point, all consistently rotating counterclockwise, as though the entire plane were a rigid record spinning at a steady rate. Compute its divergence and you get exactly zero everywhere, which makes sense, since a spinning disk of fluid isn't inflating or deflating, just going around. Compute its curl, though, and you get a constant arrow pointing straight out of the plane with a length of two, correctly capturing the fact that this fluid is rotating at a steady one radian per second about the central axis.

Two little identities come up constantly enough that they're worth memorizing outright. First: if you compute the gradient of a function and then take the curl of that result, the answer is always nothing at all, a flat zero, every time. Second: if you compute the curl of a vector field and then take the divergence of that result, you likewise always get zero. Both facts trace back to the same underlying reason, that mixed partial derivatives of a smooth function taken in either order always agree, so in a real sense they're the same fact wearing two different outfits. A third, more elaborate identity says that applying the curl twice gives the gradient of the divergence minus a certain second-derivative-based operator (applied to each component of the field separately), and this compound relationship is exactly what underlies the wave equation describing how electromagnetic waves propagate through space.

A field earns the title conservative — expressible as the gradient of some underlying potential function, with path-independent work and zero circulation around every closed loop — exactly when its curl vanishes everywhere throughout a region with no holes in it. That caveat about holes is not a throwaway detail; it's the whole point of a delightfully subtle example. Take the whirlpool-shaped field that circles around the origin, its strength dying off just fast enough with distance that its curl comes out to exactly zero at every single point where it's even defined. You'd think that makes it conservative. But walk once counterclockwise around a loop that encircles the origin, the one single point where this field isn't even defined, and the circulation around that loop comes out to two times pi, not zero at all. Nothing about the local algebra warned you this would happen; it's the hole in the domain, a fact about the region's shape rather than the field's formula, that spoils the conclusion. Topology, not just algebra, matters.

The three boundary theorems already introduced restate that same boundary-equals-interior relationship for flat regions, curved surfaces, and solid volumes respectively. And there's a beautiful structural theorem built from the same operators: a decomposition theorem which guarantees that any sufficiently smooth vector field that behaves itself far from the origin can be split apart, uniquely, into a curl-free piece added to a divergence-free piece — every such field, however complicated, is secretly built from just these two elementary ingredients.

This machinery isn't just abstract bookkeeping; it's the actual language physics is written in. Incompressible fluid flow is defined by exactly zero divergence everywhere, meaning fluid is never created or destroyed at any point, just shuffled around, while vorticity, the curl of the flow, measures the local swirl of the current. The four equations governing electricity and magnetism tie the divergence and curl of the electric and magnetic fields to the charges and currents that produce them, and the Divergence theorem and its curved-surface counterpart are exactly what convert those local statements into their integral forms over whole surfaces and around whole loops. Gauss's law, one of the four, is a particularly satisfying illustration: the total flux of the electric field through any sphere surrounding a point charge comes out exactly the same no matter how big you make the sphere, because the field's strength weakens with the square of the distance at precisely the same rate that the sphere's surface area grows with the square of the distance — the two effects cancel perfectly, leaving the total flux unchanged.

A nice thought to end on: gradient, divergence, and curl look like three unrelated operators you just have to memorize separately, but they're really three faces of one single deeper operator, called the exterior derivative, that generalizes differentiation itself to work on more elaborate geometric objects than plain functions. And once you have that one unifying operator, all three boundary theorems above — which looked like three separate results you had to learn — collapse into a single master theorem, one statement about boundaries and interiors that contains all three as special cases.

## Storey 16: Series

A series is what you get when you try to add up infinitely many numbers, one after another, and ask whether the running total settles down to some fixed value as you keep going, rather than wandering off to infinity or refusing to make up its mind. Think of paying off a debt in installments that keep shrinking, half of what's left each time: the running total you've paid keeps climbing but never overshoots the original debt, settling closer and closer to it forever — that settling-down is exactly what it means for a series to converge. When it does settle, we say the series converges, and the value it settles to is its sum. One subtlety catches almost everyone the first time they meet it, though: there are two very different flavors of convergence. If the series made from the sizes of the terms alone — ignoring their signs — also converges, you're in the well-behaved case called absolute convergence, and you can shuffle those terms into any order you like without ever changing the sum. But if a series only converges because positive and negative terms are delicately canceling each other out, that's merely conditional convergence, and it is almost unsettlingly fragile. A conditionally convergent series can be reordered — just by choosing which term to add next — to add up to absolutely any number you want, or to shoot off to infinity, or to refuse to settle at all. Same numbers, same terms, wildly different answers, purely as a function of the order you add them in. That alone should make you nervous about rearranging an infinite sum casually.

So how do you tell, before doing any of this, whether a series converges in the first place? There's a toolbox of tests for that. One family compares each term to the one before it, essentially asking whether the terms are shrinking geometrically fast enough for the tail to die out; if that comparison settles below a break-even point in the long run, you're safe, and above it, the series blows up, while exactly at it the test has nothing to say. Another approach, the integral test, treats the sum as a stand-in for the area under a related curve, and it settles a classic family of examples in one stroke: the sum of one over each whole number raised to some fixed power converges exactly when that power is bigger than one. Dial the power down to exactly one and you get the harmonic series, the sum of one over each whole number, which diverges — creeps up to infinity, however slowly — even though its individual terms are shrinking to nothing. That's a wonderful, counterintuitive fact worth sitting with: terms going to zero is necessary for convergence but nowhere near sufficient. And for series whose terms alternate in sign while steadily shrinking in size down to zero, there's Leibniz's simple, reliable test guaranteeing convergence.

Now generalize from a series of numbers to a series built from increasing whole-number powers of the offset from some fixed center point, each multiplied by its own coefficient — a power series. Such a series converges absolutely as long as you stay within a certain distance of the center, its radius of convergence, and that radius is determined entirely by how fast the coefficients themselves grow. Step outside it and the series diverges; stay strictly inside and you're always safe. The annoying part is the boundary: right at the edge, anything can happen, and you have to check each of the two endpoints separately, by hand. It's entirely possible for a power series to converge at one edge and not the other — one classic example converges across the range running from negative one third to one third, closed at the negative end but open at the positive one, a lopsided little asymmetry no formula alone will warn you about.

Where do these coefficients come from, if you're trying to represent some known function as a power series? They come from repeatedly differentiating the function at the center point and dividing by the appropriate factorial each time. A function that actually agrees with its own power series nearby is called analytic, and that's a much stronger, stricter property than merely being smooth, meaning infinitely differentiable. The humbling counterexample: the function built from an exponential decay whose exponent is negative one over the square of the input, with its value at input zero simply defined to be zero, has every single derivative equal to zero at that one special point, no matter how many times you differentiate — and yet the function is never zero at any other input, however close to zero you look. So that power series, dutifully built from all those zero derivatives, is just the flat zero function, describing nothing at all like the real curve it supposedly came from. Smooth, yes. Analytic, absolutely not.

The payoff for all this machinery is that power series behave like well-mannered polynomials: you can add them, differentiate them, integrate them, and even multiply two of them together the way you would multiply two long polynomials, and inside the range where they converge you get exactly the results you'd hope for. This turns differential equations into bookkeeping: guess that the unknown function is some power series, plug it in, and the equation collapses into a recurrence relating the coefficients to each other. Try this on the simplest possible differential equation, where a function's rate of change equals the function itself and the function starts out at one, and the coefficient recurrence you get, unwound, is exactly the series for the exponential function — rediscovered from scratch. The same trick run in reverse turns number sequences into functions: encode the Fibonacci sequence as the coefficients of a power series, and the defining recurrence of the Fibonacci numbers collapses the entire infinite series into one tidy fraction, a simple ratio of polynomials, called its generating function.

Finally there's a completely different flavor of series, built not from powers but from sines and cosines: the Fourier series. Any reasonably well-behaved periodic, repeating wave can be broken down into a sum of pure oscillations at different frequencies, each with its own strength, found by a projection integral measuring how much of that particular wave is "in" the function, much like measuring a shadow. There's a reassuring consistency check on the whole enterprise: the total "energy" packed into the original function exactly equals the total energy spread across all its frequency components — nothing created or destroyed, just reorganized. And there's one famously stubborn wrinkle: near a sudden jump in the function, the partial sums of a Fourier series don't smooth the jump out as you add more terms — they persistently overshoot by very close to nine percent of the full height of the jump, no matter how many terms you add. That overshoot never goes away; it just gets squeezed into a thinner sliver right at the jump.

## Storey 17: Fourier Transforms and FFT

Think of a glass prism splitting a beam of white light into a rainbow spread of individual colors: white light doesn't advertise which colors it's secretly made of until something forces the split, and a Fourier transform does exactly that to a signal, splitting it into the individual frequencies it's secretly built from. Imagine taking a Fourier series and letting the period of its repeating pattern stretch out longer and longer, toward infinity. The discrete comb of allowed frequencies — spaced apart like teeth — gets packed tighter and tighter until it blurs into a smooth, unbroken continuum. What's left is the Fourier transform: a way of taking a function that isn't periodic at all and expressing it as a blend of every possible frequency, weighted by how much of each is present. One of its nicest features is how cleanly it handles calculus: differentiating the original function corresponds, after the transform, to simply multiplying by the frequency, scaled by a fixed factor built from two pi and the imaginary unit — turning calculus into algebra. And there's a lovely fixed point hiding in here: the classic bell-shaped curve, suitably normalized, transforms into itself.

The transform isn't a one-way trip — an inversion process reconstructs the original function exactly from its transform, so nothing is thrown away. There's a guarantee that makes this feel less like magic and more like bookkeeping: the total energy contained in a function, measured by integrating its squared size, is exactly preserved when you pass to its transform. You haven't lost or gained anything switching between describing a signal by how it behaves over time and describing it by which frequencies it contains — you've just relabeled the same quantity of stuff.

One of the single most useful facts in all of applied mathematics is the convolution theorem. Convolution is the operation of sliding one function across another and, at each position, integrating how much they overlap — the natural description of smearing or blurring one signal by another, and computed directly it's a painful integral. The convolution theorem says this whole messy smearing operation becomes, after passing to the frequency domain, nothing more than ordinary multiplication of the two transforms. That fact underlies essentially all frequency-domain filtering. A nice concrete picture: convolve two identical rectangular pulses together and out comes a triangle, the sharp edges of two overlapping blocks softened into straight ramps.

Real-world signals are sampled, a finite list of numbers rather than a continuous function, so you need a discrete cousin of the transform — the discrete Fourier transform — which extracts the spectrum hiding inside a finite sequence of samples. Computed the obvious, brute-force way, the amount of work grows roughly with the square of how many samples you have, which becomes painfully slow as the sequence gets long. A clever algorithm, the fast Fourier transform, sidesteps this by recursively splitting the sequence into its even-indexed and odd-indexed terms, solving each half, and stitching the results back together — arriving at the identical answer for a fraction of the work, with the effort now growing only slightly faster than the length of the sequence itself, rather than with its square. For a list of a million samples, that difference is the gap between a calculation a computer finishes in a blink and one that grinds along for ages. This algorithm runs quietly and constantly underneath audio processing, image compression, and medical imaging — an MRI scanner is, at bottom, literally measuring samples of a two-dimensional Fourier transform of the object being scanned, and the fast transform is what makes inverting that data back into a picture practical.

There's also a hard limit built into the whole framework: the uncertainty principle. This is the same trade-off a musician already knows by ear: a short, percussive click has a sharply defined instant but almost no clear pitch, its energy spread thin across many frequencies at once, while a long, sustained note has a clear pitch but no sharp starting instant. The spread of a signal over time and its spread over frequency can't both be made arbitrarily small at once — multiply the two spreads together and there's a fixed floor below which you cannot go, no matter how cleverly you design the signal, and only the Gaussian bell curve actually touches that floor exactly, making it the most simultaneously time-and-frequency-concentrated signal that exists. You've likely seen a cousin of this effect at the movies: a spinning wagon wheel or helicopter rotor that appears to slow down, stop dead, or even spin backward, purely because the camera only snaps a limited number of frames per second and misses whatever happens in between. Separate from that limit is the sampling theorem, which says that to faithfully capture a signal you must sample it at a rate at least twice as fast as its fastest-varying component; sample any slower and distinct frequencies become indistinguishable from one another, an effect called aliasing. A tone at three kilohertz, sampled at only five kilohertz, doesn't record faithfully at all — it masquerades as an entirely different, lower tone at two kilohertz, a case of frequency mistaken identity baked right into the sampling process.
