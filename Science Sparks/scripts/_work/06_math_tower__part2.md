## Storey 9: Vectors & Vector Algebra

A vector is a peculiar kind of object: it has a size and a direction but no home address of its own. The instruction "walk three miles northeast" means exactly the same thing whether you start from your house or from the coffee shop down the street, because it specifies a distance and a direction and never a starting point. Draw a northeast arrow three inches long on one side of a page, then an identical arrow, same length and same direction, somewhere else on the page entirely, and mathematically those are the same vector drawn in two spots. A vector cares only how far it goes and which way it points. If you want the vector that carries you from one specific point to another, subtract the starting point from the ending point.

You add vectors by placing one arrow's tail at the previous arrow's tip and seeing where you end up, or equivalently by building a parallelogram from the two vectors and taking the diagonal that starts where they meet. The order and grouping of the additions make no difference to where you land. Walk three blocks east and then two blocks north, or two north and then three east: you trace different paths and arrive at the same corner, the order-doesn't-matter property walked out on an actual street grid. You can also scale a vector by an ordinary number, which stretches it longer, shrinks it shorter, or, if the number is negative, flips it around to point the opposite way.

The beautiful part is that these two operations, adding and scaling, obey a short, consistent list of rules, and plenty of things that look nothing like arrows obey the same rules. Polynomials are a favorite example. A polynomial has no length you can measure with a ruler and no direction you could point a compass at, yet you can add two of them and scale one by a number under exactly those rules. That is the test mathematicians use for the word "vector": an object qualifies when it obeys the arrow's rules, whatever it happens to look like. It is why "vector spaces" are studied as an idea far more general than arrows in space.

Once you pin vectors down with actual coordinates, a vector's length becomes a straightforward calculation: square each coordinate, add the squares, and take the square root of the total. Squint and you will recognize the Pythagorean theorem again, stretched from two dimensions into three. There is a standard trio of building-block vectors, one pointing purely along each of the three coordinate directions, and every vector in space can be built as exactly one combination of those three, with no other combination doing the job. That is what makes them a basis.

Two operations let you ask deeper questions about a pair of vectors. The dot product multiplies the two vectors' lengths together and scales that by the cosine of the angle between them. It measures how much of one vector's effort lands in the direction the other one points, the way only the part of a rope's pull aligned with a sled's direction of travel helps drag it forward, while any sideways component is wasted. You can also get it directly from coordinates, by multiplying matching coordinates together and adding up the results, and both routes always agree. Its most useful habit is that it comes out to exactly zero whenever the two vectors are perpendicular and nonzero otherwise, which makes it a built-in angle detector.

In three dimensions specifically there is a second operation, the cross product, which takes two vectors and produces a brand-new vector perpendicular to both originals, like the axle of a spinning wheel standing straight up out of the plane the wheel spins in. Its length equals the area of the parallelogram the original two vectors sweep out. Bring in a third vector through what's called the triple product, and you get the signed volume of the parallelepiped, the tilted box, that all three vectors span together.

Vectors also describe entire lines and planes. A line is every point you can reach by starting at some known point and walking any multiple of a fixed direction vector. A plane is every point that, once you subtract off some known point on the plane, stands exactly perpendicular to a fixed vector normal to the plane.

Stepping back to the general idea of a basis: it is any collection of vectors that are independent of one another, meaning none can be built from a combination of the others, while still being enough of them together to reach every point in the space. Painters know a version of this from mixing every color out of red, blue, and yellow. Those three are independent, since none can be mixed from the other two, and together they suffice, since nothing else is needed, and that pairing of properties is exactly what makes a basis a basis. The paint picture breaks in one place: paint only lets you add pigment in, while a genuine basis also needs negative coefficients, the equivalent of subtracting some color back out, to reach every point in the space.

And here is a remarkable fact: no matter which basis you pick for a given space, you always need exactly the same number of vectors to do the job. That count is the dimension of the space itself.

Physics leans on every bit of this. Newton's second law, relating force, mass, and acceleration, is secretly a vector equation, since force and acceleration both have direction as well as size. The work done by a force is the dot product of that force with the displacement it causes, capturing the fact that only the part of the force pointing along your direction of travel does any work. Torque, the twisting effect of a force, is the cross product of the arrow from the pivot point to where the force acts with the force itself. Push a door near its hinge and then near the doorknob: the same push produces far more twist the farther out from the hinge it's applied and the more it shoves sideways rather than straight into the door, and that combination of "how far out" and "how sideways" is precisely what the cross product measures.

Vectors even hand you a slick one-line proof of the Pythagorean theorem, using the dot product of a vector with itself. One more charming fact for the road: draw the three medians of any triangle, the lines from each corner to the midpoint of the opposite side, and all three always cross at a single point, which turns out to be nothing more exotic than the plain average of the triangle's three corners.

**Worked example.** A child drags a sled across a flat, snowy yard. The rope pulls with a force of 30 newtons toward the east and 40 newtons toward the north, so the pull has a total size of 50 newtons, since the square root of 30 squared plus 40 squared is 50. Meanwhile the sled moves 8 meters east and 6 meters north, a displacement of 10 meters, because the square root of 64 plus 36 is 10. How much work does the rope do? Work is the dot product of force and displacement, so multiply matching components and add: 30 times 8 is 240, 40 times 6 is 240, and the total is 480 joules. Had the rope pulled exactly along the sled's path, it would have done 50 times 10, or 500 joules. The ratio of 480 to 500 is 0.96, which is the cosine of the angle between rope and path, an angle of about 16 degrees. The shortfall of 20 joules is the price of pulling slightly off the line of travel.

## Storey 10: Limits

Here is an idea that sounds almost too simple until you try to pin it down. The limit of a function, as some input approaches a particular value, is whatever number the function's output keeps crowding toward as the input creeps closer and closer to that value. Note the careful wording: the input gets close without ever landing on that value, and the function doesn't even need to be defined at that exact spot for the limit to exist there perfectly well.

The usual picture is a friend walking toward a doorway in smaller and smaller steps, forever nearing it, and the place where that picture breaks is the whole lesson. With a real doorway, what ultimately matters is what's standing there when your friend arrives: solid floor, a missing floorboard, a locked door. A limit ignores all of that. It cares only about the trend of the approach, so even if the function has some entirely different value sitting right at that point, or no value at all, the limit stays exactly what the approach was heading toward. A limit is a statement about the neighborhood surrounding a point and the approach to it. The point itself may do as it pleases.

Mathematicians eventually wanted something sturdier than "gets close to," because "close" is a slippery word, so they built a rigorous version, the epsilon-delta definition. It runs like a game of chase. No matter how tiny a margin of error someone challenges you with around the target output, you must be able to answer with a small enough margin around the input that staying inside your input margin, without ever landing exactly on the target input, guarantees you land inside their output margin. Someone challenges you to land the output within a thousandth of the target; you counter with an input margin tight enough to guarantee it. They come back with a challenge a thousand times stricter; you tighten your input margin to match. The fuzzy phrase "arbitrarily close" becomes a challenge-and-response test you can prove you always win, however brutal the challenge.

Fortunately you rarely have to play that game by hand, because a handful of limit laws let you compute most limits with ordinary algebra. Limits play nicely with addition, multiplication, and division, so long as, for division, whatever you're dividing by doesn't itself have a limit of zero.

When it does, trouble starts. Consider a car's average speed over an ever-shorter stretch of a journey: the distance traveled and the time taken both shrink toward zero together, and "zero divided by zero" by itself tells you nothing about how fast the car was going at that instant. You need to know how the two quantities were shrinking relative to each other. The classic move when you run headfirst into a limit that looks like zero divided by zero is to factor or rationalize the expression until whatever was making both top and bottom vanish cancels out cleanly, and only then plug in the value you were approaching.

Tracing a function's graph with a pen, never lifting it off the paper, is continuity in a nutshell, and each way continuity can fail corresponds to a specific reason you'd be forced to lift the pen. A function is continuous at a point when the limit as you approach that point matches the value the function actually has there: no gap, no jump, no surprise. When that fails, it fails in a few characteristic ways. A removable discontinuity is a single misplaced or missing point you could patch by redefining the function right there. A jump discontinuity happens when approaching from one side gives a different answer than approaching from the other. An infinite discontinuity is where the function rockets off toward infinity instead of settling anywhere.

Functions that stay continuous across a whole interval come with a nice guarantee called the Intermediate Value Theorem. If the function takes one value at one end and a different value at the other, it must pass through every value in between somewhere along the way, since it can't jump over a value without touching it. If it was forty degrees at dawn and eighty by mid-afternoon, it was exactly sixty at some moment in between, whether or not anyone happened to be watching the thermometer. Temperature does not teleport.

Certain limits arrive in disguise, in forms like zero divided by zero, infinity divided by infinity, infinity minus infinity, or one raised to an infinite power. These forms are signals rather than answers: the algebra is unfinished, and the true value is still hiding and needs to be dug out. Separately, limits describe behavior out at the extremes. What a function settles toward as its input grows without bound gives a horizontal or slanted guide-line the graph hugs forever, while a function racing off toward infinity as its input approaches one particular finite spot gives a vertical guide-line the graph shoots up or down beside.

One of the cleverest tricks among limits is the Squeeze Theorem. It handles a function you can't pin down directly by trapping it between two functions you understand perfectly well, both closing in on the same limiting value. Squeeze the mystery function tightly enough between two known bounds and it has no choice but to land where they do. Think of a hiker whose exact position you can't track, but who you know is always walking between two other hikers, both converging on the same spot: if both bracketing hikers end up there, the one sandwiched between them has nowhere else to go.

This is exactly how you prove that the ratio of the sine of a tiny angle, measured in radians, to the angle itself approaches exactly one as the angle shrinks toward nothing: compare the area of a thin wedge of a circle to the areas of two triangles pinching it from either side. The same squeeze tames wilder, oscillating functions too, showing that a function wiggling infinitely fast within fixed bounds, when multiplied by something shrinking toward zero, still settles down to zero overall. And a lovely payoff: apply nothing but these tools to the defining ratio behind sine's own rate of change, and out pops cosine, a derivative computed from scratch with nothing more than the humble idea of a limit.

**Worked example.** A ball rolls down a ramp so that after t seconds it has traveled t squared meters. How fast is it moving at the 3-second mark? The average speed between 3 seconds and some later time t is the distance gained, t squared minus 9, divided by the time elapsed, t minus 3. Plug in t equals 3 directly and you get 0 divided by 0, which only tells you the algebra is unfinished. Factor the top: t squared minus 9 equals t minus 3 times t plus 3. The troublesome factor of t minus 3 cancels, leaving t plus 3, which behaves perfectly well at 3. Now let t approach 3, and the expression approaches 6. So the ball is moving at 6 meters per second at that instant. As a check, over the stretch from 3 to 3.01 seconds the ball covers 0.0601 meters in 0.01 seconds, an average of 6.01 meters per second, already crowding in on 6.

## Storey 11: Differential

Here is the basic question calculus was invented to answer: if something is changing, how fast is it changing right now, this instant? Forget the average over an hour or a year; the question is about this moment. A car's speedometer doesn't wait for the trip to end and then hand you the average. It gives a live reading, updated instant by instant, and a derivative is the mathematical machinery for extracting exactly that kind of reading from a quantity (position, cost, mass) that, left to itself, only ever hands you totals and averages.

The trick is to look at how much a quantity changes over a tiny interval, divide by the length of that interval to get an average rate, and then imagine shrinking the interval down to nothing. Whatever number that average rate settles toward as the interval vanishes is the derivative. Geometrically, it's the slope of the tangent line, the line that just barely grazes the curve at a single point. The idea shows up everywhere once you know to look for it: velocity if you're tracking position, marginal cost if you're tracking a factory's expenses, density if you're tracking mass spread through space. Anytime someone asks for the "instantaneous version" of some accumulating quantity, they're asking for a derivative.

For most casually sketched graphs, "drawable without lifting your pen" seems like the whole story, so this next distinction surprises people: being smooth enough to have a derivative is a much stronger condition than merely being continuous. Take the absolute value function, which measures distance from zero, so its graph looks like a V. It's perfectly continuous at the bottom of the V, with no gap and no jump. But it has no derivative there, because the slope depends on which side you sneak up from. Approach from the right and the slope is a steady climb of one unit up for every unit over. Approach from the left and it's a steady one-unit drop for every unit over. Since those two answers disagree, there is no single instantaneous rate of change at that sharp corner. Continuous, yes. Differentiable, no.

Once you accept that derivatives exist, you want a mechanical way to compute them without redoing the shrinking-interval limit every time, and four rules cover almost everything you'll need. Linearity says scaling a function or adding two functions together just scales or adds their derivatives. Nothing exotic there.

The product rule handles multiplying two changing quantities together, and you can't just multiply their two rates of change, because that misses half the story. Consider a rectangle whose width and height are both growing at once, the way a photo grows when it's enlarged in both directions simultaneously. New area appears along one edge as the width grows, and separately along the other edge as the height grows, and both slivers count. So you let one factor do the changing while you freeze the other, tally that contribution, then switch which one you freeze, and add both contributions together.

The quotient rule does the analogous bookkeeping for division, with a twist: growing the bottom quantity shrinks the overall ratio, so the bottom's rate of change works against the top's. Take the rate at which the top is changing and multiply it by the bottom's current value, then subtract the rate at which the bottom is changing multiplied by the top's current value. That difference is the tug-of-war between a growing numerator pulling the ratio up and a growing denominator dragging it down. Finally, divide the whole result by the bottom quantity squared, because the bottom doesn't just pull against the top once; it also dilutes the entire ratio a second time simply by being the thing everything is divided by.

The real star is the chain rule, for one function wrapped around another, a function of a function. Think of a chain of gears, each turning the next. If the first gear starts turning twice as fast as before, and the second gear was built to turn three times for every turn of the first, the second gear now turns six times as fast overall, because the two rates multiply. The chain rule says the same thing: take the rate of change of the outer function, evaluated wherever the inner function currently sits, and multiply it by the rate of change of the inner function itself. It's the most powerful of the four because composition can nest arbitrarily deep, functions inside functions inside functions, and the chain rule threads straight through every layer, one multiplication at a time.

With those rules in hand, a short list of standard building-block derivatives lets you differentiate almost anything you'll meet in practice. For a simple power function, the derivative drops the exponent down to become a multiplier out front and knocks the exponent itself down by one, a pattern so clean it becomes second nature almost instantly. The exponential function built on Euler's number is even more remarkable: it is its own derivative, completely unchanged by the operation, which is really the whole reason that constant deserves a name. The natural logarithm's derivative is the reciprocal of its input, a strange, tidy little fact. And the two basic trigonometric functions trade places: the derivative of sine is cosine, the derivative of cosine is the negative of sine, and differentiating four times in a row cycles right back to where you started.

Sometimes you don't have a tidy formula that spits one quantity out directly from another, only a relationship binding several changing quantities together, with none of them singled out as "the answer." Implicit differentiation, essentially the chain rule in disguise, lets you differentiate straight through such a relationship anyway, and it powers a whole genre of "related rates" problems.

The classic version: a ladder leans against a wall, its foot sliding away at a steady two feet per second. The foot's distance from the wall and the top's height up the wall are locked together, since the square of one plus the square of the other always equals one hundred (the ladder is ten feet long). Differentiate that locked relationship through time and you find how fast the top is sliding down at any moment, without ever solving for the top's height as a function of the foot's distance. Plug in the moment when the foot is six feet out from the wall, and the top is sliding down at one and a half feet per second.

You can keep differentiating past the first derivative, and each round tells you something new. The second derivative, the rate of change of the rate of change, tracks concavity: whether a curve bends upward like a smile or downward like a frown. One step further is what's whimsically called jerk, the rate of change of acceleration itself, which is why a badly driven car ride feels rough even when the average speed is perfectly smooth.

One of the nicest practical uses of the first derivative alone is linear approximation. Near any point you already understand well, the tangent line is a good stand-in for the curve close by. Take the square root function, which you know exactly at twenty-five, where it equals five. Use the tangent line there to estimate the square root of twenty-six, and you get approximately five point one. The true value is five point zero nine nine: close, and free for all practical purposes.

The Mean Value Theorem sounds almost too obvious to bother proving, and yet it underlies huge swaths of calculus. Take a function that is continuous across an interval and has a derivative at every point strictly inside it. Somewhere between the start and the end there has to be a moment where the instantaneous rate of change exactly matches the average rate of change over the whole interval. On a road trip, if your average speed was sixty miles an hour, your speedometer must have read exactly sixty at some point, even if it wandered above and below the rest of the time. Simple as it sounds, this fact is the engine behind proofs that a function with zero rate of change everywhere on an interval must be constant, and that a function with a positive rate of change everywhere on an interval must be strictly increasing.

Derivatives also tell you where a function is at its biggest or smallest, because at a peak or a valley the instantaneous rate of change either flattens to nothing or fails to exist at all. These special locations are called critical points, and finding a function's extreme values almost always starts by hunting them down. A classic example: a farmer has one hundred meters of fencing and wants to enclose the biggest possible rectangle using an existing barn wall as one side, so only three sides need fencing. Write the area as a function of how far the fence runs out from the wall, differentiate, and find where that rate of change hits zero. The optimal shape makes the side parallel to the wall exactly twice as long as each of the two sides perpendicular to it, and the enclosed area comes out to a maximum of one thousand two hundred fifty square meters.

**Worked example.** You have a square sheet of cardboard 12 centimeters on a side and want to fold it into an open-topped box by cutting an equal square from each corner and turning up the flaps. What size cut gives the biggest box? If each cut square has side x, the base is 12 minus 2x on a side and the height is x, so the volume is x times the square of 12 minus 2x. By the product and chain rules, its rate of change is the square of 12 minus 2x, minus 4x times 12 minus 2x, which factors neatly into 12 minus 2x times 12 minus 6x. That is zero when x is 6, which leaves no cardboard at all, or when x is 2. Cutting 2-centimeter squares gives a base of 8 by 8 and a height of 2, for a volume of 128 cubic centimeters. Nudge the cut to 1.9 or 2.1 centimeters and the volume drops to about 127.8 cubic centimeters either way, which is exactly what a peak should look like.

## Storey 12: Integral

If differentiation breaks a quantity down into its instantaneous rate of change, integration runs that process backward, building a whole quantity back up from a rate. A faucet's flow rate keeps changing, sped up one moment and slowed down the next. Hand someone a graph of that flow rate over time and ask how much water ended up in the bucket, and you've asked them to integrate.

Integration comes in two flavors that look similar and answer different questions. The definite integral is a single number: the signed area trapped between a curve and the horizontal axis over some specific stretch, computed as the limit of the areas of ever-thinner rectangles stacked up to approximate the region. The indefinite integral is a different animal altogether: an entire family of functions, every antiderivative of the function you started with, differing from one another only by whatever constant you tack on at the end.

Those rectangle approximations are worth dwelling on, because they show the true value getting squeezed from both sides. Take the curve that squares its input, over the stretch from zero to two, cut into four equal slices. Build rectangles using the curve's height at the right edge of each slice and they come out a touch too tall, overshooting at three point seven five. Use the height at the left edge instead and you undershoot, landing at one point seven five. As you slice the interval finer and finer, both approximations converge from above and below on the true area, which is exactly eight-thirds.

The single most important fact tying the two flavors together is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and it comes in two parts. The first says that if you build a new function by accumulating area under a curve up to some point, and then differentiate that accumulated-area function, you get back exactly the curve you started with. Accumulation and differentiation undo each other, the way a car's odometer reading, differentiated, gives you its speedometer reading, and the speedometer reading, integrated over time, gives you back the odometer's running total.

The second part is the one that makes integrals computable by hand. The signed area under a curve between two endpoints equals the difference between any antiderivative's values at those endpoints, the right-hand value minus the left-hand one. That single fact converts the geometric problem of finding an area into the algebraic problem of hunting down an antiderivative, which is why so much of a first calculus course is antiderivative-hunting practice.

The hunt is a genuine skill with its own toolbox. Substitution runs the chain rule backward, spotting a hidden inner function and its rate of change bundled together inside the thing you're integrating. Integration by parts runs the product rule backward, trading one product for another that's hopefully easier to handle. Trigonometric substitution disguises an awkward expression as a trigonometric one to unlock it, and partial fractions break an unwieldy ratio into simpler pieces integrated one at a time.

The standard functions (polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, the trig functions, and combinations of those) form a fixed pantry of ingredients. The humbling part is that some perfectly ordinary-looking functions have no antiderivative expressible from that pantry at all, no matter how skilled the cook. The bell curve, built from a negative exponential of a squared input, is the most famous example. It comes up constantly in probability, and mathematicians dealt with the problem by inventing a new function, the error function, defined to be its antiderivative, and moving on.

Once you can find areas, a whole catalog of physical and geometric quantities opens up, because they're all areas or accumulations in disguise: the area trapped between two curves, the volume of a solid formed by spinning a curve around an axis, the arc length of a curve, and the work done by a force over a distance. That last one has a lovely example in the law for a stretching spring, which says the force needed to stretch it grows in direct proportion to how far it's already stretched. The more you've pulled it, the harder it pulls back. Stretch a spring stiff enough to need two hundred newtons per meter of stretch out to half a meter, and because the force keeps growing throughout the stretch, you can't just multiply force by distance; you have to integrate the growing force over the whole stretch. The work comes out to twenty-five joules.

It's tempting to assume that anything stretching out to infinity must enclose an infinite amount of whatever it's measuring, and usually that instinct is right. Sometimes the region you're measuring doesn't stay politely bounded: it stretches off toward infinity, or the curve itself shoots up to infinity somewhere in the interval. Improper integrals handle both situations by taking a limit, gradually pushing a boundary out toward infinity or in toward the trouble spot, and seeing whether the accumulating area settles down to a finite number.

Remarkably, the infinitely long strip under the curve one over the square of the input, starting at one and heading off to infinity, encloses a perfectly finite area: exactly one square unit, despite going on forever. More generally, for curves of the form one over a power of the input, the area under such an infinitely long strip is finite only when that power is bigger than one. Drop to a power of one or below and the area blows up to infinity.

When no exact antiderivative exists, or when you're handed a table of measured data instead of a clean formula, numerical methods approximate the integral. The trapezoidal rule treats each thin slice as a trapezoid rather than a rectangle, hugging the curve a bit more closely. A more refined method, Simpson's rule, fits a curved piece, a parabola, through each little group of points, and it happens to be exact for any polynomial of degree three or lower.

**Worked example.** A rain barrel fills from a downspout, and as the storm builds the inflow rises steadily: 6 liters per minute at the start, increasing by 4 liters per minute every minute, so the rate after t minutes is 6 plus 4t liters per minute. How much water arrives in the first 5 minutes? The total is the integral of the rate from 0 to 5. An antiderivative of 6 plus 4t is 6t plus 2 t squared, since differentiating that gives back the rate. By the Fundamental Theorem, evaluate it at the two ends and subtract: at 5 minutes it is 30 plus 50, or 80, and at 0 it is 0. So 80 liters land in the barrel. A quick sanity check: the rate climbs in a straight line from 6 to 26 liters per minute, so its average is 16, and 16 liters per minute for 5 minutes is again 80 liters. For a rate that rises in a straight line, even the trapezoidal rule gets the answer exactly.

## Storey 13: Multi-Variable Functions

Up to now every function has eaten a single number and spat out a single number. Plenty of real quantities depend on more than one thing at once (temperature depends on both latitude and longitude, say), so we need functions that eat a whole point, a pair of numbers, and spit out one number. Graph such a function and instead of a curve you get a surface floating in three-dimensional space, a landscape of hills and valleys.

The trouble is that the landscape lives in three dimensions and most of us can barely doodle in two. Cartographers had already solved this problem, independently and for entirely practical reasons: level sets, better known as contour lines, are the curves you get by slicing the landscape at a fixed height and looking straight down. Where the contour lines bunch up tightly, the terrain is steep; where they spread out lazily, it's gentle. You get nearly the whole shape of the landscape without ever picturing it in three dimensions.

Limits, already a little subtle with one variable, become treacherous with two. In one variable there are only two directions to approach a point from, left and right, so checking both settles the matter. In two variables there are infinitely many directions, and infinitely many curved paths besides, and each can tell a different story. A fly approaching the edge of a tabletop might glide straight in, spiral inward, or swoop along some looping path, and in principle each route could find something different waiting at the edge.

To see it happen, take a function whose top is one variable times the square of the other and whose bottom is the square of the first variable plus the fourth power of the second. Approach the origin along any straight line whatsoever, and the function's value dutifully shrinks to zero every time; you'd be forgiven for concluding the limit is zero. Sneak up on the origin along the curved path where the first variable equals the square of the second, though, and the value settles at one-half. Since the straight-line approaches and this curved approach disagree, the limit does not exist at that point. Checking straight lines alone is never enough to prove a multivariable limit exists.

Partial derivatives handle differentiation in this world with a simple trick: freeze every variable except one, and differentiate as if that were the only variable that mattered. It's how you'd tweak a recipe one variable at a time: hold the baking time fixed and ask how the cake changes as you turn up the oven, then hold the temperature fixed and ask how it changes as you extend the time. Do this twice, differentiating first with respect to one variable and then the other, and you might worry the order matters. A reassuring guarantee steps in: as long as these mixed partial derivatives are continuous, the order makes no difference, and you land on the same answer either way.

The single most useful object in this subject is the gradient, a little arrow built out of all of a function's partial derivatives at a given point. That arrow always points in the direction the function increases fastest, straight uphill in the steepest possible sense, and its length tells you exactly how steep that steepest ascent is. It also has a clean geometric property: the gradient is always perpendicular to the contour line passing through the same point. This is why a sensible hiker follows the contour lines on a map instead of crossing them. Walking along a contour line means walking perpendicular to the steepest direction, the gentlest possible route across the terrain.

Both the chain rule and implicit differentiation carry over into this richer setting. They let you differentiate composite functions built by feeding the outputs of some functions into the inputs of others, and handle relationships between several variables that aren't solved for any one of them. The bookkeeping gets more elaborate, since there are more paths of dependency to track, but the underlying idea survives intact: rates of change multiply and add their way through a chain of dependencies.

Having derivatives in some limited sense doesn't guarantee genuine smoothness. A function can have perfectly well-defined partial derivatives at a point, behaving nicely along the two axis directions, while failing even to be continuous there. Consider the product of the two variables divided by the sum of their squares. At the origin, moving purely along either axis, the function is flat and well-behaved. Sneak up on the origin along the diagonal instead, and the function stays locked at one-half the whole way in, refusing to settle down to whatever value sits at the origin itself. Good behavior along two special directions told you almost nothing about the rest.

Finding the high and low points of a multivariable function again starts with critical points, where the gradient arrow shrinks to nothing. Here a new subtlety appears that never shows up with a single variable: a critical point might be a real peak, a real valley, or a saddle point, shaped like the seat of a horse's saddle, rising in one direction while falling in another. To tell these apart you build a single number out of the function's second partial derivatives, and its sign does most of the classifying. Negative means a saddle; positive means a peak or a valley (told apart by whether the function curves downward or upward along an axis); zero means the test cannot decide. The square of one variable minus the square of the other is the textbook saddle: at the origin it rises as you walk out along one axis and falls as you walk out along the other.

**Worked example.** A hill's height in meters is 100 minus x squared minus 2 times y squared, where x is kilometers east of the summit and y is kilometers north. You stand 3 kilometers east and 2 kilometers north, at a height of 100 minus 9 minus 8, or 83 meters. Which way is steepest, and how steep? The partial derivative with respect to x is minus 2x, which is minus 6 here; with respect to y it is minus 4y, which is minus 8. So the gradient is the arrow with components minus 6 and minus 8, pointing west and south, back toward the summit. Its length is the square root of 36 plus 64, which is 10, so the steepest climb gains 10 meters per kilometer. Walk at right angles to that arrow and you stay on the 83-meter contour. The only critical point is the summit, where the second-derivative test gives minus 2 times minus 4, minus 0, which is 8: positive, with downward curvature, so a genuine peak at 100 meters.

## Storey 14: Multi-Variable Calculus

Just as ordinary integration extends the idea of a sum to a smoothly varying single-variable function, double and triple integrals extend the same rectangle-slicing idea to regions in a plane or solids in space. Instead of chopping an interval into thin slices, you chop a two-dimensional region into tiny patches, or a solid into tiny chunks, multiply the function's value on each piece by the size of that piece, add up all those products, and take the limit as the pieces shrink to nothing. To estimate the total weight of an oddly shaped loaf of bread, you could mentally chop it into tiny cubes, weigh each one, and add up the results: a triple integral in everyday clothing.

The saving grace that makes these integrals computable is a theorem that lets you evaluate them one variable at a time, as a sequence of ordinary single-variable integrals nested inside each other. It requires the function to be reasonably well-behaved, and it requires you to have sketched the region carefully enough to know the correct boundaries for each nested step.

With this machinery, a whole catalog of physical quantities falls out as special cases, all computed by the same procedure with a different weighting function plugged in. Integrate the constant one and you get plain area or volume. Weight it by a density function and you get total mass. Weight it further and you get the center of mass, the balance point of an object, or moments of inertia, which measure how mass is distributed for the purposes of rotation. One tool does all this work; only the ingredient you feed it changes.

Sometimes the region fits another coordinate system far more naturally than the standard grid. A circular region practically begs to be described by distance from the center and an angle, rather than horizontal and vertical position. Switching coordinates like this requires a correction factor called the Jacobian, which rescales how area or volume elements stretch or shrink under the change. A Mercator map of the world distorts area so severely that Greenland looks about as big as Africa; to recover Greenland's true area from measurements on that flat map, you'd need a factor telling you how much the map stretches things at that latitude. The Jacobian is that kind of correction factor, for switching between coordinate grids.

For circular coordinates in a plane, the correction factor is simply the distance from the center itself. For spherical coordinates in space, it's the square of the distance from the center times the sine of the angle measured down from the pole. The most celebrated payoff of the trick is an integral with no elementary antiderivative at all: the bell curve integrated over the whole real line. You can't find its antiderivative directly, but square the integral, which turns it into a double integral over an entire plane, switch to circular coordinates, and the previously intractable problem collapses into something easy. The answer is the square root of pi.

Vector fields assign an arrow, rather than a plain number, to every point in a region, like the velocity of water flowing across a surface, different in strength and direction from place to place. Line integrals measure how much a vector field pushes along a specific path through it, capturing the work done moving through a force field or the circulation of a fluid around a loop. A vector field earns the title "conservative" when the work it does between two points never depends on which path you take, or equivalently when the circulation around every closed loop is exactly zero.

A clean test settles the question. On a region with no holes, a field is conservative exactly when the rate at which its first component changes as you move in the second direction matches the rate at which its second component changes as you move in the first direction. When a field passes this test, every work calculation collapses to evaluating a single potential function at the start and end points, no path-tracing required.

Surface integrals extend the same idea from curves to whole surfaces. Instead of asking how much a field pushes along a path, flux asks how much of a field flows through a surface, like measuring how much water per second passes through a net dunked in a stream. Because a surface has two sides, you first have to choose which way it faces, its orientation, since the flux measured through one face is the negative of the flux measured through the other.

Three theorems tie all of this together, each converting a statement about a boundary into a statement about the interior it encloses, or vice versa. A company's finances can be tallied two ways: add up every internal transaction between departments, or just track what crosses the company's outer boundary, money in from customers and money out to suppliers. For the right kind of bookkeeping, both totals agree. The first theorem converts the circulation of a field around the boundary of a flat region into a double integral over the region's interior of the field's local tendency to spin, its curl. A curved-surface counterpart lifts that same idea off the flat plane and onto a curved surface floating in space. The Divergence theorem, also called Gauss's theorem, does the analogous thing one dimension up: it converts the total flux out through a closed surface into a triple integral, over the enclosed solid, of the field's local tendency to spread out at each point, its divergence.

All three theorems are the same idea in different costumes: what happens on the boundary of a region exactly matches what happens throughout its interior, added up point by point. And this is no exotic higher-dimensional coincidence. It descends directly from the simplest fact imaginable about ordinary sums. Add up a string of consecutive differences, and almost everything in the middle cancels, leaving only the difference between the very last value and the very first: a telescoping sum. All three boundary theorems are that same telescoping cancellation, made to work across continuous regions instead of discrete lists.

**Worked example.** A round metal plate of radius 2 meters gets thicker toward the rim, so that its density is 3 times the distance from the center, in kilograms per square meter. What is its mass? The disk begs for circular coordinates, with distance r running from 0 to 2 and the angle running all the way around, from 0 to 2 pi. The density is 3r, and the Jacobian contributes one more factor of r, so the quantity to integrate is 3 times r squared. Do the inner integral over r first: an antiderivative of 3 times r squared is r cubed, which goes from 0 to 8 as r goes from 0 to 2. Nothing depends on the angle, so the outer integral simply multiplies by 2 pi. The mass is 16 pi, about 50.3 kilograms. Forget the Jacobian and you'd integrate 3r instead, getting 6 times 2 pi, or 12 pi, about 37.7 kilograms, short by a full quarter of the true answer.

## Storey 15: Vector Calculus

A vector field, as we've met, assigns an arrow to every point in space, such as the local wind velocity at every spot on a map. Three operators let you interrogate such a field at any point. The gradient, already familiar, starts from a plain number-valued field and produces an arrow field pointing uphill, in the direction of steepest increase.

Divergence takes an arrow field and produces a plain number at each point, measuring whether the field is locally expanding outward from that point or contracting inward toward it. You build it by adding up how fast each component of the field changes along its own corresponding direction. It tells you whether a point acts like a tiny source spraying fluid outward or a tiny sink sucking fluid in. Curl takes an arrow field and produces another arrow field that measures local spin, whether the field is swirling around a point. The direction of the resulting arrow gives the axis of rotation by the right-hand rule, and its length tells you how fast the rotation is (twice the angular speed, to be exact).

A wonderfully simple running example makes both concrete. Take a field in which every arrow is tangent to the circle around the origin through that point, all rotating counterclockwise, as though the entire plane were a rigid record spinning at a steady rate. Its divergence is exactly zero everywhere, which makes sense: a spinning disk of fluid is going around, neither inflating nor deflating. Its curl is a constant arrow pointing straight out of the plane with a length of two, correctly capturing that this fluid rotates at a steady one radian per second about the central axis.

Two little identities come up often enough to memorize outright. First, take the gradient of a function and then the curl of that result, and the answer is always a flat zero. Second, take the curl of a vector field and then the divergence of that result, and you again always get zero. Both trace back to the same underlying reason, that mixed partial derivatives of a smooth function agree in either order, so in a real sense they're one fact in two outfits. A third, more elaborate identity says that applying the curl twice gives the gradient of the divergence minus a certain second-derivative operator (applied to each component of the field separately), and this compound relationship is exactly what underlies the wave equation describing how electromagnetic waves propagate through space.

A field is conservative, meaning expressible as the gradient of some underlying potential, with path-independent work and zero circulation around every closed loop, exactly when its curl vanishes everywhere throughout a region with no holes. That caveat about holes is the whole point of a delightfully subtle example. Take the whirlpool-shaped field circling the origin, its strength dying off with distance at just the rate that makes its curl exactly zero at every point where it's defined. You'd think that makes it conservative. Walk once counterclockwise around a loop enclosing the origin, the single point where the field isn't defined, and the circulation comes out to two times pi. Nothing in the local algebra warned you. The hole in the domain, a fact about the region's shape rather than the field's formula, spoils the conclusion. Topology matters as much as algebra.

The three boundary theorems from Storey 14 restate that same boundary-equals-interior relationship for flat regions, curved surfaces, and solid volumes respectively. And the same operators yield a beautiful structural result, a decomposition theorem: any sufficiently smooth vector field that behaves itself far from the origin can be split, uniquely, into a curl-free piece plus a divergence-free piece. Every such field, however complicated, is built from just these two elementary ingredients.

This machinery is the language physics is written in. Incompressible fluid flow is defined by exactly zero divergence everywhere, meaning fluid is never created or destroyed at any point, just shuffled around, while vorticity, the curl of the flow, measures the local swirl of the current. The four equations governing electricity and magnetism tie the divergence and curl of the electric and magnetic fields to the charges and currents that produce them, and the Divergence theorem and its curved-surface counterpart convert those local statements into their integral forms over whole surfaces and around whole loops.

Gauss's law, one of the four, is a particularly satisfying illustration. The total flux of the electric field through any sphere surrounding a point charge comes out the same no matter how big you make the sphere. The field weakens with the square of the distance at precisely the rate that the sphere's surface area grows with the square of the distance, so the two effects cancel perfectly and the total flux never changes.

A nice thought to end on: gradient, divergence, and curl look like three unrelated operators to memorize separately, but they are three faces of a single deeper operator, the exterior derivative, which generalizes differentiation to geometric objects more elaborate than plain functions. With that one operator in hand, the three boundary theorems collapse into a single master theorem, one statement about boundaries and interiors that contains all three as special cases.

**Worked example.** Gas streams outward from a small source in a perfectly uniform expansion: at the point with coordinates x, y, and z (in meters), its velocity is the arrow with those same three components, in meters per second. How many cubic meters of gas per second flow out through a sphere of radius 2 meters centered on the source? The divergence adds up how fast each component changes along its own direction: 1 plus 1 plus 1, or 3, everywhere. The Divergence theorem says the outward flux equals 3 times the enclosed volume. That volume is four-thirds pi times 2 cubed, or 32 pi over 3, so the flux is 32 pi, about 100.5 cubic meters per second. Now check it from the boundary side. On the sphere the flow points straight outward with speed 2, and the sphere's area is 4 pi times 2 squared, or 16 pi. Speed times area gives 32 pi again, interior and boundary agreeing exactly.

## Storey 16: Series

A series is what you get when you add up infinitely many numbers, one after another, and ask whether the running total settles down to some fixed value as you keep going, instead of wandering off to infinity or refusing to make up its mind. Pay off a debt in installments that keep shrinking, half of what's left each time, and the running total you've paid keeps climbing without ever overshooting the original debt, settling closer and closer to it forever. When a series settles like that, we say it converges, and the value it settles to is its sum.

One subtlety catches almost everyone the first time: there are two very different flavors of convergence. If the series made from the sizes of the terms alone, ignoring their signs, also converges, you're in the well-behaved case called absolute convergence, and you can shuffle the terms into any order you like without changing the sum. If a series converges only because positive and negative terms delicately cancel each other, that's merely conditional convergence, and it is unsettlingly fragile. A conditionally convergent series can be reordered, just by choosing which term to add next, to add up to any number you want, or to shoot off to infinity, or to refuse to settle at all. Same terms, wildly different answers, purely as a function of order. That alone should make you nervous about rearranging an infinite sum casually.

So how do you tell in advance whether a series converges at all? There's a toolbox of tests. One family compares each term to the one before it, asking whether the terms shrink geometrically fast enough for the tail to die out. If that ratio settles below a break-even point in the long run, you're safe; above it, the series blows up; exactly at it, the test has nothing to say. Another approach, the integral test, treats the sum as a stand-in for the area under a related curve, and it settles a classic family in one stroke: the sum of one over each whole number raised to some fixed power converges exactly when that power is bigger than one.

Dial the power down to exactly one and you get the harmonic series, the sum of one over each whole number, which diverges, creeping up to infinity however slowly, even though its individual terms shrink to nothing. That counterintuitive fact deserves a moment: terms going to zero is necessary for convergence and nowhere near sufficient. And for series whose terms alternate in sign while steadily shrinking toward zero, Leibniz's simple, reliable test guarantees convergence.

Now generalize from a series of numbers to a series built from increasing whole-number powers of the offset from some fixed center point, each multiplied by its own coefficient: a power series. Such a series converges absolutely as long as you stay within a certain distance of the center, its radius of convergence, and that radius is determined entirely by how fast the coefficients grow. Step outside it and the series diverges; stay strictly inside and you're always safe. The annoying part is the boundary. Right at the edge anything can happen, and you have to check each of the two endpoints separately, by hand. A power series can converge at one edge and not the other: one classic example converges on the range from negative one third to one third, closed at the negative end and open at the positive one, a lopsided little asymmetry no formula alone will warn you about.

Where do the coefficients come from, if you're trying to represent a known function as a power series? You repeatedly differentiate the function at the center point and divide by the appropriate factorial each time. A function that actually agrees with its own power series nearby is called analytic, a much stronger property than merely being smooth, meaning infinitely differentiable.

The humbling counterexample is the function built from an exponential decay whose exponent is negative one over the square of the input, with its value at zero defined to be zero. Every one of its derivatives equals zero at that point, however many times you differentiate, and yet the function is never zero at any other input, however close to zero you look. Its power series, dutifully built from all those zero derivatives, is the flat zero function, describing nothing like the curve it supposedly came from. Smooth, yes. Analytic, absolutely not.

The payoff for all this machinery is that power series behave like well-mannered polynomials. You can add them, differentiate them, integrate them, and multiply two of them together as you would two long polynomials, and inside the range where they converge you get exactly the results you'd hope for. This turns differential equations into bookkeeping: guess that the unknown function is some power series, plug it in, and the equation collapses into a recurrence relating the coefficients to each other. Try this on the simplest possible differential equation, where a function's rate of change equals the function itself and the function starts out at one, and the recurrence, unwound, is exactly the series for the exponential function, rediscovered from scratch.

The same trick run in reverse turns number sequences into functions. Encode the Fibonacci sequence as the coefficients of a power series, and the Fibonacci recurrence collapses the entire infinite series into one tidy fraction, a simple ratio of polynomials, called its generating function.

Finally there's a completely different flavor of series, built from sines and cosines instead of powers: the Fourier series. Any reasonably well-behaved periodic wave can be broken down into a sum of pure oscillations at different frequencies, each with its own strength, found by a projection integral that measures how much of that particular wave is "in" the function, much like measuring a shadow. A reassuring consistency check covers the whole enterprise: the total "energy" packed into the original function exactly equals the total energy spread across all its frequency components. Nothing is created or destroyed, just reorganized.

There's one famously stubborn wrinkle. Near a sudden jump in the function, the partial sums of a Fourier series don't smooth the jump out as you add more terms. They persistently overshoot by very close to nine percent of the full height of the jump, however many terms you add. The overshoot never goes away; it just gets squeezed into a thinner sliver right at the jump.

**Worked example.** A ball dropped from 2 meters rebounds to exactly half its previous height on every bounce, forever, in the idealized version. How far does it travel in total? It first falls 2 meters. After that, each bounce is an up-and-down pair: 1 meter up and 1 down, then 0.5 and 0.5, then 0.25 and 0.25, and so on. So the bouncing adds 2 times the geometric series 1 plus one-half plus one-quarter and onward. Each term is half the one before, a ratio safely below 1, so the series converges, and its sum is 1 divided by 1 minus one-half, which is 2. The bounces therefore contribute 2 times 2, or 4 meters, and the grand total is 6 meters. After just 5 bounces the ball has covered 2 plus 2 times the sum of 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, and 0.0625, which is 5.875 meters, already within an eighth of a meter of the limit, with infinitely many bounces crammed into that last eighth.

## Storey 17: Fourier Transforms and FFT

A glass prism splits a beam of white light into a rainbow of individual colors. White light doesn't advertise which colors it's made of until something forces the split, and a Fourier transform does exactly that to a signal, splitting it into the individual frequencies it's built from.

To get there, take a Fourier series and let the period of its repeating pattern stretch out longer and longer, toward infinity. The discrete comb of allowed frequencies, spaced apart like teeth, packs tighter and tighter until it blurs into a smooth, unbroken continuum. What's left is the Fourier transform, a way of taking a function that isn't periodic at all and expressing it as a blend of every possible frequency, weighted by how much of each is present. One of its nicest features is how cleanly it handles calculus: differentiating the original function corresponds, after the transform, to multiplying by the frequency, scaled by a fixed factor built from two pi and the imaginary unit. Calculus turns into algebra. And there's a lovely fixed point hiding here: the classic bell-shaped curve, suitably normalized, transforms into itself.

The transform is a round trip. An inversion process reconstructs the original function exactly from its transform, so nothing is thrown away. A guarantee makes this feel less like magic and more like bookkeeping: the total energy in a function, measured by integrating its squared size, is exactly preserved when you pass to its transform. Switching between describing a signal by how it behaves over time and describing it by which frequencies it contains only relabels the same quantity of stuff.

One of the most useful facts in all of applied mathematics is the convolution theorem. Convolution slides one function across another and, at each position, integrates how much they overlap. It's the natural description of smearing or blurring one signal by another, and computed directly it's a painful integral. The convolution theorem says this whole messy smearing operation becomes, in the frequency domain, ordinary multiplication of the two transforms. That fact underlies essentially all frequency-domain filtering. A nice concrete picture: convolve two identical rectangular pulses and out comes a triangle, the sharp edges of two overlapping blocks softened into straight ramps.

Real-world signals are sampled, a finite list of numbers instead of a continuous function, so you need a discrete cousin, the discrete Fourier transform, which extracts the spectrum hiding inside a finite sequence of samples. Computed the obvious brute-force way, the work grows roughly with the square of the number of samples, which becomes painfully slow as the sequence gets long. A clever algorithm, the fast Fourier transform, sidesteps this by recursively splitting the sequence into its even-indexed and odd-indexed terms, solving each half, and stitching the results back together. It arrives at the identical answer for a fraction of the work, with the effort growing only slightly faster than the length of the sequence itself. For a list of a million samples, that difference is the gap between a calculation a computer finishes in a blink and one that grinds along for ages.

This algorithm runs quietly and constantly underneath audio processing, image compression, and medical imaging. An MRI scanner is, at bottom, measuring samples of a two-dimensional Fourier transform of the object being scanned, and the fast transform is what makes inverting that data back into a picture practical.

There's also a hard limit built into the whole framework: the uncertainty principle. A musician already knows it by ear. A short, percussive click has a sharply defined instant but almost no clear pitch, its energy spread thin across many frequencies, while a long, sustained note has a clear pitch and no sharp starting instant. The spread of a signal over time and its spread over frequency can't both be made arbitrarily small at once. Multiply the two spreads together and there's a fixed floor you cannot go below, however cleverly you design the signal, and only the Gaussian bell curve touches that floor exactly, making it the most simultaneously time-and-frequency-concentrated signal there is.

Separate from that limit is the sampling theorem: to capture a signal faithfully, you must sample it at least twice as fast as its fastest-varying component. Sample any slower and distinct frequencies become indistinguishable from one another, an effect called aliasing. You've likely seen a cousin of it at the movies, where a spinning wagon wheel or helicopter rotor seems to slow down, stop dead, or even spin backward, because the camera snaps only a limited number of frames per second and misses whatever happens in between. A tone at three kilohertz, sampled at only five kilohertz, masquerades as an entirely different, lower tone at two kilohertz, a case of mistaken identity baked right into the sampling process.

**Worked example.** A sound clip contains 1,048,576 samples, which is 2 to the 20th power, or about 24 seconds of CD-quality audio at 44,100 samples per second. How much faster is the fast Fourier transform than the brute-force version? Brute force takes on the order of the number of samples squared, about 1.1 trillion operations. The fast transform takes on the order of the number of samples times the number of times you can halve it, which is 20 here, so about 21 million operations. The ratio is 1,048,576 divided by 20, about 52,000. On a machine doing a billion operations per second, brute force needs roughly 18 minutes, while the fast transform finishes in about two hundredths of a second. The sampling theorem adds one more number: at 44,100 samples per second, the highest pitch the recording can capture faithfully is half of that, 22,050 hertz, comfortably above the roughly 20,000 hertz limit of human hearing.
