## Storey 18: Laplace Transform

The Laplace transform is a close cousin of the Fourier transform with one crucial difference in attitude: instead of weighing a function equally across all of time, it discounts the future. It multiplies by a steadily decaying exponential before integrating, starting from a fixed beginning point and running forward forever. A financial analyst does the same thing when discounting future cash flows to a present value, treating a dollar promised ten years from now as worth less than a dollar in hand today, precisely so that a growing stream of future payments can still add up to a sensible, finite total.

That built-in decay lets the transform tame functions that would make the Fourier transform blow up. An ordinary growing exponential is perfectly well-behaved here, because the discounting factor can be chosen to out-race it. The price is that the transform only needs, and only uses, the function's behavior from the starting point onward, and it requires the function to grow no faster than some exponential rate, or the discounting can't keep up and the integral never settles down.

The real magic, the reason this transform earns its keep in differential equations, is what happens to differentiation. Differentiating a function with respect to time becomes, after the transform, multiplying the transformed function by the new variable and subtracting the function's own value at the starting instant. So a linear differential equation with constant coefficients, together with its initial conditions, collapses directly into a plain algebraic equation for the transformed unknown. In classical solution methods the initial conditions get tacked on afterward as a separate fitting step; here they're baked into the algebra from the first line.

In practice you rarely compute a Laplace transform from its defining integral. You keep a short table of standard results and look them up. A whole-number power of time transforms into that power's factorial divided by the new variable raised to one power higher. A pure exponential transforms into one divided by the new variable minus the exponential's own growth rate. A sine wave of a given frequency transforms into that frequency divided by the sum of the new variable squared and the frequency squared. A handful of entries like these cover most of the functions you'll meet in an introductory course.

Going backward, from an algebraic expression to the original function of time, is done through partial fractions: break the expression into simpler pieces, using a quick "cover-up" shortcut when the denominator factors into distinct simple pieces, bring in terms pairing a power of time with an exponential whenever a factor repeats, and complete the square to handle any leftover piece that can't be factored into real, simple pieces at all.

Two special, almost cartoonish functions round out the toolkit: the unit step, which flips instantaneously from off to on at some chosen moment, and the Dirac delta, an idealized infinitely tall, infinitely narrow spike representing a single instantaneous jolt. Both have beautifully simple transforms. The step contributes a decaying-exponential factor divided by the new variable, and the delta contributes a bare decaying-exponential factor by itself, which turns switches and shocks in a physical system into simple algebra instead of case-by-case reasoning about before and after. Hit a simple decaying system with an instantaneous hammer-blow of unit strength, and the solution doesn't ease into a bump. It jumps by exactly one full unit at that instant and then decays smoothly away, as you'd expect from a system struck once, sharply, and then left alone.

The convolution theorem shows up here too: the transform of the convolution of two functions is the product of their individual transforms. Physically, a linear, time-invariant system's total output is its response to a single instantaneous jolt, convolved with whatever input you actually fed it. The system's reaction to any complicated input is built entirely from its reaction to one idealized punch, smeared across time according to the shape of the real input.

Finally, package a whole linear system as a transfer function, the ratio of its transformed output to its transformed input, and that single object tells you almost everything about the system's personality. The values where the transfer function blows up are its poles, and their location tells you about stability at a glance. Poles in the decaying region mean any free, unforced response dies away on its own; poles in the growing region mean the free response blows up without bound. A repeated pole on the negative real axis is the critically damped case, the borderline between a system that rings and one that merely creeps back. It produces a response shaped like time multiplied by a decaying exponential: it rises once, reaches a single peak, and settles back down smoothly with no overshoot and no oscillation, the fastest possible return to rest without any bounce.

**Worked example.** A capacitor charges through a resistor so that its voltage v, starting at 0 volts, obeys a simple rule: the rate of change of v plus 2 times v always equals 4, with time in seconds. Transform both sides. The rate of change becomes s times V minus the starting value of 0, and the constant 4 becomes 4 over s. So s times V plus 2 times V equals 4 over s, and V is 4 divided by s times s plus 2. Now the cover-up trick: cover the factor s and set s to 0 in what remains, giving 4 over 2, or 2; cover the factor s plus 2 and set s to minus 2, giving 4 over minus 2, or minus 2. So V is 2 over s minus 2 over s plus 2. Reading the table backward, v equals 2 minus 2 times e to the power minus 2t. After 1 second that is 2 minus 2 times 0.135, about 1.73 volts, creeping toward 2 volts without overshoot.

## Storey 19: Integral Geometry

Integral geometry flips the usual setup of calculus on its head. Instead of integrating some function over a fixed region of space, you fix a shape and integrate over an entire space of lines, or planes, or rigid motions, using that as a way of measuring the shape itself. The classic example: you can find a curve's length without measuring it directly, by asking how often, on average, randomly chosen lines cross it.

For that to make sense you need a sensible notion of a "random line" in the first place, and there's really only one reasonable choice. Describe each line by its signed distance from a fixed origin point together with the angle its perpendicular direction makes, and measure sets of lines using the natural combination of those two descriptions. Up to an overall scale factor, that is the unique way of measuring left unchanged by sliding or rotating the whole picture, exactly the even-handedness you want from "randomness." The ordinary description of a line, by its slope and where it crosses an axis, lacks this property: it silently piles extra weight onto certain directions, especially nearly vertical ones, and would bias any calculation built on it.

With that measure in hand, something wonderful becomes possible: a formula that recovers a curve's exact length purely from counting, on average, how many times random lines cross it, weighted by the invariant measure. Run it on a circle of radius one and the formula returns exactly two pi, the circle's true circumference, recovered without measuring an arc, purely from a counting argument about crossings. The same idea, generalized, shows that the total measure of all lines touching a convex shape is exactly equal to that shape's perimeter.

A parallel version of the formula recovers a shape's area instead of its length, by averaging the lengths of the chords random lines cut through it, which is an old slicing trick for computing areas in different clothes. There's also a companion result in three dimensions: a convex solid's total surface area equals four times the average area of its shadow, averaged over every direction you could shine light on it from. Check it against a sphere and the numbers land exactly: a sphere of radius three casts an average shadow of nine pi in area, and four times that is thirty-six pi, precisely the sphere's surface area.

Push the idea further and you get the kinematic formulas, which measure how often two shapes overlap when one of them is picked up, moved to a random position and orientation, and dropped again. They're expressed in terms of a shape's intrinsic volumes, quantities like its area, its perimeter, and its Euler characteristic (essentially a tally of its connected pieces minus its holes), which combine according to rules much like the inclusion–exclusion counting used for overlapping sets. Each intrinsic volume enters weighted by its own dimension-dependent constant.

All of this is far more than a curiosity. It's how a CT scanner sees inside a body. One transform records, for every line through an object, the total amount of stuff (density, say) that the line passes through. Collect this over every angle and position and you have exactly the raw data a CT scanner produces, called a sinogram. The projection-slice theorem is the bridge back to ordinary Fourier analysis: the Fourier transform of a single projection, taken at one viewing angle, is exactly one radial slice through the full two-dimensional Fourier transform of the whole object. Gather projections from enough angles and you've gathered enough slices to reassemble the object's complete two-dimensional transform, then invert back to a picture of the interior, all without cutting anyone open.

The subject even has a famous parlor-trick origin: the needle-drop problem. Drop a needle at random onto a floor ruled with evenly spaced parallel lines, and ask for the probability that it lands across one. That probability depends on the needle's length relative to the spacing of the lines in a way that involves the number pi, so if you throw the needle over and over and tally how often it lands on a line, your crossing frequency estimates pi. It's the line-crossing formula run as a physical experiment, and one of the very first Monte Carlo methods in history, centuries before anyone had that name for it.

A related idea says that the fraction of area occupied by some material on a random flat slice through a solid equals, on average, the fraction of volume that material occupies in the whole three-dimensional solid. It's the basic fact that makes point-counting under a microscope a legitimate way to estimate how much of some tissue or material is present, just from examining thin slices.

**Worked example.** A wooden floor has parallel seams 10 centimeters apart, and you drop a 5-centimeter needle on it at random. For a needle no longer than the spacing, the chance of landing across a seam is twice the needle's length divided by pi times the spacing: here 2 times 5 over pi times 10, which is 1 over pi, about 0.318. Now run the formula backward. Drop the needle 1,000 times and suppose 318 drops land across a seam. Solving for pi gives twice the length times the number of drops, divided by the spacing times the number of crossings: 10,000 over 3,180, about 3.145, within about three thousandths of the true 3.14159. Luck plays a large part, though. With 1,000 drops the number of crossings typically wobbles by about 15 either way, enough to move the estimate by roughly 0.15. Each extra decimal place of pi costs about a hundred times as many drops.

## Storey 20: Matrices

A matrix is nothing more mysterious than a rectangular grid of numbers. It earns a whole storey of the tower because of what that grid represents: a linear map, a rule for turning one vector into another that respects addition and scaling. Matrix multiplication pairs up a row from the first matrix with a column from the second to build each entry of the result, and it is defined that way for exactly one reason: so that applying the combined matrix to a vector gives the same answer as applying the second matrix first and then feeding the result into the first. Multiplying matrices is how you compose linear maps, the same way you compose two functions by feeding the output of one into the other.

Because applying one map after another generally differs from applying them in the opposite order, matrix multiplication inherits the same lack of symmetry. Swap the order of two matrices being multiplied and you'll generally get a completely different answer, a fact every student rediscovers the hard way exactly once. Rotate a rectangular rubber sheet ninety degrees and then stretch it wider, or stretch it wider first and then rotate it: the two final shapes end up in completely different places, even though the same two operations were involved both times.

A square matrix, one with the same number of rows as columns, is invertible exactly when its rows carry no redundant information, meaning none of them can be built from a combination of the others. That single condition is equivalent to several other useful tests: the determinant, a single number computed from the whole matrix, is different from zero; and reducing the matrix by row operations lands you exactly on the identity matrix, the matrix that does nothing at all.

Each elementary row operation in that reduction (swapping two rows, scaling one, or adding a multiple of one to another) corresponds to multiplying by a simple "elementary" matrix on the left. Tracking that bookkeeping carefully gives a very practical algorithm: bolt the identity matrix onto the side of the original and row-reduce the combined block. By the time the original side has become the identity, the bolted-on side has turned into the original matrix's inverse.

The determinant compresses an entire matrix down into one number, and that number carries real geometric meaning. It respects multiplication cleanly: the determinant of a product of two matrices is the product of their individual determinants. For a triangular matrix, one with only zeros on one side of the diagonal, the determinant is simply the product of the entries on the diagonal, no further work required. Geometrically, the size of the determinant, ignoring its sign, is exactly the factor by which the associated linear map stretches or shrinks volume: feed in a chunk of space, get out a chunk scaled by that factor.

A determinant of exactly zero is the geometric statement that the map squashes some region of space into something lower-dimensional, a plane crushed to a line, say. Once a dimension has been squashed away, no amount of algebraic effort will hand it back, which is exactly why a zero determinant means the matrix cannot be inverted.

A flashlight casting the shadow of a three-dimensional object onto a flat wall keeps some information about the object's shape and throws the rest away, flattening whatever direction pointed straight at the light. Row-reducing a matrix reveals the corresponding quantity for a matrix, its rank: the true number of independent directions its columns span once redundancy is stripped away. The rank-nullity theorem ties this to the matrix's kernel, the set of vectors it crushes down to nothing, saying that the rank plus the dimension of the kernel always adds up exactly to the number of columns. It's a lovely conservation law: whatever dimension goes missing from the rank shows up as extra freedom trapped inside the kernel. Nothing just disappears.

An eigenvector of a matrix is a rare and special kind of vector: one that the matrix doesn't rotate or skew at all, only stretches or shrinks, by a fixed scaling factor called its eigenvalue. Pull diagonally on a sheet of stretchy fabric pinned at its center, and most marked points swing off at a new angle as well as moving farther out. A few special directions, arrows drawn from the pin, end up merely longer or shorter, still pointing exactly the way they started. Those survivors are the eigenvectors. You find the possible eigenvalues by looking for exactly the scaling amounts that make the matrix, shifted by that amount, collapse into a non-invertible one, echoing the determinant condition from before.

These eigen-directions mark out the "grain" of the matrix, the natural axes along which its action is simplest, and a dynamical fact hides here. Apply a matrix to almost any starting vector, over and over, and the result drifts closer and closer to the matrix's dominant eigen-direction, the one with the largest scaling factor in magnitude. That drift is the engine behind power iteration, a simple, robust way to find a matrix's dominant eigenvector numerically, and it's also, in disguise, the core mechanism behind web-ranking algorithms that decide which pages matter most from how they link to each other. Not every matrix is so well-behaved: sometimes a matrix has fewer independent eigen-directions than its eigenvalues would suggest, a mismatch that makes it "defective" and means it cannot be broken down into its simplest, diagonal form no matter how you try.

Certain special families of matrices make all of this dramatically easier. For diagonal matrices, and for triangular ones too, the eigenvalues sit right there on the diagonal, visible with no calculation. Symmetric matrices, which mirror themselves across their own diagonal, are especially well-behaved: their eigenvalues are always real, never complex, and eigen-directions belonging to different eigenvalues always come out perpendicular to one another, a preview of the more general spectral theorem waiting further up the tower. Orthogonal matrices, whose own mirror-image transpose is also their inverse, represent pure rotations and reflections, maps that preserve length and angle and never stretch anything.

And LU factorization is nothing more than the bookkeeping of ordinary Gaussian elimination, recorded once and for all as a product of two triangular matrices. Once that factorization is paid for a single time, solving the same system again and again for many different right-hand sides becomes cheap, since each new solve is a quick pass through the already-triangular pieces instead of a fresh elimination from scratch.

**Worked example.** A rubber sheet is stretched by the map that sends the point with coordinates x and y to the point with coordinates 2x plus y and x plus 2y, so its matrix has rows 2, 1 and 1, 2. The determinant is 2 times 2 minus 1 times 1, or 3, so every patch of the sheet ends up with 3 times its original area. For the eigenvalues, subtract a scale factor from both diagonal entries and ask when the determinant vanishes: the square of 2 minus that factor, minus 1, equals 0, which gives factors of 3 and 1. The arrow with components 1 and 1 maps to 3 and 3, stretched threefold; the arrow with components 1 and minus 1 maps to itself, untouched. These two eigen-directions are perpendicular, as a symmetric matrix promises, and 3 times 1 recovers the determinant. Power iteration from the arrow 1 and 0 gives 2 and 1, then 5 and 4, then 14 and 13, then 41 and 40, swinging steadily toward the dominant diagonal direction.
