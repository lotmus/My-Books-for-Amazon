A MURDER THAT HASN'T HAPPENED YET.
A photograph from tomorrow.
And a bureaucratic error that might save his daughter—or doom her.

Derek Gent receives notification of his own death: murdered tomorrow at 11:03 AM in the Relativistic Investigation Bureau's own office. The photograph shows him dead. The filing system confirms it. His daughter Sophie stands frozen in the doorway.

But Sophie is safely at school. The Bureau's clocks show it's still morning. And the photograph was apparently taken from nowhere, by no one, recorded in a case file that shouldn't exist.

The only reasonable explanation: someone has filed an event in the wrong reference frame. A bureaucratic error in spacetime itself.

As Derek, his clever partner Penny, and his anxious colleague Trevor investigate, they discover that time isn't just wrong—it's emergent. Spacetime itself might be nothing more than bookkeeping error in a universe that works differently than anyone imagined. Each clue pulls Derek closer to a terrifying truth: solving the murder might require erasing it. And erasing it might erase Sophie along with it.

A mystery that asks the ultimate question: if you can't trust time itself, can you trust your own existence?

The Relativistic Investigation Bureau Book 1: A mystery for anyone who's ever wondered what happens when physics gets personal.

Lothar J. Musiol writes mysteries where the physics is the puzzle and the impossible is inevitable. Their work sits at the intersection of hard science fiction and detective stories—books for readers who want their murders to require an understanding of relativity to solve.

By day, they work in [relevant field]. By night, they investigate the implications of time travel, quantum mechanics, and what happens when bureaucratic systems encounter relativistic paradoxes.

"The Murder That Hadn't Happened Yet" is Book 1 of the Relativistic Investigation Bureau series. The Bureau operates under one principle: describe the crime precisely enough and you might prevent it. Which is harder than it sounds when time is the weapon.

When not writing, Lothar J. Musiol can be found reading physics papers, arguing about movie time travel, and wondering why nobody else finds filing systems suspicious.
