How to Read This Book
This is a satirical mystery with real relativity underneath. You need no physics to follow the case. The comedy is the bait. The geometry is the trap.
The novel is Case 1047: a murder that has not happened yet, and a Bureau that files first and asks later. Derek Gent investigates a death notice with his name on it, while trying not to make the paperwork worse.
The course lives after the novel as sixteen lessons. Most chapters, and the Epilogue, end the same way: a Rule, and a single link to your lesson. Follow it. The arrow follows the physics the chapter actually used, not the matching integer. Lesson 1 is not Chapter 1's twin by number; it is the object that chapter used. Each lesson should survive the hot-chair test: say the idea plainly to someone who has never heard the jargon — no phone, no notes, no hiding behind Greek letters.
Three honest ways to read this book.
For the story alone: read the chapters and stop at each Rule. You will know what the Bureau filed. You will not know why the filing was possible. That reason is the course.
To take the class: read each chapter, then the lesson it points to. Chapters 1 and 2 share Lesson 1. After that, each chapter points at one lesson, and the lesson points back. A lesson may mention another chapter where the same idea appears. That mention is not a second arrow. Lesson 1 assumes nothing. Lesson 16 assumes you have been paying attention.
If you are impatient: the photograph arrives on the first page of Chapter 1, and you may go straight to it. The class is still the worked solution. Skipping it will not punish the plot. It will punish the algebra.
If you take the class out of order, take Lesson 2 before you argue with Chapter 3, and Lesson 14 before you board the train.
One promise: where Newton cannot host this mystery, the book says so. Absolute 'now' makes Case 1047 gibberish. Relativity makes the misfiling possible — and the fear of it real.
A companion list of films lives on the COURSE page, with a one-line reminder at the end of each lesson. None of them are required.
Three demos, one job each, at https://lotmus.github.io/relativistic-site/ — simultaneity for Lesson 2, curvature for Lesson 5, and expansion so that "the universe stretches" is not filed under murder. The same address is repeated under Further Reading. It is not a fourth demo.
Author's Note
This novel is the first in The Relativistic Investigation Bureau series. Special and General Relativity, as used here, are settled physics. Holography is a live research programme: real in particular theoretical settings, not a proof that our universe is a cinema screen. The crimes and investigations are fictional. The jokes are mine.
If you find yourself questioning whether time is fundamental, whether causality can be violated, or whether a sufficiently incompetent filing system could create paradoxes... congratulations. You're thinking like an investigator.
The Bureau appreciates your attention to detail.

PROLOGUE
In Which the Universe Refuses to Behave
A form can be finished before the event it names. Derek Gent had not learned that yet. He would learn it on a Tuesday, when the form used his name.
The Relativistic Investigation Bureau had a drawer for most things. Murder was drawer one. Pending Geometry was drawer three. In seventeen years nobody had ever filed anything in drawer three, and Mrs Marsh, who kept the keys, regarded that as a sign the system was working.
By the end of the week Derek would know what had happened at eleven minutes past eleven, at four minutes past twelve, and, above all, at three minutes past eleven on a Wednesday. He would know which clocks had disagreed and why none of them had lied. He would not know everything. Nobody ever does. But he would know which drawer it went in.
Rule: A murder that has not happened yet is still a filing. Treat the paperwork as evidence.
Chapter 1
The Surprise in the Morning
At seventeen minutes past nine on a Tuesday morning at the end of October, Derek Gent received an email informing him that he had been murdered.
He had always suspected that death would arrive by email. Nearly everything else did. Electricity bills, invitations to webinars that would change his life, notices that someone had reset a password he had never owned. Death was the logical next step. What surprised him was the sender.
The message had come from the Relativistic Investigation Bureau, which was to say from Derek Gent, which was to say from the computer on the desk in front of him. The Bureau was not supposed to send him emails. He had written that rule himself, which made its violation particularly irritating.
The subject line read CASE UPDATE: DEREK GENT. He opened it.
Dear Mr Gent,
We regret to inform you that you have been
murdered.
The incident will occur tomorrow at 11:03 a.m.
The perpetrator has been identified. It is you.
Kind regards,
Derek Gent
Relativistic Investigation Bureau
He read it twice. The second time he noticed that the word murdered sat on a line of its own, in a typeface a fraction heavier than the rest, as if it had been added afterwards by someone with stronger opinions. He noticed it the way he noticed a picture hanging crooked, and for the same reason did nothing about it yet. There was an attachment. He clicked it.
The photograph showed his office. Not a room like it. This room: the scarred desk, the green filing cabinet with the drawer that never quite shut, the window onto the street, the framed photograph of his grandfather beside a Morris Minor in 1964. On the floor beside the desk lay a man, face down, his right arm folded under his chest and his head turned just far enough towards the camera to be recognised. Across the back of his shirt was a broad dark-red stain.
The man was Derek.
In the doorway behind him, one foot lifted, a school bag hanging from her shoulder, stood a girl of twelve. She had stopped in the middle of a step, the way people stop when they have opened a door onto something they were not supposed to see.
Sophie. His daughter. Who was, at that moment, supposed to be sitting in double maths.
There is a kind of thinking that happens before thinking, and Derek did it now. Whether he died tomorrow was a thing he could be told about. Whether Sophie was safe was a thing he could do something about. He was already dialling.
Mrs Halloran in the school office answered on the second ring. "Mr Gent? Sophie's in double maths. Shall I fetch her?"
"No. Is she there? Can you see her?"
Paper moved at the other end. "Second desk from the window. Looking bored, if that helps."
"It helps," he said. "Please don't tell her I rang." He put the phone down. His hand was not entirely steady, so he laid it flat on the desk until it was.
The phone buzzed again almost at once. A voicemail from Tuppence, who must have rung while he was still talking to the school office. Tuppence had been his wife, was still Sophie's mother, and had never in her life waited for a committee to finish voting. "The school's just rung me, Derek. They always ring me. You asked whether you could see her. Sophie is in double maths. I told them not to fetch her and I'm telling you the same. Do not turn up at the gates looking like a man who has just been murdered. She'll notice." A pause, and then, as if from a file of verdicts kept for reuse: "And don't try explaining it as relativistic. Relativistic, my arse."
He had told the school nothing, and the school had told Tuppence everything, in the two minutes it had taken him to read an email twice. He filed the shock under later. It was one of his more reliable skills and possibly one of his more dangerous ones.
Only then did he look up at the room the photograph had already described. The Bureau was two rooms on the second floor of a tired building near Holborn, plus a stretch of corridor that the landlord called communal and Derek called his whenever he needed to dry an umbrella. The sign on the frosted glass of the outer door said DEREK GENT, RELATIVISTIC INVESTIGATION BUREAU, and beneath that, in smaller letters, CAUSALITY, CHRONOLOGY & OTHER INCONVENIENCES. Beneath that again, in Penny's handwriting on a strip of label tape, it said NO MURDERS BEFORE 10 A.M. WITHOUT AN APPOINTMENT. Derek had objected to the label. Penny had pointed out that they had never once received a case before ten. Derek had said that this was beside the point, and Penny had said that it was the point, and the label had stayed.
It was now nine twenty. The label was still right, technically. The murder was booked for tomorrow.
Derek was thirty-eight, greying at the temples in a way he found more alarming than the email, and built like a man who had once been reasonably athletic and had since made his peace with filing cabinets. He had a habit, under stress, of straightening things that were already straight. He straightened the stapler now. Then he got up to straighten his grandfather, whose frame had had no glass in it since last Thursday, when Trevor's elbow had found it, and the movement sent the familiar hot wire down the left side of his back, and he stood still with one hand on the desk until it passed.
"Is that the back?" said Trevor from across the room.
"It's the back."
"You missed your floor."
"I did my floor at home. Tomorrow is physio." Wednesday mornings at ten, a woman in Lamb's Conduit Street dug her thumbs into him for forty minutes and sent him back with instructions to lie flat on the floor for twenty more, face down, with the heat on. The heat was a wheat bag in a red tartan cover that lived in his bottom drawer and smelled, when you microwaved it, like a bakery having a bad day. He did the floor in the office because the office had a floor and an empty hour. It was not a secret. It was not something he advertised.
Trevor Boltzman was twenty-eight and tall enough that the doorframe had views on him. He had built most of what ran on the Bureau's computers, including the case system, which he had named CASEBOOK in a moment of weakness and had never been allowed to rename. On his desk sat a glass Klein bottle from a mathematics conference, never quite forgiven for its inability to hold tea. He had taken his glasses off, which meant nothing, since he did it for insight and for despair with exactly the same face.
"Why are you looking at your computer like that?" he said.
"I've been murdered."
Trevor put his glasses back on. "When?"
"Tomorrow. Eleven oh three."
Trevor knew that the first reasonable sentence would be the wrong one, and took his time finding the second. "Is that a joke, or a case?"
"I'll let you know. Come and look."
Trevor came and looked. He was silent for some time, which in Trevor was a mark of respect. Then he said, "Tea," because it was the only form of action available to him, and went to put the kettle on. "Milk?"
"No. One sugar." And then, as Trevor reached the door, because a man who may be dead by lunchtime tomorrow is entitled to something: "Two."
The outer door opened before the kettle had boiled. Penny Gent came in the way she always came in, without knocking, already finished: coat, hair, bag, all of it arranged as if a committee had sat on it overnight and lost. She was Derek's cousin and the Bureau's other half, and she had the disconcerting ability to look as though she had somewhere more interesting to be even when she had just arrived. She put her bag on her chair, looked at Derek, and saw something.
"What's happened?"
"I've been murdered."
"Already?"
"Tomorrow."
"Who by?"
"Me."
"That's a relief," she said. "I thought for a moment it might be someone competent." She came round the desk. He turned the screen towards her. She read the email once and looked at the photograph for longer, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quick and flat, which was the voice she used when she had stopped finding things funny.
"That's Sophie."
"She's in double maths. I rang. So did Tuppence, apparently, before I'd even had the email."
"Then we aren't debating a photograph," Penny said. "We're finding out what it is. Has anyone rung the police?"
"And told them what? That a man who is standing in this office complaining about his back will be murdered tomorrow by himself, and here is a picture?" Derek heard how it sounded and did not enjoy it. "There is no crime yet. There is a document. Until it becomes a body in a place, it's ours, because nobody else has a drawer for it."
"Mrs Marsh has a drawer for everything."
"Mrs Marsh," said Derek, "has a drawer for nothing that isn't in her building."
Trevor came back with the tea. He had made three mugs, which Derek noted as an improvement on some mornings, and he handed them round and then stood behind them with his own, looking at the screen over their heads as if height might help.
"There's a clock," he said. "On the desk. In the picture."
There was. On the desk in the photograph, beside the computer, stood a small brass railway clock with a plain white face and black hands, the kind that hangs at the end of a platform and tells everyone they have missed something. It read three minutes past eleven. When Derek enlarged it, the second hand stood just past seventeen.
"Do you recognise it?" said Penny.
"No."
"Neither do I. That isn't ours."
"No."
Derek looked at the real desk. There was no brass clock on it. There was the computer, the stapler, a tray of letters he had been meaning to answer since September, and the mug Trevor had just put down. He looked at the clock on the wall, which said 9:24, and at his phone, which agreed.
"So the photograph was taken at three minutes past eleven," Trevor said, "and it's now twenty-four minutes past nine, so the photograph was taken—"
"Don't," said Penny.
"I was only going to say later."
"The clock in the photograph says three minutes past eleven," said Derek. "That's all it says. A clock in a picture is not a timestamp. It's a clock in a picture. Before we call anything wrong we need to know what kind of number we're looking at. Sometimes a clock tells you the time. Sometimes it's just showing you a label."
"And the email says tomorrow," said Penny.
"The email says a great many things." Derek enlarged the lower-right corner of the photograph, where something pale lay on the carpet just inside the door. It was a sheet of paper torn from an exercise book, the kind with a red margin ruled down the left. On it, in blue ink, someone had written three times, one under another.
11:03
11:17
12:04
And beneath them, underlined twice: DO NOT TRUST THE CLOCKS.
Penny took a pencil and wrote beside the printout Derek had just made, because she could not bear a list without a reason attached to it. 11:03, if this is a death. 11:17, if this is a departure. 12:04, if this is an arrival. She looked at what she had written and did not cross it out.
"You're guessing," Derek said.
"I'm labelling," said Penny. "You said yourself that's what clocks do."
Rule: A clock can lie by telling the wrong kind of truth.

Chapter 2
Case 1047
The email had a case number in its footer, which emails from the Bureau were not supposed to have, because emails from the Bureau were not supposed to exist. Derek opened CASEBOOK on his own screen and typed it in. 1047.
The record was there. It had been created at 09:17:02 that morning. It was short.
CASE 1047
CATEGORY: MURDER
VICTIM: DEREK GENT
DATE: WEDNESDAY
TIME: 11:03:17
PERPETRATOR:
WITNESS:
STATUS: NOT YET OCCURRED
Underneath, in the box marked ORIGINATING ADDRESS, it said LOCALHOST.
"That's this machine," said Trevor, who had come to stand behind him again with his tea. "Localhost is what a computer calls itself. It means nobody sent it from outside. Either somebody sat here and made it, or the system made it on its own."
"Did you make it?"
"No."
"Did I?"
"You were on the stairs at nine seventeen. I heard you. You make a noise on the fourth step like a man being asked for his date of birth." Trevor leaned in. "Can I log in properly and look at the audit trail? You're in through the email link. It only shows you the front."
Derek moved over. Trevor sat, typed his password, and opened the record from his own account. The audit trail was a column of lines in small grey type. 09:17:02 record created (sweep). 09:17:04 notification sent: client. Nothing else. Trevor frowned at it. Somewhere behind him, the computer made the small pleased chime it made when a record changed.
PERPETRATOR: TREVOR BOLTZMAN.
Trevor stood up so fast that his chair went over.
"I didn't type that."
"I know," said Derek.
"How do you know?"
"Because I was watching your hands. You opened the file. That's all you did. And because you'd have spelled your own name with one N and corrected it." He righted the chair. It was a small act, and he was aware of making it slowly, the way you move near an animal you do not want to startle. "Sit down, Trevor. Nobody here thinks you're going to murder me with a mug."
"The system does."
"The system thinks a great many things. Ten minutes ago it thought I was going to do it myself."
Penny had not moved from the window. "What does sweep mean?"
"It's a maintenance job," said Trevor. "Every Tuesday it goes through the folders that nobody's looked at and files whatever's in them. Scans, voicemails, the camera. It runs at seventeen minutes past nine because that's when I used to get in, and I wanted it done before I had to look at it." He brought up the sweep's own log. Tuesday 09:17. Items reviewed: 312. Items archived: 0. "Nothing. It didn't archive anything. It never does. Nobody leaves anything for it."
"It made a case," said Penny.
"Making a case isn't archiving," said Trevor, and then stopped, because he had heard himself.
Penny came and logged in from her own desk to see the record for herself, which she always did, on the principle that a thing she had not read with her own eyes had not been read. The chime came again. WITNESS: PENNY GENT. She looked at it without expression and said, "Well, I'd have preferred to be asked."
The office had a camera. It sat on the bookshelf by the window, a small white cube that Trevor had bought after a burglary three years ago, which had taken the old kettle and left the computer, a choice everyone had agreed was a fair comment on the computer. Derek looked at it, and then at the photograph, and then at the camera again.
"That's its angle," he said.
Trevor saw it too. The photograph had been taken from the height of the bookshelf, from the left of the window, looking across the desk to the door. The cube looked back at them with its one dark eye, entirely innocent, the way furniture does.
"It can't have taken a picture of tomorrow," Trevor said.
"No."
"So somebody stood exactly where it stands and held a camera at exactly its height."
"Or it took a picture," said Penny, "and the picture isn't of tomorrow."
"The email says tomorrow."
"The email," said Penny, "also says Derek did it."
Trevor opened the camera's storage. It kept a week of motion clips and then wrote over them, and it kept a separate folder for stills that someone had taken by hand from the app, which was empty. That was all. There was nothing in it about Wednesday at three minutes past eleven, and nothing could have been, and he said so with relief. At least one thing was still impossible.
There was more in the record when you scrolled. Below the fields was a box headed CASE NOTES, and in it was a scan of a single yellow sticky note, in handwriting Derek knew better than any other because it was his own.
DO NOT LET TREVOR NEAR THE STRANGE CLOCK.
"I didn't write that," said Derek.
"It's your hand," said Penny.
"It's my hand. I didn't write it." He enlarged it. The capitals had his slope and the T had his crossbar, the one that started too far to the left. There was a pinhole in the top corner of the note, as if it had once been pinned to something. "I've never seen that clock in my life. I'd remember writing a note about a clock I'd never seen."
"Would you?" said Penny, not unkindly.
"I'd remember writing a note telling Trevor to keep away from it."
"That's true," said Trevor. "You'd say it out loud. Several times."
Below the notes there was one more field, which they had not looked at because it was the kind of field nobody looked at. CONSULTANT: PHYSICS. The system had filled it from the Bureau's roster with the only name on that line: ALFRED WEINSTEIN. Beside it a small icon showed that a consultant brief had been sent, with attachments, at 09:17:05.
"It's emailed Alfred," said Derek.
"It's emailed Alfred a picture of you dead," said Penny. "Good. That'll get him out of bed."
The roster had one other line under CONSULTANT, and Penny looked at it and then at Derek, and Derek said, "No."
"I didn't say anything."
"You looked at the line."
The line said MARINE: SHERLOCK. Sherlock was a bottlenose dolphin. He had consulted for the Bureau twice, by methods nobody at the Bureau had ever been able to explain, and both times he had been right, and both times he had been no use whatever until after the fact. He was paid in mackerel. Accounts coded the receipts as Miscellaneous Marine and had stopped asking. Penny was the only person who had ever spoken to him, if that was the word, and she did not like to talk about it.
"We are not," said Derek, "ringing the dolphin about my murder."
There was a knock at the outer door, which meant Mrs Marsh, because nobody else in the building knocked and Mrs Marsh never came in. She was the building's superintendent, a broad woman with a grey perm and a clipboard, built along the lines of a filing cabinet that had decided to take up a hobby, and she had a rule that she did not cross the threshold of a tenant's office, on the grounds that once you crossed a threshold you became responsible for whatever was on the other side of it. She stood on the corridor side of the sill and held something out at arm's length.
It was a small brass railway clock with a plain white face.
"This was left with me," said Mrs Marsh. "For Mr Boltzman. It has been in my drawer three since last week. I have been waiting for Mr Boltzman to collect it, and Mr Boltzman has not collected it, so I am delivering it, which is not my job."
Nobody moved. Then Derek crossed the room and took it from her. It was heavier than it looked and warm from her hands. It was ticking, a small dry tick, perfectly ordinary. Its hands said 9:41, which was what the wall said.
"Who left it?" he said.
"I gave my word not to say."
"Mrs Marsh, I've been sent a photograph of myself dead on that floor, with this clock on that desk."
Mrs Marsh looked past him, at the floor, as if checking it for marks. "Then I'm sorry for it, Mr Gent. But I gave my word to a client, and a promise to a client is a promise. When I'm released from it, I'll tell you. Not before." She made a note on her clipboard. "And if that is a murder, it is drawer one, and it is not registered to this address. Good morning."
She went downstairs. The stairs reported her going, step by step, with no hurry at all.
On the back of the clock a small oval plate had been riveted to the brass. It said LGV in raised letters, and under that a string of numbers worn too smooth to read.
"Large Goods Vehicle," Mrs Marsh called up the stairwell, without breaking stride, as if she had heard them turn it over. "My nephew has one. He drives a lorry. There's nothing mysterious about it."
Trevor held out his hand for the clock, the way a man holds out his hand for a screwdriver. Derek looked at the hand, and at the screen, where his own handwriting told him not to let Trevor near the strange clock, and he put the clock on top of the filing cabinet, out of reach.
"I just want to see how it works," said Trevor.
"I know," said Derek. "That's what worries me."
Rule: When a file fills its own blanks, ask what it filled them with.

Interlude
Sophie
Sophie Gent was small for twelve and treated this as a technicality rather than a fact, the way she treated most inconvenient data. She wore her dark hair scraped back hard enough to argue with, on the theory that anything loose was a distraction, and she had her father's habit of going very still when she was thinking, which teachers mistook for daydreaming and which her father would have recognised at once.
She kept a notebook about clocks that lied. It had started as a science homework on measurement and had simply not stopped. The kitchen oven was fourteen minutes fast and had been since the power cut in June. Mum's car still said French time, an hour ahead, because Mum had changed it on the ferry in August and said she would change it back when she had a minute, and Mum had not had a minute since 2019. The clock on the landing lost two minutes a week. Her phone, the school bell and the pips on the radio agreed with each other, and she had put them in a column headed PROBABLY TRUE. Everything else went in a column headed DO NOT TRUST.
The best clock in the notebook was not in the house at the moment. She had bought it at the car-boot sale on a Sunday in September for four pounds, a brass railway clock with a plate on the back, and it kept better time than anything she owned. That was what was interesting about it. A clock that kept good time ought to be boring. This one was not boring, because it ought not to have been that good, and she had wanted somebody who knew about clocks to tell her why without taking it to pieces. That was the whole of what she had wanted. She did not think about where the clock was now. She had decided not to think about it on purpose, which she knew was not the same as not thinking about it.
There was a car outside school this afternoon. It had been parked at the end of the road at lunch, engine off, a man inside who did not get out, not close enough to the gates to be picking anyone up and not far enough away to be anything else. When she came out at twenty to four it was still there. She made herself walk past it slowly, the way you are supposed to test ice, and the man inside never once looked at his phone. That was the detail that frightened her. Everyone looked at their phone. A man who did not was a man doing something else with his eyes, and the something else was her.
She walked home the long way, past the library, and it did not follow her, and that was almost worse.
Dad had texted at lunch. Stay at school today, sweetheart. Don't walk anywhere on your own. I'll explain. He never wrote sweetheart. He wrote things like ok and see you Sat and once, memorably, the oven is lying to you. She had read sweetheart four times and felt the floor of the day tilt slightly, the way the lift in the shopping centre did when it stopped between floors.
She thought about telling him about the car. She thought about telling him about the other thing, the stupid thing she had done last week, the one nobody knew about. She decided against both, on the grounds that he was clearly already frightened of something, and one Gent's quota of quiet dread seemed like enough for one Tuesday. Instead she wrote the car's number plate on the inside back cover of the notebook, under the clocks, beneath a heading she gave more thought to than she would ever have admitted: PROBABLY NOTHING.
She knew even as she wrote it that it was not a proper scientific category. She kept it anyway. Some data you collect before you know which drawer it belongs in.
At home there were boxes in the hall.
They had been arriving all week, one or two at a time, in the back of Julian's car, and Julian carried them in with the apologetic care of a man bringing furniture into a church. They were labelled in his small, square handwriting, which was the handwriting of someone who wrote on whiteboards for a living and had learned to make every letter readable from the back row: BOOKS (GEOG), BOOKS (OTHER), KITCHEN, MAPS — FRAGILE. One long flat box said HANGERS, and rattled when he put it down, and when Sophie asked what kind of person owned a whole box of hangers he had looked genuinely surprised that anyone might not.
He was moving in on Sunday. Mum had told her on Tuesday night, the Tuesday before last, at the kitchen table, in the voice she used for decisions that had been made somewhere else and were now being announced to the membership. Sunday the twenty-fifth. Two in the afternoon. "It's the weekend the clocks go back," Mum had said, "so he gets an extra hour to unpack," and had smiled as though that were a joke, and Sophie had understood that it was the kind of joke grown-ups make when they are hoping you will laugh so that they do not have to find out what you actually think.
She had not laughed. She had said, "Okay." Then she had gone upstairs and written it in the notebook, because that was where things went when she did not know where else to put them. SUNDAY 25 OCTOBER, 02:00. ALL CLOCKS GO BACK ONE HOUR. She had looked at that until it stopped being words. Then, underneath, smaller: J. MOVES IN, 14:00 (NEW TIME).
Julian was fine. That was the problem. He was quiet and kind and he taught geography at a school in Hackney and wore cardigans as if it were a political position, and he never once pretended to be her father, and he never asked to see the notebook. If he had been awful she could have been angry, and anger was at least a thing you could write down. Fine was harder. Fine went in no column at all.
Dad did not know yet. Mum was going to tell him at the weekend. Sophie knew this because Mum had said so on the phone to Auntie Ros, in the garden, in the voice she thought did not carry. At the weekend, Ros. At a table. He'll be very reasonable about it, and then he'll go home and lie on the floor.
She had done the stupid thing the morning after Mum told her. The Wednesday. She did not think the two facts were connected. She had written them on different pages, to make sure.
Chapter 3
The Lorentz Transform
By ten o'clock the whiteboard said EVENT, FRAME, TIME, in that order, in Derek's capitals, and Trevor had been forbidden to write anything under them until he had a number.
"Here's the only respectable way a photograph of tomorrow could be honest," Derek said. "Relativity lets two observers disagree about when things happen. If you're moving fast enough relative to me, my now and your now come apart. So: suppose the picture is a real picture of a real event, and the only problem is that the camera and this office don't share a now. Then there is some relative velocity that turns their eleven oh three tomorrow into our nine twenty today. Find it."
"That's the Lorentz transformation," said Trevor, as if naming a friend.
"I know what it's called. I want to know what it says."
Trevor wrote it up, because he could not help himself, and Derek let him, because it was short.
t' = γ (t − vx/c²)
"The time you assign to an event depends on the time I assign, the place I assign, and how fast you're going," Trevor said. "The vx over c squared is the bit that does the damage. It's the bit that says two people in different states of motion can't agree what's happening right now somewhere far away. But it only does real damage if x is big or v is enormous. For the photo and this office, x is a few metres. It's the same room."
"So how fast would the camera have to go?"
Trevor did the sum on his phone and then on the board, because the phone had seemed too pleased with itself. To shift an event in this room by a day and three quarters, at a distance of a few metres, the camera would have to be moving at trillions of times the speed of light.
"That isn't a velocity," he said. "That's a confession."
"Whose?"
"Ours. If the algebra needs something faster than light, the premise is wrong, not the universe. We assumed one event seen from two frames. There's no frame that does that. Not for a room."
"What about the light?" said Penny. "Light takes time to arrive. The picture's of light that left the room."
"It left the room and went about two metres into a camera. That's a few billionths of a second. You can't buy a day with that unless the camera was leaving the solar system in a huff."
"Then the picture isn't of tomorrow seen from somewhere strange," said Derek. "Good. One explanation dead. Write it down."
Penny wrote it on the board, under FRAME, in her own small hand: Not one event in two frames. Relativity refuses. Then, under TIME, she wrote: So: a forgery, a prophecy, or not tomorrow.
"It isn't a forgery," said Trevor. "Or if it is, it's a very good one. The light's right. Look at the shadows." The photograph showed a bright morning. A slab of low sunlight came through the window from the left and lay across the desk and up the side of the filing cabinet, and the shadow of the brass clock fell long and sharp towards the door. "I'd have to check, but that's what the sun does in here about eleven at this time of year. Faking that properly is work."
"Prophecy," said Penny, and made a face as if the word tasted of something.
"Or not tomorrow," said Derek.
"The email says tomorrow."
"The email is a sentence. Sentences are cheap."
The phone on his desk rang. It was a Munich number, and Derek put it on speaker, and a dry voice with a faint Bavarian clip said, "Good morning. This is Herbert Matkowski. I am ringing on behalf of Alfred, who is too excited to use a telephone."
Herbert Matkowski had once been Alfred Weinstein's mathematics professor. He mentioned it rarely and enjoyed it every time. He held the frequently reconfirmed opinion that his former student was very clever and very lazy, and that grading his coursework had been the first reliable evidence for both. Where Weinstein worked in leaps, Matkowski worked in steps, and he regarded the steps as the honest method.
"Alfred has received an email from you," said Matkowski. "It contains a photograph of you dead. He says it was very thoughtful of you to include him."
"I didn't send it."
"He assumed not. You never send him anything interesting." There was a scuffle at the far end, and a second voice took over, older-sounding than its years, unhurried and slightly accented, with a habit of dropping to a whisper exactly when a thing interested it.
"Derek. It is Alfred. I have looked at your photograph for one hour. You are not dead in it."
"That's reassuring."
"I do not mean you are alive. I mean the photograph does not say you are dead. It says a man is lying on a floor with something red on his shirt. The file says murder. The file is not the photograph. Do not let the file do your thinking."
"The file's done quite a lot of thinking already," Derek said. "It thinks Trevor did it."
"Then the file is a fool, and you should treat it as a witness who is a fool, which is to say carefully." Weinstein paused. "You have tried the Lorentz transformation."
"It wants something faster than light."
"Of course it does. You asked it a stupid question and it gave you a stupid answer, politely. That is what a good equation does. Now. I have one practical suggestion and one scientific one. The practical suggestion is this: if a photograph says you will die in that room at three minutes past eleven tomorrow, do not be in that room at three minutes past eleven tomorrow. Come to Munich. It is ninety minutes. Nobody in Munich has ever been murdered before lunch; it would be considered rude."
Penny was already nodding. Derek was not.
"Sophie is in the photograph."
"Yes. Behind you. And if you are not on the floor, there is nothing for her to stand behind. The picture needs you. Take yourself out of it. The scientific suggestion is that you bring the clock."
"How do you know about the clock?"
"It is in the photograph, Derek. Please. I am a patent attorney. I read drawings for a living. There is a brass railway clock on the desk, and you have a voice this morning like a man who has one in the room."
"I do, as it happens. The building superintendent brought it up ten minutes ago. Someone left it for Trevor last week."
There was a silence on the line of the kind Derek had learned, over years of these calls, to leave alone.
"Last week," said Weinstein.
"Last week."
"Then bring it, and bring the man who can't be trusted near it, and bring Penny, who can be trusted near anything. I will meet you at the airport. And Derek, one more thing. Julius Barbarian is in Munich this week. If you are going to have a crisis about time, you may as well have it in front of the man who says it doesn't exist."
He rang off. Matkowski's voice came back for a moment, faintly, from further off. "He means well. He is very bored. Bring an umbrella. Your forecast for tomorrow is rain."
After the call Derek stood at the window. The street below was grey and wet, and a bus pulled away from the stop with a noise suggesting that several of its components had voted against it.
"He's right," Penny said. "You can't stop a photograph. You can refuse to pose for it."
"I'm not leaving Sophie in London with a picture of herself in a murder."
"You're not leaving her. You're leaving the room she's standing in." Penny's voice softened a fraction, which was as far as it went. "I'll ring Tuppence. I'll tell her nothing that will frighten her and enough that she keeps Sophie close. And I'll ring Greaves."
Kenneth Greaves had been a detective constable for twenty-six years and was now, by his own description, a man who sat in cars. The Bureau paid him by the day to sit in one outside places. Derek thought about a man in a car outside his daughter's school, and about what that would look like to a twelve-year-old who noticed everything, and said yes anyway, because the alternative was worse and he could not think of a third thing.
"Don't tell Sophie," he said.
"I wasn't going to tell Sophie."
"And don't tell Tuppence about Greaves. She'll think I'm being dramatic."
"You've been murdered," Penny said. "You're allowed to be a bit dramatic."
He rang Tuppence from the stairwell, where nobody could hear him except Mrs Marsh, who could hear everything and repeated nothing.
"I'm in a meeting," said Tuppence, which meant she had stepped out of one.
"I won't keep you. I've had something at work. A case. It's got Sophie's name near it. I don't think she's in any danger. I'd like you to keep her close for a couple of days anyway."
There was the silence of a woman deciding which of several questions to ask first. "Near it how?"
"In a photograph. It's not real. I mean it's a real photograph, but it's not of anything real. I'm not explaining this well."
"No."
"I'm going to Munich for the night. Alfred Weinstein thinks I should be out of London tomorrow morning."
"Why?"
He considered lying and found he did not have the energy. "Because the photograph says something happens in my office tomorrow at three minutes past eleven, and he thinks it would be sensible if I wasn't in it."
"Something."
"Something bad. To me. Not to her."
Another silence. When she spoke again the meeting voice had gone. "Derek, are you in trouble?"
"I don't know yet. I'll know by the weekend."
"Then you'll tell me at the weekend," said Tuppence, "in words, at a table, and until then I'm not going to frighten my daughter because of a photograph you can't explain. She goes to school. She comes home. She does her homework. That's what close means in this house." A pause. "And Derek. Don't send her texts that sound like a man who's been murdered."
"I won't."
He had already sent one. He did not tell her that either.
The phone rang again before he had reached the bottom of the stairs. Tuppence never rang back. She regarded ringing back as an admission that the first call had been badly chaired.
"One more thing," she said, without hello. "It was going to be this weekend anyway, so don't read anything into it. When you come back we need to talk about Sunday."
"What's Sunday?"
"At the weekend, Derek," said Tuppence. "At a table." And she was gone again, and he stood on the half-landing with the phone in his hand and the distinct feeling that a second case had just been opened in his name, by someone with far better filing than CASEBOOK.
Trevor booked three seats on the half past one from Heathrow without being asked, which was his way of apologising for something that was not his fault. Derek put the brass clock in a padded envelope and the envelope in his bag, and the clock ticked inside it all the way to the door like something that wanted to be let out.
He locked the door. He tried the handle twice, which he never did, and then a third time, which he was ashamed of, and went down the stairs, and on the fourth step the step made its noise.

Chapter 4
The Weight of Time
At a quarter to twelve, Munich time, Alfred Weinstein was sitting in a patent attorney's office in Schwabing, being bored on purpose.
He was in his early fifties, compact and faintly stooped from a lifetime of leaning over desks that were never the right height, and his hair had gone grey-white early and stayed undisciplined since. He wore the same jacket in every weather, on the stated grounds that changing it more than once a season introduced an unnecessary variable. At nineteen he had wanted to be a physicist. At twenty-three Herbert Matkowski had told him, kindly, that he would make a superb one if he ever finished anything, and Weinstein had become instead a patent attorney, which was a profession that paid you to read other people's unfinished things. He was, by common consent, the only patent attorney in Bavaria who could read a spacetime metric, and the Bureau in London kept him on its books as its consulting physicist, which pleased him more than he would admit.
The firm's senior partner was explaining an application for an improved mechanical regulator. Weinstein had followed the first two minutes. The third had been optimistic. The fourth had involved a spring. The fifth had involved the spring behaving in a way Weinstein considered personally insulting.
"It won't do that," he said.
The senior partner stopped. "Herr Weinstein?"
"You've assumed the spring is the same spring in January as it is in July. It isn't. Its stiffness changes with temperature, and your regulator will gain in winter and lose in summer, and the examiner in Den Haag will say so in the first paragraph, and he will enjoy it." He turned the drawing round and marked the coefficient with his pencil. "Ask the inventor which alloy. If he says ordinary steel, ask him whether he has ever owned a watch."
The senior partner looked at the drawing, and then at Weinstein, with the face of a man deciding whether to be grateful. He chose gratitude, narrowly, and went away to telephone the inventor, and Weinstein was alone with the photograph again.
He had printed it at nine thirty, when it arrived, and looked at it ever since in the intervals of other people's springs. He was not a detective. He did not know about blood. He knew about pictures of things, which in patent work were the only things you were ever allowed to look at, and the first rule of a picture of a thing was that it showed you exactly what was there and nothing else. A man lying on a floor. A red stain. A girl in a door. A clock on a desk. Light from a window.
The light interested him most, because light was the only witness in the picture that could not have been told what to say.
It came in low from the left. The shadow of the clock was long. That was a late-autumn sun or a winter one, in the morning, and a London morning in October would put the sun about there at around eleven by the clocks, give or take. Fine. The picture was honest about the season. What it was honest about beyond that he did not yet know. He looked at the framed photograph on the wall of the London office, the old man and the old car, and saw, in the glass over them, a small bright rectangle: the window, reflected. Someone kept that glass clean. He noted it, as he noted everything, and moved on, because he had not been asked about the frame.
He looked up from the print at the clocks in his own office, because a man who has just been sent a picture of a clock cannot help it.
There were three. The wall clock was radio-controlled and took its time every night from the long-wave transmitter at Mainflingen, near Frankfurt, which was in turn steered by the atomic clocks in Braunschweig. His wristwatch was mechanical and gained six seconds a day, which he forgave because it had been his father's. The clock on his secretary's desk had been set by hand by his secretary, who had views about punctuality and none about accuracy. They said, respectively, 11:52:40, 11:52:58 and 11:49.
Three honest clocks, three different times. Not one of them was lying. Each was reporting a fact about itself: where it took its orders from, how its spring had been treated, who had set it and how carefully. That was the first thing you had to understand about clocks, and almost nobody did.
The second thing was stranger, and it was true.
If he took the radio clock off the wall and put it on the floor, it would run slow compared with a twin left on the wall. Not because the floor was cold or the battery was tired, but because it was a metre deeper in the Earth's gravity, and time itself, measured by any clock at all, runs more slowly deeper in a gravitational field. Einstein had predicted it. Pound and Rebka had measured it in 1960 by firing gamma rays up a tower at Harvard. In 2010 a group in Boulder had seen it with two optical clocks a third of a metre apart in height. One metre of height was worth about one part in ten thousand million million: about a quarter of a millionth of a second over a human lifetime. The effect was real, and it was useless for murder. No difference in height on any floor in London could put a day and three quarters between a photograph and a room.
Height is cheap, he thought. Disagreement is not. If honest clocks disagree by a day, you are not looking at physics. You are looking at somebody's paperwork.
He wrote that in the margin of the regulator application, which he would have to erase later, and then wrote underneath it: The file is a clock too. Who set it?
The senior partner came back. The inventor had said ordinary steel. Weinstein nodded, as a doctor nods at a symptom he expected, and stood up and put on the jacket he had not taken off.
"Are you leaving?"
"A friend of mine has been murdered in London tomorrow," said Weinstein. "I am meeting him at the airport."
The senior partner had worked with Weinstein for eleven years and had learned that the quickest way out of such sentences was to accept them. "Will you be back?"
"On Thursday, probably. Or Monday. It depends how long it takes him to stop being dead."
In the corridor Herbert Matkowski was waiting with his coat over his arm and an expression of mild, practised disapproval. He was past eighty and walked with a stick he did not need, which he used chiefly to point at things.
"You looked at the light," Matkowski said.
"Of course I looked at the light."
"And what does it say?"
"It says the sun was shining in London at about eleven in the morning, in autumn, on a day when somebody photographed Derek on a floor."
"And the forecast for tomorrow in London is rain until evening." Matkowski pointed the stick at the window, which was full of Bavarian sunshine and meant nothing. "Steady rain, ninety per cent, all day. I looked."
"Forecasts are wrong."
"Forecasts are wrong by an hour. Not by the sun." Matkowski began walking towards the stairs at his own pace, which was the only pace he had ever had. "You will want to say the photograph is of tomorrow, because the email says tomorrow and it would be exciting. You will want to say it is a forgery, because then it would be somebody's fault. Before you want either, Alfred, ask the picture what day it is. It will not tell you. But it may tell you what day it isn't."
Weinstein followed him down. He did not argue. He had been graded by this man in public since he was nineteen, and the man had been right about the pencil every time.
Rule: Height is cheap. A day's disagreement between honest clocks is paperwork, not gravity.

Chapter 5
The Man Who Had Mislaid Time
Julius Barbarian lived in a house in Bogenhausen that looked as if it had been designed by someone who had once been told what a house was and had decided to improvise. There was no sign on the gate. Beside the front door there was a small brass plaque that said, in German and then in English, PLEASE DO NOT ASK WHAT TIME IT IS.
"He's going to be one of those people," said Derek.
"He is the original one of those people," said Weinstein, who had met them at the airport in the jacket and had talked about springs all the way from the S-Bahn. "Everyone else is a copy."
Barbarian opened the door before they rang. He was in his late sixties, slim and silver-haired, in an open-necked shirt and good trousers with a small orange stain on one knee. He had written a book, very respectable and very widely misunderstood, arguing that time does not exist: that the universe is not a film running from frame to frame but a vast collection of frames, each complete in itself, with no projector. Derek had read the first half on the plane. He reflected, with an irritation he recognised as unworthy, that he already had one Julian in his life, Tuppence's, and that a Julian and a Julius in the same week was pushing coincidence past its structural limits.
"You have brought me a dead man and a clock," said Barbarian, shaking Derek's hand. "Come in. I collect both."
The hall was lined with books on physics, philosophy, gardening and mathematics, shelved by a principle that only Barbarian understood. One shelf held nothing but clocks: pocket watches, a carriage clock, a cheap digital alarm, a sundial that had come indoors, an egg timer. None of them was going. Each had been stopped, deliberately, at a different time.
"I keep the moments," said Barbarian, seeing Derek look. "Not the time. Each of these is a moment that mattered to somebody. That one is when my mother died. That one is when I finished the book. The egg timer is when I learned I could not boil an egg." He led them into a sitting room with a large wooden table and five chairs. "Sit. Show me the dead man."
Derek put the printout on the table. Trevor put the brass clock beside it, still in its envelope, and Barbarian took it out and turned it over and read the plate and put it face up next to the photograph of itself, so that the clock and the picture of the clock lay side by side, ticking and not ticking.
"Good," said Barbarian. "Now there are two of them. Tell me which one is now."
"The one that's ticking," said Trevor.
"The ticking one is a mechanism. The other is a picture. Both are here, on my table, at once. Neither is earlier. Neither is later. What makes you call one of them a record of the past?"
"The picture was taken before."
"Before what? You have not seen it taken. You have seen it on a table." He picked an apple out of a bowl and put it down on the table, then moved it six inches to the left. "Here is an apple. Here is the apple again, somewhere else. Do I need time to describe that? Or do I need only two arrangements of the world and a relationship between them? A physicist will tell you the apple moved. I tell you there are two arrangements, and the second contains, in a sense, a memory of the first. Its bruise. Your memory of where it was. The ring it left on the wood. The arrangements that contain such records are the ones we call later. I call them time capsules."
"That's very beautiful," said Penny, "and my cousin's daughter is in that photograph."
"Yes." Barbarian looked at her properly for the first time, and his voice changed, not much. "That is why I am being so tiresome. Forgive me. You have come to me with a photograph and an email. The email has told you what the photograph means. I am asking you to put the email in a drawer for one hour and ask the photograph what it contains. Not what it predicts. What it contains. A picture is a time capsule. It is full of records of other things. Which of them are records you can check?"
Weinstein, who had been standing at the bookshelf with his coat on reading spines, turned round. "The shadow," he said. "It records the sun."
"The paper on the floor," said Penny. "It records whoever wrote on it."
"The stain," said Trevor. "It records whatever made it." He swallowed. "If it's blood, it records—"
"It records something red," said Barbarian. "You do not know it is blood. You know it is red and it is on a shirt."
"The clock," said Derek. "It records three minutes past eleven."
"Does it?" Barbarian touched the real clock with one finger. "This clock records what its owner set it to and how well it has been looked after since. Anything more you must earn."
Weinstein had come to the table. He did not sit. "Julius is wrong about time," he said to Derek, pleasantly, as if Barbarian were not there. "General relativity has no single universal time, that much is right, and no clock above the universe ticking for everybody. But every clock that exists measures something real: its own proper time, along its own path. You cannot vote proper time out of existence. You can only stop confusing it with the labels people write on things."
"I am not voting anything out of existence," said Barbarian. "I am asking you what a label is."
"A label is what's on the email," said Derek, before either of them could start again. "Tomorrow. The photograph doesn't say tomorrow anywhere. The clock in it says eleven oh three. The email says tomorrow."
"And which of them," said Barbarian, "is the time capsule, and which is the gossip?"
Nobody answered. Outside, the light was going. Barbarian took them through the back door into the garden, which was larger than the house had any right to support: a lawn, a pond, a stone path, and along the path a series of sundials, each a little different, each set for a slightly different longitude, so that at any moment of sunshine they would all have disagreed. Now, at dusk, none of them said anything. A heron stood at the edge of the pond, so still that for a moment Derek thought it was another of Barbarian's objects. Then it moved one foot.
"Everything here is a now," said Barbarian. "Every arrangement is complete. The heron does not need the next one to be a heron." He looked at Derek in the half-dark. "Your photograph is complete too, Mr Gent. It is not waiting for tomorrow to happen to it. It has already happened, because it is a photograph. The only question is when, and that question, I promise you, is in the picture, not in the email."
They walked back to the S-Bahn in the cold. Penny was quiet. Trevor carried the clock. Weinstein talked about apples, which nobody minded, because it was easier than talking about anything else.
"Eleven oh three in London," said Trevor on the platform, "is twelve oh three here."
"I know," said Derek.
"I'm only saying. If we stay here till one o'clock tomorrow, Munich time, it'll be over. Whatever it is."
"Whatever it is," said Derek, "it's in London, with Sophie, and I'm not."
Matkowski was waiting for them in town and took them to dinner, because he said a man who had been murdered deserved a schnitzel, and because Weinstein could not be trusted to feed anybody. The restaurant was old and wood-panelled and full of Munich families making a great deal of noise about very little, and Matkowski chose a table in the corner and sat with his back to the wall, like a man who had been a professor for fifty years and knew where the questions came from.
Penny and Trevor and Weinstein argued at the other end of the table about whether Barbarian was a genius or a fraud, which in Weinstein's case was the same argument he had been having since 1994. Matkowski ignored them. He cut his schnitzel into exact squares and looked at Derek.
"You are frightened for the girl," he said.
"Yes."
"Not for yourself."
"Myself as well. But I can do something about myself. I can get on a plane." Derek moved a potato round his plate. "I can't get her on a plane. I can't even tell her why. She's twelve. If I told her there was a picture of her in my office with my body on the floor, she'd never sleep again. So I've put a man in a car outside her school, and I've texted her sweetheart, which I never do, and I've run away to Germany."
"You have done three foolish things for one good reason," said Matkowski. "That is the usual ratio for fathers. I had a son. He is fifty-three now and lives in Hamburg and still does not forgive me for the summer I made him learn logarithms." He ate a square of schnitzel. "Let me tell you what I told Alfred when he was twenty-two and came to me with a proof that I could see was wrong in the third line. He was very upset. He thought a mistake in the third line meant the whole of mathematics was against him. I said to him: ignorance is not a paradox. You do not know where the error is. That does not mean the error is everywhere. It means you have not found it yet. Go and find it, and it will be in one place, and it will be small, and you will feel stupid, and that is the best feeling in mathematics."
"Was it small?"
"It was a sign. A minus where there should have been a plus. He did not speak to me for a week." Matkowski put his knife and fork together. "Your photograph is not a paradox, Mr Gent. It feels like one because you do not yet know where the error is. But it is in one place. Somewhere, somebody has put a minus where there should be a plus, or a tomorrow where there should be a yesterday. When you find it, it will be very small. And you will feel very stupid. I look forward to it."
Derek looked at his plate. "Can I ask you something about your son?"
"You may ask. I may answer."
"When he was small. Was there ever anyone else? In the house. Another man." He heard how it sounded and did not withdraw it. "I'm sorry. It's none of my business."
Matkowski considered him for a moment over the ruins of the schnitzel, with the expression of a professor deciding whether a question from the back row was impertinent or merely good.
"No," he said. "His mother and I stayed married for fifty-one years, out of stubbornness and a shared dislike of her sister. So I cannot tell you what you want to know." He folded his napkin. "But I can tell you that he had a mathematics teacher, when he was fourteen, a young man from Kiel, whom he loved more than me for three years. He did his homework for him. He quoted him at dinner. He took up sailing because of him, which I have never forgiven." He smiled thinly. "And I can tell you that it did not subtract anything. I had thought it would. It was not that kind of operation. A child is not a cake, Mr Gent. Somebody else having a slice does not make yours smaller. It makes the child larger."
"Is that a theorem?"
"It is an observation. I have seen it hold in every case I have personally examined. Which is one." Matkowski raised a finger for the bill. "Is there someone else in your house?"
"There will be," said Derek, and was surprised to hear himself say it, because until that moment he had not admitted, even to himself, that he had seen it coming. "Soon, I think."
"Then I have said the useful thing," said Matkowski, "and now I am going to make Alfred pay."
At the other end of the table Weinstein had reached the point of the evening where he drew on napkins, and was explaining to Trevor, with salt cellars, how two clocks that went up and down a mountain would disagree when they met again at the bottom. Trevor was listening the way he listened to everything, with his whole face. Penny caught Derek's eye down the table and lifted one eyebrow a quarter of an inch, which in Penny was a speech.
Derek found, to his surprise, that he was hungry.
He did not sleep much in the hotel. At two in the morning he got up and looked at the photograph again by the light of his phone, and tried to do what Barbarian had asked: to put the email in a drawer and read the picture. He could not. Every time he looked at the red stain he saw the word in the heavier type, sitting on its own line. Murdered. As if someone had wanted to be sure he read it.
He put the phone down on the hotel blanket. Somewhere below, a tram went by, late and empty, its bell ringing for nobody.
He thought, for no reason, of teaching Sophie to tell the time. She had been five. They had sat on the floor of the flat in Clerkenwell, the one that had been a flat for three, with a cardboard clock from a cereal packet whose hands were held on with a split pin, and he had explained the big hand and the little hand, and she had listened with total concentration and then asked why the little hand was in charge when it was the slow one. He had not had an answer. He still did not. He had told her it was the rule, and she had written it down in her first notebook, in letters a centimetre high, under a heading she had invented herself: RULES THAT ARE PROBABLY WRONG.
He lay back on the hotel bed in the dark. The photograph glowed on the blanket beside him until the screen timed out, and then it was only a dark rectangle, and he found he could look at that.
Rule: A photograph is already finished. Ask it when, and don't let the caption answer.

Chapter 6
The Apple Problem
At twenty to six on Wednesday morning Alfred Weinstein was on the S-Bahn to the airport, thinking about apples, because Derek Gent had decided at a quarter to five that he was going home.
He had knocked on all their doors. He had not explained, beyond saying that Julius was right about one thing: the photograph had already happened. If it had already happened, hiding from it in Munich changed nothing except where he was when he found out what it was, and he would rather find out in London, where Sophie was. Penny had argued for four minutes and then packed. Trevor had packed first and argued afterwards. Weinstein had simply put on the jacket.
Now they sat in a near-empty carriage under hard white light, with Munich going past outside in the dark, and Weinstein took a pencil out of his pocket and held it over the tray table.
"Watch," he said, and let go.
The pencil fell. It hit the table, rolled, and stopped against the clock in its envelope.
"Riveting," said Trevor.
"Newton would say the Earth pulled it. A force, reaching up through the floor of the train, through the table, into the pencil. Invisible, instantaneous, from everywhere at once." Weinstein picked the pencil up. "Einstein noticed something very simple. The pencil doesn't feel anything. If you were the pencil, falling, you would feel weightless. A man falling off a roof feels no force at all until he meets the ground. Everything falls the same way, at the same rate, whatever it is made of, feather and hammer, as the astronaut showed on the Moon. So perhaps falling isn't something being done to you. Perhaps falling is what you do when nothing is being done to you, and the floor is the thing pushing."
"The equivalence principle," said Trevor.
"Yes, thank you, it has a name. Names are not explanations." He dropped the pencil again. "In a small enough box, falling freely, you cannot tell whether you are falling near a planet or floating in empty space. No experiment inside the box can tell you. That is the principle. Lesson 1
Special Relativity Is Rude
Module A · Foundations of Special Relativity · Case link: Chapters 1-3. Learning objective: State Einstein's two postulates and explain why constant c forces space and time to transform together. The first thing to understand about Special Relativity is that it does not begin with the statement:
"Everything is relative."
That is a popular summary and is also rather misleading. Einstein's Special Relativity rests on two principles: The laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames.
The speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers. It's the second one that causes all the trouble. Suppose you are sitting on a distant train.
You throw a ball forward at 10 m/s. Someone standing on the platform sees the ball moving at roughly: train speed + 10 m/s.
Perfectly ordinary. Now replace the ball with light.
Classical intuition replied that the person on the platform should measure:
train speed + speed of light.
Nature responds:
No.
The light still travels at exactly c.
This forces us to abandon the old assumptions about space and time. And that is where things become interesting.
Here is the classic way to see why — not just that it is true, but why it comes out as an actual number.
Imagine a clock built from a single photon of light, bouncing straight up and down between two mirrors a fixed distance apart. Each round trip is one 'tick.'
Nothing exotic: light, two mirrors, geometry.
Now put that clock on a train, moving past a platform at speed v.
From Trevor's point of view, standing on the platform, the light doesn't simply go straight up and down — the train is moving, so the photon has to travel diagonally to keep up with the departing mirror, tracing a longer zigzag path for each tick.
Here is the part that actually causes the trouble. The speed of light does not change for Trevor either.
Longer path, same speed, more time. That is the whole derivation. One tick of the train's clock, measured from the platform, simply takes longer than one tick measured on the train.
Do the geometry properly — a right triangle, Pythagoras, nothing worse — and out drops exactly the same γ you are about to meet properly in the next chapter, without asking anyone to take the mathematics on faith.
Call one round trip of the train's own clock Δτ — proper time, measured by the clock that is actually there. With the mirrors a distance L apart, light covers 2L each tick, so:
Δτ = 2L / c
From the platform, that same tick takes longer — call it Δt — because the photon travels the diagonal, not the vertical. By the time the photon reaches the top mirror — half the round trip, at time Δt/2 — the train has advanced by vΔt/2, while the photon has climbed the full height L. Pythagoras supplies the hypotenuse:
(cΔt/2)² = L² + (vΔt/2)²
Substitute L = cΔτ/2, and multiply both sides by 4:
c²Δt² = c²Δτ² + v²Δt²
Move the v²Δt² term to the left and factor out Δt²:
Δt² (c² − v²) = c²Δτ²
Divide both sides by (c² − v²):
Δt² = c²Δτ² / (c² − v²)
Divide top and bottom inside the fraction by c², then take the square root:
Δt = Δτ / √(1 − v²/c²)
Which is to say, Δt = γΔτ, where:
γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)
One geometric fact — light does not speed up just because you are already moving — and Pythagoras does the rest. Nobody had to take γ's word for it.
The photon takes a longer path in the clock but still travels at speed c — so the tick takes longer.
This is why time dilation is not a story about clocks getting confused. It is what geometry does when one side of the triangle refuses to change length no matter how fast you are moving.
→ Glossary: Time Dilation
None of this is hypothetical.
The satellites that make a phone's map work are moving fast enough, and sitting high enough out of Earth's gravity well, that both effects discussed in this book — Special Relativity's time dilation from speed, and General Relativity's time dilation from gravity — apply to their onboard clocks simultaneously, pulling in opposite directions.
Engineers correct for it deliberately, every single day, or a phone's position would drift by several kilometres a day. The Bureau finds this less remarkable than most people do. The Bureau finds most things less remarkable than most people do.
The Bureau's brass clock is not a satellite. It is doing a ruder job: displaying a label as if it were a tick.
One more term worth planting here, since the checkpoint below uses it: two observers can disagree about whether two events happened "at the same time" - that disagreement is called simultaneity, and it's exactly what Lesson 2 examines in detail. Nothing about it breaks physics. It just means "at the same time" needs a frame attached.
Lesson checkpoint
Special Relativity starts with two postulates, and the constancy of light forces space and time to transform together.
"Relative" does not mean "anything goes." The spacetime interval and other invariant quantities remain the same.
→ Glossary: Spacetime Interval
Exit Form

No. The disagreement is what the frames are for. A broken clock is a different fault.

Lesson 2
Lorentz Finally Gets Involved
Module A · Foundations of Special Relativity · Case link: Chapter 3. Learning objective: Use the Lorentz transformation to relate coordinates between inertial frames and name at least one invariant.
→ Glossary: Lorentz Transformation
The mathematical machinery connecting two inertial observers is the Lorentz transformation. An inertial observer moves at a constant velocity without accelerating.
Let A describe an event using position (x) and time (t). B describes that same event using (x′) and (t′). If B moves to A's right at speed (v), and their coordinate origins coincide when both clocks read zero, then:
x′ = γ(x − vt)
t′ = γ(t − vx/c²)
Here c is the speed of light in vacuum, and γ is the Lorentz factor.
Do not panic. γ is merely a number that becomes increasingly annoying as your speed approaches the speed of light. The important thing is that space and time transform together.
A quick worked example, with v = 0.6c (so γ = 1.25, a clean number to check by hand): suppose A clocks an event at x = 3 light-seconds, t = 4 seconds. Then x′ = 1.25 × (3 − 0.6×4) = 0.75 light-seconds, and t′ = 1.25 × (4 − 0.6×3) = 2.75 seconds. B assigns the same event different numbers on both counts - and that's the whole transformation, run once, by hand.
Look at the time equation: B's time depends on both A's time and A's position. That position term is why two events happening at the same time in A's frame can happen at different times in B's.
This explains the diagram. Each coloured line shows what one observer considers to happen at the same time as Event 1.
A's teal line passes through both events. A says they happen at the same time.
B's red line passes through Event 1, but Event 2 lies below it. B says Event 2 happened earlier than Event 1.
Event 2 does not belong on the red line because B does not consider it simultaneous with Event 1. The coloured lines represent equal times, not paths that the events must follow.
This disagreement is not simply about light taking time to reach someone's eyes. It remains after each observer accounts for light travel time.

Different observers can therefore disagree about distances, elapsed times, and whether two separated events happened simultaneously.
Drag the velocity yourself and watch the red and teal lines tilt apart at the simultaneity demo.
They do not, however, disagree about everything.
A key invariant is the spacetime interval. For the one-dimensional motion used here:
→ Glossary: Spacetime Interval
s² = c²Δt² − Δx²
Here Δt is the time between the two events, and Δx is their separation in space. All inertial observers calculate the same value of s² for the same pair of events, provided they use the same sign convention.
The individual space and time measurements can change. This combination stays the same.
Worked check, using the same event as before: in A's frame, s² = c²Δt² − Δx² = 4² − 3² = 7 (in units where c = 1 light-second per second). In B's frame, using the transformed values from a moment ago, s² = 2.75² − 0.75² = 7.5625 − 0.5625 = 7. Same number, different coordinates - that's the invariance, not just an assertion of it.
s² is what stays the same when the stationery changes. Case 1047 kept rewriting its fields because the Bureau was editing labels and calling the edits news. The interval does not care which drawer you stamp.
This chapter uses the timelike-positive convention — one of two equally valid ways to assign plus and minus signs to space and time in the interval formula. Chapter 7 of the novel uses the other fork (spacelike-positive: ds² = dx² + dy² + dz² − c²dt²). Same events; the sign of s² flips. Do not mix the two mid-calculation, or you will mis-file timelike as spacelike. In the novel Weinstein flags the fork in his notebook: Herbert prefers the other signs. The physics does not change. The drawer label does. Symptom of mixing the forks: a timelike pair suddenly looks spacelike, or the invariant comes out negative when the other convention would have made it positive. Name the fork before you interpret the sign.
The first major lesson of relativity is this:
Coordinates are descriptions of reality. The events they describe do not change when we change coordinates.
And that distinction will become increasingly important.
Lesson checkpoint
Lorentz transformations tell you how space and time coordinates change between inertial frames.
Do not add velocities using the old Newtonian rule. Ordinary velocity addition is only an approximation at low speeds. When speeds become a significant fraction of c, use the relativistic addition formula instead of adding the numbers as if they were ordinary miles per hour.

WORKSHOP
The Twin Paradox
 (do this after Lesson 2)
Workshop problem: One twin boards a very fast spaceship, travels out and back near the speed of light, and returns to find their sibling considerably older. Both twins, the objection runs, saw the other one moving — so shouldn't each expect the other to have aged less?
The two twins do not take the same route through spacetime between their parting and their reunion — only the travelling twin's path bends, out and back, while the twin on the sofa travels a straight, unaccelerated line between the same two events.
That is the entire resolution: proper time depends on the path taken, not merely on relative motion, and the straight path between two events always racks up more of it than any bent one. Turning around is what makes the paths different — it is not, by itself, what makes the travelling twin younger. There is a genuine fact about which twin aged less, and it belongs to whoever took the bent path.
This is the same idea as the invariant spacetime interval you have just met in Lesson 2, wearing a different coat. Proper time, like ordinary distance, depends on the path you actually take.
Do not explain this with petrol. On a road, the direct driver burns less fuel. In spacetime, the straight timelike path between two events accumulates more proper time, and the path that turns around accumulates less. The analogy runs backwards, which means it is the wrong analogy. Use the paths. The twin who stays takes the straight route and ages more. The twin who turns around takes the bend and ages less.
Workshop result: proper time depends on the path taken.
The same idea reappears in the novel whenever two honest clocks disagree.
Trevor still wants it filed under Pending Geometry. Mrs Marsh still refuses to stamp the form.

Lesson 3
Time May Be Less Fundamental
Module A · Foundations of Special Relativity
Case link: Chapter 5. Learning objective: Distinguish coordinate time from proper time along a worldline; reject universal Newtonian now.
→ Glossary: Proper Time · Worldline
Proper time: two worldlines between the same two events accumulate different amounts of elapsed proper time. Fly an atomic clock on a plane and keep an identical one on the ground, and the difference is measurable, not hypothetical. Two effects act on the airborne clock at once, though, and they pull in opposite directions: its speed slows it, its extra height in Earth's weaker gravity speeds it up. Which one wins depends on the flight.
→ Glossary: GPS Correction
Julius Barbarian's claim that time does not exist is deliberately provocative. But there is serious physics hiding underneath the joke. In relativity, there is no single universal time shared by the entire universe.
Different observers can experience different amounts of proper time between events. Proper time is the time measured by a clock travelling along a particular worldline.
So, instead of asking:
"What time is it everywhere?"
relativity encourages us to ask:
"How much proper time elapsed along this particular path through spacetime?"
That is a much better question.
And General Relativity goes further.
Gravitational fields affect the rate at which clocks run relative to one another.
A strange clock deeper in a gravitational field can accumulate less proper time than one farther away. So, clocks do not merely measure some universal substance called Time.
They measure something associated with their physical histories.
But notice what kind of claim that is. Relativity denies a single shared now across the universe. That part is mainstream, uncontested physics, settled since 1905. Barbarian is reaching for something further along the same road, and the two should not be filed under the same drawer.
His actual claim, spelled out in full, not the tea-time version: a clock does not measure a flowing substance called time. It compares one physical process against another — the swing of a pendulum against the turn of the Earth, the tick of a balance wheel against the fall of an apple. Take away every process in the universe, and there is nothing left over called "time" for a lone clock to still be measuring.
That is why, in Chapter 5, he does not answer Derek's question about the apple with a clock. He answers it with a second configuration. Two arrangements of the same table, and a relationship between them — not because he is being difficult, but because on his account that relationship is the only thing that was ever there. Call each arrangement a Now. On the relational view, reality is not a river of such moments; it is closer to a vast collection of them, most bearing no special relationship to their neighbours — a landscape, not a timeline.
If that is right, an honest question follows immediately: why does it feel like a river at all? Barbarian's answer is not that your memories are lies. It is that some configurations happen to contain something that looks exactly like a record of others — a photograph, a scar, a notebook Sophie kept, and tested seventeen times. A configuration rich in such records is experienced as a moment with a past, whether or not anything actually flowed to get there. The Bureau, of all institutions, should recognise the danger in that: a record that looks like memory and a record that looks like evidence file the same way. Case 1047 opened because a filing system trusted a record over the geometry it was supposedly reporting on. Barbarian would say that was the mistake, not the murder.
None of this is settled physics the way proper time is settled physics. It is a live, disputed research programme, not a conclusion in a textbook. But the core move — stop assuming a flowing background and start asking what the honest relationships between configurations actually are — is the same move Special and General Relativity already made once. Barbarian just made it twice.
Unfortunately, he also has an alarming tendency to explain it while eating biscuits.
Lesson checkpoint
Relativity removes universal Newtonian time; each clock measures proper time along its own worldline.
1. Each observer measures the other's moving clock as ticking slower — a relationship between frames, not one clock being objectively slow.
2. A clock in a weaker gravitational field, e.g. on an aeroplane at 10 km flight height, gains compared to a clock on the ground — though its own speed cuts into that gain; the net result depends on both.
3. Relativity's "no universal now" and Barbarian's "time is not fundamental" are different claims — the first is settled physics, the second is a live research programme.

Lesson 4
Enter General Relativity
Module B · Gravity as Geometry · Case link: Chapter 4. Learning objective: Explain gravity as spacetime geometry and state what the equivalence principle does and does not allow. Special Relativity deals with inertial frames and flat spacetime. But gravity causes a problem.
→ Glossary: Equivalence Principle
Einstein noticed something wonderfully simple:
If you are inside a freely falling lift, you cannot locally distinguish falling freely in a gravitational field from drifting weightlessly in empty space, far from any gravity. This is the equivalence principle.
That suggested something profound:
Perhaps gravity isn't fundamentally a conventional force.
Perhaps it is geometry.
General Relativity makes precisely that leap.
Matter and energy affect spacetime geometry.
Objects then move through that geometry.
The famous slogan is:
Matter tells spacetime how to curve;
Spacetime tells matter how to move.
That is considerably more accurate than blaming gravity on the apple.
Although the apple remains available for consultation.
Imagine dropping a ball inside a lift. You hold the ball at some height and release it.
Everything is normal if the lift is at rest.
If the ball and the lift are both falling together, at the same rate, relative to the observer, the ball simply floats.
Now imagine the lift is far away from any gravitational field and accelerating upward. The ball now moves downward towards the floor — just as it would in an ordinary gravitational field.
Notice the phrase "far away from any gravitational field" in that last sentence. It is doing a great deal of work.
Real gravity — the kind that comes from an actual planet — is not perfectly uniform. It points towards the centre of the Earth, which means "down" at Heathrow and "down" in Munich are very slightly different directions. Two balls dropped a mile apart do not fall on exactly parallel paths; they fall very slightly towards each other, both aiming at the same distant centre. An accelerating lift cannot reproduce that. Acceleration pushes everything inside it in exactly the same direction, with exactly the same strength, everywhere in the lift. Real gravity does not.
That difference is the tell, and it has a name: a tidal effect. Locally — in a lift small enough, for a short enough time — acceleration and gravity are genuinely indistinguishable, which is the whole content of the equivalence principle. Stretch the region large enough to notice the two balls converging, and the disguise fails.
→ Glossary: Tidal Force
This is why General Relativity cannot simply be "acceleration, but everywhere." A single accelerating frame can cancel gravity inside one small lift. No single frame cancels it in every lift on Earth at once, because the leftover — what no accelerating frame can absorb — is the actual curvature of spacetime. Chapter 14 puts Derek in a corridor built entirely out of that leftover: two clocks agreeing perfectly nearby and disagreeing badly forty-seven metres away, for exactly this reason.
→ Glossary: Curvature
Pound and Rebka, 1960, Harvard. A gamma-ray source and an absorber separated by 22.5 metres of tower. The frequency shift matched gravitational time dilation to about ten per cent. Height is cheap. Disagreement between honest clocks is not. The Bureau's anomaly is larger than a tower. That is the interesting part, not a licence to invent a new theory before lunch.
Lesson checkpoint
General Relativity replaces flat spacetime with a dynamical geometry that responds to matter and energy.
<- Chapter for this lesson: Chapter 4

Lesson 5
The Apple
Module B · Gravity as Geometry
Case link: Chapter 6.
Learning objective: Contrast Newtonian force with geodesic free fall
→ Glossary: Geodesic
Say when Newton remains an excellent approximation.
Newton vs. Einstein: the same falling apple, explained as a force or as motion along a geodesic.
Newton answered: There is a gravitational force.
Einstein eventually answered: The object is following a natural path through curved spacetime.
That natural path is called a geodesic.
Above the Earth, an orbiting spacecraft is actually in free fall.
It is not being "held up" by a mysterious absence of gravity.
It is following its geodesic.
The astronauts inside feel weightless because they are falling together with the spacecraft.
The surface of the Earth prevents us from following our natural free-fall path.
The floor pushes upward on our feet.
Which is why standing still is, in a relativistic sense, rather more strenuous than orbiting Earth.
The floor is what keeps you off your geodesic. Derek's lift with Sophie, later, is the same joke told at department-store volume.
The apple was therefore not really falling because gravity "pulled" it downward in the Newtonian sense.
It was following the geometry.
Newton wasn't wrong.
He had simply discovered an extraordinarily good approximation to something deeper.

Weinstein nearly lectured Derek on reconstructing the trajectory from the ground up — position, velocity, field, geometry, the whole local configuration — and then remembered he was on a train. The reconstruction is still the right homework. If you run the path backwards, you find you cannot recover the apple without recovering its environment: the geometry contains the matter, the matter contains the geometry, and a falling body is a probe of both. That is why Chapter 6 lets the pencil and the clock disagree and then stops. The essay belongs here, not in the interrogation.

Lesson 6
Singularities
Module B · Gravity as Geometry
Case link: Chapters 7-8.
Learning objective: Define a singularity as a breakdown of the classical spacetime description, not merely 'very strong gravity.'
→ Glossary: Singularity
Here Derek makes an important distinction. A singularity is not simply a place where gravity is very strong.
That would be too easy.
A singularity represents a breakdown of the classical spacetime description. One useful way of expressing this is through geodesic incompleteness. Normally, you should be able to keep extending that path. At a singularity, the classical theory can tell you that the path simply cannot be continued in the expected way.
→ Glossary: Geodesic
Imagine following a freely falling observer's worldline.
→ Glossary: Worldline
Singularity: a worldline that cannot be extended — a breakdown of the classical description, not a place.
This tells us something profound: The theory has reached its limit.
This is why physicists don't normally say:
"There's a really strong gravitational field over there, so that's a singularity."
They ask whether the mathematical structure of spacetime itself becomes pathological.
And that distinction matters enormously when we discuss black holes and the early universe.
A singularity is not necessarily an object sitting somewhere waiting to be photographed.
It may be telling us that General Relativity is incomplete at that point.
Which is physicist-speak for:
"The equations have stopped being helpful. Someone should invent some better ones."
If the Underground appears to end a path, the honest question is whether the description failed — not whether London has installed a black hole between stations.
Two related ideas are worth naming here, though neither is this story's actual diagnosis: a white hole is a black hole's time-reverse, a region that can only emit and never absorb, and a wormhole is a hypothetical tunnel connecting two distant regions of spacetime. Both are solutions on paper. Neither has ever been observed.
Lesson checkpoint
A singularity is best understood as a failure or incompleteness of the classical spacetime description. Black holes contain singularities (classically) hidden behind event horizons. White holes are their time-reverse: regions that can emit but not absorb. Wormholes are hypothetical tunnels connecting distant regions of spacetime.
→ Glossary: Event Horizon · Wormhole

Lesson 7
Why SR and GR Don't Fight
Module B · Gravity as Geometry
Case link: Chapters 8 & 11
Learning objective: Explain SR as the local freely falling limit of GR; identify when frame-stitching, not physics, is the problem.
Special Relativity as the local limit of General Relativity: any sufficiently small freely falling patch looks flat.
General Relativity contains Special Relativity locally. In a sufficiently small region of spacetime, a freely falling observer can construct a local inertial frame. Within that small region, physics looks like Special Relativity.
So: SR = local flat-spacetime physics while
GR = the larger theory describing curved spacetime and how those local frames fit together. This is why the theories can coexist perfectly well.
Here is the intuition without the mathematics. Stand on a football pitch and the ground looks flat. Look at the whole planet from orbit and it is obviously a sphere. Nothing about the Earth changed between those two views — only how much of it you were looking at. A small enough patch of a curved surface always looks flat, to whatever precision you happen to be measuring with. Spacetime behaves the same way: zoom in on a small enough patch, for a short enough time, and General Relativity's curved geometry is indistinguishable from Special Relativity's flat one. Zoom back out and the curvature reappears — not because SR broke, but because the patch was never the whole pitch.
The cold rain going sideways is the book refusing to stay in the small patch. That is a tidal hint, not a licence to throw SR away.

→ Glossary: Tidal Force

The Hologram
Module D · Dual Descriptions
Case link: Chapter 12
Learning objective: State the area-law clue for black-hole entropy and what holographic duality does and does not claim.
Derek's filing cabinet was full. This was an established fact, supported by the drawer that no longer shut.
Ordinarily, the remedy would be a larger cabinet. Double its width, height and depth, and there would be eight times as much room for files. Trevor would fill it in a fortnight, but that was a staffing problem.
Black-hole physics suggests a stranger kind of accounting. When physicists calculate a black hole's entropy (roughly: how disordered or unpredictable its microscopic details are), the quantity that matters is the area of its horizon. That unexpected result helped inspire the holographic principle: a theory describing a region with gravity may have an equivalent description on a boundary with one fewer spatial dimension.

→ Glossary: Holographic Principle
The filing cabinet is an analogy. Please do not attempt the experiment with the Bureau's furniture.
What the black holes tell us: A black hole's event horizon is the boundary beyond which light cannot escape to distant observers. It is not a solid shell.
→ Glossary: Event Horizon
Bekenstein and Hawking connected the horizon's area to entropy. Here, entropy measures how many microscopic possibilities are compatible with the black hole's large-scale properties. More precisely, it depends on the logarithm of that number (roughly: how many digits the number has, not the number itself, which is why entropy grows far more slowly than the raw count of possibilities). It can also be expressed as an amount of information in bits (a bit is the answer to one yes/no question).
For a spherical horizon, area is 4πR². Double the radius, and the area becomes four times as large. The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy therefore becomes four times as large too.
An ordinary ball with twice the radius has eight times the volume. This contrast is the clue: the black hole's entropy follows an area rule.
Penny would describe this as being charged for storage by the size of the wrapping paper. Derek would ask whether the wrapping paper was included.
What information means here: "Information" does not mean that someone has printed a list of everything that fell in on the outside of the black hole. It concerns the microscopic distinctions needed to specify a physical state.
Think of the difference between knowing that a room contains warm air and specifying the detailed state of that air. Many microscopic arrangements can fit the same broad description. Entropy quantifies that multiplicity.
The area rule helped motivate the idea that gravity limits the independent information needed to describe a region in an unexpectedly economical way. Turning that insight into a general rule requires care about which surfaces and regions are being compared.
It does not give Trevor permission to store the accounts on the office wallpaper.
The holographic principle: information about a volume can be encoded on its lower-dimensional boundary.
How to read the picture
The upper panel shows the black-hole clue. The larger horizon has twice the radius and four times the entropy. Its grid is a drawing aid, not a photograph of tiny storage compartments.
The lower panel shows the next idea. The bulk is the higher-dimensional interior, described using gravity. The boundary carries a quantum theory that describes the same system without its own dynamical gravity.
The double arrow means that physical questions can be translated between the descriptions. It is not a beam projecting objects into the interior. The drawn waves suggest field activity; they are not a literal map of individual particles to matching boundary bumps.
The boundary in this lower panel represents the boundary used in a holographic duality. It should not be confused with the black-hole horizon pictured above. Time is omitted from the drawing.
It is also not Trevor's Klein bottle, whatever the passing resemblance. His desk ornament has no well-defined inside or outside because of its topology, a fact about the shape of glass and nothing to do with holography. The boundary above has two perfectly ordinary sides; the interesting part is that each side's physics can be translated into the other's.
Pause here for a moment - that's the black-hole half of the lesson, and it's already a real, settled result, not speculation. What follows is a specific, much more debated idea about how far that area-rule clue might generalise. Worth slowing down for.
An example that does some work: holographic duality (often taught under the label AdS/CFT). AdS is Anti-de Sitter space, a particular negatively-curved gravitational geometry; CFT is a Conformal Field Theory, a quantum theory living on its boundary with no gravity of its own. In plain language: a world with gravity in one description can match a world without gravity in another — same bookkeeping, different nouns. Do not marry the acronym. Keep the idea: two descriptions, one structure.
In plain terms: "negatively curved" means space that bends away from itself, like a saddle rather than a ball, and "conformal" means a description that only cares about angles and shapes, not absolute size.
In Special Relativity, different observers use different coordinates. In General Relativity, different coordinate systems can describe the same geometry. In holographic duality, dramatically different-looking theories can describe the same physical system.
That is considerably more useful than saying the universe looks a bit like a hologram. It lets physicists turn a difficult problem into a different problem they may be able to solve. Derek would approve of the method. He would object to being billed for both calculations.
None of this establishes that our own universe has the particular holographic description shown here. It does not make Derek imaginary, prove a multiverse, or provide a mechanism for tomorrow's murder notice to arrive today. Penny's cinema trip may help her ask a better question. It cannot supply the experimental evidence.

Curved Corridors and Tides
Module B · Gravity as Geometry
Case link: Chapter 14
Learning objective: Recognise curvature via tidal effects / relative free-fall paths, not via a single 'downward force.'
→ Glossary: Curvature · Tidal Force
Tidal gravity: two initially parallel free-fall paths converge as they pass near a mass — curvature made visible.
The corridor that counted twelve doors one way and thirteen the other turned out to contain a cupboard, not curvature. The underlying idea is real all the same. In curved spacetime, local measurements can differ from measurements made over larger regions.
<- Chapter for this lesson: Chapter 14
The crucial concept is tidal gravity. If gravity were perfectly uniform everywhere, you could locally reproduce its effects simply by accelerating your reference frame. But real gravitational fields vary from place to place. That variation produces tidal effects.
For example, Earth's gravitational field is slightly stronger closer to Earth's centre than farther away.
Two freely falling objects initially moving together can therefore gradually move apart or together.
Picture two people falling towards the Earth, a few metres apart. Both are falling "straight down" — but down means towards the centre of the planet, and the centre is a single point. Their two straight-down directions are not quite parallel; they tilt very slightly towards each other, the way two people walking due north from different points on the equator slowly converge towards the pole. Nobody pushed them together. The geometry did. Chapter 14 puts exactly this argument in Derek's mouth, with two people rather than two clocks, right before the corridor stops behaving.
That gradual convergence — or, elsewhere, a gradual separation — is what "geodesic deviation" means. Geodesics are the straight-as-possible paths freely falling objects follow. Deviation is the technical word for the fact that two nearby ones do not stay the same distance apart in curved spacetime. Flat spacetime has no such effect; parallel geodesics stay parallel forever, which is more or less the definition of flat. Curved spacetime always has some deviation, however small, and its size and direction are exactly what the curvature tensors from Lesson 15 are built to keep track of.
→ Glossary: Geodesic · Tensor
The narrow corridor joke is really about something quite respectable:
Curvature reveals itself through how neighbouring free-fall paths behave relative to one another. The narrow corridor did not actually gain a door. Probably.

Tensors: Vectors' slightly more ambitious cousins. A vector is already rather clever.
It can tell you something like:
"Go three metres east and two metres north."
It has components — for instance, v = (vx, vy, vz) — and those components change when you change coordinates, but the underlying vector itself is the same physical thing.
A tensor takes this idea one step further. At an introductory level, you can think of a tensor as a mathematical object that describes relationships between several directions at once.
Very roughly:
Scalar: one number.
Vector: directional components.
Tensor: components describing relationships between directions.
So, in the spirit of the Relativistic Investigation Bureau:
A tensor is what happens when a vector gets promoted and starts having opinions about several directions simultaneously - the same promotion Lesson 12 already gave it, worth repeating because the field equation is about to lean on it hard.
That is a useful analogy. It is not the formal mathematical definition.
A tensor is not simply a "higher-dimensional vector." What matters is that its components obey specific transformation rules when we change coordinates, so that the underlying physical object remains well-defined.
And that is exactly why tensors are so useful in General Relativity. Spacetime can be curved. Coordinates can be awkward.
Observers can accelerate. Someone can be falling into a black hole while someone else is sitting safely in an office complaining about the coffee. We need mathematical objects that behave properly when we change coordinates.
Tensors do precisely that. The most important tensor is called the metric tensor, written gμν. It tells us how spacetime is measured: ds² = gμν dxμ dxν, a combination of regular distances in space plus a time component.
μ and ν each range over the four spacetime directions — one time, three space — and a repeated index means: add up the matching term for all four. That bookkeeping convention is why the equation looks short. It is quietly adding up sixteen terms in shorthand.
→ Glossary: Metric
The metric can vary from place to place.
So physicists combine the changes in all the relevant directions according to the metric, and get the spacetime interval.
→ Glossary: Spacetime Interval
The metric is therefore rather like the instruction manual for measuring spacetime. It tells us about: distances, times, angles, causal relationships, and the geometry of spacetime itself. Once you know the metric, an enormous amount of physics follows.

→ Glossary: Curvature
"Space is a bit bent over there."

It can affect neighbouring paths differently. It can produce tidal effects. Tensors are the machinery that lets us describe all of this without losing the physics every time somebody changes coordinates.
→ Glossary: Tidal Force

The sunlight in the frame, the glass still in the grandfather frame, and the girl's feet pointing out of the door were all measurements, and every one of them belonged to the earlier Wednesday. MURDER was a classifier's label and the perpetrator was a template's default. The 11:03, 11:17 and 12:04 on Sophie's page were her own timetable, honestly measured by a girl catching a bus.
Derek's own watch, ticking his own proper time throughout, reached 11:03 on the second Wednesday like any ordinary clock doing an ordinary job, and nothing happened, because the event the file described was already a week in the past. Causal order never flipped. Only the filing did.
None of this required the Bureau's computer to predict anything. It required a label to be read as a measurement, and one Wednesday to be read as another. That was the confusion this entire investigation turned out to be.
Chapter 16's resolution turns on a distinction this book has been building since Chapter 3: coordinates are not reality, they are descriptions of reality.
Lesson checkpoint
An event coordinate is a label within a description of spacetime; proper time is what a clock actually measures along its own path. Confusing the two produces exactly the kind of "impossible" result this book has been full of.
A record that looks like it "knows the future" may simply be labelled with someone else's coordinate rather than your own proper time.
Exit Form
If a clock shows a value nobody's watch actually ticked through, is the clock lying — or is somebody reading the wrong number off it?
The clock isn't lying. Somebody's reading a coordinate as if it were a measurement.

GLOSSARY
Andromeda Paradox. Penrose's illustration that a small change of velocity on Earth can swing a distant "now"-slice across days on Andromeda. It is a statement about simultaneity for spacelike separation, not a method for sending news to Andromeda.
Block Universe. The view that past, present, and future events are equally part of a four-dimensional whole. Relativity's geometry is friendly to it; it is not a theorem that free will is a clerical error.
Causal Structure. The pattern of which events can influence which others, determined by spacetime's light cones and how they connect. Coordinate systems are negotiable; these causal relationships are not.
Curvature. The departure of spacetime geometry from flatness, revealed by effects such as nearby freely falling objects accelerating relative to one another. Matter and energy influence curvature, but spacetime can be curved even in empty regions. No external dimension is required for it to bend into.
Decoherence. The suppression of observable quantum interference as a system becomes entangled with its environment. It helps explain why everyday objects behave classically. By itself, it does not explain why a measurement yields one particular outcome.
Einstein Equation. The central equation of general relativity, usually called the Einstein field equations. It relates spacetime curvature to the energy, momentum, and stresses of matter and fields. Matter influences geometry; geometry guides motion. The mathematics takes somewhat longer.
Einstein Synchronisation. The convention that two distant clocks are set by assuming the one-way speed of light is the same in both directions. The two-way speed is measured; the one-way split is chosen. A clock that displays an event label instead of its own ticks is breaking a different rule, but the same family of mistake: treating a convention as a measurement.
Equivalence Principle. In a sufficiently small, freely falling laboratory, nongravitational experiments behave as they would in an inertial laboratory without gravity. Over larger regions, tidal effects can reveal the difference. A foundation of general relativity, and the reason lifts feature so heavily in physics thought experiments.
Event Coordinate. A name for where-and-when inside a chosen description of spacetime. Not proper time. A label, not a measurement of elapsed time along any particular clock's path.
Event Horizon. The boundary of a black hole's region of no escape: once inside, no future-directed signal can reach the distant exterior. Not light. Not a strongly worded letter.
Geodesic. The straightest possible path in a given geometry. Ideal freely falling objects follow spacetime geodesics when no nongravitational forces act on them.
GPS Correction. The adjustment applied to satellite clocks for both special-relativistic slowing from orbital speed and general-relativistic speeding from weaker gravity. Both effects are real, and they point in opposite directions; skipping either term produces a map that lies.
Hafele–Keating Experiment. A 1971 measurement in which atomic clocks flown around the world accumulated different proper time from clocks that stayed behind, in the direction relativity predicted once both speed and altitude were accounted for.
Holographic Principle. The proposal, motivated by black-hole entropy, that a gravitational region's physical information can be described by degrees of freedom associated with a boundary of one fewer spatial dimension. Holographic dualities give this idea concrete form in particular theoretical settings. Its application to our universe remains an open question.
Light Cone. The boundary between lightlike directions and the timelike or spacelike directions at an event. Its future and past portions show the local limits on where signals can go and where they can come from.
Lorentz Transformation. A transformation relating space and time coordinates in inertial frames while preserving the spacetime interval. Transformations between frames in relative motion underpin time dilation, length contraction, and the relativity of simultaneity.
Metric. The mathematical object that determines local spacetime intervals and allows lengths and elapsed times to be calculated along paths. Its components depend on the coordinates chosen. Complicated coordinates can make even flat spacetime look mathematically unwelcoming.
Pending Geometry. The Bureau's name for a file whose time-label and whose measurement will not sit in the same box. Read the case before you decide what the box contained.
Pound–Rebka Experiment. A 1960 measurement of gravitational redshift over 22.5 metres of a tower at Harvard. Honest clocks, a small height difference, and a real, measurable disagreement.
Proper Time. The elapsed time measured by an ideal clock along its own path through spacetime. Observers may use different coordinates, but they agree on what that clock records between two events on its path.
Reference Frame. A systematic way of assigning coordinates to events, usually tied to an observer's state of motion. An inertial frame is one in which free objects keep constant velocity.
Simultaneity. The assignment of the same time to different events. Spatially separated events that are simultaneous in one inertial frame need not be simultaneous in another. Neither frame is mistaken; their definitions of "now" differ.
Singularity. A breakdown or incompleteness of the classical spacetime description, often associated with unbounded curvature. As Lesson 6 puts it: a key sign is geodesic incompleteness - a free-fall path that simply cannot be continued further, the way an ordinary path normally could be. A singularity need not be an ordinary point within spacetime. The map itself has run out.
Spacetime Interval. The invariant combination of spatial and temporal separation. In special relativity, inertial observers agree on it even when they disagree about distance and duration separately. In curved spacetime, the metric defines the corresponding local interval between infinitesimally nearby events.
Stress-Energy Tensor. The tensor describing energy density, momentum density, energy flow, pressure, and shear stresses. It supplies the matter-and-fields side of the Einstein field equations. It constrains curvature, although it does not by itself determine the entire spacetime geometry.
Tensor. A mathematical object whose components change according to precise rules when coordinates change, leaving the object itself unchanged. Scalars and vectors are simple examples; the metric and curvature tensors are more elaborate ones. Different numbers, consistent physics.
Tidal Force. The relative acceleration of nearby freely falling objects, producing stretching or squeezing. In general relativity, these effects reveal spacetime curvature. Their strength depends on the circumstances.
Time Dilation. Differences in clock rates associated with relative motion or gravitational circumstances. Two inertial observers in relative motion can each assign a slower rate to the other's clock, using their own frame's definition of simultaneity. Gravitational comparisons are different: for stationary clocks around Earth, the lower clock runs more slowly than the higher one. The mutual-slowing argument does not apply to that comparison.
White Hole. The time-reverse of a black hole in the equations: a region from which matter and light can only emerge, never enter. A mathematical solution, not an observed object. Lesson 6 names it so the Underground's bad day is not promoted into one.
Worldline. The path an object traces through spacetime: where it is and when it is there. Your biography, with fewer adjectives and more coordinates.
Wormhole. A hypothetical connection between otherwise separate or distant regions of spacetime. Some solutions of general relativity contain wormholes, but traversable versions typically require exotic stress-energy. Mathematical availability does not guarantee a usable passage. Lesson 6 names it so the impossible station is not promoted into one.

BIBLIOGRAPHY
Einstein, Albert. Relativity: The Special and General Theory. Translated by Robert W. Lawson. Methuen, 1920. Originally published in German in 1916. COMMENT: Einstein's original exposition. Difficulty: High — dense and mathematical, but this is where the real physics lives. Patience required.
Epstein, Lewis Carroll. Relativity Visualized. Insight Press, 1985. COMMENT: Visual approach to relativity. Difficulty: Medium — highly recommended for intuitive understanding, much more accessible than Einstein's original.
Barbour, Julian B. The End of Time: The Next Revolution in Physics. Oxford University Press, 2000. COMMENT: Explores whether time is fundamental or emergent. Difficulty: Medium-High — more speculative, but grounded in modern physics.
Minkowski, Hermann. "Space and Time." In The Principle of Relativity: A Collection of Original Memoirs on the Special and General Theory of Relativity, translated by W. Perrett and G. B. Jeffery. Methuen, 1923 (often reprinted by Dover). COMMENT: Minkowski's Cologne lecture, geometric spacetime. Difficulty: Very High — short but exceptionally dense. The 1920 Calcutta Saha and Bose volume is a different collection with a different Minkowski text; do not conflate the two.
Mermin, N. David. It's About Time: Understanding Einstein's Relativity. Princeton University Press, 2005. COMMENT: Possibly the most readable relativity book on this list — conversational, minimal maths, written for genuinely curious non-physicists.
Gamow, George. Mr Tompkins in Wonderland. Cambridge University Press, 1940. COMMENT: The original relativity-made-friendly book, decades before the rest of this list existed. Mr Tompkins falls asleep and dreams of a city where the speed of light is walking pace — trams contract, cyclists age slower than pedestrians, and nobody finds any of it as alarming as they should. Difficulty: Low. If you want to know what it might feel like to actually live inside Special Relativity, rather than merely calculate it, start here.
Muller, Richard A. Now: The Physics of Time. W. W. Norton, 2016. COMMENT: A working physicist's account of why "now" feels real when relativity refuses a universal now. Difficulty: Medium. He argues that time's flow may be more fundamental than most physicists admit. That is a different programme from Barbour's.
Cox, Brian, and Jeff Forshaw. Why Does E=mc²? (And Why Should We Care?). Da Capo Press, 2009. COMMENT: A patient derivation of the equation, from the same two postulates as Lesson 1. Difficulty: Medium.
Susskind, Leonard. The Black Hole War: My Battle with Stephen Hawking to Make the World Safe for Quantum Mechanics. Little, Brown and Company, 2008. COMMENT: The argument over whether information falling into a black hole is lost, and the area-law clue behind holographic duality. Difficulty: Medium. Read it after Lesson 11, not instead of it.
Wheeler, John Archibald. A Journey into Gravity and Spacetime. Scientific American Library, 1990. COMMENT: Wheeler practically invented the modern intuition for curved spacetime, and this is his own attempt to hand that intuition to a general reader — geometry and pictures doing the work equations do everywhere else on this list. Difficulty: Low-Medium. The natural next stop after Lesson 5's apple and Lesson 13's tidal corridor, from the physicist who saw GR most clearly in pictures.
Carroll, Sean. Spacetime and Geometry. Addison-Wesley, 2004. COMMENT: For the reader who finishes Lesson 15 and wants the tensors to stop being metaphors. Difficulty: High. Not required for Case 1047.
Geroch, Robert. General Relativity from A to B. University of Chicago Press, 1978. COMMENT: Almost no tensors; almost all geometry. Difficulty: Medium. The apple chapter, stretched into a short book.
Norton, John D. Einstein for Everyone. Online lecture notes, University of Pittsburgh. Accessed 30 September 2026. COMMENT: Honest about history and about what the equations do not say. Difficulty: Low-Medium. A second start, beside Mermin, that costs nothing.
Penrose, Roger. The Emperor's New Mind. Oxford University Press, 1989. COMMENT: Use the simultaneity illustration; leave the rest of Penrose's argument for another case. Difficulty: Medium, where it stays on now-slices.
Rovelli, Carlo. The Order of Time. Riverhead Books, 2018. COMMENT: A working physicist arguing that time's apparent flow is not fundamental. Difficulty: Low-Medium. File next to Barbour, not on top of him — they are not the same programme.
Schutz, Bernard. A First Course in General Relativity. 3rd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2022. The 1985 first edition is a different printing. COMMENT: The standard next course after this one. Difficulty: High.
Taylor, Edwin F., and John Archibald Wheeler. Spacetime Physics. W. H. Freeman, 1992. COMMENT: The cleanest introductory drill on intervals, proper time, and diagrams. Difficulty: Medium. This is Lesson 2 with homework.
