The Warning That Was Sent Too Late
A Relativistic Investigation Bureau Mystery
Book Two
By Lothar J. Musiol
writing as Spezala Genara Relavi (S.G.R.)


PROLOGUE
In Which a File Arrives Before Its Author
A warning is only useful if it changes what you do. A warning that changes what you do may remove the reason anyone sent it. The Bureau had a name for most contradictions. It did not yet have a name for a sender who had not been born.
Rule: A message from outside your past is not a message you can file as received. Find out what it is before you call it advice.

Chapter 1
The Door Is Already Open
The first thing anybody at the Bureau did, once the office door had opened onto a railway, was look at a clock. It was a professional reflex. As professional reflexes went, it was slightly late.
The corridor clock read 10:05. Trevor read it out by leaning through the outer room until most of him was in the corridor and his glasses were in serious danger. Nobody had looked at anything with numbers on it while the door was opening. They had been looking at Weinstein, and Weinstein had been looking at the sheet, and the sheet had been looking, in its flat official way, at Derek.
DO NOT ALLOW DEREK GENT TO BOARD THE MYSTERIOUS TRAIN.
Derek had folded it and put it in his pocket, which had felt like a decision at the time and now felt like a man putting a lit match in his jacket so that nobody else would be tempted to strike it.
His own voice, from somewhere later, had already improved on the paper. Don't open the door. The door had not waited for advice from either of them.
Beyond it was a platform.
It was not a large platform. It was the kind of platform a railway builds when it has been told to build one and has asked for nothing further: a strip of grey slabs with a yellow line painted along the edge by someone in a hurry, a lamp, a bench nobody would ever choose, and a train standing in the dark beyond, lit at the windows and empty at all of them. The doorway smelled of brake dust, which nobody in the office had ordered. Above the train hung a destination board in amber dots, and the board said one word, and the word was the sort of thing a board should not be permitted to say.
YESTERDAY.
Derek did not step through. He stood a pace back from the sill with his hands at his sides, because his hands, left to themselves, would have tried to tidy the platform.
Penny Gent stood at his shoulder with her handbag and the look she kept for mornings that had started without permission. "You can't obey both."
"I'm obeying the one that is still possible."
"The door is open."
"I didn't open it."
"The voice didn't say 'don't notice it.'"
Trevor Boltzman came back in from the corridor with his glasses off. Behind him, on his desk, the Klein bottle went on not holding tea, which was the only thing in the building still doing its job. "The board says YESTERDAY."
"Yes."
"Yesterday was Sunday."
"The board is not offering you Sunday," Weinstein said.
He was visiting. Same jacket, as on every day of the year, on the grounds that changing it introduced a variable. He had no door on this corridor, and he had taken no wound from the lack of one. He was perched on the corner of Derek's desk with the expression of a man who has come to London for a polite few days and been handed, at no extra charge, the most interesting morning of his decade.
"Yesterday, written like that, is a direction. Not a date. You do not find a date on a train. Herbert will call it a coordinate. He can do it from Munich, where he has a blackboard and no railway in his wall."
"What day is the room?"
"Monday," Derek offered.
"Tuesday," Penny countered.
"It is Monday."
"Disasters are Tuesdays. I am not opening a new day for this one. I haven't the stationery."
The platform did nothing. That was the worst of it. Disasters, in Derek's experience, had the decency to keep moving. This one stood in his doorway like a delivery waiting for a signature.
Footsteps came along the corridor at the unhurried rate of a woman who has never once been late because she has never once agreed that anything started without her. Mrs Marsh arrived at the frame with a clipboard and did not look at the railway. She looked at Weinstein.
"Dr Weinstein does not have a door on this floor."
"I'm visiting."
"Visiting is not a tenancy." She turned her head at last and regarded the platform as she regarded a damp patch: something that would become the landlord's opinion before it became anybody's fault. "From the corridor it looks like a door with a station in it. I take it from in there it looks like a station with a door round it."
"Roughly," Derek conceded.
"Roughly is how leaks start. If it is still there at lunch I shall tell the landlord. The landlord does not believe in platforms. He did not believe in the damp, either, until it came through his ceiling."
She made a small mark on the clipboard and went back down the stairs. The platform remained. The corridor clock went on to 10:06, and then to 10:07, with the indifference of a thing that had never been asked its opinion, while four people stood in front of a door that had already been open when anyone thought to look at the time.
Derek patted the pocket. "Nobody boards."
"The instruction names you," Penny pointed out. "It does not name me."
"No."
"So I could."
"You could also set fire to the filing cabinet."
"I've thought about it."
"Don't."
"I said I'd thought about it. I didn't say I'd booked it."
Trevor opened a drawer that had no business containing a tape measure and produced one anyway. He knelt at the sill, hooked the end against the frame on the London side, and drew the yellow tape out across the threshold until it lay on the first grey slab of the platform. He held the case in both hands.
"London this side. Station that side. I am not letting go."
"Good."
"I want it on record that I thought of it."
"It's on record."
"I mean properly. In the file. A line that says 'Trevor measured the door.' If something goes through, I want to know how far."
"And if something comes through?"
Trevor looked at the tape as if it had personally promised him it would not. "Then I'll know that too."
He also, while his courage was up and his hands were occupied, got his phone out with two fingers and texted his sister the single word YESTERDAY. He did not add anything. Then he set the phone face-down on the carpet beside his knee and said, to the tape, "That was the board."
Nobody asked him to explain. Penny's eyebrows did it for them.
Weinstein recited the back of the paper from memory, the way some men recite poems and he recited footnotes. "Please note. The previous warning was sent too late."
"Then this sheet is not that warning," Penny said. "The back is pointing behind itself."
"The voice is the late one," Derek said. "Don't open a door that had already opened."
"The front can still be early. Nobody has boarded."
It was, Derek thought, a remarkably small sentence to be holding up an entire morning.
They had heard the knocks once already. Three of them, soft, from somewhere inside the carriage, the way someone knocks on a bathroom door when they suspect the answer. Then the voice, which was his own and was not, which had the same flat register he used for murders and for tea, and also something else in it, worn smooth, like a step that has been used by a great many feet.
Derek? Whatever you do, do not open the door. You already know what's inside.
He did not want to hear it again. He found he was waiting for it anyway, the way you wait for a dripping tap after you've decided to ignore it.
"Beyond the door," Penny itemised, "platform, train, word. This side is us. Don't help it."
"I'm not helping it."
"You're standing like you're about to apologise to it."
The board flickered. For an instant it showed nothing at all, a black rectangle like an eye that has closed to think. Then it went back to YESTERDAY, a little brighter, as if it had checked and was sure.
Weinstein did not apologise for anything, which was his gift. "Let us be precise before we are frightened. Case 1047 is closed. It is in drawer three, and Mrs Marsh will defend that drawer with her body. This paper is not that case. The heading says NOT YET OCCURRED. The note in the corner says the sender has not been born."
"Then we don't inherit the last clerk."
"LOCALHOST sent the photograph. This did not. Different paper, different hand, different lie. I do not want anybody in this room solving last week again because it was comfortable."
Trevor put his glasses back on. The platform failed to improve. "A person who has not been born cannot post a letter."
"No."
"So who posted it?"
"That," Derek said, "is the case."
The kettle clicked off in the corner. Somebody had boiled it for a closed file, out of habit, sometime before the sheet had appeared; it had taken this long to notice. Derek did not pour it. A kettle was for tea and for cases that had finished. He was not prepared to tell it which this was.
Instead he looked at the corridor clock again, because Trevor was looking at it, and Trevor was wearing the expression of a man about to enjoy a subtraction.
"Ten twenty-two," Trevor announced.
"And?"
"Mrs Marsh came through at ten oh five. The door was already open. It's ten twenty-two now and we have just finished reading the back of the sheet aloud. Seventeen minutes."
"Don't," Penny warned.
"I'm only saying it."
"You're saying it in the voice. The seventeen voice. The one you used all last week."
"It is seventeen. I didn't make it seventeen."
"No," Penny said. "We did. Seventeen minutes is how long it took four adults to finish reading a sheet of paper in front of an open door. It isn't the opening. It isn't when the sheet arrived. The sheet was in this room before the door opened. Weinstein was holding it. Then the door. Then the voice. Write that. The clock only has the reading."
Trevor wrote it, kneeling, in capitals on the back of an envelope, with the tape measure clamped between his knees.
SHEET, THEN THE DOOR. BOTH BEFORE 10:05. TIMES UNKNOWN. VOICE AFTER THE DOOR: LATE FOR DON'T OPEN. PAPER READ 10:22. THAT IS READING. FRONT STILL EARLY FOR BOARDING.
He looked at it. "It's very short for a morning."
"It's the right length," Weinstein said. "Now. Somebody will want a frame."
"I don't want a frame," Penny objected.
"Trevor wants a frame. Last week a frame saved his life, or at least his afternoon. He has the look of a man reaching for a tool because it was in his hand recently."
Trevor did not deny it. "In special relativity the order of two events can depend on who's measuring. You told us that. You drew it for us last week."
"I did. For some pairs."
"So from somewhere moving fast enough, the voice could come before the door."
"Do the sum."
Trevor did the sum. He did it on the envelope, under the capitals, and Derek watched the glasses come off again, slowly, the way a man takes his hat off at a funeral he had been hoping was somebody else's.
"The order only flips if nothing could get from one event to the other. Not even light. Seventeen minutes, at the speed of light, is about three hundred and six million kilometres."
"And the door?"
"Is about four metres from the desk."
"So."
"So to swap a door and a voice seventeen minutes apart, the door would need to be on the other side of the Sun." Trevor put the pencil down. "It's not on the other side of the Sun. I'm holding it."
"In English," Penny said.
"Same room, minutes apart," Weinstein said. "Everybody in the universe agrees which came first. Munich does not get a vote. Neither does a train."
"Good," Derek decided. "Then nobody's voting."
He went to shut the door. It was a reasonable thing to try. He took hold of the handle and swung it inwards, and it travelled a third of its arc, met station air, and stopped with a small soft refusal, as if it had walked into a curtain. He tried once more, with the patience of a man who already knows. It stopped in the same place. The platform was in the hinge.
"Open door," Penny said.
"I'm here."
"That's what it means, on this corridor. An open door means we don't know where you've gone. A shut one means you're in there, and Trevor is not invited to discover in what sense."
"Trevor is invited to stop standing in the exit."
"The exit is a platform. Sit down, Trevor."
Trevor, who had been kneeling, managed to sit down without letting go of the tape, which was a feat of engineering in itself.
Derek rang Tabitha.
He had not decided to. He found the phone in his hand and her name on the screen and the ring already going, and he let it, because it was the first thing all morning he had done on purpose. She answered on the third ring, from home, already on a different subject.
"I'm not discussing a train."
"I haven't mentioned one."
"Trevor sent YESTERDAY in capitals and will claim it was a pocket. It was not a pocket. His pocket has never once used capitals." A pause, in which he could hear a radio being turned down. "I'm discussing your door."
On the carpet, Trevor put his face in his free hand.
"It's open."
"It isn't, in the evenings. I have this from a brother. The dog waits in the hall at half past six. I didn't tell him to. He's worked it out on his own, which is more than either of you has managed in words."
"The door is open because a platform is in it. I tried to shut it."
"That's a train."
"You asked about the evenings."
Somewhere at her end, audibly, a small dog did not get up. Derek knew the sound. It was the sound of a dog that had considered the morning and found it inadequate.
"The evenings are you not having a sentence. I need to know if the shut door was you. And if it's still you, now the door won't shut. Because if the only reason it was shut was a door, I'd rather know before I find out from a hinge."
"It was me. It is still me. The door is a separate failure."
"Good." It was said in the voice she used for coffee orders, and he heard, under it, the thing she had not said. "I'd hate to be a platform."
"You aren't a platform."
"I know I'm not. I wanted to hear whether you did." The radio went off altogether. "Gucci won't come. He looked at the lead and went back to sleep. I'm not translating."
"Don't bring him."
"I wasn't going to. He'd stand in the frame and refuse to choose, and I'd have to carry him, and he'd make it my fault. Lunch. Without him. I'll stand where London is."
She rang off. He stood holding the phone for a moment longer than the phone required, because he had been asked a question and allowed to answer it, and he was not used to the second part.
Trevor lowered his hand. "You rang my sister."
"You texted her a destination."
"I did not text her a feeling."
"She received one."
"I don't have a procedure for this."
"You have a back," Penny advised. "Use it when she gets here. The door won't close, and you are going to be in the same room, and you are going to face the filing cabinet like a gentleman."
Trevor looked at the filing cabinet as if he had been introduced to it before and had not enjoyed the conversation.
On the platform the train did not depart and did not arrive. It waited, which is the most expensive thing a train can do, in a doorway already booked as a lunchtime complaint. The amber word above it held steady. Derek stood with his hand flat over the pocket where the sheet was, and for the first time since Tuesday, the old Tuesday, the one with the photograph, he understood exactly what he was supposed to do, and found that knowing it did not make the morning any shorter.
Rule: If one instruction is still possible and the other has already happened, obey the possible one. Write that the voice was late for the door. Do not give that lateness to the sheet.

Chapter 2
A Sender Requires a Birthday
The Bureau's intake form had been designed, many years earlier, by a man who believed that every problem in the world could be reduced to a name, a date of birth, and an address with legs. Derek had inherited it along with the filing cabinet and a stapler that only worked on Thursdays. He had never seen a reason to change it. Most of the people who arrived at the Bureau had all three.
Trevor sat at the keyboard. Derek stood behind him. Penny weighted the sheet flat on the desk with a small unsuccessful apple, the only object in her handbag heavy enough for the job and light enough to be lunch if the morning recovered.
Weinstein had refused a chair. His real train was at four, from a real station, to a real airport, and he had the air of a man who intended to catch it without once sitting down in between, in case sitting down became residence. Lunch was the nearer threat.
Trevor had surrendered the tape measure to Mrs Marsh, who had weighted its case on the London side of the sill with the fire extinguisher and accepted responsibility for it in the manner of a woman taking in a parcel for a neighbour: provisionally, and with no admission that she knew what was in it.
"Name," Trevor began.
"Unknown."
He typed UNKNOWN. The form allowed it. The form had seen a great many unknowns. It was the next field it cared about.
"Date of birth."
"The sheet says not born," Penny said. "Sheets lie. This one gives a status, not a birthday."
"NOT YET OCCURRED is the case," Derek put in. "Not the person."
Trevor typed it into the date field anyway, because it was the only answer on the paper. The form sent the cursor back to the start of the box and made a noise that was both polite and final, the noise of a bank teller who has seen your face before.
"It wants a date."
"Yesterday."
"We didn't have the paper yesterday."
"The date printed on it, then. Tomorrow."
He typed tomorrow's date. The form considered it for long enough to suggest that someone, somewhere, was being consulted. Then it removed tomorrow from the box and wrote, underneath, in the soft grey it reserved for the confused:
CAUSE MUST PRECEDE EFFECT.
They looked at it.
"That's not one of ours," Trevor observed.
"It is now."
"Did you write that? Into the validation?"
"I have never written anything into the validation. I'm frightened of the validation."
"In English," Penny requested.
"A letter cannot arrive from a person who is not here yet," Weinstein said. "The sender has to be in the past of the letter. Before. Somewhere a pigeon or a postman or a photon could have got from them to us. The form is saying, in its small grey voice, that a person whose birthday is tomorrow is not in the past of anything that happened this morning."
"The form agrees with you."
"I dislike that. I have spent thirty years being agreed with by forms. It has never once been a good sign."
Derek leaned in. "Try someone who has been born. Me."
The Bureau already held his date of birth. It had been entered two years ago when a courier refused to leave a crate of decommissioned station clocks without a signature, a surname, and proof that the signatory had existed for long enough to be blamed. Trevor typed it into the sender field.
The form took the date. It refused the name.
SENDER IS THE SUBJECT OF THE WARNING. A FILE MAY NOT BE RECEIVED FROM ITS OWN PROHIBITION.
"Again," Derek instructed.
Trevor read it again, slower, in the voice he used for things that were going to be on a test.
"So it isn't you. Later. Writing back."
"Later me has been born. He's only further along. He's not a different species. He just knows what the next bit of Monday is like."
"Then why did the form refuse him?"
Weinstein had begun pacing, which in the Bureau's outer room meant three steps and a turn, like a man walking a very small dog. "Because the form has done something the last case never made us do. Suppose later Derek posted this. He wrote it after he knew the sentence. Do not let Derek Gent board. How does he know to write that?"
"Because he boarded," Penny supplied, "and it was bad."
"Then he boarded. Which means the warning did not stop him. Which means it is a record of a failure, not a warning, and he has sent his younger self a souvenir. Or he did not board. In which case nothing happened to him on any train, and he never learned the sentence, and why on earth would he send it?"
"Because he got the warning," Trevor ventured, slowly. "From himself. And then he sent it back. To himself."
"Where did the words come from, the first time?"
Trevor opened his mouth.
"They didn't," Weinstein said. "That is the trouble with a letter that is its own reason. It has no first draft. Novikov would say the universe only lets you post the letters that were always going to be posted. Very tidy. It still does not tell you who held the pen."
"Oh," Trevor said.
"Leave the oh on the floor," Penny said. "We are not boarding. The letter is here anyway. That is all the oh we have."
"I'm only saying it's a good oh."
"It's an excellent oh. It's also lunchtime. Neither of those is a finding."
Mrs Marsh's head arrived in the stair doorway, without, so far as anyone could see, the rest of her having climbed the last flight. "Lunch," she announced. "The landlord asked if the platform was a metaphor. I told him it was a lease violation. He preferred the metaphor. I told him you were leaving."
"I am leaving at four," Weinstein said. "Not at lunch. Lunch is not an airport."
"Then I've lied to the landlord, which I shall enjoy." The head considered the room. "Your tape measure has not moved, Mr Boltzman. Nor has the train. If either of them does, you'll hear me. I do not shout. I won't need to."
The head withdrew. They heard her settle on the stairwell like a sandbag.
The street door opened and did not shut, in the manner of a woman who already knew the floor and saw no reason to negotiate with its doors. Feet on the stairs, not hurried. Tabitha came up without the dog, in a coat dressed for weather nobody else had forecast, her dark hair pulled back with the same practical impatience Sophie used on hers. She said good afternoon to Mrs Marsh. Mrs Marsh said good afternoon back, which nobody in the Bureau had heard her say in three years.
Tabitha did not look at the board. She did not look at the doorway with its yellow tape lying across the sill like an unfinished sentence. She looked at the kettle.
"It's clicked."
"Last case," Derek admitted.
"Wrong kettle for that face." She took it to the sink, emptied it, and filled it again, which was not science, and which Derek recognised as the most competent thing anyone had done since ten. "Trevor. Corridor. You've had the morning."
"The door won't close."
"Then your back will have to do."
Trevor faced the filing cabinet. He did it with dignity. It was a very slight dignity, and it was entirely sincere.
"The board says yesterday," Penny reported.
"I didn't ask what the board says." Tabitha set out a mug. "Tea?"
"Yes."
"Milk?"
"No."
"Sugar?"
"One."
Her hand had already gone for a second spoon, because she had read his face, and his face had been a two-sugar face since ten past ten. The hand came back without it. She did not ask why the face and the number disagreed. She stirred once and put the mug in front of him, and he understood that she had filed the disagreement somewhere private and would bring it out later, at a time of her choosing, possibly in a doorway.
Trevor, facing the cabinet, saved the form. It was the only thing left to do with it, and saving was the Bureau's one reliable act of faith. The save took its time. The little wheel turned. And then the status line changed with no one's fingers on the keyboard at all.
CASE STATUS: NOT YET OCCURRED.
SENDER: PENDING AUTHOR.
BOARDING: REFUSED, THIS ROOM, THIS NOW.
"I didn't type that," Trevor told the cabinet.
"We know."
"I want it on record that I didn't type the third line. I agree with it. I didn't type it."
Under the third line, a fourth appeared. It came up letter by letter, the way a sentence comes up when somebody is thinking about each word.
THE NEXT WARNING WILL NOT BE SENT TO YOU.
Derek straightened the apple. It was already straight. It had been straight since Penny put it there, and it was round, and there was no such thing as straight for an apple, and he straightened it anyway.
"Then who is it for?" Penny said.
The board changed.
Everybody saw it, through the open door, over the empty train. Not to a time. Not to a station. The amber dots rearranged themselves the way they had rearranged themselves once before, in another week, around another word, and they spelled a name. Six letters. It held for three seconds. Derek counted them. He would remember, later, that he had counted, and be ashamed of how steady the counting was.
SOPHIE.
Then YESTERDAY again, as though nothing had been said.
Nobody in the room supplied a theory. That was the measure of it. In seventeen years Derek had never known the Bureau to go a full three seconds without a theory.
Tabitha put the mug she was holding down on the London side of the sill, where the carpet still was, and kept her hand on it for a moment, as if it were something that might be taken.
"She's at school," she said. "You are not going to the gates."
"No."
"Say it so I believe it."
"I am not going to the gates."
His phone buzzed in his hand. Sophie. He read the message twice, which was once more than Sophie would have approved of.
Dad. A Bureau heading opened on my phone and closed. I didn't read it. I didn't fetch it back. Double maths. Don't be weird at the gates.
The phone buzzed again before he could think about what to type, which was, he would decide later, the universe's single act of mercy that day. Tuppence. She did not wait for hello. She never had.
"She texted me. The school rang me first. They always do. She is in double maths. She is at the second desk from the window and she is bored, and she is going to stay bored until a quarter past three, and you will not turn up looking like a railway. If this is an explanation, save it." The faintest pause, as if a committee were checking its minutes. "Relativistic, my arse."
Gone.
Penny already had the school office. She had not been asked. She did not, as a rule, need to be. "Don't fetch her. Don't tell her we rang. Yes. Her mother already did. We're the other one." She listened for a moment longer, said thank you in a voice that ended the call, and did not add a moral. The office receptionist would, Derek suspected, be telling that story for years without ever knowing what it was about.
Weinstein had a sleeve half on. He looked at the sleeve. He took it off again, and folded the jacket over the back of the chair he had refused, which was the closest he would ever come to sitting.
"I am not leaving until I know which act is still in front of us," he said. "The four o'clock can be caught at ten to. I have caught trains at ten to for forty years. A child is not a tense I am willing to guess."
"Drink that before it files itself," Tabitha said to Derek.
He drank it. One sugar. It was exactly right and it did nothing at all, and he was grateful to it anyway.
Trevor, still facing the cabinet, informed it that he was still present.
"We know," Penny said.
"I was saying it for the cabinet."
"The cabinet knows too."
Derek put the mug down and looked at the form on the screen, at its four lines of grey certainty, and at the gap underneath the fourth where something else might yet appear. Two acts, the sheet had named, if you counted the voice. A door, which was done. A train, which was not. And now a name with no act attached to it at all, hung in amber above a railway like washing.
"Two times," he said, "and a sign on each gap. Before we touch the name."
"That's the first useful sentence since ten," Penny judged.
"It's the only one I've got."
"It's the one we needed. Don't spoil it with a second."
Rule: Do not name the next act until you have two times and a sign on the gap. A name is not a sign.

Chapter 3
The Late Sheet
It was Penny who said they needed a control, and Penny who said it as though she had been waiting all morning for the men to stop enjoying the problem.
"Every number we have came off a clock in a room with a railway in the wall," she said. "I want a clock in a room with a railway where a railway is supposed to be. I want to stand next to a train that leaves when the board says it leaves, and see if anything about us is lying."
"Paddington," Weinstein proposed. "I have to buy a ticket in any case."
"You could buy it on your phone."
"I could. Then I would not have been to the station. I like to visit the place I am leaving from. It makes the leaving honest."
So they left the platform in the doorway and went to a station that existed.
Trevor drove Penny and Weinstein in the Mini, which was the colour of a bruise in its second week and made a noise on corners like a man being asked about his tax return. Derek walked, because Derek walked; he had no licence, and he mentioned this to Weinstein on the stairs without being asked, out of a lifelong sense that people ought to be warned. Tabitha walked with him. She did not ask about the board. He did not offer. They covered most of a mile in a silence that was not empty, past a bus shelter, a florist, two men arguing about a van, and a school that was not Sophie's, which Derek looked at anyway and then made himself stop looking at.
At the corner of Praed Street she slowed, and stopped, and did not go further.
"I said I'd stand where London is," she said. "This is London. That's a station. I've had enough doorways today, and stations are mostly doorways with a timetable."
"I'll be an hour."
"Then I'll be here in an hour."
Paddington was doing Monday in the ordinary way. Brunel's great roof hung over everything in its long arches of iron and glass, letting in a grey light that made the whole concourse look like it had been developed slightly too long. Pigeons conducted their business at ankle height with the air of tenants who had been there first. The departure board changed for reasons, as departure boards do, rearranging Reading and Oxford and Bristol Temple Meads with no apparent malice. Among them, in the same tired white as everything else, was a train to the airport at 16:00, with Weinstein's name not on it because he had not yet bought the ticket.
He bought the ticket. He did it at a machine, standing very upright, and for the forty seconds it took he looked briefly like a member of the public, which he resented and Penny enjoyed more than anything that had happened since the weekend.
"There," she announced. "You exist. The railway knows about you."
"The railway knows about my card."
"Same thing, these days."
He put the ticket in the inside pocket of the jacket, the only pocket he trusted. "There. Now when I leave at four, I shall leave after I have got on. In that order. You may all watch."
"Greece is a week Thursday," Penny said.
She said it without preamble, looking at the board and not at him. Weinstein did not pretend not to understand.
"Send me the number from the air."
"You want to be annoyed in Munich."
"I am counting on it. You will be about ten billionths of a second older than the rest of us by the time you land, and I shall be the only person in Bavaria who knows. That is a very exclusive sort of annoyance. One does not get it from just anybody."
"Then I'll send it."
She said it as she said most decisions, once, and put the itinerary back in her handbag under the face-down photograph, where it had lived since the old Tuesday. Neither of them looked surprised. Trevor, standing slightly to one side with his hands in his pockets, looked as if he had been shown a second procedure he did not have, and was trying to work out where in the manual it had been all this time.
"Lost property," Derek suggested.
"Why lost property?"
"Because if a station has anything that doesn't belong to it, that's where it keeps it."
Lost property, at Paddington, was a window and a man who had already decided their problem was not his. He had the moustache of a man who had once meant to grow a better one and had settled. He was not Pendleton. Pendleton was a specialist; Pendleton had spent decades refining the art of a form being in another building. This man was merely tired. He had the tiredness of someone who had spent twenty years being handed umbrellas by people who were sure they had left a violin.
"Bureau," Penny said, and laid nothing on the counter, because they had nothing he was obliged to take.
He looked at them for a while. Then, with the resignation of a man who knows the answer before the question has finished arriving, he pulled a slip from a wire tray at his elbow. The heading was theirs.
RELATIVISTIC INVESTIGATION BUREAU.
Under it: CASE STATUS: ALREADY OCCURRED.
Under that, yesterday's date. Sunday. An act, typed in the same flat capitals as the sheet in Derek's pocket: OPENING THE DOOR. Two times, in a hand that was not theirs: small, upright, careful in the way of someone who did not expect to be believed. 18:00. 18:17.
And at the bottom, in the typewriter's capitals again:
PLEASE NOTE: THIS WARNING WAS SEVENTEEN MINUTES LATE.
"We weren't here on Sunday," Trevor said.
"No one is saying you were." The man slid the slip across the counter with one finger, as if it might stick. "It was in the tray this morning. Nobody handed it in. Nobody's come for it. I don't file metaphysics. I file objects people pretend they meant to come back for."
"Is this your form?"
Pendleton would have asked it with sorrow, Derek thought. Penny asked it without.
"It is not anyone's form," the man said. "Take it off my counter."
They took it off his counter.
On the concourse, under the board and beside a bench that was mostly pigeon, Weinstein held the Sunday slip next to Trevor's envelope with the capitals on it. He held them the way he held patent drawings in Schwabing, one in each hand, at arm's length, as if the truth were a matter of focal distance.
Trevor read them both twice. Then he said what Derek had known he was going to say from the moment the slip came out of the tray.
"Seventeen and seventeen."
"No," Penny said.
"I haven't said anything else yet."
"You were going to. You were going to say it's a signature. You were going to say the seventeens are talking to each other and we should listen. The last case was fond of the number. I'm not."
"It's the same number."
"It's the same numeral. Look at what it's counting." She took the slip out of Weinstein's hand, which nobody else would have dared. "Sunday is a notice at 18:17 for an opening at 18:00. That gap is the warning arriving after the act. Somebody opened a door, and seventeen minutes later a piece of paper said please don't. That's a miss. That's how late a warning can be. Today we finished reading at 10:22. That seventeen is how long we stood there. Same numeral. Not the same gap." She put the slip back, very precisely, into Weinstein's fingers. "And this slip is the previous warning. The back of this morning's sheet was pointing at Sunday, not at us."
Trevor looked at the slip, and at his envelope, and at the slip again, and Derek watched him take the glasses off and put them on again, which in Trevor meant a theory being lowered gently into the ground.
"The previous warning was sent too late. And it was. By seventeen minutes. It's written on it."
"It's the only honest thing on it."
"The sign on Sunday is late," Weinstein said. "That is clean. Today the voice is late for the opening, and we do not know by how much, because nobody wrote the opening down. We were all looking at the paper. The sheet was already in the pocket before the door. Finishing the reading at 10:22 does not move it. A cause after an act cannot be the cause of the act. Munich cannot vote the order backwards."
"Nobody's asked Munich to vote."
"Trevor has asked Munich to vote. With his eyebrows."
"And we are not making a signature out of being slow," Penny said. "Herbert can sort Sunday. He cannot give us a clock for a voice we only know came after."
Trevor had his pencil out. He was doing, Derek saw, the thing Trevor did when a number had been taken away from him: he was finding out exactly what it had been worth.
"If you only had the numerals, you'd think they were the same thing. If you had the events, you'd never confuse them. One is a gap between an act and its warning. One is a gap between us noticing and us finishing. The second one isn't about the sender at all. It's about us. It's how long it takes four people to read a sheet of paper."
"Four people and a frightened one," Penny amended.
"I wasn't frightened."
"I meant Derek."
Derek had not spoken since the slip. He was standing a little apart from them, watching the departure board as if it might put a child on it.
It did not put a child on it. It put cities on it, one after another, in the patient white of a public service: Reading, Didcot Parkway, Swansea, Penzance. The airport at 16:00. It said nothing about yesterday. It said nothing about anyone he loved. It was, he realised, the first board he had looked at all day that was simply doing its job, and he could have kissed it, and he would have been arrested, and Tuppence would have heard about it before the police did.
"It's a train station," Penny murmured, at his elbow. "It's only allowed to be a train station."
"I know."
"Then stop reading it like a ransom note."
He stopped. Mostly.
Tabitha had come as far as the pavement and no further. She was exactly where she had said she would be, on Praed Street, by a bollard, and had plainly not moved except to be rained on. She had said she would stand where London was, and Paddington's pavement qualified.
She did not ask what the slip said. She asked him.
"Say it here. Not in a doorway. Doorways have started lying."
He had not planned it. He had been planning, if anything, the opposite: some later evening, some other door, a better sentence with more architecture in it. He found that the morning had removed his architecture along with his door.
"The shut evenings were me wanting you in the room," he said. "I still want that. The door can stay open. I want you there anyway."
A bus went past. Somebody's suitcase wheel shrieked. A pigeon considered them and moved on, unpersuaded.
"That's the sentence," Tabitha said.
"Yes."
"Keep it. I'm still not a platform."
"You're not a platform."
"Good. Then you can go back in there and do your sums, and I'll be here when you come out, and it will still be the sentence."
She stayed on the pavement. He went back inside with the slip, which was a worse kind of courage, and she let him, which was the same kind.
Rule: Sunday's notice was seventeen minutes after Sunday's opening, and that notice could not prevent it. Monday's seventeen minutes is how long the office took to read. The voice is late for the door. The sheet is not. Do not copy the number from one gap onto the other.

Chapter 4
Two Times, No Third Person
They made the calls from the bench that was mostly pigeon, because it was the only seat on the concourse with a view of both the departure board and the street door, and Derek wanted both in sight: the honest board, and the pavement where Tabitha was.
Herbert Matkowski answered on the second ring, as if the telephone were a blackboard he intended to keep clean.
"Matkowski."
"It's Derek Gent. In London."
"I know where you are. Alfred told me on Saturday that he was going to London to be polite for a week, and I told him he had never in his life been polite for a week, and that something would happen to rescue him. What has happened to rescue him?"
Derek told him. He had rehearsed it on the walk, without meaning to, the way a man rehearses a phone call to a bank, and it came out in the order the morning had taught him.
"Sunday," Derek said. "An opening at eighteen hundred. A notice at eighteen seventeen. That notice is late. Today the sheet was already in the pocket, then the door opened, then the voice said don't. We noticed the open door at ten oh five. We finished reading at ten twenty-two. That interval is us. The voice is late for the opening. I cannot tell you the size. The sheet is not."
There was a silence on the line. It was not an empty silence. It was the silence of a man drawing something.
"That is a coordinate," Herbert said. "The voice is after the door. The sheet is not the late one. You have stopped enjoying the wrong order."
"We weren't enjoying it."
"Alfred was enjoying it. I could hear him enjoying it from here." A pause, in which, Derek suspected, a ruler was laid very carefully along a line. "Mr Gent, I will tell you what I have drawn, and then I will go back to my afternoon. Your office has a history. It goes up the page, one line, because your office does not go anywhere. Everything that happened in it this morning is a point on that line. The sheet is a point. The door is a point. The voice is a point. They are on one line, and one line has one order. You do not need me to give you the order. You need a pencil."
"And the train?"
"The train is a point you have not reached. It is further up the line. You may or may not put a mark there. That is your business, not geometry's."
"And the form. It wants a date of birth for the sender."
"Then the form is asking for the wrong point." Herbert said it with the satisfaction of a man correcting a student who has written a perfectly good answer to a different question. "A birthday is where a person's line begins. It tells you nothing about where on that line they did anything. Your sheet was not caused by somebody being born. It was caused, if it was caused, by somebody acting. Ask the form for the act. Acts have times. Births are only the first of them."
"Alfred is not enjoying it now," Derek said, because Weinstein was standing three feet away making a face.
"Alfred is always enjoying it. It is how I know he has not finished." A pause, in which a lifetime of marking Weinstein's homework considered its options. "If the order is already fixed in one room, you do not need me. You need to stop asking the order to be a person."
He ended the call.
Weinstein, who had heard his own name twice and the word homework once, took it with the calm of a man who had been graded in public since he was nineteen.
"He hung up."
"He does that when he is right and lazy about the rest," Weinstein said. "I learned it from him. He thinks he learned the laziness from me. The records are unclear."
"He said we need a pencil."
"He always says that. He said it to me in nineteen ninety-one about a problem that took me eleven years. He was right, the pencil was what I needed. I had to find out which way up to hold it." Weinstein sat down on the end of the bench, then remembered that he was not sitting today and stood up again. "Ring Julius. He will refuse to come. Let him. The refusing is where he keeps his best ideas."
Julius Barbarian declined the journey in his first sentence, before Derek had suggested it.
"I am not coming to London to watch you notice before and after."
"I wasn't going to ask you to come."
"Everybody is always going to ask me to come. It is the second thing people want from me, after an apology for the first thing I said." His voice was amused and entirely unhurried. Behind it Derek could hear, faintly, something ticking, and then something else ticking out of step with it, and then a third thing that might have been a clock or might have been a man tapping a spoon to prove a point. "Tell me what you have."
Derek told him. Barbarian let him get as far as the slip from lost property, and then interrupted, which Derek had been warned he enjoyed more than agreement.
"Events are not a substance, Mr Gent. You keep trying to put them in a drawer as if they were cutlery. The relation is the case. Door before voice. Sheet before door. Boarding not yet. You have the relation. It is in your hand, and you are staring past it at the board, hoping the board will be a person."
"The board showed a name."
"A name is a very small substance. Smaller than a teaspoon. You can stir an entire morning with it and it will still be a name." The ticking went on behind him. "Boarding has not happened. The door has. That is two. If you want a third event, a sender, a printer, a man in a hat behind a curtain, you are lonely, not scientific."
"That's a hard thing to tell a man whose daughter's name was on a railway."
There was a short pause, and when Barbarian spoke again, the amusement had gone out of his voice and something plainer had come in to replace it.
"It is. I am sorry. I am still right. The two things are both true, and I have found, at my age, that the second does not make the first go away. Look at the relations, Mr Gent. Look at what she has done and what she has not done. Do not look at the letters. Letters are what people use when they want you to feel something before you know something."
"I'll tell Alfred you said hello."
"Tell Alfred he is still very clever and still in a hurry. He will know which half to be offended by. I remain in Munich, where the doors stay doors."
"I heard," Weinstein called, close enough to the phone to be sure he was overheard. "Tell him the hurry has a ticket."
"I heard that," Barbarian said, and rang off, delighted.
Sherlock came in as Sherlock came in, which was not in person.
Trevor's phone woke on his knee without being touched. The screen did not show a number. Nobody had ever seen Sherlock's number, and Accounts had stopped asking; the line on the Bureau's books still read Miscellaneous Marine, and the receipts were for mackerel. A single line of text arrived in the middle of a white screen, the way a stone arrives at the bottom of a pond.
No.
Trevor looked at it for a long moment.
"He's refusing the case."
"He can't refuse the case," Derek objected. "We haven't offered it to him."
"He's refusing it in advance. Before the offer. That's—" Trevor stopped. "That's very on-theme."
"Then he isn't closing it," Penny said. "We still have to write the times."
Nobody argued. There had been a week, not long ago, when the dolphin had seen the shape of the whole thing before the rest of them had seen its corners, and the memory of that week sat over the bench like weather. It was tempting, very tempting, to wait for him. Derek found that he did not want to. A witness who declines is a witness who has decided this one belongs to the people in the room.
Derek looked at the name on Trevor's envelope. Trevor had written it down in the outer room, in capitals like everything else, because it had been on the board and the Bureau wrote down what was on boards. SOPHIE. It sat at the bottom of the list, under the times, unstruck.
"Sophie is a person," Derek said.
"Sophie is a name on a board for three seconds," Penny said. "That's not yet a finding. I'm not striking it in a station."
"I'm not asking you to strike it."
"You're asking me to do something with it. Your face is asking. Nothing is what we do with it. For now."
They did not ring Sophie again. The school had been told. The gate had been refused, twice, by the two people with the right to refuse it. The name sat on Trevor's envelope, unstruck, unexplained and unmoved, which was, Derek thought, the most honest thing on the page; it was the only line on it that did not pretend to know more than it did.
The board above them clicked over. The 16:00 to the airport moved up a place. Somewhere in Munich, a man with very good handwriting had gone back to his afternoon, and another had gone back to his clocks, and in an office in London a train that was going nowhere was costing the landlord a fortune in metaphor.
Rule: A witness who declines is not a solution. Write the times yourself.

Chapter 5
What She Didn't Write
The hour between the calls and the end of school was the longest of the day, and nobody filled it with anything useful. Weinstein bought a newspaper he did not read and a coffee he described, after one sip, as an argument against the European project. Trevor sat with the slip and recopied the times onto a second envelope in case the first was confiscated by a pigeon. Penny rang Mrs Marsh twice and was told, twice, that the platform was still a platform, and on the second occasion that the landlord had now sent a second email, which was in capitals, which Mrs Marsh did not hold with. Derek stood. When he sat, he got up again. Every time a family went past with a child in a school jumper, he looked, and every time he made himself look away, and every time Penny watched him do it and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of commentary.
At a quarter past three, the school let go of its children in the ordinary way, all at once and with no regard for anyone's afternoon. At seventeen minutes past, Derek's phone rang. He noticed the minute and declined to have an opinion about it.
It was Sophie. She had not texted first. Waiting to be managed was not a hobby she kept.
"I still haven't written it down," she said, without hello, in the voice she used for reading out results. "The heading. I didn't read it, so it isn't data. If you want it in the notebook, say so. If you don't, say that. Don't be weird, and don't send Mum a second time. She already did the gates."
Behind her he could hear the particular roar of three hundred children discovering that it was no longer double maths. He could also hear that she had walked a little way from it, to somewhere with a wall, so that he would get her sentences whole.
"Don't write it."
"Then I won't. That's data too." A pause, while she rearranged something in her bag. "I'll put a line that says I was asked not to file a thing I didn't see. Otherwise you'll decide later that the gap was a mystery."
"The gap is a mystery. You are not the gap."
She was quiet.
It was the quiet he went into himself, when a problem was too large to be looked at directly and had to be approached from the side, like a horse. He had never once admitted to recognising it in her, and she had never once pretended not to know where she'd got it. It went on for four seconds. He did not count them. He noticed that he had wanted to.
"Somebody sent me a heading with no body," she said at last. "I didn't open it. If I write it down because it had my name near it, then it's in my notebook because it had my name near it, and then it's evidence it was about me because it's in my notebook. That's circular. Mr Okafor says circular is how you can tell somebody's selling you something."
"Mr Okafor is right."
"He's right about most things except fractions. He's frightened of fractions." Another pause. "Good. Double maths was ordinary. The phone wasn't. I'm going home. Julian's hangers are in the hall, in case you were about to ask whose life is whose. I'm not discussing them. Bye."
She rang off. She was twelve. She was not on a train.
Derek sat down on a station chair as if the chair had been recommended by a doctor. It was a metal chair with holes in it, bolted to three others in a row, designed by somebody who had wanted people to wait but not to enjoy it. He sat in it with his elbows on his knees and the phone in both hands.
"She won't file it," he said.
"Then she has no act on the sheet," Penny said.
Penny had remained standing. Penny generally did, in stations; she regarded a seat on a concourse as an admission that the railway had won. She stood over him now with her handbag on her arm and the photograph in it and the itinerary under the photograph, and she did not touch his shoulder, which he was grateful for, because if she had he would have had to notice that he needed it.
"The prohibition we were given names boarding. It does not name a notebook. It does not name a heading on a phone. The board named her anyway."
"Why?"
"Because the board wanted a person and she was the nearest one."
Trevor had been sitting at the end of the row of chairs with the slip on his knee, the way a man might sit with a sleeping cat he has been told not to disturb. He looked up.
"The last file reached for the nearest name when it wanted a person," he offered, with the care of a man stepping onto ice he had fallen through once. "It reached for me. I was in the room. Everybody looked at me. I wasn't the murder."
"Don't solve 1047 again," Penny said.
"I'm not solving it. I'm saying it happened. The photograph wanted somebody in the doorway and somebody in the doorway was what it got."
"Solve the habit, then. Not the case." Penny ticked it off on her fingers, which she did only when she was angry and wished to appear organised. "An empty person-field. It wants filling. It fills with the nearest name it can find. Last week that was you. This week it's a twelve-year-old who wasn't on the platform, wasn't in the room, didn't touch the sheet and didn't read the heading. She is the nearest name it has for a child-shaped blank, and it is not getting one."
"We haven't struck her yet."
"No."
"Why not?"
"Because striking her before we know the open act is how you delete a girl to tidy a form." Penny's voice had gone quick and flat, the voice she used when agreement and sarcasm were briefly the same thing. "The open act is still boarding. Sixteen hundred hasn't happened. She has refused the only act anyone could pretend was hers. Those are two different sentences. Keep them in two different lines. If we strike her now, we're striking her because we're frightened, and the next time a board puts a name up we'll strike that one because we're frightened too, and one day it'll be a name that did have an act and we'll have struck it out of habit."
"So we wait."
"We wait until four. Then we strike her because it's true, not because it's soothing."
Weinstein, who had been standing with his back to a pillar studying the departure board as if it owed him money, turned round. "She is right about the loop, the girl," he said. "Your daughter. Not Penny. Penny is also right, but your daughter is right first, and she is right about the harder thing."
"Which thing?"
"That a name in a notebook becomes evidence because it is in the notebook. That is how people get themselves into closed curves, Mr Gent, and not only in physics. The file says it is about her; she writes it down; the writing makes it about her; the file was right. Very tidy. Nobody has done anything at all, and yet a girl is guilty of a sentence." He considered this. "She refused to be the pen. That is the best thing anybody has done today, and she did it at a school with a wall to lean on."
Derek did not answer. He did not trust his voice, which had never changed register in seventeen years and was not going to start now in front of Paddington.
Tabitha, on the pavement, had heard none of this and understood the posture. She had seen him through the glass of the doors, bent forward on a station chair with a phone in both hands, and she had read it the way she read weather. She came as far as the doors and no further. She did not come in.
"Alive?" she asked, when he got up and came to her.
"At home. Not writing it down."
"Good." She looked at him for a moment, at the hands, at the shoulders, at whatever part of a man shows that he has spent three seconds of the afternoon counting a child's name. "Then you can stop standing like a platform. Come and be a man who has a train to put someone else on."
"She said something," Derek told her.
"She usually does."
"She said if she wrote the heading down because it had her name near it, it would be evidence it was about her because it was in her notebook. She said that was circular."
Tabitha considered the traffic on Praed Street, a taxi, a cyclist with a dog in a basket who was not Gucci and would never be forgiven by Gucci for the impersonation.
"So the board wanted her to do its homework for it," she remarked, in exactly the tone she would have used to order a flat white. "Put her name on its own answer, so it could mark itself right."
"Weinstein said closed curve."
"Weinstein would. It's the same thing with a longer word." She pulled her coat a little tighter, against nothing in particular. "She didn't do it."
"No."
"Then she's cleverer than the board. That's not news, but it's nice when the board finds out." She looked at him, and for a moment the flat delivery went somewhere less flat. "And you didn't go to the gates."
"I didn't go to the gates."
"I know. I was here. I'd have seen you go." Her hair was pulled back the way Sophie's was, with the same impatience, and he had noticed that before, at lunch, and had decided not to mention it, and decided again now. "Nearly four. He's got a ticket. Go and watch him use it."
Rule: An empty field will take the nearest name. Nearness is not guilt. Do not strike the name until the act it was standing in for is known.

Chapter 6
The Sign on the Gap
They wrote it on the back of the Sunday slip, because the front was already a warning and the back had been left for people who could subtract.
Trevor wrote. He had the best handwriting of the three of them, which was not saying a great deal, and he was the only one who could be trusted not to add an adjective. He knelt at the end of the row of metal chairs and used the seat as a desk. Penny stood over him. Derek stood over Penny. Weinstein stood over everybody, technically, by virtue of being on the far side of the chairs and reading upside down, which was unnecessary and typical.
"Start with what we saw," Penny directed.
"We saw the door already open."
"When?"
"Ten oh five. When Mrs Marsh came through. That's the first time anybody looked at a clock."
"Then write that. Not that it opened at ten oh five. That it was noticed."
Trevor wrote.
DOOR NOTICED ALREADY OPEN: 10:05. OPENING EARLIER. TIME UNKNOWN.
"Earlier by how much?" he asked.
"We don't know."
"Can I put a question mark?"
"You've put TIME UNKNOWN. That is a question mark that's been to school." Penny tapped the slip. "Now the order. Before the clock."
"The sheet," Derek answered. "Alfred was holding it. Then the door. Then the voice."
"You're sure of that order?"
"I was there for it. I can't tell you when. I can tell you which way round."
"That's all order is," Weinstein remarked, upside down. "Which way round. The when is a luxury."
ORDER IN THE ROOM, BEFORE THAT CLOCK: SHEET, THEN THE DOOR, THEN THE VOICE.
"Now the signs. One gap at a time. Voice against the door."
"Late," Trevor said. "The voice said don't open the door. The door was already open. The voice came after. It's late."
"How late?"
"Unknown." He wrote it, and then sat back on his heels and looked at the line as if it had insulted him. "And it can't have done anything. Can it. A warning that arrives after the door has opened can't keep the door shut."
"No," Weinstein agreed. "It can be recorded. It can be regretted. It cannot be obeyed. The door is in its past. You can only send advice to the future, Trevor. Anything in your past has already made its decision without you."
VOICE AGAINST THE OPENING: LATE. SIZE: UNKNOWN. CANNOT HAVE KEPT THE DOOR SHUT.
"Sheet against the door."
"Earlier," Trevor reasoned. "The sheet was in the room first. So it's not late for the door. It's not the late one at all."
"And who sent it?"
"Write the truth," Derek told him.
SHEET AGAINST THE OPENING: EARLIER. NOT LATE. SENDER STILL UNKNOWN.
"The reading next," Penny said. "And put it in its box, Trevor, and shut the lid on it. Ten twenty-two."
Trevor wrote it with the care of a man laying a dangerous object in a padded case.
PAPER READ: 10:22. THAT GAP IS HOW LONG WE READ. NOT THE SHEET'S ARRIVAL. NOT THE LATENESS OF THE OPENING.
"I still want it on record that it was seventeen," he said.
"It's on record. It's in the line. It's in its box. It's doing nothing."
"I know it's doing nothing. I just want it on record that I noticed."
"Everybody noticed, Trevor. Noticing is not the same as meaning. You of all people know that. You're a statistician by temperament. You know what a coincidence costs."
"Not much," Trevor admitted. "There are only sixty numbers on a clock. Two of them were going to match something eventually."
"Then let them match. Sunday."
SUNDAY'S OPENING: 18:00. SUNDAY'S NOTICE: 18:17. THAT GAP IS A WARNING AFTER AN ACT. DIFFERENT MEASUREMENT. SAME WORD, LATE.
Trevor looked at what they had. Five lines, and four of them the same answer in different clothes: whatever this was, it had come too late for anything already done. He felt, Derek could see, the way a man feels when he has checked every door in the house and found them all locked, and only then remembers there is a window.
"Boarding," Trevor said.
"Boarding," Penny agreed.
"The airport train is at sixteen hundred. It's twenty-five past three. That's ahead. The sheet says don't let Derek board the mysterious train. The mysterious train is still in the doorway. Nobody's on it." He paused with the pencil. "So that's the only one we can still do anything about."
"What's the sign?"
Trevor did not answer at once. Derek watched him take the glasses off, and this time it was not the funeral removal, or the theory being lowered into the ground. It was the other kind: the rare one, the one that came before something went right.
"Early. The sheet is early for boarding. The boarding is in its future. It can still change it." He looked up at Weinstein. "That's the light cone. You drew it for us last week. A message can only reach the things in front of it. The door wasn't in front of the voice. The train's in front of the sheet."
"It is in front of all of us," Weinstein said. "It is the only thing that is. Write it, before you start enjoying it."
BOARDING: NOT DONE. AIRPORT TRAIN: 16:00. FROM 10:22, STILL AHEAD. SIGN: EARLY. CAN STILL BE REFUSED.
"And her," Derek said.
Penny took the pencil from Trevor, which was a thing she had never done in his presence before and which he would later describe, to his sister, as like having a tooth removed by a friend. She wrote the last line herself, in her own handwriting, which was faster and less legible than his and entirely unambiguous.
SOPHIE: NO ACT ON THE SHEET. ASKED NOT TO FILE. REFUSED THE FILING. NAME UNSTRUCK UNTIL BOARDING IS REFUSED IN FACT.
She handed the pencil back. Nobody said anything about the fact that she had written it.
Weinstein read the whole slip upside down from start to finish, moving his lips very slightly on the times, like a man checking a bill.
"Same room," he said. "The order does not reverse because Herbert would prefer a cleaner coordinate."
"In English."

COURSE
Tense for Investigators
A short course from the Relativistic Investigation Bureau
Book Two's case was not Case 1047. Three labels for one event are the previous file. This one is one message and two acts, and the acts do not share a tense.
You should finish able to say, without this page: a cause has to sit in the past of its effect; noticing an open door is not the time it opened; being in the same room does not let you reorder what already happened; the voice can be late for the door while the sheet is still early for the train; a repeated numeral is not a repeated gap; a birthday is the wrong field for a record you are writing now; a loop will not explain a sheet that was already in your pocket, and neither will the refusal you type afterwards.
The story can be read without this course. The course is how you check the story.
Six lessons. Chapters 1 to 6 follow them in order. Chapters 7, 8, and the epilogue use Lesson 6, because by then the only honest work left is the close. No Lorentz transformation is repeated here. If you want that algebra, it is in Book One, and it was the wrong tool for a door that had already opened.

Lesson 1
Too Late Is Not a Frame
Learning objective: Say why a later event in the same room cannot be the cause of an earlier one, and why a change of frame will not rescue it.
Special relativity lets the time-order of some pairs flip. Those pairs are spacelike: nothing you can ride, light included, connects them. Two events in one office, minutes apart, are not that pair. They are timelike. Every inertial observer agrees which one happened first. Munich does not get a vote.
The door was already open at 10:05. That is when they noticed. The opening is earlier, and nobody wrote the clock time. The sheet was already in the pocket, and the room's order is sheet, then door, then the voice that said not to open it. Finishing the reading at 10:22 is not the time the sheet arrived. The voice is after the opening, so it is late for don't open, and the size of that lateness is unknown. The reading cannot be given the sheet's arrival, and it cannot be the cause of a door that was already open. Calling 10:05 the act is how you file a noticing as an event. That was the previous case's mistake, under a different noun.
Keep this: same room, minutes apart, order fixed.
Common trap: reaching for a frame change because the book before this one used frames. That tool answers a different question.
Quick test: A notice pinned at 18:17 tells you not to open a door at 18:00. Can it be the cause of the door staying shut? No.

Lesson 2
One Message, Two Acts
Learning objective: Assign a separate tense to each act a warning names.
A warning is not a single moment. It is a message paired with an act, and this file has two messages. Pair the voice with the door: the voice comes after, so that sign is late. Pair the front of the sheet with boarding: the sheet was already in the pocket before the door, and nobody has boarded, so that sign is early. Nothing in that requires the two acts to be the same event. They are not. That was the other case.
Keep this: tense belongs to the pair, not to the paper alone.
Common trap: averaging the signs into one mood called "the warning was mysterious." Mystery is not a tense.
Quick test: The paper is in your hand. The door is open. The train has not been boarded. Which pair can still change what you do? The train.

Lesson 3
Seventeen Minutes
Learning objective: Refuse to treat the time it took you to read as a warning's lateness.
Sunday's notice was seventeen minutes after Sunday's opening. That seventeen is the size of a miss: the warning came after the act. Monday's seventeen minutes runs from 10:05, when the open door was noticed, to 10:22, when the reading finished. That seventeen is how long the office stood there. The opening had already happened, and the sheet had already arrived before it. The voice's lateness against the opening has the sign LATE and an unknown size. The two seventeens share a numeral. They are not the same gap, and the numeral is not a force.
Keep this: sign first. Do not copy a number from your own slowness onto someone else's act.
Common trap: stacking the seventeens into a signature because an earlier case was fond of the digit. Fondness is not a mechanism.
Quick test: You take four minutes to finish reading a warning about a door that was already open. Is the opening four minutes in the past of the reading? No. The opening is earlier than the moment you noticed, and you do not have that time. Do you board? Still no.

Lesson 4
The Wrong Field
Learning objective: Explain why a date of birth is the wrong datum for this author.
The form asked for a birthday because it assumed a person. A person who has not been born is not in the past of the paper, so the form correctly refused tomorrow. The refusal recorded at 16:41 can author that record. It has a time, and the time is after the acts it does not claim to have caused. It is not the sender of the morning sheet. The morning sheet was already in the pocket. Acts have times. A sheet that arrived earlier still needs its own sender, and this case does not have one.
Keep this: TIME OF ACT can be in the past of the record even when DATE OF BIRTH cannot.
Common trap: searching the building for a baby because the field said not yet born. The field was the wrong question.
Quick test: What did the form accept in the end, a birth or a time? A time.

Lesson 5
The Loop You Don't Need
Learning objective: State the closed-curve trap, and why this file did not require it.
If later Derek posts the warning, he learned the sentence from a life in which he boarded, or from a life in which he did not. The first makes the warning a record of failure. The second never teaches him the sentence. That paradox is real in the sense that stories get stuck in it. It is not required here, and it is not replaced by pretending the 16:41 refusal posted the morning sheet backwards. Ordinary order tells you the sheet arrived before the door, the voice came after the door, and the front of the sheet is still early for boarding. It does not tell you who printed the sheet. Hawking's chronology protection conjecture says the laws we have do not let closed timelike curves form. This office did not test that conjecture. It declined to explain a lost-property slip, or a sheet already in a pocket, by means of one.
Keep this: do not hire a loop, and do not hire today's refusal, to explain a sheet that arrived this morning.
Common trap: treating "unborn sender" as a child. A blank is not a birth. Nearness of a name is not parenthood and not guilt.
Quick test: Did anyone in this case have to travel into their own past for the file to close? No.

Lesson 6
Capstone: File the Later Time
Learning objective: Close a case of this kind in one paragraph.
Write the earlier act first, and if you do not have its time, write that the time is unknown and earlier than the noticing. Admit the voice is late for the opening, and that the sheet is not. Write the later act second. Refuse it while the sign is still early. The record of the refusal gets a time. It does not get the morning sheet's byline. If a name appeared in an empty person-field, check whether that person had an act. If they had none, strike the name. Do not strike it to make the page calm, and do not delete the platform by saving.
The control is a real departure: a passenger on the platform before the doors shut, a train that leaves after the boarding. If your own record cannot be put in that order, you have not finished. The printer of the earlier sheet is a different question, and it may stay open.
Keep this: earlier opening left in the past, boarding refused while it was still future, sender of the morning sheet left blank.
Common trap: congratulating yourself for not boarding, calling 10:05 the opening, and leaving a child's name in the recipient line because the board was frightening.
Quick test: Say the close without looking up. Sheet, then the door, both before 10:05. Voice after the door, late, size unknown. Reading at 10:22 is how long they read. Sunday's notice, 18:17 after an 18:00 opening, a different seventeen, and the previous warning the back of the sheet meant. Boarding refused before it happened. The record at 16:41 is the refusal. Sophie had no act. The name comes off. The morning sheet's sender does not.

GLOSSARY
Cause. What sits in the past of its effect. If it doesn't, it is not the cause of that effect, however official the paper looks.
Early. The sign on a warning that still comes before the act you pair it with. You can still refuse.
Late. The sign on a warning that comes after the act you pair it with. You can record it. You cannot undo it.
Seventeen minutes. On Sunday, the gap between an opening at 18:00 and a notice at 18:17. On Monday, the gap between noticing an open door at 10:05 and finishing the reading at 10:22. Same numeral. Not the same measurement. Not a force. See Lesson 3.
Tense per act. The rule that one message gets a separate before-or-after for each act it is paired with.
Time of act. The field that replaced a birthday. An author who is a refusal has one of these and does not have the other.
Pending author. The morning sheet still has none. The record written at 16:41 names the refusal and does not claim to have printed the sheet.
Recipient. Whoever a file claims the next warning is for. Empty person-fields attract the nearest name. Nearness is not an act. Strike only when the act is known and is not theirs.
Closed curve. A history in which a signal runs back along a life and helps produce the signal. A known trap in this subject. Not the mechanism of this case.
Chronology protection. Hawking's conjecture that the laws we have do not let closed timelike curves form. Named in Lesson 5 so you do not confuse a conjecture with the subtraction that closed the file.
LOCALHOST. The originating address of the photograph in Case 1047. Not the sender of this paper. Still unidentified. Not solved by being mentioned.
YESTERDAY. On the board, a direction. On the calendar of this opening, Sunday. They are not the same object.
