APPENDIX — THE PHYSICS, APPARENTLY

PROLOGUE
In Which the Universe Is Found to Be Entirely Unhelpful
There are few things more irritating than discovering that the universe does not operate according to common sense.
This is particularly true if you are a physicist, because physicists have spent several centuries developing common sense specifically for the purpose of ignoring it.
Newton was at least fortunate in this respect. He had an apple.
An apple falls.
Everyone understands an apple falling.
One can point at it and say, with considerable confidence, "There goes the apple."
Then Einstein—or, more accurately, Alfred Weinstein—came along and ruined the whole arrangement.
Suddenly the apple was not merely falling.
It was following a geodesic through curved spacetime.
Which was considerably less satisfying.
Nobody had ever looked at a falling apple and thought, Ah yes, the affine connection appears to be doing something interesting today.
They thought, That bloody apple fell on my head.
Science progressed.
Unfortunately.
Special Relativity arrived and announced that there was no universal time.
This was inconvenient.
Human civilisation had spent thousands of years inventing clocks, calendars, appointments, trains, deadlines and people who say, "You're five minutes late."
Special Relativity looked at all this and essentially replied:
No.
Time depended upon the observer's state of motion.
Distances depended upon the observer.
Simultaneity was not absolute.
The speed of light was.
This was regarded by some as deeply profound.
By others as deeply annoying.
British railway companies, wisely, refused to comment.
Then came General Relativity.
Weinstein took the rather unfriendly step of allowing spacetime itself to become dynamical.
Gravity was no longer simply a force pulling things around.
Spacetime curved.
Matter told spacetime how to curve.
Spacetime told matter how to move.
And everybody nodded solemnly as though this were perfectly reasonable.
It wasn't.
But it worked.
That was the irritating part.
The equations worked so well that there was nothing respectable one could say against them.
Black holes appeared.
Gravitational time dilation appeared.
Light bent.
Mercury behaved itself.
Clocks disagreed exactly as predicted.
The universe, having apparently been consulted about the matter, continued doing what the equations said it should.
This left only one sensible response.
Keep asking questions.
That was how the Relativistic Investigation Bureau came into existence.
It was not founded because anyone had wanted to start a Bureau.
Nobody wants to start a Bureau.
Bureaux require stationery, filing systems, regulations, meetings and someone whose entire career consists of asking whether Form 17B has been completed in triplicate.
The Bureau existed because Derek Gent had begun investigating things.
This was unfortunate.
Derek had a habit of noticing things other people preferred not to notice.
A clock stopping at 11:17.
A train arriving before it departed.
A corridor containing twelve doors when counted by one person and thirteen when counted by another.
Rain falling upwards.
An apparently dead Derek turning up perfectly alive.
And, eventually, a cucumber.
Nobody had initially considered the cucumber important.
This was before the cucumber became involved in relativistic measurements, holography and what Penny rather regrettably called "the deeper end of the universe."
The Bureau had other problems.
There was Trevor Boltzman, who was every bit as intelligent as the others but occasionally needed a small slap to make the obvious obvious.
There was Penny, Derek's brilliant brunette cousin, who could explain tensor calculus to a roomful of bored teenagers but whose vocabulary contained an alarming number of words beginning with "bonk" and "shaft."
There was Julius Barbarian, who maintained that time did not exist.
His argument was that time was merely a convenient device for preventing everything from happening at once.
Nobody had yet found a convincing objection.
There was Hermann Minkowski, who had long since decided that spacetime was perfectly capable of speaking for itself and therefore had no patience whatsoever with people whining about it.
And then there was Alfred Weinstein.
The real inventor of Special and General Relativity.
This was, historically speaking, a slight problem.
History had attributed the work to Einstein.
Weinstein regarded this as plagiarism on a cosmological scale.
He had once attempted to correct the matter through ordinary academic channels.
The academic channels had responded by requesting Form 17B.
He had never recovered.
And now there was Sherlock.
Sherlock was a dolphin.
Nobody knew exactly how Sherlock had acquired his knowledge of differential geometry.
Nobody particularly wanted to know.
He communicated telepathically.
He had an irritatingly good understanding of General Relativity.
And he had, on one memorable occasion, sent Weinstein what amounted to the final conceptual interpretation of the Einstein field equations while Weinstein was trying to drink tea.
Weinstein had found the timing inconsiderate.
The dolphin had not apologised.
By this point, the Bureau had accepted several principles.
The first was that if an office door was open, the occupant had vanished into virtuality.
Obviously.
If the door was closed, the occupant was inside.
Obviously.
They were either working hard or shafting equally hard.
Also obviously.
The second principle was that if a clock disagreed with another clock, one should not immediately blame the clock.
The third was that if two theories appeared to contradict one another, one should first check whether one had accidentally invented a universal frame.
The fourth was that if a cucumber appeared to be involved, one should not underestimate it.
And the fifth was perhaps the most important:
If everything begins to make sense, you have almost certainly misunderstood something.
This was the world into which Derek Gent, Penny, Trevor, Weinstein, Minkowski, Barbarian and the mysterious Sherlock were about to disappear.
They would investigate relativity.
They would investigate gravity.
They would investigate time.
They would investigate holography.
They would investigate singularities.
They would investigate the London Underground.
They would investigate a train that had apparently never been built.
They would investigate whether the universe itself was merely an extremely elaborate description of something nobody had yet thought to ask about.
And, because this was Britain, they would do most of it while it was raining.
Naturally.
There was no particular reason to believe that any of this would end well.
On the other hand, there was no particular reason to believe that the universe itself knew what it was doing.
That, as it turned out, was rather comforting.
After all, if the universe was confused, there was no reason for the Bureau to feel embarrassed about it.
And if the universe wasn't confused—
well, that was considerably worse.
Because then somebody was going to have to explain it.
And nobody at the Bureau wanted that job.

APPENDIX — THE PHYSICS, APPARENTLY

A Completely Respectable Explanation of What Has Just Happened
By now you have encountered:
clocks that disagree,
trains that arrive before they leave,
corridors that acquire extra doors,
rain that occasionally develops opinions,
cucumbers,
holograms,
singularities,
dolphins,
and a man who apparently exists in more than one place.
You may reasonably be wondering whether any of this has anything whatsoever to do with physics.
Annoyingly, quite a lot of it does.
The purpose of this section is to separate the actual physics from the bits Derek made up.
This is important because Derek has a tendency to say something technically correct and then immediately ruin it with a cucumber.
The good news is that you do not need university-level mathematics to understand the central ideas.
You need arithmetic.
A little algebra.
A willingness to tolerate letters standing for things.
And the ability to accept that the universe does not care what you personally think "obviously" means.
Chapter 1 — Special Relativity Begins by Being Rude
The first thing to understand about Special Relativity is that it does not begin with the statement:
"Everything is relative."
That is a popular summary and is also rather misleading.
Einstein's Special Relativity rests on two principles:
The laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames.
The speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers.
The second statement is the troublesome one.
Suppose you are sitting on a train.
You throw a ball forward at 10 m/s.
Someone standing on the platform sees the ball moving at roughly:
train speed + 10 m/s.
Perfectly ordinary.
Now replace the ball with light.
Classical intuition says that the person on the platform should measure:
speed of train + speed of light.
Nature says:
No.
The light still travels at exactly (c).
This forces us to abandon the old assumptions about space and time.
And that is where things become interesting.
Chapter 2 — Lorentz Finally Gets Involved
The mathematical machinery connecting two inertial observers is the Lorentz transformation.
For motion along one spatial direction:
x′ = γ(x − vt)
and
t′ = γ(t − vx/c²)
where
γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)
Do not panic.
γ is merely a number that becomes increasingly annoying as your speed approaches the speed of light.
The important thing is that space and time transform together.
This is why different observers can disagree about:
distances,
elapsed times,
and whether two separated events happened simultaneously.
They do not, however, disagree about everything.
The quantity
s² = c²t² − x² − y² − z²
is invariant.
That means different observers can assign different coordinates to events while agreeing on the underlying spacetime interval.
This is the first major lesson of relativity:
Coordinates are not reality.
They are descriptions of reality.
And that distinction will become increasingly important.
Chapter 3 — Enter General Relativity
Special Relativity deals with inertial frames and flat spacetime.
But gravity causes a problem.
Einstein noticed something wonderfully simple:
If you are inside a freely falling lift, you cannot locally distinguish being in free fall from being weightless.
This is the equivalence principle.
Imagine dropping a ball inside a falling lift.
The ball and the lift are both falling.
So relative to you, the ball simply floats.
Now imagine the lift is far away from any gravitational field and accelerating upward.
You release the ball.
It appears to fall toward the floor.
Locally, the two situations can look identical.
That suggested something profound:
Perhaps gravity isn't fundamentally a conventional force.
Perhaps it is geometry.
General Relativity makes precisely that leap.
Matter and energy affect spacetime geometry.
Objects then move through that geometry.
The famous slogan is:
Matter tells spacetime how to curve; spacetime tells matter how to move.
That is considerably more accurate than blaming gravity on the apple.
Although the apple remains available for consultation.
Chapter 4 — Time May Be Less Fundamental Than You'd Like
Julius Barbarian's claim that time does not exist is deliberately provocative.
But there is serious physics hiding underneath the joke.
In relativity, there is no single universal time shared by the entire universe.
Different observers can experience different amounts of proper time between events.
Proper time is the time measured by a clock travelling along a particular worldline.
So instead of asking:
"What time is it everywhere?"
relativity encourages us to ask:
"How much proper time elapsed along this particular path through spacetime?"
That is a much better question.
And General Relativity goes further.
Gravitational fields affect the rate at which clocks run relative to one another.
A clock deeper in a gravitational field can accumulate less proper time than one farther away.
So clocks do not merely measure some universal substance called Time.
They measure something associated with their physical histories.
Barbarian therefore has a point.
Unfortunately, he also has an alarming tendency to explain it while eating biscuits.
Chapter 5 — The Apple
Newton's apple is useful because it raises the fundamental question:
Why does something fall?
Newton answered:
There is a gravitational force.
Einstein eventually answered:
The object is following a natural path through curved spacetime.
That natural path is called a geodesic.
On the surface of the Earth, an orbiting spacecraft is actually in free fall.
It is not being "held up" by a mysterious absence of gravity.
It is following its geodesic.
The astronauts inside feel weightless because they are falling together with the spacecraft.
The surface of the Earth prevents us from following our natural free-fall path.
The floor pushes upward on our feet.
Which is why standing still is, in a relativistic sense, rather more strenuous than orbiting Earth.
The apple was therefore not really falling because gravity "pulled" it downward in the Newtonian sense.
It was following the geometry.
Newton wasn't wrong.
He had simply discovered an extraordinarily good approximation to something deeper.
Chapter 6 — Singularities
Here Derek makes an important distinction.
A singularity is not simply a place where gravity is very strong.
That would be too easy.
A singularity represents a breakdown of the classical spacetime description.
One useful way of expressing this is through geodesic incompleteness.
Imagine following a freely falling observer's worldline.
Normally, you should be able to keep extending that path.
At a singularity, the classical theory can tell you that the path simply cannot be continued in the expected way.
That tells us something profound:
The theory has reached its limit.
This is why physicists don't normally say:
"There's a really strong gravitational field over there, so that's a singularity."
They ask whether the mathematical structure of spacetime itself becomes pathological.
And that distinction matters enormously when we discuss black holes and the early universe.
A singularity is not necessarily an object sitting somewhere waiting to be photographed.
It may be telling us that General Relativity is incomplete at that point.
Which is physicist-speak for:
"The equations have stopped being helpful. Someone should probably invent some better ones."
Chapter 7 — Why SR and GR Don't Fight
Derek's Underground investigation eventually gets to the central point.
Special Relativity and General Relativity are not competing theories in the sense that one must defeat the other.
General Relativity contains Special Relativity locally.
In a sufficiently small region of spacetime, a freely falling observer can construct a local inertial frame.
Within that small region, physics looks like Special Relativity.
So:
SR = local flat-spacetime physics
while
GR = the larger theory describing curved spacetime and how those local frames fit together.
This is why the theories can coexist perfectly well.
The Underground was therefore not going to discover that SR was "wrong."
It was more likely to reveal that Derek had chosen a sufficiently inconvenient coordinate system.
This is an important distinction.
Chapter 8 — The Door
The Bureau's open-door rule is obviously ridiculous.
It is also an excellent metaphor for something real.
Physics is full of boundaries between descriptions.
An observer may describe an object as being in one place while another observer assigns different coordinates to it.
The underlying physics doesn't change merely because the description changes.
This is another recurring lesson:
A coordinate description is not an invariant physical fact.
If the Bureau's doors behaved like spacetime coordinates, Derek could quite legitimately say:
"The door is open in my coordinate system."
And Penny could reply:
"Yes, but it's closed in mine."
Trevor would then require the aforementioned slap.
Chapter 9 — Relativistic Travel
Now we get to the fun bit.
Suppose a spacecraft travels extremely fast relative to Earth.
Its clock measures its own proper time.
An observer on Earth measures a different elapsed time.
The relationship involves the Lorentz factor:
γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)
At ordinary speeds, γ is extremely close to 1.
At speeds approaching (c), it becomes large.
This produces time dilation.
It also produces length contraction in the direction of motion.
But remember:
Nobody experiences their own clock as running slowly.
Your own clock always appears normal to you.
It is the comparison between different worldlines that produces the effect.
So if Derek flies to another star at enormous speed and returns, he might have aged considerably less than people who stayed on Earth.
This is not because his watch malfunctioned.
His watch correctly measured his proper time.
And this is why booking Ryanair is not actually an established method of achieving relativistic time travel.
Probably.
Chapter 10 — The Cucumber
The cucumber is useful because it is boring.
Boring objects are excellent physics experiments.
If you move a cucumber rapidly relative to an observer, Special Relativity predicts that its measured length along the direction of motion is contracted by a factor of 1/γ.
The cucumber itself does not feel squashed.
Its own rest-frame measurement remains unchanged.
Again:
different observers assign different measurements.
But the underlying relativistic structure is consistent.
And this is precisely why the cucumber becomes such a useful running joke.
It is an ordinary object.
If the cucumber obeys relativity, so does the universe.
Probably.
The cucumber has, however, declined to comment.
Chapter 11 — The Hologram
Now we enter much more speculative territory.
The holographic principle arose from thinking about black-hole entropy.
Black holes have an entropy proportional to the area of their event horizon, not simply the volume inside.
That suggested something extraordinary:
Perhaps the information describing a region of space can be encoded on its boundary.
In certain theories, this idea becomes mathematically precise.
The most famous example is AdS/CFT duality, associated especially with Juan Maldacena.
Very roughly:
A gravitational theory in a higher-dimensional spacetime can be equivalent to a quantum field theory without gravity living on a lower-dimensional boundary.
This does not mean the universe is literally a two-dimensional cinema screen.
It means that two apparently different mathematical descriptions can encode the same underlying physics.
And that connects beautifully with relativity.
In Special Relativity, different observers use different coordinates.
In General Relativity, different coordinate systems can describe the same geometry.
In holographic duality, dramatically different-looking theories can describe the same physical system.
The common philosophical lesson is:
The description is not necessarily the underlying reality.
Penny watching Everything Everywhere All at Once therefore doesn't prove holography.
It merely gives her an unusually entertaining way of thinking about different descriptions of reality.
And Jamie Lee Curtis proves nothing whatsoever.
Except possibly that Hollywood has its own theory of conservation of actresses.
Chapter 12 — The Cinema Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics
Penny has decided that Derek is becoming unbearable.
Not because he is wrong. That would be easier.
So she does the sensible thing about a man who is annoyingly correct.
She goes to the cinema. Alone.
She watches Everything Everywhere All at Once.
She becomes unexpectedly absorbed in its multiverse logic, live-texts Derek an increasingly competent theory of holography from row F, and ends the evening staring at a cucumber on the screen while, somewhere in London, a clock stops at 11:17.
Again.
That is what happened.
The joke is that a film about interdimensional laundromats and sentient hot-dog fingers accidentally becomes the most rigorous physics seminar in the book.
Largely because Jamie Lee Curtis's remarkable career longevity is doing all the comedy, while Penny quietly reconstructs two chapters of relativity using nothing but popcorn and mild contempt.
Now for the actual physics, which Penny gets right and the poster does not.
First, the disclaimer Penny makes herself: the film's multiverse is not the multiverse that occasionally shows up in serious physics.
Real physicists do debate a handful of multiverse-shaped ideas — different interpretations of quantum mechanics, different regions produced by an early, rapidly expanding universe, that sort of thing.
None of them look remotely like a laundromat, and you do not need to know a single one of their names to follow this chapter.
The film is a storytelling device. Treat it exactly the way you would treat the cucumber: an excellent aid to intuition and completely inadmissible as evidence.
What the film is quietly useful for is an idea this book has been building since Chapter 2. It doesn't need a single equation.
Physics is whatever every honest description of a situation agrees on. It is not the description itself.
Here is the plain-English version.
Say the same true sentence in English and in German.
The words are completely different. The grammar is completely different.
What the sentence means has not changed at all.
Physicists call the thing that survives translation an observable — something you could actually go out and measure.
Everything else is just which language you happened to use.
You have already watched this happen twice, in plainer clothes.
In Chapter 2, two observers disagreed about distances, durations, even which of two events happened first — yet agreed on one particular combination of those numbers, the invariant interval.
Different measuring sticks. Same underlying fact.
In Chapter 11, a theory of gravity in a larger, higher-dimensional space turned out to be secretly identical to a gravity-free theory living on that space's boundary. Two descriptions that share no vocabulary whatsoever, describing the exact same physics.
It is a bit like Weinstein filing his patent notes in German and Trevor filing the same conclusions in English. Argue about the language all you like — the invention still works.
Penny's contribution, from row F, is to notice the pattern and ask the next obvious question.
If measuring sticks and entire mathematical languages are optional, what about a person's own life story?
A “worldline” is just the technical term for someone's path through space and time — their personal history.
If that history is only one way of describing some deeper underlying state, then the story you tell about yourself may be exactly as optional as which ruler you used.
Convenient. Not fundamental.
This is why she texts Derek to stop investigating the clocks and start investigating the descriptions.
Without quite meaning to, she has beaten Sherlock the dolphin to the punch. His idea, delivered properly in Chapter 15, is that spacetime itself — and maybe even the order events happen in — might emerge from relationships, rather than being the stage those relationships happen on.
And here is why this chapter earns its place directly before the tensors.
A tensor exists purely to solve the problem Penny has just described: a mathematical object whose components are allowed to look totally different depending on your point of view, as long as the underlying thing they describe never changes.
As Chapter 15 puts it, a tensor is what happens when a vector gets promoted and starts having opinions about several directions at once.
Which is, roughly, what just happened to Penny.
What the reader should take away:
1. A film multiverse is a metaphor, nothing more — do not cite Marvel in an exam.
2. The idea actually worth keeping: rulers, languages, coordinate systems, and maybe even life stories, can all be different descriptions of one and the same underlying reality.
3. An “observable” is simply whatever survives the translation — the one thing every honest description has to agree on.
4. This is exactly why, three chapters from now, General Relativity needs tensors: they are built specifically to say the same thing in every language.
Jamie Lee Curtis, in accordance with the previously established Law of Conservation of Actresses, once again survives.
Americans.
That settles it.
Chapter 13 — Curved Corridors and Tidal Gravity
The corridor with twelve or thirteen doors is absurd.
The underlying idea is not.
In curved spacetime, local measurements can differ from measurements made over larger regions.
The crucial concept is tidal gravity.
If gravity were perfectly uniform everywhere, you could locally reproduce its effects simply by accelerating your reference frame.
But real gravitational fields vary from place to place.
That variation produces tidal effects.
For example, Earth's gravitational field is slightly stronger closer to Earth's centre than farther away.
Two freely falling objects initially moving together can therefore gradually move apart or together.
Mathematically, this is described by geodesic deviation.
So the corridor joke is really about something quite respectable:
Curvature reveals itself through how neighbouring free-fall paths behave relative to one another.
The corridor did not actually gain a door.
Probably.
Chapter 14 — The Train That Arrived Before It Left
This is where relativity becomes particularly dangerous for ordinary English.
The train appears to arrive before it leaves.
That sounds like a violation of causality.
But before screaming "time machine", we need to distinguish coordinate descriptions from actual causal structure.
In relativity, different observers can disagree about the temporal ordering of spacelike-separated events.
They cannot disagree about the order of causally connected events in the way required to produce an ordinary causal paradox.
The light cone is the key.
For an event, spacetime can be divided into:
its causal past,
its causal future,
and regions that cannot be causally connected to it without exceeding the speed of light.
The causal structure is invariant.
So if Derek sees something that appears to happen before its cause, we should first ask:
Are those events actually causally connected?
If not, the apparent reversal may simply be a consequence of the coordinate system.
If they are causally connected, then we have a much more serious problem.
At which point Derek should probably stop buying train tickets.
Chapter 15 — The Dolphin and the Final Geometry
And now Sherlock.
A dolphin communicating General Relativity telepathically is not established physics.
The Einstein field equations are.
The central equation is:
[
G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}.
]
But before we explain that equation, we need to meet the things lurking inside it.
Tensors — Vectors' Slightly More Ambitious Cousins
A vector is already rather clever.
It can tell you something like:
"Go three metres east and two metres north."
It has components, and those components change when you change coordinates, but the underlying vector itself is the same physical thing.
A tensor takes this idea one step further.
At an introductory level, you can think of a tensor as a mathematical object that describes relationships between several directions at once.
Very roughly:
Scalar: one number.
Vector: a quantity with directional components.
Tensor: a quantity whose components describe relationships between directions.
So, in the spirit of the Bureau:
A tensor is what happens when a vector gets promoted and starts having opinions about several directions simultaneously.
That is a useful analogy.
It is not the formal mathematical definition.
A tensor is not simply a "higher-dimensional vector." What matters is that its components obey specific transformation rules when we change coordinates, so that the underlying physical object remains well-defined.
And that is exactly why tensors are so useful in General Relativity.
Spacetime can be curved.
Coordinates can be awkward.
Observers can accelerate.
Someone can be falling into a black hole while someone else is sitting safely in an office complaining about the coffee.
We need mathematical objects that behave properly when we change coordinates.
Tensors do precisely that.
The Metric Tensor
The most important tensor to meet first is the metric tensor, written:
gμν.
It tells us how spacetime is measured.
In flat spacetime, the interval can be written, depending on sign convention, as:
ds² = −c²dt² + dx² + dy² + dz².
In General Relativity, the metric can vary from place to place:
ds² = gμν dxμ dxν.
Don't let the Greek letters intimidate you.
They are essentially telling us:
"Combine the changes in all the relevant directions according to the metric, and you get the spacetime interval."
The metric is therefore rather like the instruction manual for measuring spacetime.
It tells us about:
distances,
times,
angles,
causal relationships,
and the geometry of spacetime itself.
Once you know the metric, an enormous amount of physics follows.
The Stress-Energy Tensor
Next comes:
Tμν.
This is the stress-energy tensor.
It describes the distribution and flow of physical quantities such as:
energy density,
momentum density,
pressure,
stresses,
and energy and momentum flowing through spacetime.
You can think of it as an extraordinarily sophisticated inventory of what matter and energy are doing.
Trevor once called it "the universe's spreadsheet."
Weinstein did not approve.
He did, however, admit that the analogy wasn't completely useless.
The Curvature Tensors
Then things become progressively more tensorish.
The Riemann curvature tensor contains the full local information about spacetime curvature.
The Ricci tensor packages an important part of that curvature.
And the Einstein tensor, (Gμν), packages the relevant curvature information in precisely the form required by Einstein's field equation.
There is a reason physicists need all these objects.
Curvature is not adequately described by saying:
"Space is a bit bent over there."
That would be charming but mathematically inadequate.
Curvature has structure.
It can differ in different directions.
It can affect neighbouring paths differently.
It can produce tidal effects.
Tensors are the machinery that lets us describe all of this without losing the physics every time somebody changes coordinates.
And now, finally, we can look at the equation.
[
G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}.
]
The left-hand side is geometry.
The right-hand side is matter and energy.
The Einstein field equation therefore says, in gloriously compressed mathematical form:
The geometry of spacetime is related to the distribution of matter and energy.
Or, as Derek would put it:
"Matter tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells matter how to behave."
Trevor would then say:
"Well, that's settled."
And for once, he would be right.
But Is General Relativity the Final Answer?
Not necessarily.
General Relativity works extraordinarily well.
But it does not appear to provide the complete description of nature at the quantum level.
Black-hole singularities and the earliest moments of the universe are obvious warning signs.
This is where quantum gravity enters.
Ideas involving:
quantum mechanics,
holography,
entanglement,
information,
and emergent spacetime
suggest that spacetime itself might not be fundamental.
If so, General Relativity would not become "wrong."
It would become something like thermodynamics.
Temperature is real.
Pressure is real.
Neither is a fundamental microscopic object.
They emerge from deeper physics.
Spacetime might conceivably be similar.
And this brings us back to Weinstein's original problem.
Perhaps the final theory does not replace General Relativity.
Perhaps it explains why General Relativity works.
That would be a considerably more interesting achievement.
And Sherlock, being a dolphin, naturally has no tenure problems.
So... Do You Finally Understand Relativity?
Possibly.
Let's find out.
If you can now explain why:
1. Two observers can disagree about elapsed time without either clock being broken,
2. the speed of light is the same for all inertial observers,
3. Special Relativity works locally inside General Relativity,
4. gravity can be understood as spacetime geometry,
5. curvature produces tidal effects,
6. a singularity represents a breakdown of the classical description rather than merely "very strong gravity",
7. coordinate descriptions can disagree while invariant physics remains the same,
8. holographic duality does not mean that the universe is literally a cinema screen,
9. tensors are the mathematical language that lets us describe geometry and physical quantities consistently under coordinate changes,
and
10. a cucumber does not, in fact, constitute experimental evidence for quantum gravity—
then congratulations.
You understand the essential ideas.
You may now safely discuss relativity at dinner.
You will, however, immediately discover that somebody knows a YouTube video proving Einstein was wrong.
At this point you have two choices.
You can explain Lorentz transformations.
Or you can have another piece of cake.
The second option is generally shorter.
And there is one final question.
Derek's question.
The question that has been lurking underneath the entire book:
If space and time can be observer-dependent, if gravity is geometry, if geometry might itself emerge from deeper physics, and if different descriptions can encode the same reality...
what, exactly, is reality?
Well.
That's the problem.
We haven't finished.
But the good news is that neither has the universe.
Which is rather reassuring.
