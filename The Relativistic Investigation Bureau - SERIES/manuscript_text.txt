How to Read This Book
This is a satirical mystery with real relativity underneath. You need no physics to follow the case. The comedy is the bait. The geometry is the trap.
The novel is Case 1047: a murder that has not happened yet, filed early by a Bureau that confuses event labels with corpses. Derek Gent investigates his own future death while trying not to erase his daughter from a doorway she should never have stood in.
The course lives in the Appendix as sixteen lessons. Most chapters end the same way: a Rule, and a single link to your lesson. Follow it. Each lesson should survive the hot-chair test: say the idea plainly to someone who has never heard the jargon - no phone, no notes, no hiding behind Greek letters.
Three honest ways to read this book.
For the story alone: read the chapters and stop at each Rule. No plot point lives only in the lessons.
To take the class: read each chapter, then its lesson, in order. Lesson 1 assumes nothing; Lesson 16 assumes you have been paying attention.
If you are impatient: skim the COURSE page and Lesson 16. You will still meet the brass clock.
One promise: where Newton cannot host this mystery, the book says so. Absolute 'now' makes Case 1047 gibberish. Relativity makes the misfiling possible - and the sacrifice real.

In Which the Universe Refuses to Behave
The murder had not happened yet. This would have been unremarkable, except that Derek Gent was already dead on paper—and someone had remembered to file the necessary forms.
The time of death had been entered. The location had been entered. The victim had been identified.
The victim was Derek Gent. Derek was alive. He was also the sort of man who noticed discrepancies in official paperwork. This particular discrepancy was rather difficult to ignore.
The paperwork predicted his death three days before it was due to occur. Derek therefore did what any sensible investigator would do.
He checked the date, then the time, then the old clock.
Then the clock again, because the first check had produced a result he found professionally inconvenient. 11:17. The mysterious clock had stopped.
Then there was Sherlock, a dolphin who worked remotely as an occasional Bureau consultant and was paid in fish. Nobody had yet found this arrangement stranger than the cases he commented on.
For the same reason, when Derek found a murder notice announcing his own future death, he did not immediately conclude that he was being hunted by a time traveller, a quantum ghost or an unusually vindictive neighbour.
The case would eventually force an even less comfortable possibility: that the Bureau's own computer was not merely recording the crime, but mis-filing a future event as one.
He began with the evidence.
A stopped clock.
A future date.
A murder that had not happened.
And, eventually, a distant, mysterious train.
There was only one sensible response. Investigate.
Preferably before 11:17.
Rule: A murder that has not happened yet is still a filing. Treat the paperwork as evidence.

Chapter 1 - The Surprise in the Morning
At precisely 9:17 on a Tuesday morning, Derek Gent received an email informing him that he was dead. His daughter, Sophie, was twelve years old. He checked the time on his phone and decided that one of these facts might be wrong.
Derek had always suspected that death would arrive by email. In this, he was, as matters go, ahead of the general population—comforting and depressing in equal measure. The alternatives were rarely an improvement, a fact Derek had verified more than once, usually at personal cost and never with a sense of novelty.
Not because he was especially pessimistic, but because nearly everything important seemed to arrive by email these days. Electricity bills. Convenient ways to re-enter bank account numbers for verification. Invitations to life-changing webinars. A unique opportunity to cooperate with a prince in Nigeria. Notifications that someone had changed a password Derek had never used. Insurance recommendations. Death was simply the logical next step. What surprised him was the sender.
The message had come from the Derek Gent Relativistic Investigation Bureau. Derek stared at the screen. The Bureau was not supposed to send him emails. Derek had written the rule himself, which made its violation particularly irritating.
It was his Bureau. More precisely, it was a small private investigative organization occupying two rooms on the second floor of an old office building in London, plus a corridor which the landlord insisted was a communal area and Derek insisted belonged to his Bureau whenever he needed somewhere to dry his brolly. The Bureau dealt primarily with unusual cases.
This was partly because Derek liked unusual cases and partly because ordinary cases paid terribly. The sign on the frosted glass door read:
DEREK GENT
RELATIVISTIC INVESTIGATION BUREAU
Underneath, in smaller letters:
CAUSALITY, CHRONOLOGY &
OTHER INCONVENIENCES
And underneath that, added by Penny several years earlier:
NO MURDERS BEFORE 10 A.M.
WITHOUT AN APPOINTMENT
Derek had objected. Penny had pointed out that they had never actually received a case before ten.
Derek had said that this was beside the point.
Penny had stated that this was precisely the point.
Derek had lost.
He was now looking at an email from the Bureau. The subject line was:
CASE UPDATE: DEREK GENT
He opened it.
Dear Mr Gent,
We regret to inform you that you have been
murdered.
The incident will occur tomorrow at 11:03 a.m.
The perpetrator has been identified. It is you.
Kind regards,
Derek Gent
Relativistic Investigation Bureau.
Derek read it again.
Then a third time. He glanced at the sender's address. It was correct.
Then he stared at the Bureau's computer. It was his computer.
Everything appeared to be where it ought to be. Derek did not find this reassuring.
The filing cabinet was locked. The kettle was where the kettle normally was, which was not necessarily where it ought to be.
Outside, a bus pulled away from the kerb with a noise suggesting that several of its components had voted against the decision. Rain tapped the window. Somewhere downstairs, the landlord was drilling into something which, judging by previous work, would shortly become the Bureau's responsibility.
Derek gazed at the email again, with the particular stillness of a man who has just been handed a problem that refuses to sit neatly in any of the usual categories. The Bureau had categories for almost everything. This was not among them. A new category would no doubt be invented once the damage was complete.
There was, he decided, a useful distinction between the things a man could act on and the things he could merely be informed of. Whether he died tomorrow fell into the second category, and no amount of staring at the screen would move it into the first. Whether Sophie was safe did not. That one, at least, he could still do something about, and it seemed the more efficient use of the morning.
Derek was forty-one, greying at the temples in a way he found more alarming than the murder notice, and built like a man who had once been reasonably athletic and had since made his peace with filing cabinets instead. He had a habit, under stress, of straightening things that were already straight - a stapler, a mug, the corner of a report nobody would ever read - as though order in small objects might eventually negotiate order in larger ones. "That's inconvenient," he commented, as if the matter were primarily a filing error. He did not yet know that the joke was the correct diagnosis - and that the email was not a warning so much as Exhibit Zero, delivered early.
Being confronted with "Apparently I've been murdered" made Derek feel considerably less useful. Being confronted with a Bureau category that did not yet exist made him feel worse.
Across the empty room, Trevor Boltzman looked up. Trevor was twenty-eight, tall in the specific way that seemed designed to make doorframes personal, with the kind of permanently rumpled sandy hair that suggested he had once owned a comb and lost the argument with it. He was highly intelligent, technically qualified, and possessed of the unfortunate tendency to think about one problem at a time - a trait that made him excellent at mathematics and intermittently disastrous at life. Trevor thinking deeply: a Windows system mid-reboot.
He had a sister, Tabitha, who was generally considered the more dangerous of the two, a fact Trevor mentioned only when cornered, and sometimes not even then. Derek, for his part, had Sophie. Sophie's mother, Tuppence - a name whose irony Derek had once pointed out, to no discernible improvement in the conversation - had left three years earlier, for a man named Julian who had made an improbable fortune manufacturing clothes hangers and bore, Derek privately maintained, a distracting resemblance to Hugh Grant in his more apologetic period. Derek would later discover, while researching the case, that a physicist named Julian Barbour had written a very respectable book arguing that time itself might not exist - and would discover rather more slowly that this was, in fact, Barbarian's real name, the front-facing one having been adopted for reasons Barbarian described as "professional" and Derek privately filed under "understandable." A third Julian, Derek felt, was pushing coincidence well past its structural limits. She had told him, with the specific clarity she reserved for important announcements, that he did not make enough money for her needs - a diagnosis Derek found difficult to dispute, given that the Bureau's ordinary cases paid terribly and its unusual ones paid worse. When Derek had attempted to point out that the Bureau offered a more relativistic kind of reward, she had considered this for exactly as long as it deserved. "Relativistic, my arse," she said, and was gone by Thursday. Derek had filed the entire matter under Processed - Do Not Reopen, which worked well enough, most days.
At his desk, Trevor removed his glasses - a gesture that, in the Bureau, usually preceded either insight or complete bafflement, with no reliable way of telling which in advance. "What?"
Derek had stopped trying to predict it years ago. "I've been murdered."
"Recently?"
"Tomorrow."
Trevor stared at him. "Tomorrow?"
"Yes."
Trevor considered this carefully. "You're telling me you've been murdered tomorrow?"
"Yes."
Trevor nodded. "Ah."
Derek waited. Trevor put his glasses back on.
Derek continued waiting, "Why did you say 'ah'?"
"I wasn't sure what else to say. 'Ah' seemed to cover the available options."
"How about 'that's impossible'? It has a certain traditional appeal."
"I thought you'd already know that. You usually do."
Derek peered at him. "Trevor, I have been working in this field for twenty-three years."
"Yes."
"I know the difference between something being impossible and something being highly inconvenient."
"Which is this now?"
Derek held up the screen. "I'm not sure yet."
Trevor stood. "Tea?"
"Yes."
"Milk?"
"No."
"Sugar?"
"One."
Trevor nodded and walked toward the kitchen. Derek called after him, "Two."
Trevor stopped. "Two?"
"Yes."
Trevor continued. Derek watched him disappear. A moment later there was a knock at the door.
Before Derek could answer, the door opened. Penny Gent, Derek's cousin, stepped in without waiting for an invitation. She had never been much given to waiting for invitations, a trait that had served her well and other people less so. She also had the disconcerting ability to look as if she had somewhere more interesting to be, even when she was standing in a room full of stopped clocks.
She was also one of those people who always appeared to have been designed specifically to make other people lose their train of thought. She was a striking brunette, though Derek had never used the word in her presence because he valued his life and had no particular desire to become the subject of a police report - or worse, a footnote. She wore a dark coat, carried a handbag, and had the calm expression of someone who had already dealt with several irritating people before breakfast and was prepared, if necessary, to deal with several more.
She eyed Derek. "Morning."
"Morning."
She put her handbag down. "Why do you look like that?"
"Like what?"
"Like you've just discovered the Inland Revenue has found you."
"I've been murdered."
Penny paused. "Already?"
"Apparently."
"Who did it?"
"I did."
She nodded. "Fair enough."
Derek watched her. "You're taking this remarkably well."
"It's Tuesday."
"That doesn't explain anything."
"It explains most things."
She walked over to the computer. "Show me."
Derek turned the monitor toward her. Penny read the email. Her expression changed.
Only slightly. Penny was clever. Very clever.
Unlike Trevor, who approached every new piece of information as if it might require a spreadsheet, Penny seemed to absorb things almost before anyone had spoken. She read the mysterious message again. "Tomorrow at eleven-oh-three."
"Yes."
"Not approximately eleven."
"No."
"Eleven-oh-three."
"Yes."
"That's very specific."
"I thought so."
"And the murderer is you."
"Apparently."
Penny regarded Derek. "Are you planning to murder yourself?"
"Not consciously."
"Good."
She pulled out a chair. "I'd hate to think you'd become predictable."
He smiled lightly, somewhat against his own will. Trevor came back carrying three mugs.
"Milk?"
"None," Derek noted.
"How about 'that's impossible'?"
Trevor handed Penny her mug. "Thanks."
Then he handed Derek the other one. Derek took a sip before looking sharply at Trevor. "Where's yours?"
"I didn't make one."
"Why not?"
"I thought there were only two of us."
Penny met his gaze. Trevor regarded Penny. Then at Derek.
"Oh." Derek sighed softly.
"Trevor."
"Yes?"
"That's Penny."
"I know."
"My cousin."
Trevor replied, "I know."
"She's been here for approximately ninety seconds."
"Yes."
"And yet somehow you've already forgotten that she exists," countered Derek.
"I haven't forgotten she exists."
"You made two cups of tea."
"I thought you didn't want one."
"I have one."
Trevor stared silently at Derek's mug. "Right."
Penny smiled, clearly amused. "Don't worry. He's usually this bright."
"I'm sitting right here."
"We know."
Derek turned back to the computer. Something was still bothering him, and it was not the murder itself.
The date was the problem. He clicked the attachment.
A photograph appeared. For a moment Derek stopped breathing. He recognised the feeling: it was the same one that arrived just before a theory collapsed, or a relationship, or both. Somewhere beneath the professional irritation, something colder and more personal had stirred - the quiet recognition that the face in the photograph was not merely a case, but a deadline.
Derek had never liked deadlines that looked back at him. Most deadlines had the decency to remain abstract.
Derek filed the sensation under 'to be examined later' and continued working. He was good at filing things under 'later'. It was one of his more reliable skills, and possibly one of his more dangerous ones.
Trevor noticed.
Penny asked, "What?"
Derek turned the monitor toward them. The photograph showed their office. Not a similar office.
Not a room that resembled it. The actual room. The same battered desk.
The same green filing cabinet. The same window overlooking the street. The same framed photograph of Derek's grandfather standing beside a Morris Minor in 1964.
And on the floor, face-down beside the familiar desk, was Derek. He was face-down, his head turned sufficiently towards the camera for identification. His right arm was beneath his chest. Blood had spread across the back of his shirt. In the doorway behind him stood a girl of twelve, frozen mid-step, holding a school bag—because she was supposed to be at school. Sophie. Alive in the picture. Dead in his absence. His daughter. Who was, at that moment, supposed to be safely at school.
Trevor stared intently.
Penny leaned closer. "That's you. And that's Sophie."
Penny's voice went flat and fast. "Then we're not debating photographs. We're finding out where she actually is. Now."
"Yes." Derek's voice had gone thin. "She isn't in this room."
He was already dialling. School office. Two rings. "Mr Gent? Sophie's in double maths. Shall I fetch her?" "No," he said, too quickly. "Just - is she there? Can you see her?" A pause. Paper rustle. "She's at the second desk from the window. She's checking her watch against the wall clock. Comparing them." Derek's grip on the phone tightened so hard his knuckles went white—his fingernails bit crescents into his palm. His breath came shallow. Behind his eyes, he could still see her standing in the photograph, standing in the doorway he'd promised she'd never enter. "Is that unusual?" "Very. The girl doesn't fidget." Derek's hand was shaking now. He forced the words out. "Don't tell her I called. She can't know I'm afraid."
Penny watched him. "She's at school."
"She's at school," Derek repeated, as if repeating could keep the photograph from being true. "And she is also in that doorway. I will not choose which one is real until I understand the frame."
Trevor cleared his throat. "Shouldn't we ring the actual police? There's blood in that photograph. Real detectives exist. Scotland Yard, presumably, still answers its own telephone."
Penny didn't look up from the screen. "And tell them what? That you've been murdered tomorrow, the body is also you, and it's currently standing in this room complaining about the coffee? They'd section you before they'd file it." She tapped the timestamp. "This isn't a crime yet. It's a filing error with excellent production values. The moment it becomes an actual body in an actual location, we call. Until then, it's ours, because nobody else's paperwork has a drawer for it."
Trevor considered this. "That seems like a very convenient rule."
"It's the rule that keeps us out of a police interview room explaining reference frames to a sergeant. Pick your battles." Penny allowed herself a small, unkind smile. "Besides. Scotland Yard would spend the first six weeks establishing whose jurisdiction a murder that hasn't happened yet actually falls under, and would consider the question far too important to rush on your account. This office, whatever else is wrong with it, doesn't require a crime to have the decency of occurring before we're willing to investigate it."
Trevor, carefully not looking at either of them, zoomed the image. "There's something on the desk."
Derek peered at the screen. A small, silent brass railway clock stood beside the computer, looking entirely ordinary and entirely wrong, which was, in Derek's experience, the most dangerous combination. He had the sudden, unwelcome sense that the clock had been waiting for him.
"Fair enough."
Penny examined him. "Of course you do."
"No."
"You bought it last Thursday."
"I did?"
"You described it as historically significant."
"What is it?"
"An old railway clock."
Derek looked again. The old clock showed 11:03.
He inspected the clock on his own desk. 9:21.
He considered his computer: 9:21.
His phone and the wall clock both read 9:21.
He looked back at the photograph. 11:03.
Derek leaned back. "Interesting."
Penny's brow tightened. "What?"
"That photograph wasn't taken tomorrow."
Trevor frowned. "Then when was it taken?"
Derek pointed at the picture. "The clock reads eleven-oh-three."
"So?"
"Tomorrow's murder is at eleven-oh-three."
"Yes."
"And the photograph was apparently taken at the moment of my alleged death."
"Yes."
Trevor waited. Derek glanced at him.
Trevor turned back. "So?"
Derek looked at him. "Trevor, recognise a mystery when it's standing on the table screaming."
Trevor stared at the photograph. "Oh."
Penny smiled. "There he is."
Trevor leaned closer. "Hang on."
"What?"
"If that's a photograph of tomorrow - "
"It's not."
"Right."
"If it's a photograph taken tomorrow, then why does the clock show eleven-oh-three?"
Penny replied, "Because that's when Derek dies."
"But the clock here reads nine-twenty-one."
"Yes."
"So, the photograph was taken two hours later."
"Yes."
Trevor wore the pleased expression of a man who had just solved something. "That's what I meant."
"No," Penny replied. "It isn't."
Trevor's expression collapsed with the quiet dignity of a man who has just watched a carefully constructed theory meet reality and lose. It was a look Derek had come to recognise, and almost respect. "Oh."
Derek got up and walked to the window.
Outside, London was grey and wet. A bus stopped at the kerb, and another stopped behind it.
Derek watched the traffic. London was, as usual, being mildly inconvenient.
Penny raised her eyes. "Your impending murder?"
"Other than that."
"Your Bureau?"
"Also other than that."
Trevor thought. "Your cousin?"
Penny gazed at him. Trevor immediately reconsidered his life choices.
But Derek smiled softly. "No."
He tapped the photograph. "We have a clock reading eleven-oh-three and a room full of clocks reading nine-twenty-one. Before we call either one wrong, we need to ask what kind of number we are looking at."
Trevor frowned. "A time. Obviously. Times are what clocks show - until they aren't."
"Sometimes," Derek said. "Sometimes a clock is labelling an event. Those are not the same job."
Penny looked at Sophie in the photograph. "So the death is the event. Eleven-oh-three might only be a label. What I care about is that she is standing inside it."
"Yes. And a photograph records light arriving somewhere, not a view from nowhere. If this image came out of our own system, it may not be a camera at all."
Trevor looked at the clock, then at Penny, then at the maths he trusted more than either. "Then we test the labels. Carefully."
Derek nodded at the brass clock, not at either of them. "Measure. Then fight. In that order."
Penny enlarged the lower-right corner of the image. She did not speak for a long time. The three lines sat there like a spell the Bureau had not meant to cast: Death. Departure. Arrival. The room felt briefly as if it had more dimensions than furniture.
There was something in the lower-right corner. A piece of paper.
Penny zoomed in. It contained three lines.
11:03 - DEATH
11:17 - DEPARTURE
12:04 - ARRIVAL
Below them:
DO NOT TRUST THE CLOCKS.
Rule: A clock can lie by telling the wrong kind of truth.
-> Lesson for this chapter: 1 - Special Relativity Begins by Being Rude
Chapter 2 - Case 1047
For several seconds, nobody spoke. The three-line note sat on the screen like a timetable written by someone who already knew the journey.
Penny read it. "That's ominous."
"Very."
"Could 'departure' mean the train?"
"Perhaps."
"Arrival?"
"Perhaps."
"Death?"
"Almost certainly me."
Trevor shifted uneasily.
Penny turned to him. "That's a surprisingly weak argument."
"It has served me well."
Derek zoomed in again.
The railway silent clock in the photograph wasn't showing exactly 11:03.
It showed 11:03:17.
Derek stared intently.
He looked at his own clock. 9:27:17.
He checked his phone. 9:27:17.
The wall clock. 9:27:17.
Derek pointed. "Seventeen seconds."
He said it quietly, as if the number itself might change if spoken too loudly.
Trevor looked sharply. "Same."
Derek nodded. "That's interesting."
Penny leaned closer. "Why?"
"Because if this were simply a badly set clock, there's no reason for the seconds to line up."
Trevor thought about that. "Could someone have set it deliberately?"
"Yes."
Penny fixed him with a look.
Trevor nodded quickly. "Exactly."
Derek opened the Bureau's internal records.
The system showed no record of the ancient brass clock.
No purchase.
No inventory entry. No photograph. No mention of it.
Derek searched the building's security footage. Nothing. He checked the office camera.
At 9:16, the old desk was empty. At 9:17, the old desk was empty. At 9:18, the desk was empty.
At 9:19, the old desk was empty. At 9:20, the ancient brass clock appeared. Trevor stared. "How?"
Derek replayed it. The clock simply suddenly appeared. There was no hand.
There was no person and no shadow. One frame showed nothing.
The next showed the clock. Penny watched Derek. "That's not normal."
"No."
"No."
"Could the camera have glitched?"
"Yes."
"Did it?"
"No."
"How do you know?"
"Because the clock casts a shadow."
Penny studied it again, more carefully this time. It did. The shadow moved normally as the light from the window changed.
Derek replayed the footage. The silent clock existed. Then it didn't.
Then it existed again. Trevor's expression deteriorated by degrees.
"Could someone have edited the footage?"
"Yes."
"Could they have edited the photograph?"
"Yes."
"So, how do we know anything is real?"
Derek met his eyes. "Now you're asking the right question."
Trevor didn't look reassured. Derek slowly opened another file. "Let's start with the clocks."
He took the ancient brass clock from the photograph and compared its mechanism with the Bureau's clocks.
They were different. He compared the hands.
Different. He compared the second hand. Identical.
Seventeen seconds.
Derek frowned. Penny noticed.
"Another problem?"
"Yes."
"How many are we up to?"
"Seven."
Trevor raised his eyebrows. "Seven?"
"At least."
Derek turned the clear photograph upside down. There was a small number written on the back. 1047.
He reviewed the case database. Case 1047 did not exist. He searched deleted cases.
Nothing. He searched archived cases. Nothing.
He searched future-dated cases. Nothing.
Then the computer made a noise. A soft chime. A new case appeared.
CASE 1047
CLIENT: Derek Gent
STATUS: ACTIVE
Derek stared intently. Penny leaned over. "What does it say?"
Derek scrolled.
The case contained only three pieces of information.
CLIENT: Derek Gent
SUBJECT: Murder
DATE: WEDNESDAY
TIME: 11:03:17
Derek clicked "Assigned Investigator."
The field was blank.
He clicked "Perpetrator."
Blank.
He clicked "Victim."
Derek Gent.
He clicked "Case Notes."
There was one note. Written in Derek's own handwriting.
DO NOT LET TREVOR NEAR THE STRANGE CLOCK.
Trevor read it. "I'm insulted."
Derek turned toward him. "Don't."
"I wasn't going to."
"You were."
"I was."
Penny smiled. "This is becoming quite good."
Derek stared at the screen. "Something is using the Bureau."
"What?"
"Someone - or something - is creating cases inside our system."
"From where?"
"I don't know."
"Can we trace it?"
Derek checked. "No."
"Why?"
"Because the originating address is..."
He stopped. "What?"
Derek turned the monitor toward them. The address read:
LOCALHOST
Trevor frowned. "That's the computer itself. So either someone here typed a murder into existence, or the system executed a transformation we have not written down yet. I prefer the second. People lie. Equations, when honest, merely disappoint."
"Or the machine filed a record that arrived without a person attached," Derek said. "Localhost means the Bureau processed it. It does not mean one of us intended it."
"That's negotiable causality," Trevor said. "Negotiable causality is what you get when you trust feeling over frame. I don't. Argue with the transform, not with me."
"I'll argue with both," Penny said. She tapped the doorway in the photo once, lightly, as if knocking. "She answers first."
Penny surveyed the room. There were three people in it. Derek.
Trevor. Penny. Nobody else.
She folded her arms. "Well."
Trevor met her gaze. "What?"
"You've got an interesting problem."
"Which one?"
"One of us is lying - or the filing cabinet is. Mrs Marsh keeps a form for Pending Geometry. I've never seen anyone use it."
Derek looked at the three chairs. Then the silent clock. Then the computer.
"Perhaps."
Penny smiled. "Or one of us hasn't done it yet."
Derek regarded her. The office suddenly felt colder. Not the ordinary London cold - something more precise, as if a draft had found its way in from a place that did not appear on any map. Trevor swallowed. "You mean..."
Derek didn't answer. The computer chimed again. All three studied the screen.
A new message suddenly appeared.
CASE UPDATE
PERPETRATOR IDENTIFIED
Derek opened it. The name appeared.
TREVOR BOLTZMAN
Trevor stared silently. "What?"
Trevor sat down without deciding to. "No - one thing at a time. Either I'm a suspect, or the case is wrong, or the frame is wrong. I need you to tell me which one I'm allowed to panic about first."
Derek gave him a measured look.
Trevor and Penny exchanged a glance.
"That wasn't me."
"I know," Derek added.
"How do you know?"
"Because you're still here."
Trevor blinked. "That's your evidence?"
"For the moment."
Penny walked to the window. "Then who is framing him?"
Derek glanced at the clock. 9:31:17.
He stared at the photograph.
11:03:17. He returned to the case file.
Then he looked at Trevor. "Maybe nobody. Maybe Case 1047 is not a confession. Maybe it is a misfiled event - and every seventeen on this desk is the same fingerprint wearing different gloves."
Trevor frowned. "What does that mean?"
Derek walked to the whiteboard. He wrote:
EVENT
Then:
OBSERVER
Then:
FRAME
Then:

Chapter 6 - The Apple Problem
Alfred Weinstein had never entirely trusted apples. This was not because of any defect in the fruit. Apples were perfectly respectable.
They were roundish, nutritious, reasonably portable and, unlike pears, rarely gave the impression that they were about to collapse under their own moral burden. His distrust concerned their reputation. For centuries, apples had been given far too much credit.
One particular red apple, in particular, had been blamed for an astonishing amount of physics. Weinstein was thinking about it on the mysterious train.
He was on his way back to Munich after the meeting with Julius Barbarian, although "on his way back" was perhaps an unnecessarily confident description of the situation.
The mysterious train had stopped. It had stopped between two stations. Nobody seemed particularly concerned.
The conductor had announced that there was a "minor delay due to circumstances."
Weinstein had spent enough time on trains to know that "circumstances" meant absolutely anything.
He sat by the window. Across from him, a woman was reading a novel. Beside her, a man was asleep.
Two rows ahead, a child was eating an apple. Weinstein watched the child. The child took a bite.
Then another. Then, apparently deciding that the apple had become philosophically unacceptable, dropped it. It fell to the floor.
Weinstein intently watched it. The apple rolled beneath the seat. He stared at the space where it had disappeared.
Then he smiled. Newton. He thought of Newton's famous ordinary apple.
The story was almost certainly too neat. Science rarely arrived with such excellent stage management. One could imagine the scene.
Newton sits under a tree. An apple falls. Newton looks at it.
Newton thinks about gravity. Physics is transformed. It was a charming story.
It was also misleading.
The apple had not explained gravity. It had offered Weinstein a decision: treat falling bodies as geometry probes, or keep inventing forces until the Bureau's clocks looked polite.
It had merely demonstrated that something fell. The really interesting question was why. Weinstein leaned back.
Why did the apple fall? Everyone knew the answer. Gravity.
But that was not an answer. It was a name. He had spent most of his life telling physicists not to confuse naming a phenomenon with explaining it.
Newton had described gravity extraordinarily well. The inverse-square law worked. The equations worked.
The predictions worked. The planets obeyed them. The Moon obeyed them.
The apple obeyed them. Everything seemed to obey them. Then General Relativity had come along and changed the question.

Chapter 16 - Sherlock and the Final Geometry
Weinstein was having a perfectly ordinary morning. This meant, naturally, that the universe was about to become ridiculous. He was sitting in his quiet office in Munich, staring at a sheet of paper on which he had written the same equation three times.
He had crossed it out three times. The fourth version looked promising. The fifth looked worse.
The sixth looked suspiciously like the first.

It still worked. That was the insult.

Not a telephone. Not the sound of someone opening his empty old office door. It was a voice.

"A railway designation," Weinstein repeated, with the particular weariness of a man who had built an entire theoretical framework around three letters that turned out to mean nothing more than fast train. "That's it. That's the whole geometry." "That's it," Penny agreed, not unkindly. Trevor, overhearing, announced that he stood by Local Gravitational Vector as the more interesting answer, correctness notwithstanding, and nobody had the heart to argue with him twice in one week.

He filed actual, ordinary cases under actual, ordinary categories, and none of them concerned him personally. Sophie texted twice about nothing in particular, which he had learned to recognise as the highest compliment she had available. Penny stopped watching him sideways when she thought he wasn't looking. He allowed himself, cautiously, to believe it might simply be over.

On the Friday, Penny ordered curry for the whole Bureau, on the grounds that a team that had spent a year arguing about whether the dead could still receive post deserved at least one meal that didn't come out of a vending machine. They ate it straight from the containers, at desks still faintly haunted by chalk diagrams of spacetime. Derek discovered, somewhere around his second popadom, that he was extremely and uncomplicatedly glad to be alive - not in the abstract, philosophical way the case had spent a year making him consider it, but in the specific, practical way that meant he was eating a chicken jalfrezi instead of, as had at various points seemed likely, pushing up daisies. He mentioned this to no one, on the grounds that saying it aloud would have obliged someone to make a joke about it, and he wanted, just this once, to enjoy being not dead in silence.

Weinstein, predictably, ruined the silence. He pointed out, through a mouthful of naan, that the curry had taken forty minutes to arrive by the delivery driver's watch and thirty-nine by the Bureau's, on account of a set of traffic lights that had briefly convinced two clocks they disagreed about the order of events. Trevor asked, not unreasonably, whether the delivery firm could simply hire a tachyon and skip the traffic altogether. Weinstein said that a tachyon would arrive before it had been dispatched, refuse the order retroactively, and leave the Bureau owing a refund to a version of itself that had never placed one - which, he added, was a worse system than the Northern line, and that was saying something. Nobody found any of this as fascinating as Weinstein did. Derek found it, on reflection, exactly fascinating enough - the universe was still out there disagreeing with itself about details, same as ever, and for once none of the disagreements involved anyone's death. He decided he could live with a universe like that.

Later that week, Derek took Sophie shopping for school shoes, on the grounds that fatherhood, like the universe, occasionally demanded ordinary maintenance. The department store's lift stuttered between the second and third floors, held, and dropped an inch with a lurch that emptied both their stomachs at once. Sophie gripped the handrail. "Are we falling?"
Derek considered this with more care than the question strictly required. "Technically," he said, "there's no experiment you could run inside this lift that would tell you whether we're falling freely or just floating in a lift far from any gravity at all. It's called the equivalence principle. Chapter Six of my life, roughly." The lift shuddered and resumed its climb, gravity reasserting its usual bureaucratic authority. "Turns out it also can't tell you whether the culprit is a cable or a motor that hates Tuesdays."
Sophie considered this with the weary patience of a twelve-year-old whose father had spent a year investigating a filing error. "Dad. It's a lift."
"Everything's a lift, if you corner it correctly."
"That's not a real sentence."
"It's a real sentence. It's just doing several jobs at once." The doors opened onto Ladies' Footwear. Derek stepped out first, mostly to confirm the floor was still there.

Somewhere near the bandstand, a stranger on a bicycle whistled at him and pedalled off before Trevor could construct a suitable retort. By the time the reply arrived, fully formed and reasonably witty, the stranger had already left whatever light cone might have delivered it to him - which Trevor decided, generously, made it a problem of physics rather than wit. He settled, a full block later, for muttering it to the empty park instead. "I'm flattered, obviously," he informed a duck, "and also extremely cold." Preparatory workshop - The Twin Paradox (do this before Lesson 1)
Workshop problem: One twin boards a very fast spaceship, travels out and back near the speed of light, and returns to find their sibling considerably older. Both twins, the objection runs, saw the other one moving - so shouldn't each expect the other to have aged less?
It cannot, and it isn't. The two twins do not take the same route through spacetime between their parting and their reunion - only the travelling twin's path bends, out and back, while the twin on the sofa travels a straight, unaccelerated line between the same two events.
That is the entire resolution: proper time depends on the path taken, not merely on relative motion, and the straight path between two events always racks up more of it than any bent one. Turning around is what makes the paths different - it is not, by itself, what makes the travelling twin younger. There is a genuine fact about which twin aged less, and it belongs to whoever took the bent path.
This is the same idea as the invariant spacetime interval you are about to meet in Chapter 2, wearing a different coat. Proper time, like ordinary distance, depends on the path you actually take.
Drive the direct road between two towns and you burn less petrol than the driver who takes the scenic route, even though both of you started and finished in the same two places. Nobody finds that paradoxical. The twin paradox is exactly this, except the odometer is a wristwatch and the scenic route is nearly the speed of light.
Workshop result: proper time depends on the path taken. The same idea reappears as the invariant interval in Lesson 2, and as the other Derek problem in the novel. Trevor still wants it filed under Pending Geometry. Mrs Marsh still refuses to stamp the form. Proceed to Lesson 1.

Module A · Foundations of Special Relativity · Case link: Chapters 1-3. Learning objective: State Einstein's two postulates and explain why constant c forces space and time to transform together. The first thing to understand about Special Relativity is that it does not begin with the statement:
"Everything is relative."
That is a popular summary and is also rather misleading. Einstein's Special Relativity rests on two principles: The laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames.
The speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers. It's the second one that causes all the trouble. Suppose you are sitting on a distant train.
You throw a ball forward at 10 m/s. Someone standing on the platform sees the ball moving at roughly: train speed + 10 m/s.
Perfectly ordinary. Now replace the ball with light.
Classical intuition replied that the person on the platform should measure:
train speed + speed of light.
Nature responds:
No.
The light still travels at exactly c.
This forces us to abandon the old assumptions about space and time. And that is where things become interesting.
Here is the classic way to see why - not just that it is true, but why it comes out as an actual number.
Imagine a clock built from a single photon of light, bouncing straight up and down between two mirrors a fixed distance apart. Each round trip is one 'tick.'
Nothing exotic: light, two mirrors, geometry.
Now put that clock on the mysterious train, moving past a platform at speed v.
From Trevor's point of view, standing on the platform, the light doesn't simply go straight up and down - the train is moving, so the photon has to travel diagonally to keep up with the departing mirror, tracing a longer zigzag path for each tick.
Here is the part that actually causes the trouble. The speed of light does not change for Trevor either.
Longer path, same speed, more time. That is the whole derivation. One tick of the train's clock, measured from the platform, simply takes longer than one tick measured on the train.
Do the geometry properly - a right triangle, Pythagoras, nothing worse - and out drops exactly the same γ you are about to meet properly in the next chapter, without asking anyone to take the mathematics on faith.

The photon takes a longer path in the clock but still travels at speed c - so the tick takes longer.
This is why time dilation is not a story about clocks getting confused. It is what geometry does when one side of the triangle refuses to change length no matter how fast you are moving.
None of this is hypothetical.
The satellites that make a phone's map work are moving fast enough, and sitting high enough out of Earth's gravity well, that both effects discussed in this book - Special Relativity's time dilation from speed, and General Relativity's time dilation from gravity - apply to their onboard clocks simultaneously, pulling in opposite directions.
Engineers correct for it deliberately, every single day, or a phone's position would drift by several kilometres within hours. The Bureau finds this less remarkable than most people do. The Bureau finds most things less remarkable than most people do.

Lesson 2 - Lorentz Finally Gets Involved
Module A · Foundations of Special Relativity · Case link: Chapter 3. Learning objective: Use the Lorentz transformation to relate coordinates between inertial frames and name at least one invariant. The mathematical machinery connecting two inertial.
The mathematical machinery connecting two inertial observers is the Lorentz transformation. An inertial observer moves at a constant velocity without accelerating.
Let A describe an event using position (x) and time (t). B describes that same event using (x′) and (t′). If B moves to A's right at speed (v), and their coordinate origins coincide when both clocks read zero, then:
x′ = γ(x - vt)
t′ = γ(t - vx/c²)
Here c is the speed of light in vacuum, and γ is the Lorentz factor.
Do not panic. γ is merely a number that becomes increasingly annoying as your speed approaches the speed of light. The important thing is that space and time transform together.
Look at the time equation: B's time depends on both A's time and A's position. That position term is why two events happening at the same time in A's frame can happen at different times in B's.
This explains the diagram. Each colored line shows what one observer considers to happen at the same time as Event 1.
A's teal line passes through both events. A says they happen at the same time.
B's red line passes through Event 1, but Event 2 lies below it. B says Event 2 happened earlier than Event 1.
Event 2 does not belong on the red line because B does not consider it simultaneous with Event 1. The colored lines represent equal times, not paths that the events must follow.
This disagreement is not simply about light taking time to reach someone's eyes. It remains after each observer accounts for light travel time.
Different observers can therefore disagree about distances, elapsed times, and whether two separated events happened simultaneously.
They do not, however, disagree about everything.

Here Δt is the time between the two events, and Δx is their separation in space. All inertial observers calculate the same value of s2 for the same pair of events, provided they use the same sign convention.
The individual space and time measurements can change. This combination stays the same.

Coordinates are descriptions of reality. The events they describe do not change when we change coordinates.

Lesson 3 - Time May Be Less Fundamental Than You'd Like
Module A · Foundations of Special Relativity · Case link: Chapters 5 & 15. Learning objective: Distinguish coordinate time from proper time along a worldline; reject universal Newtonian now.
Proper time: two worldlines between the same two events accumulate different amounts of elapsed proper time.
Even if the gravity is only caused by earth, the time difference can be clearly measured using atomic clocks.

Due to gravity difference, the clock on the plane runs slower than the one on the ground.
Julius Barbarian's claim that time does not exist is deliberately provocative. But there is serious physics hiding underneath the joke. In relativity, there is no single universal time shared by the entire universe.
Different observers can experience different amounts of proper time between events. Proper time is the time measured by a clock travelling along a particular worldline.
So, instead of asking:
"What time is it everywhere?"
relativity encourages us to ask:
"How much proper time elapsed along this particular path through spacetime?"
That is a much better question.
And General Relativity goes further.
Gravitational fields affect the rate at which clocks run relative to one another.
A strange clock deeper in a gravitational field can accumulate less proper time than one farther away. So, clocks do not merely measure some universal substance called Time.
They measure something associated with their physical histories.
Barbarian therefore has a point.
Unfortunately, he also has an alarming tendency to explain it while eating biscuits.

Lesson 4 - Enter General Relativity
Module B · Gravity as Geometry · Case link: Chapter 4. Learning objective: Explain gravity as spacetime geometry and state what the equivalence principle does and does not allow. Special Relativity deals with inertial frames and flat spacetime. But gravity causes a problem.
Einstein noticed something wonderfully simple:
If you are inside a freely falling lift, you cannot locally distinguish falling freely in a gravitational field from drifting weightlessly in empty space, far from any gravity. This is the equivalence principle.
That suggested something profound:
Perhaps gravity isn't fundamentally a conventional force.
Perhaps it is geometry.
General Relativity makes precisely that leap.
Matter and energy affect spacetime geometry.
Objects then move through that geometry.
The famous slogan is:
Matter tells spacetime how to curve;
Spacetime tells matter how to move.
That is considerably more accurate than blaming gravity on the apple.
Although the apple remains available for consultation.
Imagine dropping a ball inside an elevator. You hold the ball at some height and release it.
Everything is normal if the elevator is at rest.
If the ball and the elevator are both falling relative to the observer, the ball simply floats.
Now imagine the elevator is far away from any gravitational field and accelerating upward. The ball now moves downward toward the floor - just as it would in an ordinary gravitational field.

Lesson 5 - The Apple
Module B · Gravity as Geometry · Case link: Chapter 6. Learning objective: Contrast Newtonian force with geodesic free fall; say when Newton remains an excellent approximation.
Newton vs. Einstein: the same falling apple, explained as a force or as motion along a geodesic.
Newton answered:
There is a gravitational force.
Einstein eventually answered:
The object is following a natural path through curved spacetime.
That natural path is called a geodesic.
On the surface of the Earth, an orbiting spacecraft is actually in free fall.
It is not being "held up" by a mysterious absence of gravity.
It is following its geodesic.
The astronauts inside feel weightless because they are falling together with the spacecraft.
The surface of the Earth prevents us from following our natural free-fall path.
The floor pushes upward on our feet.
Which is why standing still is, in a relativistic sense, rather more strenuous than orbiting Earth.
The apple was therefore not really falling because gravity "pulled" it downward in the Newtonian sense.
It was following the geometry.
Newton wasn't wrong.

Lesson 6 - Singularities
Module B · Gravity as Geometry · Case link: Chapters 7-8. Learning objective: Define a singularity as a breakdown of the classical spacetime description, not merely 'very strong gravity.'
Here Derek makes an important distinction. A singularity is not simply a place where gravity is very strong.
That would be too easy.
A singularity represents a breakdown of the classical spacetime description. One useful way of expressing this is through geodesic incompleteness. Normally, you should be able to keep extending that path. At a singularity, the classical theory can tell you that the path simply cannot be continued in the expected way.
Imagine following a freely falling observer's worldline.
Singularity: a worldline that cannot be extended - a breakdown of the classical description, not a place.
This tells us something profound: The theory has reached its limit.
This is why physicists don't normally say:
"There's a really strong gravitational field over there, so that's a singularity."
They ask whether the mathematical structure of spacetime itself becomes pathological.
And that distinction matters enormously when we discuss black holes and the early universe.
A singularity is not necessarily an object sitting somewhere waiting to be photographed.
It may be telling us that General Relativity is incomplete at that point.
Which is physicist-speak for:
"The equations have stopped being helpful. Someone should invent some better ones."

Nothing has to happen to the object itself.

Lesson 11 - The Hologram
Module D · Dual Descriptions · Case link: Chapter 12. Learning objective: State the area-law clue for black-hole entropy and what holographic duality does and does not claim. Derek's filing cabinet was full. This was an established fact, supported by the drawer that no longer shut.
Ordinarily, the remedy would be a larger cabinet. Double its width, height and depth, and there would be eight times as much room for files. Trevor would fill it in a fortnight, but that was a staffing problem.
Black-hole physics suggests a stranger kind of accounting. When physicists calculate a black hole's entropy, the quantity that matters is the area of its horizon. That unexpected result helped inspire the holographic principle: a theory describing a region with gravity may have an equivalent description on a boundary with one fewer spatial dimension.
The filing cabinet is an analogy. Please do not attempt the experiment with the Bureau's furniture.
What the black holes tell us: A black hole's event horizon is the boundary beyond which light cannot escape to distant observers. It is not a solid shell.
Bekenstein and Hawking connected the horizon's area to entropy. Here, entropy measures how many microscopic possibilities are compatible with the black hole's large-scale properties. More precisely, it depends on the logarithm of that number. It can also be expressed as an amount of information in bits.
For a spherical horizon, area is 4πR². Double the radius, and the area becomes four times as large. The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy therefore becomes four times as large too.
An ordinary ball with twice the radius has eight times the volume. This contrast is the clue: the black hole's entropy follows an area rule.
Penny would describe this as being charged for storage by the size of the wrapping paper. Derek would ask whether the wrapping paper was included.
What information means here: "Information" does not mean that someone has printed a list of everything that fell in on the outside of the black hole. It concerns the microscopic distinctions needed to specify a physical state.
Think of the difference between knowing that a room contains warm air and specifying the detailed state of that air. Many microscopic arrangements can fit the same broad description. Entropy quantifies that multiplicity.
The area rule helped motivate the idea that gravity limits the independent information needed to describe a region in an unexpectedly economical way. Turning that insight into a general rule requires care about which surfaces and regions are being compared.
It does not give Trevor permission to store the accounts on the office wallpaper.

The holographic principle: information about a volume can be encoded on its lower-dimensional boundary.

Consider a sentence translated from English into German. The words change. The grammar changes. With a good translation, the meaning survives.

Different numbers. One invariant relationship.
There is a distinction worth keeping here. An observable is a quantity associated with a possible measurement. An invariant is a quantity unchanged under the transformations being considered. Those are not interchangeable definitions. A measured speed, for example, can depend on the observer.

Lesson 13 - Curved Corridors and Tidal Gravity
Module D · Dual Descriptions · Case link: Chapter 14. Learning objective: Recognise curvature via tidal effects / relative free-fall paths, not via a single 'downward force.'
Tidal gravity: two initially parallel free-fall paths converge as they pass near a mass - curvature made visible.
The narrow corridor with twelve or thirteen doors is absurd. The underlying idea is not. In curved spacetime, local measurements can differ from measurements made over larger regions.
<- Chapter for this lesson: Chapter 14
The crucial concept is tidal gravity. If gravity were perfectly uniform everywhere, you could locally reproduce its effects simply by accelerating your reference frame. But real gravitational fields vary from place to place. That variation produces tidal effects.
For example, Earth's gravitational field is slightly stronger closer to Earth's centre than farther away.
Two freely falling objects initially moving together can therefore gradually move apart or together.
Mathematically, this is described by geodesic deviation. The narrow corridor joke is really about something quite respectable:
Curvature reveals itself through how neighbouring free-fall paths behave relative to one another. The narrow corridor did not actually gain a door. Probably.

Lesson 14 - The Distant Train That Arrived Before It Left
Module E · Causality & Capstone · Case link: Chapter 15. Learning objective: Separate spacelike from timelike separation; say when time-order can and cannot reverse between frames.

But before screaming "time machine", we need to distinguish coordinate descriptions from actual causal structure.
In relativity, different observers can disagree about the temporal ordering of spacelike-separated events. For causally connected events, all inertial observers agree on their temporal order. Relativity does not let an ordinary cause arrive after its effect merely by changing coordinates. The light cone is the key.
For an event, spacetime can be divided into: its causal past, its causal future,
and regions that cannot be causally connected to it without exceeding the speed of light. The light-cone structure - what can causally influence what - is invariant.

Tensors - Vectors' Slightly More Ambitious Cousins A vector is already rather clever.
It can tell you something like:
"Go three metres east and two metres north."
It has components - for instance, v = (vx, vy, vz) - and those components change when you change coordinates, but the underlying vector itself is the same physical thing.
A tensor takes this idea one step further. At an introductory level, you can think of a tensor as a mathematical object that describes relationships between several directions at once.
Very roughly:
Scalar: one number.
Vector: directional components.
Tensor: components describing relationships between directions.
So, in the spirit of the Relativistic Investigation Bureau:
A tensor is what happens when a vector gets promoted and starts having opinions about several directions simultaneously.
That is a useful analogy. It is not the formal mathematical definition.
A tensor is not simply a "higher-dimensional vector." What matters is that its components obey specific transformation rules when we change coordinates, so that the underlying physical object remains well-defined.
And that is exactly why tensors are so useful in General Relativity. Spacetime can be curved. Coordinates can be awkward.
Observers can accelerate. Someone can be falling into a black hole while someone else is sitting safely in an office complaining about the coffee. We need mathematical objects that behave properly when we change coordinates.
Tensors do precisely that. The most important tensor is called the metric tensor, written gμν. It tells us how spacetime is measured: ds² = gμν dxμ dxν, a combination of regular distances in space plus a time component.
The metric can vary from place to place.
So physicists combine the changes in all the relevant directions according to the metric, and get the spacetime interval.
The metric is therefore rather like the instruction manual for measuring spacetime. It tells us about: distances, times, angles, causal relationships, and the geometry of spacetime itself. Once you know the metric, an enormous amount of physics follows.
The next is the stress-energy tensor, written Tμν, describing the distribution and flow of matter and energy. Trevor once called it "the universe's spreadsheet."
Weinstein did not approve. He did, however, admit that the analogy wasn't completely useless.
Then come the curvature tensors - refined and packaged for the field equation as the Einstein tensor, Gμν. Curvature is not adequately described by a single number; the tensors keep track of how directions fail to stay parallel when carried around spacetime.
"Space is a bit bent over there."
That would be charming but mathematically inadequate. Curvature has structure. It can differ in different directions.
It can affect neighbouring paths differently. It can produce tidal effects. Tensors are the machinery that lets us describe all of this without losing the physics every time somebody changes coordinates.
The Einstein field equation answers, in compressed mathematical form, how the geometry of spacetime is related to the distribution of matter and energy: Gμν + Λgμν = 8πG/c⁴ Tμν.
Or, as Derek would put it:
"Matter tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells matter how to behave."
Trevor would then say:
"Well, that narrows it down."
And for once, he would be right.
Nobody spoke for a moment. Even Trevor seemed to sense that the equation deserved it.
But Is General Relativity the Final Answer?
Not necessarily.
General Relativity works extraordinarily well. But it does not appear to provide the complete description of nature at the quantum level. Black-hole singularities and the earliest moments of the universe are obvious warning signs.
This is where quantum gravity enters. Research programmes involving: quantum mechanics,
holography, entanglement, information,
and emergent spacetime raise the possibility that spacetime itself might not be fundamental.
That is an active research idea, not an established conclusion.

It would become something like thermodynamics.
Temperature is real. Pressure is real.

Glossary
A quick-reference guide to the terms the Appendix explains at greater length - and with considerably more cucumbers.
Causal Structure. The pattern of which events can influence which others, determined by spacetime's light cones and how they connect. Coordinate systems are negotiable; these causal relationships are not.
Curvature. The departure of spacetime geometry from flatness, revealed by effects such as nearby freely falling objects accelerating relative to one another. Matter and energy influence curvature, but spacetime can be curved even in empty regions. No external dimension is required for it to bend into.
Decoherence. The suppression of observable quantum interference as a system becomes entangled with its environment. It helps explain why everyday objects - including cucumbers - behave classically. By itself, it does not explain why a measurement yields one particular outcome.
Einstein Equation. The central equation of general relativity, usually called the Einstein field equations. It relates spacetime curvature to the energy, momentum, and stresses of matter and fields. Matter influences geometry; geometry guides motion. The mathematics takes somewhat longer.
Equivalence Principle. In a sufficiently small, freely falling laboratory, nongravitational experiments behave as they would in an inertial laboratory without gravity. Over larger regions, tidal effects can reveal the difference. A foundation of general relativity, and the reason lifts feature so heavily in physics thought experiments.
Event Horizon. The boundary of a black hole's region of no escape: once inside, no future-directed signal can reach the distant exterior. Not light. Not a strongly worded letter.
Geodesic. The straightest possible path in a given geometry. Ideal freely falling objects follow spacetime geodesics when no nongravitational forces act on them.
Holographic Principle. The proposal, motivated by black-hole entropy, that a gravitational region's physical information can be described by degrees of freedom associated with a boundary of one fewer spatial dimension. Holographic dualities give this idea concrete form in particular theoretical settings. Its application to our universe remains an open question.
Light Cone
The boundary between lightlike directions and the timelike or spacelike directions at an event. Its future and past portions show the local limits on where signals can go and where they can come from. Cucumbers must remain within the speed limit.
Lorentz Transform. A transformation relating space and time coordinates in inertial frames while preserving the spacetime interval. Transformations between frames in relative motion underpin time dilation, length contraction, and the relativity of simultaneity.
Manifold. A space that can be described in small patches using ordinary coordinates, even when its overall shape or connectivity is more complicated. Earth's surface is a familiar example. A manifold provides the underlying space; a metric supplies its geometry.
Metric. The mathematical object that determines local spacetime intervals and allows lengths and elapsed times to be calculated along paths. Its components depend on the coordinates chosen. Complicated coordinates can make even flat spacetime look mathematically unwelcoming.
Proper Time. The elapsed time measured by an ideal clock along its own path through spacetime. Observers may use different coordinates, but they agree on what that clock records between two events on its path.
Simultaneity. The assignment of the same time to different events. Spatially separated events that are simultaneous in one inertial frame need not be simultaneous in another. Neither frame is mistaken; their definitions of "now" differ.
Singularity. A breakdown or incompleteness of the classical spacetime description, often associated with unbounded curvature. Technically, a key sign is a freely falling or lightlike path that cannot be continued despite reaching its end in a finite path parameter. A singularity need not be an ordinary point within spacetime. The map itself has run out.
Spacetime Interval. The invariant combination of spatial and temporal separation. In special relativity, inertial observers agree on it even when they disagree about distance and duration separately. In curved spacetime, the metric defines the corresponding local interval between infinitesimally nearby events.
Stress-Energy Tensor. The tensor describing energy density, momentum density, energy flow, pressure, and shear stresses. It supplies the matter-and-fields side of the Einstein field equations. It constrains curvature, although it does not by itself determine the entire spacetime geometry.
Tensor. A mathematical object whose components change according to precise rules when coordinates change, leaving the object itself unchanged. Scalars and vectors are simple examples; the metric and curvature tensors are more elaborate ones. Different numbers, consistent physics.
Tidal Force. The relative acceleration of nearby freely falling objects, producing stretching or squeezing. In general relativity, these effects reveal spacetime curvature. Their strength depends on the circumstances.
Time Dilation. Differences in clock rates associated with relative motion or gravitational circumstances. Two inertial observers in relative motion can each assign a slower rate to the other's clock, using their own frame's definition of simultaneity. Gravitational comparisons are different: for stationary clocks around Earth, the lower clock runs more slowly than the higher one. The mutual-slowing argument does not apply to that comparison.
Worldline. The path an object traces through spacetime: where it is and when it is there. Your biography, with fewer adjectives and more coordinates.
Wormhole. A hypothetical connection between otherwise separate or distant regions of spacetime. Some solutions of general relativity contain wormholes, but traversable versions typically require exotic stress-energy. Mathematical availability does not guarantee a usable passage.

Difficulty: Medium. Argues time's flow may be more fundamental than most physicists admit, which Weinstein would have opinions about.

Cox, Brian, and Jeff Forshaw. Why Does E=mc²? (And Why Should We Care?). Da Capo Press, 2009.
COMMENT: A patient, readable derivation of the equation everyone quotes and almost nobody can actually get to. Difficulty: Medium. Builds the result up from the same postulates this book keeps joking about, minus the dolphin.

One Last Thing

If you enjoyed this book, a short review wherever you bought it helps other readers find the Bureau.

Readers who would like the mathematics behind Derek's cases explained directly can find it in Lothar J. Musiol's nonfiction Physics, Actually series, which covers relativity with equations and considerably fewer fictional clocks.
